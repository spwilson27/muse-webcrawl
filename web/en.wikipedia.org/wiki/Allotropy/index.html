Allotropy

Allotropy or allotropism is the property of some chemical elements to exist in two or more different forms in the same physical state, in which the atoms are bonded together in different arrangements. The different forms are called allotropes of that element. The term was coined by Jöns Jacob Berzelius in the 1840s.

Examples

Carbon is the best-known allotropic element: diamond and graphite differ radically — diamond is a transparent, extremely hard three-dimensional covalent network, whereas graphite is soft, opaque and layered; other carbon allotropes include graphene, fullerenes (such as buckminsterfullerene, C60), carbon nanotubes and amorphous carbon.

Oxygen exists as dioxygen (O2) and ozone (O3). Phosphorus occurs as white, red and black phosphorus. Sulfur has numerous allotropes, including the yellow S8 rings of rhombic and monoclinic sulfur. Among metals, iron changes between body-centred cubic (ferrite) and face-centred cubic (austenite) forms with temperature — a transformation fundamental to steelmaking — and tin undergoes "tin pest", converting from metallic white tin to brittle grey tin below 13.2 °C.

Enantiotropy and monotropy

Allotropes that transform reversibly at a definite transition temperature (such as rhombic and monoclinic sulfur) are called enantiotropic; those in which only one form is stable at all temperatures, the other being metastable (such as diamond relative to graphite), are called monotropic. Allotropy applies to elements only; the analogous phenomenon in compounds is called polymorphism.
