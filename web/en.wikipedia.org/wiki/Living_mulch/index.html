Living mulch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
White clover living mulch in a vegetable field

A living mulch is a cover crop grown together with, or underneath, a main cash crop for most or all of the growing season, performing the functions of a mulch — suppressing weeds, protecting the soil surface from erosion and evaporation, and improving soil structure — while remaining alive rather than being killed and left as residue. The practice is a form of intercropping used in sustainable, organic and regenerative agriculture and horticulture.

Management and species

The classic living mulch is a low, shade-tolerant legume such as white clover (Trifolium repens) or subterranean clover, sown under cereals, maize, brassicas or orchard trees. Because the mulch competes with the crop for water, light and nutrients, it is usually managed — mown, grazed, or partially suppressed with narrow herbicide bands or by strip-tillage along the planting row — so that the cash crop keeps the advantage. In return, legume mulches fix atmospheric nitrogen and can contribute substantial amounts to the following crop, the permanent ground cover shelters beneficial insects and pollinators, and the living roots feed soil biology year-round. Living mulches are also used in vineyards and orchards as permanent sward, and in no-till systems where a rolled or winter-killed cover crop shades into the related concept of a "dead mulch". Research trials report weed suppression comparable to herbicides in some systems, traded against yield penalties of roughly 5–15% in dry years if competition is not controlled.
