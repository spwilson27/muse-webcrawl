Jacobean literature is the body of English and Scottish literature produced during the reign of James I (1603–1625), named from Jacobus, the Latin form of James. It followed the flowering of Elizabethan literature and is especially celebrated for its drama, much of it darker and more cynical in tone than its predecessor.

Drama

The later plays of William Shakespeare — including King Lear, Macbeth, Antony and Cleopatra, and the late romances such as The Tempest — belong to this period, as do the comedies and masques of Ben Jonson (Volpone, The Alchemist). The revenge tragedies of John Webster (The Duchess of Malfi) and the city comedies of Thomas Middleton exemplify the era's fascination with corruption, violence, and social satire.

Prose and poetry

The King James Bible (1611), commissioned by the king, became the most influential prose work of the age. Poetry included the metaphysical verse of John Donne, the "Sons of Ben" classicism of Jonson's circle, and the works of George Herbert and Aemilia Lanyer. In prose, Francis Bacon's Novum Organum (1620) set out a new philosophy of science.

Character

Jacobean writing is often marked by a questioning, disillusioned mood — a response to political anxiety, court corruption, and shifting religious certainties — that distinguishes it from the relative optimism of the Elizabethan age.
