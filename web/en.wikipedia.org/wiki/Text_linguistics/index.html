Text linguistics

Text linguistics is a branch of linguistics that deals with texts as systems of communication. Instead of taking the sentence as the largest unit of language, text linguistics studies how sentences combine into coherent, meaningful wholes, and how texts are produced and understood in context.

Texts are studied as communicative units larger than the sentence

Standards of textuality

In their influential 1981 model, Robert-Alain de Beaugrande and Wolfgang U. Dressler proposed seven standards of textuality:

  • Cohesion — the grammatical and lexical connections that link one part of a text to another.
  • Coherence — the underlying logical connectedness of the ideas expressed.
  • Intentionality — the writer's intention to produce a meaningful text.
  • Acceptability — the reader's willingness to treat the sequence as a text.
  • Informativity — the balance of known and new information.
  • Situationality — the relevance of the text to its situation of occurrence.
  • Intertextuality — the text's dependence on knowledge of other texts.

History and scope

The field grew out of discourse analysis and the Prague School's work on functional sentence perspective, and from stylistics and rhetoric. Text linguists study such phenomena as anaphora, theme and rheme, text types and genres, and the ways readers construct meaning. It is closely related to discourse analysis, though text linguistics traditionally focuses more on written texts and their structural properties, while discourse analysis emphasises language in social interaction.
