Outside broadcast

An outside broadcast (OB) is a television or radio production made outside a permanent studio, at the location of the event being covered — a stadium, concert hall, street or battlefield. In North America such productions are often called "remote broadcasts" or remotes. Outside broadcasts are the standard means of televising sport, state occasions, elections and breaking news.

Production

A traditional OB is centred on a scanner or production truck — a mobile control room carrying a vision mixer, sound desk, camera control units, replay servers and graphics. Multiple cameras are cabled back to the truck by multicore or fibre (triax), and the finished programme is returned to the broadcaster by microwave link, satellite uplink, or increasingly by bonded cellular and IP contribution networks. Large events may deploy dozens of cameras, separate trucks for replay and audio, and scaffold-mounted or aerial camera positions.

Newer methods

Remote integration ("REMI") production, accelerated from the 2010s, keeps only cameras and microphones on site: raw feeds are sent over fibre or the internet to a centralised studio gallery where the programme is directed, cutting cost and travel. At the smallest scale, a single reporter with a smartphone or a "live shot" backpack unit can produce a basic outside broadcast for news.

History

Early OBs date to the first experimental television services of the 1930s, including coverage of the 1937 coronation of George VI and the 1936 Berlin Olympics. Post-war, OB technology matured alongside televised sport, and innovations from instant replay to super slow motion were largely driven by outside broadcasts.
