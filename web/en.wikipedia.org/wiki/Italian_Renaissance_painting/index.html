Italian Renaissance painting

[Image: The Holy Trinity by Masaccio, Santa Maria Novella, Florence]
Masaccio's Holy Trinity (c. 1427), a landmark of linear perspective.

Italian Renaissance painting is the painting of the period in Italian history known as the Renaissance, conventionally divided into the Early Renaissance (roughly 1400–1495) and the High Renaissance (c. 1495–1527), followed by Mannerism. It developed a revolutionary new naturalism, applying linear perspective, anatomy and classical themes to religious and secular subjects.

Origins

The proto-Renaissance work of Giotto around 1300, with its solid, emotionally direct figures, is usually taken as the starting point. In Florence in the 1420s Masaccio's frescoes — the Brancacci Chapel cycle and the Holy Trinity — demonstrated one-point perspective as systematised by Brunelleschi, while writers such as Alberti codified the theory of painting in treatises like De pictura (1435).

Early Renaissance

Artists such as Fra Angelico, Paolo Uccello, Piero della Francesca, Andrea Mantegna and Sandro Botticelli spread the new style through the courts and cities of Italy, working mainly in fresco and tempera, later adopting oil paint partly under Netherlandish influence (notably through Antonello da Messina and Giovanni Bellini in Venice).

High Renaissance

Around 1500 the brief High Renaissance produced the canonical masters: Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo and Raphael, followed in Venice by Giorgione and Titian, whose colour-driven (colorito) approach contrasted with Florentine emphasis on drawing (disegno). The Sack of Rome in 1527 dispersed artists and encouraged the stylised Mannerism of Pontormo, Parmigianino and Bronzino.

Legacy

Italian Renaissance painting established the conventions of Western representational art — perspective space, idealised anatomy, chiaroscuro and balanced composition — that dominated European art academies for four centuries.
