Literary mode

In literary theory, a mode is a broad, transhistorical manner or attitude of writing — such as the pastoral, the satirical, the elegiac or the heroic — that recurs across different periods, genres and media. Modes are distinguished from genres, which are more historically specific kinds (such as the sonnet, the detective novel or the revenge tragedy), and from the three classical "modes of narration" (lyric, epic and dramatic). A single work may belong to one genre while drawing on several modes.

Northrop Frye's theory of modes

The most influential modern account is that of Northrop Frye in Anatomy of Criticism (1957). Frye classified fictional "modes" according to the hero's power of action relative to other people and to the environment:

  • Myth — the hero is a god, superior in kind to both men and nature.
  • Romance — the hero is superior in degree to men and nature; a world of marvels and quests (the "high mimetic" of legend).
  • High mimetic — the hero (king, leader) is superior in degree to other men but not to nature; the mode of epic and tragedy.
  • Low mimetic — the hero is one of us; the mode of the realistic novel and comedy of manners.
  • Ironic — the hero is inferior to the reader in power or intelligence; the mode of much modern fiction.

Frye suggested that European literature had moved cyclically through these modes over two millennia.

Other modal categories

  • Pastoral — idealised rural life and shepherds, from Theocritus and Virgil onward.
  • Elegiac — mourning for the dead; the elegy.
  • Satirical — ridicule of vice and folly.
  • Comic / tragic — the two master modes of drama, oriented to integration or isolation of the hero.
  • Gothic — horror, the supernatural and decayed grandeur in later literature.
  • Picaresque — episodic adventures of a roguish hero.

Mode versus genre

Critics such as Alastair Fowler (in Kinds of Literature, 1982) treat modes as partial or adjectival extensions of genres: a work may be a novel (genre) written in a pastoral or gothic mode. Modes thus name a selection of a genre's features — a mood, tone or structural attitude — abstracted and applied across kinds.
