Computable analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Computable analysis studies which functions on real numbers a machine can compute

Computable analysis is the study of which parts of real analysis and functional analysis can be carried out in a computable manner. It is closely related to constructive analysis and to the theory of computation over the real numbers. The field asks, for example, which real numbers can be approximated by algorithms, which functions on the reals are computable, and which theorems of analysis remain true when all objects are required to be computable.

Computable real numbers

A real number is computable if there is an algorithm (equivalently, a Turing machine) that produces rational approximations to it with arbitrarily small, specified error — for instance, a machine that on input n outputs a rational number within 2−n of the target. The computable reals form a countable real-closed subfield of the real numbers containing π, e, and every algebraic number. However, most real numbers are not computable, and there exist definable reals — such as the number encoding the halting problem (a form of Chaitin's constant Ω) — that provably cannot be computed.

Computable functions

A function on the reals is computable when a machine can transform arbitrarily good approximations of the input into approximations of the output. Two main frameworks formalize this: the Type-2 Theory of Effectivity (TTE), developed in the tradition of Grzegorczyk and Lacombe and systematized by Klaus Weihrauch, in which machines operate on infinite sequences representing points; and the Markov/Banach–Mazur approach of the Russian school of constructive mathematics. A celebrated consequence is that every computable function on the reals is continuous, so discontinuous operations such as deciding whether a real number equals zero are not computable. TTE also yields clean computability results for operations of analysis: integration of computable functions is computable, while differentiation is not in general.

Relation to constructive mathematics

Computable analysis supplies models for constructive analysis and clarifies the computational content of classical theorems: results such as the intermediate value theorem hold only in weakened or approximate forms for computable functions, while others, like the attainment of maxima by computable continuous functions on [0,1], remain valid. The subject also connects with complexity theory, which asks not just whether analytic problems are computable but how efficiently — for example, the complexity of computing solutions of differential equations.
