Trajan's Dacian Wars

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trajan's Dacian Wars (101–102 and 105–106 AD) were two military campaigns fought between the Roman Empire and the kingdom of Dacia under King Decebalus, during the reign of the emperor Trajan. The wars ended with the Roman conquest of Dacia, which became a Roman province; they are among the most extensively commemorated campaigns in Roman art, chiefly through the spiral relief of Trajan's Column in Rome.

Background

Dacia, roughly corresponding to modern Romania and Moldova, had long been a powerful and wealthy kingdom north of the Danube, enriched by gold and silver mines. Under Domitian, an earlier war (85–89 AD) had ended inconclusively: Decebalus had defeated a Roman army yet accepted a peace that made him a client king, receiving Roman subsidies and engineers while continuing to strengthen his kingdom. Trajan, who became emperor in 98 AD, regarded the arrangement as humiliating and Dacia's growing power as a threat to the Danube frontier.

First war (101–102)

In 101 Trajan led a large army across the Danube, building roads and a great stone bridge designed by Apollodorus of Damascus near Drobeta. After hard campaigning, including the victory at the Second Battle of Tapae, Roman forces advanced on the Dacian capital, Sarmizegetusa Regia. Decebalus sued for peace in 102 on terms considerably harsher than before: he surrendered territory, demolished fortresses, gave up his artillery and deserters, and renounced an independent foreign policy.

Second war (105–106)

Decebalus used the peace to rebuild his strength and attacked Roman garrisons in 105, prompting Trajan to launch a second, final campaign of conquest. Roman columns converged on Sarmizegetusa, which fell after a siege in 106. Decebalus fled but, pursued by Roman cavalry, took his own life rather than be captured; his head and right hand were sent to Rome as proof. Organised resistance collapsed, though Roman mopping-up operations continued.

Aftermath

Dacia was annexed as a province, its gold mines passing under imperial control and greatly enriching Rome; the victory funded Trajan's extensive building programme, including his Forum. The Dacian population was substantially displaced or assimilated, and the province was settled with colonists from across the empire. Roman Dacia endured until Aurelian withdrew the administration south of the Danube in 271 AD.

Trajan's Column, dedicated in 113 AD, narrates the wars in a continuous spiral frieze of some 155 scenes and remains one of the principal visual sources for Roman warfare, arms, and engineering — including depictions of the Danube bridge, legionary camps, and Dacian dress.
