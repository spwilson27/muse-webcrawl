Holodomor

The Holodomor (Ukrainian: Голодомор, from holod, "hunger", and moryty, "to kill by starvation") was a man-made famine in Soviet Ukraine from 1932 to 1933 that killed millions of Ukrainians. It is also known as the Great Famine and was part of the wider Soviet famine of 1930–1933, which affected other grain-growing regions of the USSR, including the North Caucasus, the Volga region, and Kazakhstan.

Estimates of the death toll in Ukraine vary, with scholarly estimates generally ranging from about 3.3 million to around 5 million deaths. The famine coincided with Joseph Stalin's policies of forced collectivization of agriculture, grain procurements imposed on collective farms and villages, the "blacklisting" of settlements accused of failing to meet quotas, restrictions on movement that prevented starving peasants from leaving Ukraine to seek food, and campaigns against Ukrainian cultural and political elites.

Causes and course

Beginning in 1928, the Soviet regime collectivized agriculture, expropriating the land of peasants — including the relatively prosperous kulaks, who were deported in large numbers. In 1932, unrealistically high grain procurement quotas were imposed on Ukraine. When villages failed to meet them, punitive measures followed: confiscation of all foodstuffs, including seed grain; fines in kind; and the closure of borders. A secret decree of January 1933 sealed Ukraine's borders to prevent peasants from fleeing. Mortality peaked in the spring of 1933, while the USSR continued to export grain abroad and refused international relief efforts.

Recognition and memory

Ukraine and numerous other countries officially recognize the Holodomor as a genocide of the Ukrainian people committed by the Soviet government, a view supported by many historians, while others describe it as a crime against humanity arising from criminal policy without specific genocidal intent against Ukrainians as such. The Russian Federation rejects the genocide characterization. The Holodomor is commemorated in Ukraine on the fourth Saturday of November, and memorials stand in Kyiv and in many cities worldwide.
