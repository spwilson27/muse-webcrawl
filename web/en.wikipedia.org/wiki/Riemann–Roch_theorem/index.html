Riemann–Roch theorem

The Riemann–Roch theorem is one of the central results of algebraic geometry. It relates the geometry of a compact Riemann surface (or, more generally, a smooth algebraic curve) to the dimensions of spaces of meromorphic functions and differentials on it.

FieldAlgebraic geometry
Named afterBernhard Riemann, Gustav Roch
Introduced1850s–1860s (Riemann); 1865 (Roch)

Statement

For a divisor D on a compact Riemann surface X of genus g, the theorem states that

l(D) − l(K − D) = deg(D) + 1 − g,

where l(D) is the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions whose zeros and poles are bounded by D, and K is a canonical divisor.

History

Bernhard Riemann proved an inequality, l(D) ≥ deg(D) − g + 1, in his work on Abelian functions. His student Gustav Roch identified the correction term as the dimension of a space of differentials, giving the full equality. The theorem was later generalized to higher dimensions by Hirzebruch and Grothendieck (the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem).

Applications

The theorem is fundamental for computing dimensions of linear systems of curves, studying embeddings of curves into projective space, and proving results such as the existence of nonconstant meromorphic functions on every compact Riemann surface.
