Gordian Knot

The Gordian Knot is a legend of Phrygian Gordium associated with Alexander the Great. It is often used as a metaphor for an intractable problem that can be solved easily by finding a loophole or by thinking in an unconventional way ("cutting the Gordian knot").

According to the legend, the Phrygians, in need of a king, consulted an oracle which decreed that the next man to enter the city driving an ox-cart should become king. A peasant farmer named Gordias arrived in this manner and was declared king. In gratitude, his son Midas dedicated the ox-cart to Zeus, tying it up with a highly intricate knot of cornel bark.

Later tradition added that whoever could untie the knot would become ruler of Asia. When Alexander the Great arrived at Gordium in 333 BC, he attempted to untie the knot. In the most famous version of the story, he cut it in half with a stroke of his sword. In another account, he loosened it by pulling out the linchpin of the cart.

The knot may have been a dynastic emblem of the Phrygian royal house, possibly a religious symbol, and its association with Gordias and Midas links it to the founding of the Phrygian capital.
