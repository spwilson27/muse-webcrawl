Term limit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A term limit is a legal restriction on the number of terms an officeholder may serve in a particular elected office. Term limits exist for presidents in most republics (typically two terms), for many governors, mayors and legislators (common in US states), and are a classic device for preventing the personal entrenchment of power and regularising political succession.

History and debates

The US two-term tradition was set by George Washington's retirement in 1797, broken only by Franklin D. Roosevelt (elected four times, 1932–44), and codified by the Twenty-second Amendment (1951). Worldwide, term-limit evasion — constitutional amendments, court rulings, placeholder successors — is a documented mechanism of democratic backsliding (debated in Russia, China after 2018's removal of the presidential term limit, and several Latin American and African states where limits have both restrained and failed to restrain incumbents). Political science finds term limits increase rotation and newcomer entry but shift power towards executives' staffs, bureaucracies and lobbyists, and weaken legislative expertise; effects on policy and corruption are mixed.
