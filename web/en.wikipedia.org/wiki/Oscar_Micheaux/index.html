Oscar Micheaux

Oscar Micheaux, pioneer of African-American cinema.

Oscar Devereaux Micheaux (January 2, 1884 – March 25, 1951) was an African-American author, film director and independent producer of more than 44 films. He is regarded as the first major African-American feature filmmaker and the most successful African-American filmmaker of the first half of the 20th century, producing "race films" for Black audiences outside the Hollywood system.

Career

Born in Metropolis, Illinois, Micheaux worked as a Pullman porter and then homesteaded in South Dakota, experiences he fictionalised in his novel The Conquest (1913). He founded the Micheaux Book & Film Company and adapted his novel The Homesteader into his first film in 1919. His best-known work, Within Our Gates (1920), was a response to D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation, depicting racial violence and lynching. Micheaux shot quickly on low budgets, sold his films directly to Black theatres, and continued into the sound era with films such as The Exile (1931), the first all-Black-cast sound feature.

Legacy

Micheaux's films addressed colourism, passing, religion and economic uplift within Black communities. He died in Charlotte, North Carolina, in 1951; he was later honoured with a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame and induction into the Black Filmmakers Hall of Fame.
