Holy chrism

Holy chrism, also called myrrh, holy anointing oil, or consecrated oil, is a consecrated oil used in the sacramental rites of the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and some other churches. In Western practice chrism is olive oil mixed with balsam or another fragrant resin; in Eastern practice it is compounded from olive oil and dozens of aromatic ingredients according to traditional recipes. It is blessed (in the Latin Church, solely by a bishop) at the annual Chrism Mass during Holy Week.

Uses

Chrism is used in the administration of the sacraments of Baptism (anointing after the water rite), Confirmation (where it is the essential matter of the sacrament, given with the words "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit"), and Holy Orders (anointing of a priest's hands and a bishop's head). It is also used in the dedication of churches and altars and in the blessing of bells and sacred vessels. In the Eastern Orthodox Church, chrism (holy myrrh) is the matter of the sacrament of Chrismation, ordinarily administered immediately after baptism, including to infants; its preparation is a solemn prerogative of autocephalous churches, traditionally performed during Holy Week by the patriarch or primate.

Symbolism

The word "chrism" derives from the Greek chrisma (χρῖσμα), "anointing" — the same root as Christos, "the Anointed One." Anointing with perfumed oil signifies the gift of the Holy Spirit, consecration and setting apart for a sacred purpose, spiritual strengthening, and the "fragrance of Christ" (cf. 2 Corinthians 2:15). In the Latin rite, the balsam's perfume is specifically cited as a symbol of the sweetness of the Christian life and virtues.
