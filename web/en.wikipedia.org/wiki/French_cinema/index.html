Cinema of France

The cinema of France is among the oldest and most influential national cinemas. France was the birthplace of cinema as a commercial medium: the Lumière brothers gave the first public film screening in Paris in 1895, and pioneers such as Georges Méliès and Alice Guy-Blaché built the first film industries there. France today sustains one of the world's largest film outputs, supported by the CNC (Centre national du cinéma) and a system of levies and broadcast investment designed to preserve the "cultural exception."

Early cinema and the interwar years

Before the First World War, companies such as Pathé and Gaumont dominated world film markets. The 1920s saw a French avant-garde of impressionist and surrealist filmmakers, and the coming of sound brought directors such as René Clair and Jean Vigo. The 1930s produced the poetic realism of Jean Renoir (La Grande Illusion, La Règle du jeu), Marcel Carné, and Jean Gabin's screen persona.

Postwar and the New Wave

After the war, critics of the journal Cahiers du cinéma — François Truffaut, Jean-Luc Godard, Claude Chabrol, Éric Rohmer, and Jacques Rivette — became the directors of the French New Wave (Nouvelle Vague) around 1959–1960, transforming world cinema with location shooting, low budgets, and the auteur theory.

Contemporary cinema

Modern French cinema ranges from international art-house successes to broad popular comedies such as The Intouchables (2011), one of the highest-grossing French-language films worldwide. The Cannes Film Festival, the César Awards, and France's dense network of cinemas — among the highest per capita in Europe — remain central institutions.
