Jain philosophy

Jain philosophy is the system of thought associated with Jainism, one of the oldest Indian religions, whose most recent teacher (tirthankara) was Mahavira (6th century BCE). Jain philosophy is distinguished by its radical commitment to non-violence (ahimsa), its doctrine of the many-sidedness of reality (anekantavada), and its belief that liberation is achieved by purifying the soul of karma.

Metaphysics

Jain thought divides reality into soul (jiva) and non-soul (ajiva), the latter including matter, space, time, and the media of motion and rest. Karma is conceived as subtle matter that sticks to the soul, obscuring its innate omniscience and bliss and binding it to rebirth. Liberation (moksha) comes when karmic influx is stopped and accumulated karma is shed through ascetic practice and right conduct.

Epistemology: anekantavada

The doctrine of anekantavada ("many-sidedness") holds that reality is too complex to be captured by a single viewpoint. Related doctrines are syadvada, the sevenfold predication expressing that every statement is true only in some respect ("somehow"), and nayavada, the theory of partial standpoints. Together they underpin the Jain ethic of intellectual non-violence and respect for opposing views.

Ethics

The five great vows of Jain monks — non-violence, truth, non-stealing, chastity, and non-possession — and their lay counterparts structure Jain life. Ahimsa extends to all living beings, motivating strict vegetarianism and care to avoid harming even insects and microbes.
