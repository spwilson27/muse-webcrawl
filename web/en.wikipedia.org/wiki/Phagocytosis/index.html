Phagocytosis (from Ancient Greek phagein, "to eat", and kytos, "cell") is the process by which a cell uses its plasma membrane to engulf a large particle (≥ 0.5 μm), giving rise to an internal compartment called the phagosome. It is one type of endocytosis. A cell that performs phagocytosis is called a phagocyte.

In the immune system of multicellular animals, phagocytosis is a major mechanism used to remove pathogens and cell debris. In single-celled organisms such as amoebae it is a means of feeding — one of the oldest cellular behaviours, evolutionarily conserved across eukaryotes. Élie Metchnikoff's discovery of phagocytes in the 1880s founded the field of cellular immunology and earned him a share of the 1908 Nobel Prize.

Steps

  1. Recognition and attachment. The phagocyte's receptors bind the particle directly (via pattern-recognition receptors detecting microbial molecules) or via opsonins — antibodies or complement C3b coating the particle ("opsonisation").
  2. Engulfment. Actin-driven pseudopods extend around the particle in a "zipper" fashion until it is enclosed in a phagosome.
  3. Phagosome maturation and killing. The phagosome fuses with lysosomes to form a phagolysosome, acidifies, and the particle is attacked with reactive oxygen species (the respiratory burst), antimicrobial peptides and degradative enzymes.
  4. Digestion and presentation. Debris is exocytosed; in professional antigen-presenting cells, peptides from the particle are displayed on MHC class II molecules to activate T cells.

Phagocytes

In mammals the "professional phagocytes" are neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and mast cells. Neutrophils are short-lived first responders; macrophages are long-lived tissue residents that also clear apoptotic cells silently (efferocytosis) — an essential housekeeping role, as billions of cells die daily. Defects in phagocyte function, as in chronic granulomatous disease (failure of the respiratory burst), cause recurrent severe infections.
