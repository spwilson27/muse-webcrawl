The Cathedral of Vasily the Blessed (Russian: Собор Василия Блаженного, Sobor Vasiliya Blazhennogo), commonly known as Saint Basil's Cathedral, is an Orthodox church in Red Square in Moscow, Russia, and is one of the most popular cultural symbols of the country. It was built from 1555 to 1561 on the orders of Ivan the Terrible and commemorates the capture of Kazan and Astrakhan. Its official name is the Cathedral of the Intercession of the Most Holy Theotokos on the Moat, after the moat that once ran along the Kremlin wall.

History

Ivan IV commissioned the church to celebrate the conquest of the Khanate of Kazan in 1552, with the central chapel consecrated to the Intercession of the Theotokos, whose feast fell on the day of the final assault. The architects are traditionally named as Barma and Postnik Yakovlev; a persistent legend holds that Ivan had them blinded so they could never build anything more beautiful, though historians consider this apocryphal, since Postnik is documented working on later projects. A ninth chapel was added in 1588 over the grave of Saint Basil (Vasily), a revered "fool for Christ" of Moscow, from whom the cathedral takes its popular name.

The building survived fires, the French occupation of 1812 (Napoleon allegedly ordered its destruction, which was never carried out), and the Soviet era: it was confiscated, secularised in 1929, and converted into a museum, narrowly escaping demolition plans said to have contemplated clearing Red Square for parades. Since 1990 it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site together with the Kremlin and Red Square; occasional religious services resumed in the 1990s, but the building remains a branch of the State Historical Museum.

Architecture

The cathedral is a cluster of nine chapels raised on a single foundation: a tall central tent-roofed church surrounded by eight chapels, four large and axial, four smaller between them, each crowned with a distinctive onion dome in different colours and patterns. The riotous colouring largely dates from the 17th century; in Ivan's time the church was white with golden domes. Brick galleries, ornamental kokoshnik gables, and two porches unify the composition. The plan has been interpreted as a symbolic, stone version of the heavenly Jerusalem and as an ensemble portrait of the feasts of the campaign against Kazan. The interior chapels are small and dim, decorated with iconostases and murals spanning the 16th to 19th centuries.
