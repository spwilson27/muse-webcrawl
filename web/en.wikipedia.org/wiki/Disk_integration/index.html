Disk integration

Disk integration, also known in integral calculus as the disk method, is a method for calculating the volume of a solid of revolution. When a plane region is revolved around an axis in its plane, the resulting solid can be approximated by thin disks (cylinders), and the volume is obtained as the limit of the sum of their volumes — that is, as an integral.

Method

If a curve y = f(x) is rotated about the x-axis between x = a and x = b, each cross-section perpendicular to the axis is a disk of radius f(x), so the volume is:

V = π ∫ab [f(x)]2 dx.

Washer method

When the region does not meet the axis, the cross-sections are washers — disks with holes — and the volume subtracts the inner radius: V = π ∫ ([R(x)]2 − [r(x)]2) dx. The related cylindrical shell method integrates along the axis instead of across it and may be simpler for some regions.
