Fine art

In European academic traditions, fine art is art developed primarily for aesthetics or creative expression, distinguishing it from decorative art or applied art, which also have to serve some practical function, such as pottery or most metalwork. The traditional fine arts are painting, sculpture, architecture, music, and poetry; in modern usage the term commonly covers painting, sculpture, drawing, printmaking, photography, and newer media such as film and installation art. The word "fine" denotes not so much the quality of the artwork as the purity of the discipline: fine art is made for its own sake rather than for utility.

History of the concept

The distinction emerged in 18th-century Europe as academies of art separated the "liberal arts" of painting and sculpture from guild crafts. Charles Batteux's Les beaux-arts réduits à un même principe (1746) grouped the beaux-arts around the imitation of nature, and the academy system institutionalised the hierarchy of genres, with history painting at the top. Romanticism and then modernism progressively challenged the academic definition: the readymades of Marcel Duchamp, conceptual art, and performance art of the 20th century questioned whether fine art must involve traditional skill or even a physical object at all. Today the term is used loosely for art intended for exhibition in galleries and museums and for the market of collectors, and is taught in art schools as the BFA (Bachelor of Fine Arts) discipline.

Media

  • Two-dimensional: drawing, painting, printmaking, calligraphy, photography.
  • Three-dimensional: sculpture, installation, land art, ceramics (when non-utilitarian).
  • Time-based: film, video art, performance art, sound art.
  • Conceptual and digital: conceptual art, digital art, new media art.

Fine art versus applied art

The boundary with applied art and design — where aesthetics are joined to function, as in architecture, industrial design, fashion, and illustration — is historically contingent and often contested. Movements such as the Arts and Crafts movement and the Bauhaus explicitly sought to erase it, while the art market and institutions (museums, art fairs, degree programmes) continue to maintain the categories in practice.
