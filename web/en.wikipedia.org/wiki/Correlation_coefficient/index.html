Correlation coefficient

A correlation coefficient is a numerical measure of some type of linear correlation, meaning a statistical relationship between two variables. The variables may be two columns of a given data set of observations, often called a sample, or two components of a multivariate random variable with a known distribution.

Several types of correlation coefficient exist, each with its own definition and own range of usability and characteristics. They all assume values in the range from −1 to +1, where ±1 indicates the strongest possible correlation and 0 indicates no correlation. As tools of analysis, correlation coefficients present certain problems, including the possibility that the conclusion of a strong association may be distorted by outliers, and the fact that correlation does not imply causation.

Pearson's correlation coefficient

The most common correlation coefficient is the Pearson correlation coefficient, denoted r, which measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship between two variables. It is defined as the covariance of the two variables divided by the product of their standard deviations. Karl Pearson developed the measure from a related idea introduced by Francis Galton in the 1880s, building on work by Auguste Bravais.

Rank correlation coefficients

Rank correlation coefficients, such as Spearman's rank correlation coefficient and Kendall's tau, measure the degree to which two variables tend to increase or decrease together monotonically. They are computed from the ranks of the data rather than the raw values and are less sensitive to outliers and non-normal distributions.

Interpretation

As a rough guide, absolute values around 0.1 are considered small, 0.3 moderate, and 0.5 or above large, though interpretation depends on context. A correlation coefficient near zero indicates little linear association, but the variables may still be strongly related in a non-linear way. Importantly, a correlation alone does not establish that one variable causes the other.
