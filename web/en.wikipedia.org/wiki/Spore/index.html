Spore

In biology, a spore is a unit of sexual or asexual reproduction that may be adapted for dispersal and for survival, often for extended periods, in unfavourable conditions. Spores form part of the life cycles of many plants, algae, fungi, and protozoans. They are usually single-celled and haploid, and unlike seeds they contain little stored food.

Plant spores

In seedless vascular plants such as ferns and lycophytes, and in bryophytes such as mosses, the haploid spore is produced by meiosis in the sporangium of the diploid sporophyte generation and germinates into a free-living haploid gametophyte. Most such plants are homosporous, producing one type of spore; heterosporous plants produce small microspores and larger megaspores, a condition that preceded the evolution of the seed habit in seed plants, where the megaspore is retained and develops into the female gametophyte within the ovule.

Fungal spores

Fungi produce enormous numbers of spores both asexually (mitospores, such as conidia) and sexually (meiospores, such as ascospores within asci and basidiospores on basidia). Fungal spores are the principal means of dispersal; airborne spores also act as allergens and are important in plant pathology.

Bacterial endospores

Some bacteria, notably in the genera Bacillus and Clostridium, form endospores — highly resistant dormant structures produced inside the cell for survival rather than reproduction. Endospores withstand boiling, desiccation, radiation and many disinfectants, and can remain viable for years, making them a central concern in food sterilization and in diseases such as anthrax, tetanus and botulism.

Dispersal and survival

Spores are disseminated by wind, water and animals. Their resistant walls — in plants and fungi often containing the exceptionally durable biopolymer sporopollenin — permit survival through desiccation and temperature extremes, and fossil spores document some of the earliest evidence of life on land, from Ordovician cryptospores onward.
