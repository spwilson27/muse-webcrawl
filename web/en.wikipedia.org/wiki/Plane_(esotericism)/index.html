Plane (esotericism)

In esotericism and occultism, a plane is a level, region, or state of reality believed to exist alongside or beyond the ordinary physical world. Different traditions divide existence into different numbers of planes, commonly conceived as stacked in order of increasing subtlety or spirituality: the physical plane is the densest and lowest, while above it lie planes associated with emotion, mind, and spirit. The concept is central to Theosophy and to many systems derived from it, and analogous ideas appear in Hermeticism, Rosicrucianism, and modern New Age thought.

Theosophical planes

In Theosophy, as systematised by Helena Blavatsky and developed by later writers such as Charles Webster Leadbeater and Annie Besant, the cosmos consists of seven planes:

  1. Physical plane — the world of matter, subdivided into solid, liquid, gaseous, and four etheric sub-planes.
  2. Astral plane — the world of emotion and desire, also called the kamic plane; the astral body is said to travel here during sleep and after death.
  3. Mental plane — the world of thought, divided into a lower concrete part (manas) and a higher abstract part, the "causal" level.
  4. Buddhic plane — the plane of intuition and spiritual unity.
  5. Atmic plane — the plane of pure spirit or will.
  6. Monadic plane — the level of the monad or divine spark.
  7. Adi plane — the highest, divine plane.

Each plane is said to be composed of its own kind of matter, interpenetrating the planes below it, so that all planes occupy the same space at different "frequencies" or densities.

Related concepts

The astral plane is the best-known of these levels in popular culture, figuring in accounts of astral projection, out-of-body experiences, and lucid dreaming. In Hermetic and Kabbalistic traditions, layered worlds such as the four worlds of Kabbalah (Atziluth, Beri'ah, Yetzirah, and Assiah) offer a parallel scheme. Hindu philosophy's lokas and the Buddhist cosmology of realms likewise divide existence into ascending levels, though with different metaphysical foundations.

Criticism

The planes have no empirical support and are not recognised by science; critics regard them as unfalsifiable metaphysical constructs. Believers and practitioners respond that the planes are apprehended through meditation and clairvoyant investigation rather than physical instruments.
