In Greek mythology, the Minotaur (Ancient Greek: Μινώταυρος, "the Bull of Minos") is a mythical creature with the head and tail of a bull and the body of a man. He dwelt at the centre of the Labyrinth, an elaborate maze-like construction designed by the architect Daedalus and his son Icarus, on the command of King Minos of Crete. The Minotaur was eventually killed by the Athenian hero Theseus.

Myth

Minos, having prayed to Poseidon for a sign of divine favour, received a magnificent white bull from the sea but refused to sacrifice it as promised. In punishment, Poseidon (in some accounts with Aphrodite's help) caused Minos's wife Pasiphaë to lust after the bull; with the aid of Daedalus, who built her a hollow wooden cow, she conceived the Minotaur. The creature, whose proper name was Asterion, grew ferocious and fed on human flesh. Minos had Daedalus construct the Labyrinth to contain him.

After defeating Athens in war, Minos demanded a tribute of seven Athenian youths and seven maidens every nine years (some sources say annually) to be fed to the Minotaur. Theseus, son of the Athenian king Aegeus, volunteered as one of the tribute youths, intending to kill the monster. Minos's daughter Ariadne, having fallen in love with Theseus, gave him a ball of thread — devised by Daedalus — by which he retraced his path through the Labyrinth after slaying the Minotaur, typically shown in ancient art killing the beast with a sword.

Interpretation

The myth has been linked to the historical prominence of bull imagery in Minoan Crete — bull-leaping frescos, bull-head rhytons, and horned altars at palaces such as Knossos, where the sprawling palace complex itself may have inspired the idea of the Labyrinth. The tribute story may reflect a folk memory of Athenian subjection to Minoan sea power. The Minotaur remains a potent symbol in Western art and literature, from Dante's Inferno, where the Minotaur guards the violent, to Picasso's Minotauromachy and modern fantasy.
