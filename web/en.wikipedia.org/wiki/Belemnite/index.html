Belemnites are an extinct group of marine cephalopod molluscs, superficially squid-like, that lived from the Late Devonian (or possibly the Carboniferous) until the end of the Cretaceous period, when they died out in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event along with the ammonites and non-avian dinosaurs. They belonged to the order Belemnitida within the coleoid cephalopods, the group that also includes modern squid, cuttlefish and octopuses.

The name derives from the Greek belemnon, meaning a dart or javelin, a reference to the shape of their most commonly fossilised part: the bullet-shaped internal shell known as the guard, or rostrum.

Anatomy

Belemnites possessed an internal shell divided into three parts: the solid, calcitic guard (rostrum) at the rear, a chambered phragmocone in the middle, and a thin pro-ostracum extending forward. Like modern squid, they had ten arms equipped with hooks rather than suckers, a mantle, fins, and a siphon for jet propulsion. Exceptionally preserved specimens show they also had an ink sac, as in modern coleoids.

Fossil record

The guards are among the most common fossils in Jurassic and Cretaceous marine rocks and are important index fossils for dating strata. In British folklore, belemnite guards were known as "thunderbolts", believed to have fallen from the sky during storms. Well-known genera include Belemnites, Hibolithes and the giant Megateuthis, whose guards could reach 46 cm in length.
