Lundenwic

Lundenwic was the Anglo-Saxon trading settlement and port of London, established from about AD 600 to the west of the abandoned Roman city of Londinium, in the area now covered by Covent Garden and the Strand in the modern West End. At its height in the 8th century it covered around 60 hectares and was one of the great emporia (trading ports, or wics) of early medieval north-western Europe.

History

After the Roman withdrawal from Britain, settlement at London initially contracted. From around 600, Saxon traders re-established a port on the gravel terraces north of the Thames, outside the Roman walls (which appear to have remained largely empty). Bede called London an "emporium of many peoples", and Lundenwic traded with Dorestad, Quentovic, and other continental ports, and with other English wics such as Hamwic (Southampton) and Gipeswic (Ipswich).

Repeated Viking attacks — recorded assaults in 841, 851, and 871 — made the exposed site untenable. In 886, Alfred the Great re-founded London within the defensible Roman walls (Lundenburh), and Lundenwic was abandoned, its site reverting to open ground known as the "old wic", preserved in the name Aldwych ("old trading settlement").

Archaeology

The location of Lundenwic was a long-standing puzzle until excavations in the 1980s and 1990s, notably at the Royal Opera House site and Jubilee Hall, revealed planned streets, workshops, and imported pottery confirming an extensive middle-Saxon settlement west of the City.
