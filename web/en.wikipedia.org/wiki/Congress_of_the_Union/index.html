The Congress of the Union (Spanish: Congreso de la Unión), formally the General Congress of the United Mexican States (Congreso General de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos), is the legislature of the federal government of Mexico. It consists of two chambers: the Senate of the Republic (Cámara de Senadores) and the Chamber of Deputies (Cámara de Diputados). Its composition, powers and procedures are set out in Articles 50–79 of the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States of 1917.

Composition

The Senate has 128 members: two elected by plurality in each of the 31 states and Mexico City, one additional "first minority" senator per state awarded to the runner-up party, and 32 elected by national proportional representation; senators serve six-year terms. The Chamber of Deputies has 500 members — 300 elected in single-member districts and 200 by proportional representation — serving three-year terms. A 2014 constitutional reform permitted consecutive re-election of legislators (deputies for up to four terms, senators for two), ending the no-re-election rule that had characterised Mexican politics since the Revolution.

Powers

The Congress enacts federal laws, approves the federal budget (the expenditure budget being the exclusive prerogative of the Chamber of Deputies), declares war, ratifies treaties (a Senate function), confirms certain appointments, and may impeach high officials. Each chamber also has exclusive powers: the Senate analyses foreign policy and ratifies the appointment of ambassadors and senior military officers, while the Chamber of Deputies oversees public accounts through the Superior Auditor of the Federation. Ordinary sessions run from 1 September to 15 December and from 1 February to 30 April; a Permanent Commission sits during recesses.

History

Mexican legislatures date from the Congress of Chilpancingo (1813) and the Constitution of Apatzingán; the bicameral federal Congress was created by the Constitution of 1824, abolished under the centralist regimes, restored in 1857 (initially unicameral) and given its present bicameral form in 1874 and under the 1917 Constitution. During the long hegemony of the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI) in the 20th century, Congress was largely subordinate to the presidency; since the opposition won a Chamber majority in 1997, it has become a genuine centre of political negotiation.
