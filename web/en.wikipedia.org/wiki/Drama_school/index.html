A drama school is an institution that provides professional vocational training for actors and other theatre practitioners. Distinct from university drama departments, which tend to mix theory and practice, conservatoire-style drama schools concentrate on intensive practical training in acting technique, voice, movement and stagecraft, usually over two or three years, culminating in public productions and industry showcases.

Training

Core acting curricula draw on the systems of Stanislavski and his successors, alongside approaches such as Meisner technique, Michael Chekhov technique and Lecoq-based physical theatre. Voice work develops breath, resonance and dialect; movement classes cover stage combat, dance, mask and animal studies. Students perform in progressively larger productions and are introduced to screen acting, radio and audition technique as graduation approaches. Many schools also train stage managers, technicians, directors, designers and playwrights.

History and notable schools

Systematic actor training emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with institutions such as London's Royal Academy of Dramatic Art (RADA, founded 1904), the Central School of Speech and Drama (1906) and LAMDA (1861), and in the United States the Juilliard School's drama division (1968) and the Yale School of Drama. In the UK, the Federation of Drama Schools represents the leading conservatoires, and accreditation standards are overseen by industry bodies.

Admissions and debates

Entry is by highly competitive audition, and schools are regularly debated over access, cost and diversity: with few places relative to applicants, critics argue drama schools can entrench privilege, prompting outreach schemes and audition-fee reforms. Graduates enter a precarious profession in which a small minority achieve prominence, but alumni lists of major schools include many of the most celebrated stage and screen actors of the past century.
