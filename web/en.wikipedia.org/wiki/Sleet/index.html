Sleet

Sleet is a term whose meaning differs between countries. In the United States, sleet refers to ice pellets — small, translucent balls of ice formed when raindrops or melted snowflakes freeze while falling through a sub-freezing layer of air near the ground. In the United Kingdom, Ireland and most Commonwealth countries, sleet instead means a mixture of rain and snow, i.e. partially melted snowflakes or snow falling together with rain (sometimes called rain-snow mix in North America).

Ice pellets (American usage)

Ice pellets form in winter storms when snow falls into a warm layer aloft (a "warm nose" in the temperature profile), melts wholly or partly, then enters a deep freezing layer near the surface deep enough for the drops to refreeze before landing. The pellets are generally less than 5 mm in diameter, bounce on impact, and make a characteristic ticking sound. They differ from freezing rain, in which drops remain liquid in a shallow cold layer and freeze on contact with surfaces, forming glaze ice; and from graupel, which is soft, opaque and formed by riming in convective clouds.

On aviation and weather observations (METAR), ice pellets are coded PL. Significant sleet accumulations can make roads treacherous, though usually less damaging than the ice storms produced by freezing rain.

Rain and snow mixed (British usage)

In British usage, sleet is precipitation that falls as partly melted snowflakes or as a mixture of rain and snow — the wet, slushy precipitation of marginal winter days when temperatures hover just above freezing. This usage corresponds to the American "rain-snow mix" or "wintry mix".
