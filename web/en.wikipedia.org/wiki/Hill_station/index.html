Hill station

A hill station is a town located at a higher elevation than the nearby plain, developed during the colonial era in Asia and Africa as a retreat from the summer heat and disease of the lowlands. The term is particularly associated with British India, where dozens of hill stations were established in the Himalayas, the Western Ghats, and other ranges.

History

From the 1820s onward, British officials, soldiers, and their families migrated seasonally to the hills. Simla became the summer capital of British India in 1864, with the entire government relocating there each year; other major stations included Darjeeling, Ooty (Udhagamandalam), Nainital, Mussoorie, and Munnar. Similar settlements arose in other colonies: the Dutch in Java (Bogor), the French at Da Lat in Vietnam, the Americans at Baguio in the Philippines, and the British in Malaya (Cameron Highlands) and at places such as Maymyo in Burma.

Features

Hill stations characteristically reproduce the architecture of the colonists' homelands — churches, clubs, racecourses, boarding schools, and promenades such as Darjeeling's Mall — and many became centres of tea cultivation and elite education. Mountain railways built to serve them, including the Darjeeling Himalayan Railway, are now UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Today

After independence the hill stations remained popular, passing to domestic tourists and elites; most continue as resorts and administrative or educational centres, though many suffer from overdevelopment, congestion, and water scarcity.
