Amaro (liqueur)

Amaro (Italian for "bitter"; plural amari) is an Italian herbal liqueur that is commonly consumed as an after-dinner digestif. It is usually bitter-sweet and syrupy, sometimes thick, with an alcohol content typically between 16% and 40%. Amari are produced by macerating herbs, roots, flowers, bark and citrus peels in alcohol, either neutral spirits or wine, then mixing with sugar syrup and ageing in casks or bottles.

History

Amaro descends from the medicinal herbal infusions prepared by monks and apothecaries from the Middle Ages onward. In the 19th century many recipes were commercialised: Bernardino Branca launched Fernet-Branca in Milan in 1845, Gaspare Campari developed his bitter aperitivo in the 1860s, and Averna (1868), Braulio (1875), Montenegro (1885) and Nonino followed. Hundreds of regional amari exist, often produced to closely guarded secret recipes.

Production and flavour

Common flavouring agents include gentian, wormwood, quinine (china bark), rhubarb root, orange peel, angelica, myrrh, juniper and dozens of other botanicals. The bittering agent and base determine the style: wine-based amari such as Cardamaro are lighter, while fernet-style amari are intensely bitter and less sweet. Amaro is normally served neat or on ice after a meal, though it is increasingly used in cocktails such as the Black Manhattan.

Well-known brands

Widely exported amari include Fernet-Branca, Averna, Braulio, Montenegro, Ramazzotti, Lucano, Vecchio Amaro del Capo, and the strongly Alpine and Sicilian styles of dell'Etna and Amaro dell'Etna producers. Related categories include German Kräuterlikör, French amer, and Alpine génépi preparations.
