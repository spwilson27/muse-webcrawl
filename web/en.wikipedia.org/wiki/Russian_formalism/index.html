Russian formalism was an influential school of literary criticism in Russia from roughly 1915 into the 1930s. It sought to place literary studies on a scientific footing by analysing the formal devices of literature — sound, metre, plot construction, style — rather than its biographical, historical, or ideological content. The movement's central theoretical concept was literariness (literaturnost): the qualities that make a verbal work a work of literature.

Groups and figures

The movement had two centres: the Moscow Linguistic Circle (founded 1915), which included Roman Jakobson and Pyotr Bogatyrev, and the Petrograd society OPOYAZ (Society for the Study of Poetic Language, 1916), centred on Viktor Shklovsky, Boris Eichenbaum, and Osip Brik. Yuri Tynyanov, Boris Tomashevsky, and Lidiya Ginzburg were closely associated. The school's name was originally a term of abuse by its opponents, later adopted by the formalists themselves.

Key ideas

  • Defamiliarisation (ostranenie): in his landmark essay "Art as Technique" (1917), Shklovsky argued that art renews perception by making the familiar strange, countering habitual automatisation.
  • Fabula and syuzhet: the distinction between the chronological story-material and its artistic arrangement as plot, later foundational for narratology.
  • Dominant and literary evolution: Tynyanov's systems model of genres evolving as hierarchies of devices shift.
  • Poetic vs. practical language: Jakobson's account of the poetic function of language.

Suppression and legacy

Formalism was attacked by Marxist critics (Trotsky devoted a chapter of Literature and Revolution to it) and suppressed under Stalin in the 1930s; Shklovsky publicly recanted. Through Jakobson's emigration, the Prague Linguistic Circle, and Tzvetan Todorov's 1965 French translations of the formalists' texts, the school profoundly influenced structuralism, the New Criticism (indirectly), film theory, and modern narratology.
