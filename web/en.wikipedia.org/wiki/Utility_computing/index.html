Utility computing

Utility computing is a service-provisioning model in which computing resources — processing power, storage, and software — are supplied to customers on demand and charged by usage, like a traditional public utility such as electricity or water. The customer pays for what is actually used rather than buying and maintaining fixed capacity. Utility computing is widely regarded as the conceptual forerunner of modern cloud computing.

History

The idea dates to the 1960s time-sharing era and was articulated by computer scientist John McCarthy in 1961: "computation may someday be organized as a public utility." Mainframe bureaus and time-sharing services of the 1960s–70s (such as Tymshare and GE Information Services) sold metered access. The vision revived in the late 1990s and 2000s with application service providers, grid computing, and products such as Sun Microsystems' Sun Grid (which briefly offered computing at $1 per CPU-hour) and HP's Utility Data Center. Amazon Web Services' launch of EC2 in 2006 — metered virtual servers billed by the hour — turned the utility model into the dominant paradigm, soon generalized as cloud computing with IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS delivery layers.

Characteristics

  • Metered billing — charges based on consumption of compute, storage, or bandwidth;
  • Elasticity — capacity scales up or down with demand without capital expenditure;
  • Virtualization — resources abstracted from physical hardware and pooled across many customers (multitenancy);
  • Self-service provisioning — users obtain resources through automated interfaces rather than procurement cycles.

Relationship to cloud computing

The terms overlap heavily: cloud computing is generally understood as the delivery mechanism and architectural model, utility computing as the business and pricing model. Not all cloud services are metered utilities (private clouds may not bill by use), and utility pricing can apply to services not described as "cloud." The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology's definition of cloud computing (2011) incorporated utility-style "measured service" as one of five essential characteristics.
