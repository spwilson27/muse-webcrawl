Symphonic poem

A symphonic poem or tone poem is a piece of orchestral music, usually in a single continuous movement, that illustrates or evokes the content of a poem, short story, novel, painting, landscape or other non-musical source. The form was developed in the mid-19th century, above all by Franz Liszt, who composed thirteen symphonic poems and coined the term (Symphonische Dichtung). Although the genre is associated with single-movement works, related forms include the concert overture and multi-movement programmatic works such as symphonic suites.

An orchestra performing a symphonic poem.

Origins and Liszt

The symphonic poem grew out of the Romantic concert overture (Mendelssohn's The Hebrides, Berlioz's overtures) and Beethoven's programmatic precedents. Liszt, working in Weimar in the 1850s, sought a flexible one-movement form in which musical themes are transformed to follow a poetic programme; his cycle includes Les préludes, Prometheus, Mazeppa, Orpheus and Hamlet. Unlike a classical symphony's abstract forms, the symphonic poem's structure is partly dictated by its subject.

Later composers

The genre flourished across Europe: Bedřich Smetana's cycle Má vlast (including Vltava) and Antonín Dvořák's late tone poems in Bohemia; Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's Romeo and Juliet and the Saint-Saëns tone poems (Danse macabre) in Russia and France; César Franck, Paul Dukas (The Sorcerer's Apprentice) and Claude Debussy (Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune) in France; Jean Sibelius (Finlandia, The Swan of Tuonela) in Finland; and, most expansively, Richard Strauss, whose Don Juan, Tod und Verklärung, Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche, Also sprach Zarathustra, Don Quixote and Ein Heldenleben represent the summit of the form. In the 20th century the genre shaded into works such as Respighi's Roman trilogy and tone poems by Rachmaninoff and others, and its techniques of programmatic transformation influenced film music.
