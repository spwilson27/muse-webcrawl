Great Books of the Western World

Great Books of the Western World is a series of books originally published in the United States in 1952, by Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc., to present the Great Books in a 54-volume set. The series is now in its second edition and contains 60 volumes of fiction, non-fiction, poetry, drama and other literature.

History

The project grew out of the Great Books discussion movement and the conviction of its editors, University of Chicago president Robert Maynard Hutchins and philosopher Mortimer J. Adler, that a liberal education should centre on sustained engagement with the great texts of the Western tradition. The first volume of the set is Adler's introduction, The Great Conversation, and the second and third volumes constitute the Syntopicon, a two-volume index and inventory of the "Great Ideas" — 102 ideas such as Truth, Justice, Love and Democracy — discussed across the works in the set.

The collection ranges from Homer, Plato and Aristotle through Augustine, Aquinas and Dante to Shakespeare, Newton, Locke, Kant, Darwin, Marx and Freud. A second edition appeared in 1990, expanding the set to 60 volumes, adding works by authors such as Jane Austen, Balzac, Ibsen, Dostoevsky and several twentieth-century writers, and incorporating new translations.

Criticism

The set has been criticised for its selection, which in the first edition included no works by women and few from outside a narrow European canon, and for the claim that its choices represented a timeless canon. Britannica marketed the set widely to middle-class households as the basis of self-education, and it became a prominent piece of furniture in many American homes. Sales were handled in part through door-to-door distribution alongside the encyclopedia.
