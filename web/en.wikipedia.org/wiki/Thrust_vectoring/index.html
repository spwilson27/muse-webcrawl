Thrust vectoring

Thrust vectoring
Also called: Thrust vector control (TVC)
Applications: Fighter aircraft, missiles, rockets, VTOL aircraft
Examples: F-22 Raptor, Su-35, Harrier, Space Shuttle

Thrust vectoring, also known as thrust vector control (TVC), is the ability of an aircraft, rocket, or other vehicle to manipulate the direction of the thrust from its engine(s) or motor(s) to control the attitude or angular velocity of the vehicle. By deflecting the exhaust jet away from the vehicle's centreline, the pilot or guidance system can generate pitching, yawing, or rolling moments independent of the aerodynamic control surfaces.

A 2D vectoring nozzle, as used on the F-22 Raptor, pitching the jet exhaust.

Aircraft applications

In combat aircraft, thrust vectoring provides post-stall manoeuvrability and extreme agility at low speeds and high angles of attack, supplementing or replacing conventional control surfaces. The F-22 Raptor uses two-dimensional nozzles that pitch the thrust up to 20 degrees; Russian fighters such as the Sukhoi Su-35 and Su-57 use three-dimensional nozzles for pitch and yaw control. Experimental aircraft such as the X-31 demonstrated Herbst manoeuvres and other post-stall tactics. A distinct application is vertical flight: the Harrier "jump jet" family and the F-35B direct thrust downward through rotating nozzles or lift fans to take off and land vertically.

Rockets and missiles

Rockets that fly outside the atmosphere — where aerodynamic surfaces are useless — steer principally by thrust vectoring, typically by gimbaling the engine or nozzle, injecting fluid into the exhaust, or using movable vanes in the jet. The Saturn V, the Space Shuttle, and modern launch vehicles all relied on gimballed main engines. Many missiles likewise use TVC, sometimes combined with aerodynamic fins, for rapid manoeuvre shortly after launch.

Advantages and drawbacks

Thrust vectoring increases agility, enables flight at extreme angles of attack, and allows shorter or vertical takeoffs, at the cost of additional weight, mechanical complexity, and maintenance. It is most valuable in fighter aircraft designed for close air combat and in vehicles operating beyond the reach of aerodynamic control.
