Bridge camera

A bridge camera is a type of digital camera that "bridges the gap" between simple point-and-shoot compact cameras and interchangeable-lens cameras such as DSLRs and mirrorless cameras. Bridge cameras typically resemble DSLRs in size and handling, offer extensive manual controls, an electronic viewfinder, and a fixed (non-interchangeable) zoom lens — often with a very large zoom range, in which case they are also marketed as superzoom cameras.

Characteristics

Bridge cameras generally use small image sensors, similar in size to those in compact cameras, which allows their fixed lenses to achieve very long effective focal lengths in a relatively compact package. Zoom ratios of 30×, 50× or even more than 80× are available on some models. Because the lens is fixed, the camera is sealed against dust on the sensor, and there is no need to carry additional lenses.

Compared with compacts, bridge cameras offer:

  • An electronic viewfinder in addition to the rear LCD screen.
  • Full manual exposure controls (aperture, shutter speed, ISO) and often RAW image capture.
  • A deep hand grip and SLR-like controls.
  • A hot shoe for external flash on some models.

History

The term arose in the film era for cameras that bridged the gap between compacts and SLRs, and was widely adopted for digital models in the 2000s, such as the Fujifilm FinePix S-series and the Panasonic Lumix FZ series. Later "superzoom" models, such as the Nikon Coolpix P900 and P1000, pushed zoom ranges to extremes popular with birdwatchers and amateur astronomers. A smaller number of premium bridge cameras use larger 1-inch sensors, trading zoom range for improved image quality.
