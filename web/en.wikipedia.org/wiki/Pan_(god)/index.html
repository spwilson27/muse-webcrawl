In ancient Greek religion and mythology, Pan (Greek: Πάν) is the god of the wild, shepherds and flocks, nature, the mountain wilds, rustic music and impromptus, and companion of the nymphs. He has the hindquarters, legs and horns of a goat, in the same manner as a faun and a satyr. With his homeland in rustic Arcadia, he is also recognised as the god of fields, groves, wooded glens and often affiliated with sex; because of this, Pan is connected to fertility and the season of spring.

The word panic ultimately derives from Pan, after the god's sudden shout in lonely places, said to strike terror into travellers and armies. In Roman religion and myth, Pan's counterpart was Faunus.

Origins and cult

Pan's name probably derives from the Greek paein, "to pasture"; the popular ancient folk etymology connected it with pan, "all". His cult originated in Arcadia, where he was worshipped in caves and wild places rather than temples. After the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC, where the Athenians credited Pan with sowing panic among the Persians, his worship spread to Athens, which dedicated a shrine to him on the Acropolis slope.

Mythology

Pan's parentage is given variously in the sources; the Homeric Hymn to Pan makes him a son of Hermes and a daughter of Dryops. When his goat-like appearance was revealed, the gods on Olympus delighted in him and named him Pan, "because he delighted all their hearts".

Pan pursued the nymph Syrinx, who, fleeing his advances, was transformed by her sister nymphs into marsh reeds; from these Pan fashioned the first panpipes, the syrinx. He likewise pursued Pitys, who was turned into a pine tree. Pan also boasted of his musical skill and challenged Apollo to a contest, judged by the mountain god Tmolus in Apollo's favour; King Midas, who disagreed, was given ass's ears.

Pan and music

Pan is the inventor of the pan flute or syrinx, an instrument of bound reeds of graduated length. His rustic music was said to be capable of charming — or terrifying — all of nature.

"The great god Pan is dead"

Plutarch, in his essay On the Obsolescence of Oracles, records a story that during the reign of Tiberius a sailor named Thamus, passing the island of Paxi, heard a voice instructing him to announce that "the great Pan is dead". Later Christian writers seized on the anecdote as marking the end of the pagan age at the time of Christ.

Later influence

Pan remained a potent figure in later literature and occultism — from Milton and Shelley to Arthur Machen's novella The Great God Pan (1894) and the piper at the gates of dawn in Kenneth Grahame's The Wind in the Willows. His goat-legged image contributed to early modern depictions of the Devil. In the 20th century Pan became an emblem for neopagan and nature movements.
