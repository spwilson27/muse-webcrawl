Stuttering

Early speech therapy greatly improves outcomes for children who stutter

Stuttering, also known as stammering, is a speech disorder characterised by involuntary disruptions in the flow of speech — repetitions of sounds, syllables or words ("li-li-like this"), prolongations ("llllike this"), and blocks in which no sound emerges despite effort. It typically begins in early childhood (developmental stuttering), affects roughly 1% of adults, and is about four times more common in males.

Causes

Stuttering is neurodevelopmental: twin and family studies show a strong genetic component, and brain imaging reveals differences in speech-motor networks and white-matter connectivity. It is not caused by anxiety or parenting, though stress and speaking demands can aggravate it. Rarer acquired forms follow stroke or brain injury (neurogenic stuttering) or psychological trauma (psychogenic stuttering). Many people who stutter are fluent when singing, choral speaking, or talking to animals, a phenomenon exploited in some fluency-shaping techniques.

Treatment

There is no cure, but speech-language therapy — especially early intervention programmes for children such as the Lidcombe Program — can greatly improve fluency. Approaches for adults include fluency shaping, stuttering modification (learning to stutter more easily), and cognitive-behavioural work on avoidance and speaking anxiety. Well-known people who stuttered include King George VI, whose therapy formed the plot of the film The King's Speech.
