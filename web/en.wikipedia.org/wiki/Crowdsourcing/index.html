Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing is the practice of obtaining information, ideas, work or funding by soliciting contributions from a large group of people, typically an online community, rather than from traditional employees or suppliers. The term, a portmanteau of "crowd" and "outsourcing", was coined by journalist Jeff Howe in a 2006 Wired article, "The Rise of Crowdsourcing". The practice itself is much older — competitions and public appeals for solutions long predate the internet — but networked computing made it cheap to coordinate thousands or millions of contributors.

Definitions and types

Scholars have proposed dozens of definitions; a widely cited 2012 review by Estellés-Arolas and González-Ladrón-de-Guevara distilled eight common features, including an open call, a crowd of variable size and knowledge, and mutual benefit to initiator and contributors. Common types include:

  • Crowdvoting — mass judgements, ratings and rankings (for example reCAPTCHA-style human computation).
  • Crowdfunding — raising money from many small donors, as on Kickstarter and GoFundMe.
  • Microwork — decomposing jobs into small paid tasks distributed on platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk.
  • Inducement prize contests — open competitions, from the Longitude Prize of 1714 to Kaggle data-science contests.
  • Citizen science — volunteer data collection and classification, such as Galaxy Zoo and eBird.
  • Open collaboration — the model of Wikipedia itself and of free and open-source software.

Historical examples

The British Longitude Act of 1714 offered a large prize for a practical method of determining longitude at sea — ultimately won by clockmaker John Harrison. The Oxford English Dictionary was compiled in part from quotations submitted by thousands of volunteer readers answering public appeals issued from 1879. In 1936 Toyota's logo was chosen through a public design contest that drew tens of thousands of entries.

Internet-era applications

The internet dramatically lowered coordination costs. Threadless (founded 2000) built a business on crowdsourced T-shirt designs; iStockphoto disrupted stock photography with amateur contributors; Foldit turned protein folding into a game played by volunteers who sometimes outperformed algorithms; and seti@home-style volunteer computing aggregates idle processor cycles. Cities and governments have used crowdsourcing for participatory budgeting, crisis mapping (such as Ushahidi after the 2010 Haiti earthquake) and reporting infrastructure faults.

Criticism

Crowdsourcing is criticised for underpaying contributors — microwork wages can fall far below minimum wage — for spec work that exploits designers, for quality and bias problems in mass-contributed data, and for "digital sweatshop" labour conditions. Supporters reply that participation is voluntary, can be intrinsically rewarding, and aggregates knowledge ("the wisdom of crowds") that no small team could assemble.
