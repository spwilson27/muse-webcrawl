Eaves

The eaves are the edges of a roof that overhang the face of a wall and normally project beyond the side of a building. They form an overhang to throw water clear of the walls, protect the wall and foundations from rain, shade windows from the sun, and provide a finished appearance to the roofline.

Parts

The underside of the eaves is the soffit, which may be left open to show the rafter ends or closed in with boards or panels. The vertical board covering the ends of the rafters is the fascia, to which gutters are often attached. In classical architecture, eaves may be elaborated with a cornice; in Japanese and other East Asian architecture, very deep eaves are a characteristic feature, protecting timber and paper walls.

Function

By projecting beyond the wall, eaves direct rainwater away from the building fabric, reducing damp penetration and erosion of foundations. In hot climates, wide eaves shade walls and windows, reducing solar heat gain; in cold climates, their width must be balanced against the desire for winter sunshine. Eaves also shelter ventilation openings to the roof space and, in many jurisdictions, are regulated for fire safety where buildings stand close together.

Idiom

The word gives rise to "eavesdropping": originally standing under the eaves — within the "eavesdrop," the ground on which water falls from the roof — to listen secretly at a wall or window.
