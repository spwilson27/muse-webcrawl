Egg yolk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A raw egg yolk, deep orange and glossy, sitting in a small glass bowl]
A raw egg yolk, deep orange and glossy, sitting in a small glass bowl

The yolk is the nutrient-bearing portion of the Egg, serving as the food source for the developing embryo. Suspended in the egg white (albumen) by the chalazae, it contains the egg's fat, most of its protein quality, fat-soluble vitamins and the emulsifier Lecithin that makes it indispensable in cooking — the binding heart of Mayonnaise, Hollandaise, custards, carbonara and fresh pasta. A large hen's egg yolk weighs about 17 grams and provides roughly 55 calories.

Composition and colour

Yolk is about half water, a third fat and a sixth protein, rich in choline, vitamin D, B12 and the carotenoids lutein and zeaxanthin, which give its colour — deepened by marigold or paprika in hens' feed and ranging from pale lemon to near-red by diet and breed. Its cholesterol (about 185 mg per large yolk) drove decades of dietary caution, largely relaxed by later evidence for most people; its lecithin and proteins stabilise emulsions and foams.

Culinary role

Yolk's emulsifying power underlies sauces and ice cream; its proteins set custards and curds between 65–70 °C (overheating curdles them); yolk-rich doughs colour and enrich pasta, brioche and noodles; salted and cured yolks grate like cheese in modern kitchens. Separated yolks whip far less than whites, so the last trace of yolk ruins a meringue — the cook's oldest separation anxiety. Tempera painters, meanwhile, bound pigments in yolk for five centuries of luminous panels.
