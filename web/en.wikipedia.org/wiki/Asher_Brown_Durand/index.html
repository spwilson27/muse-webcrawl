Asher Brown Durand (August 21, 1796 – September 17, 1886) was an American painter of the Hudson River School. Trained first as an engraver, he became one of the leading American landscape painters of the mid-19th century and a central figure in the cultural life of New York.

Early career as an engraver

Durand was apprenticed to an engraver and rose to prominence with his engraving of John Trumbull's Declaration of Independence (1823), which established his reputation as one of the finest engravers in the United States. He helped found the National Academy of Design in 1825 and turned increasingly to oil painting in the 1830s, encouraged by the patron Luman Reed and by his friendship with Thomas Cole.

Landscape painting

After Cole's death in 1848, Durand became the acknowledged leader of American landscape painting. His Kindred Spirits (1849) depicts Cole and the poet William Cullen Bryant standing together in a Catskill gorge, and is among the most famous American paintings. In his "Letters on Landscape Painting", published in The Crayon in 1855, Durand urged artists to study nature directly and faithfully — advice embodied in his own meticulously observed forest interiors such as The Beeches and In the Woods.

Later life

Durand served as president of the National Academy of Design from 1845 to 1861. He retired to his native Maplewood, New Jersey, where he died in 1886. He is not to be confused with the goddess Asherah, despite the similar name.
