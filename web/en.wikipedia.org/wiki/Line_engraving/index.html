Line engraving

Line engraving is a term for the engraving of images on metal plates — usually copper or steel — using a burin (graver) to cut lines into the surface. The printed image is composed entirely of lines whose width and spacing create tonal effects. It was the dominant method of reproducing images in print from the 16th to the 19th century, and is closely associated with banknote and stamp production.

Technique

The engraver pushes a burin — a steel tool with a lozenge-shaped point — across a polished copper plate, raising a thin curl of metal that is removed with a scraper. The characteristic line swells in the middle and tapers at both ends as the tool enters and leaves the plate. Ink is held in the incised lines and transferred to damp paper under the high pressure of a rolling press (intaglio printing).

History

Engraving on copper developed in the 15th century in the workshops of goldsmiths; Martin Schongauer and Albrecht Dürer raised it to an art form. In the 17th and 18th centuries, reproductive line engraving spread the compositions of famous paintings across Europe. Steel engraving, introduced in the early 19th century, allowed much larger editions and became standard for book illustrations and banknotes, whose intricate guilloche patterns resist forgery. The technique was displaced for commercial illustration by photomechanical processes in the late 19th century but survives in security printing and fine-art printmaking.
