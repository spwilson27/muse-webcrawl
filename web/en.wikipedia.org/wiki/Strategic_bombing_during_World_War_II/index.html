Strategic bombing during World War II

Strategic bombing during World War II was the sustained aerial attack on railways, harbours, cities, workers' housing, and industrial districts in enemy territory, intended to destroy the enemy's military thought and infrastructure and undermine civilian morale and production. All major belligerents engaged in it, including Germany's Blitz against Britain, the Allied Combined Bomber Offensive over Europe, and the American campaign against Japan culminating in the firebombing of Tokyo and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Europe

Germany's bombing of Warsaw, Rotterdam, London, and Coventry opened the era of large-scale attacks on cities. The RAF, unable to bomb precisely by night, adopted area bombing under Arthur Harris, attacking cities such as Hamburg (1943) and Dresden (1945) with devastating firestorms. The USAAF pursued daylight "precision" bombing of industry — ball bearings, aircraft plants, and, decisively in 1944, oil — while the Combined Bomber Offensive's transport plan helped isolate the Normandy battlefield. The Luftwaffe later retaliated with V-1 and V-2 weapons.

Pacific

Against Japan, XXI Bomber Command B-29s shifted in 1945 to low-level night incendiary attacks; the 9–10 March 1945 raid on Tokyo was the deadliest air raid of the war. Conventional bombing, naval blockade, and mining (Operation Starvation) strangled Japanese industry before the atomic bombings of August 1945.

Effects and debate

Strategic bombing killed hundreds of thousands of civilians; its contribution to victory remains debated. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey concluded that Allied air power was decisive in Western Europe, particularly against oil and transportation, while finding morale effects ambiguous. The ethics of area attacks on civilians were contested during the war and remain a central subject in the historiography of total war and in just-war theory.
