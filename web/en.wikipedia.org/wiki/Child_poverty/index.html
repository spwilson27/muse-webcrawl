Child poverty

Child poverty refers to the phenomenon of children living in poverty: growing up in households whose income or resources fall below a defined threshold, whether an absolute measure (such as the World Bank's international poverty line) or a relative measure (such as 60 percent of median household income, the threshold commonly used in the European Union and OECD).

According to UNICEF and World Bank estimates, hundreds of millions of children worldwide live in monetary poverty, and roughly a billion children are multidimensionally poor — deprived in dimensions such as nutrition, health, housing, water, sanitation, or education. Child poverty is strongly associated with poorer health, lower educational attainment, and reduced earnings in adulthood.

Measurement

Two broad approaches dominate. Absolute poverty lines fix a standard of living in real terms and track progress against it; relative poverty lines move with the general standard of living of a society and so capture social exclusion. Multidimensional measures, such as the global Multidimensional Poverty Index and UNICEF's MODA framework, count simultaneous deprivations in children's lives rather than income alone.

Causes and effects

Causes include parental unemployment and low wages, family structure, disability, discrimination, and weaknesses in welfare systems. Research associates childhood poverty with food insecurity, chronic stress, housing instability, and worse outcomes in health and schooling, with effects that often persist across generations.

Responses

Policy responses include cash transfers (conditional and unconditional), child benefits, free school meals, subsidized childcare, public housing, and minimum-wage and labour-market policies. The temporary expansion of the United States child tax credit in 2021, for example, coincided with a sharp fall in measured U.S. child poverty that year. The UN Sustainable Development Goal 1 commits governments to ending poverty in all its forms, including for children, by 2030.
