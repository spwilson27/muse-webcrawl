Poliomyelitis, commonly called polio, is a highly infectious viral disease that mainly affects children under five. Most infections cause no symptoms, but in a small fraction of cases the virus invades the nervous system and causes irreversible paralysis, typically of the legs; it can be fatal if breathing muscles are affected (historically treated in 'iron lungs').

Two vaccines transformed the disease: Jonas Salk's inactivated (injected) vaccine (1955) and Albert Sabin's oral vaccine (1961). The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988 by the WHO, Rotary International, the CDC and UNICEF, has reduced cases by more than 99.9%: from about 350,000 cases a year to a handful. Wild poliovirus remains endemic only in Afghanistan and Pakistan, though vaccine-derived outbreaks in under-immunised areas complicate the final push.

History

Polio existed in antiquity but epidemic paralysis emerged in the late 19th and 20th centuries as improved sanitation delayed first exposure beyond infancy. Franklin D. Roosevelt, paralysed at 39, founded the March of Dimes fundraising campaign that bankrolled vaccine research.
