Bhakti movement

RegionIndian subcontinent
Periodc. 7th–17th century CE
Key figuresAlvars, Nayanars, Ramanuja, Kabir, Mirabai, Guru Nanak, Tulsidas, Chaitanya
Core ideaLoving devotion (bhakti) to a personal god

The Bhakti movement was a significant religious movement in medieval Hinduism that sought to bring religious reforms to all sections of society by adopting the method of devotion to achieve salvation. Originating in Tamilakam in South India during the seventh century CE, it spread northwards from the fifteenth century onwards, reaching its zenith between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries. The movement emphasised a personal, loving relationship between the devotee and a chosen deity — such as Vishnu, Shiva, or the goddess — over ritual, priestly mediation, and caste distinctions.

Origins

The earliest bhakti poetry was composed by the Alvars, devotees of Vishnu, and the Nayanars, devotees of Shiva, in Tamil-speaking South India. Their hymns, sung in the vernacular rather than Sanskrit, expressed intense emotional devotion and were later compiled into canonical collections. Philosophers such as Ramanuja (11th–12th century) gave the movement theological foundations with qualified non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita), arguing that devotion was open to all regardless of birth.

Spread and major figures

As the movement travelled north it produced a succession of influential saint-poets who composed in regional languages: Kabir, a weaver who blended Hindu and Muslim ideas and rejected caste and ritual; Mirabai, the Rajput princess devoted to Krishna; Tulsidas, author of the Ramcharitmanas; Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, who inspired ecstatic congregational singing (kirtan) in Bengal; and Guru Nanak, whose devotion-centred teaching became the foundation of Sikhism. In Maharashtra, saints such as Dnyaneshwar, Tukaram, and Eknath shaped the Varkari tradition centred on the god Vithoba.

Social impact

Bhakti teachers frequently challenged caste hierarchy, Brahminical ritualism, and the exclusion of women and low-born people from religious life, though the extent to which the movement transformed social structures remains debated by historians. Its most enduring legacy is literary and cultural: it stimulated devotional literature in numerous vernacular languages, enriched Indian music and temple culture, and promoted ideals of equality and direct access to the divine that continue to resonate.
