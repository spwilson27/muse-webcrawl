Butt Report

Placeholder image: RAF bombers on a night raid.

The Butt Report, prepared on 18 August 1941, was a report by David Bensusan-Butt, a civil servant in the War Cabinet Secretariat, assessing the accuracy of RAF Bomber Command night bombing during the Second World War. It produced the shocking finding that British bombers were largely failing to reach, let alone hit, their targets.

Contents

Butt examined some 650 aerial photographs taken by participating aircraft during night raids in June and July 1941 and compared where the bombs had actually fallen with the intended targets. Of the aircraft recorded as attacking their target, only about one in three got within five miles (8 km) of it. Over the heavily defended Ruhr, the proportion fell to about one in ten.

Consequences

The report forced a major rethink of British bombing policy. It contributed to the February 1942 area bombing directive, which accepted that factories and workers' housing in whole cities — rather than precision targets — were what the bombers could realistically attack, and it accelerated investment in navigation aids such as Gee and H2S radar, target-marking Pathfinder forces, and improved training.
