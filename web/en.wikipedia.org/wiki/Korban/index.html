Korban

Offerings at the Temple in Jerusalem

A korban (Hebrew: קָרְבָּן, qorbān, plural korbanot), also spelled corban, is an offering or sacrifice in ancient Israelite and Jewish religious practice, consisting of an animal, grain, or other substance presented to God, most notably at the Tabernacle and later at the Temple in Jerusalem. The word derives from the root meaning "to draw near," understood as that which brings the offeror near to God.

Types of korbanot

The Torah prescribes several categories of offerings. The olah (burnt offering) was wholly consumed by fire on the altar. The shelamim (peace offering) was shared between the altar, the priests and the offeror. The chatat (sin offering) and asham (guilt offering) atoned for transgressions, while the mincha was a meal offering of flour and oil, and the todah was offered in thanksgiving. Daily offerings (the tamid) were supplemented by additional sacrifices on Sabbaths and festivals.

Ritual procedure

Offerings generally required the laying of hands (semikhah) on the animal, its slaughter, the sprinkling or application of its blood by the priests (kohanim), and the burning of prescribed portions on the altar. Only animals deemed ritually pure and free of blemish could be offered.

After the Temple

Sacrificial worship ceased with the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. In rabbinic Judaism, prayer, repentance, Torah study and acts of charity came to be understood as substitutes for the korbanot, and the liturgy includes detailed recitation of the sacrificial passages. Orthodox prayer still includes petitions for the restoration of the Temple service.
