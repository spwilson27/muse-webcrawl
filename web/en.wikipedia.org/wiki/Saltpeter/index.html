Saltpeter

Chemical namePotassium nitrate (KNO₃)
Other namesNitre; saltpetre
AppearanceWhite crystalline solid
UsesGunpowder, fertilizer, food curing, fireworks, rocket propellant

Saltpeter (or saltpetre) is the common name for potassium nitrate (KNO₃), a naturally occurring mineral and chemical compound that was historically the essential oxidising ingredient of gunpowder. The term is sometimes also applied loosely to related nitrates, such as sodium nitrate ("Chile saltpeter") and calcium nitrate ("Norwegian saltpeter").

Sources

Potassium nitrate forms naturally as white encrustations on soil and rock in warm climates, produced by the bacterial oxidation of nitrogenous waste. Major natural deposits were exploited in India and China, where saltpeter workers scraped and leached nitrate-rich earth. In pre-industrial Europe it was produced in "nitre beds" — heaps of manure, urine, ash and earth tended for months or years — and by leaching earth from stables, dovecotes and cellar walls. Bat guano in caves was another significant source; during the American Civil War the Confederacy mined saltpeter from caves such as Mammoth Cave.

Uses

Mixed with charcoal and sulfur, saltpeter makes black powder (gunpowder), for which it was strategically vital for centuries — governments maintained saltpeter monopolies and searched for secure supplies. It is also used as a fertilizer (supplying nitrogen and potassium), in fireworks and model rocket propellant, as a food preservative (E252) in cured meats, in toothpaste for sensitive teeth, and historically as a refrigerant component and in medicine.

Production today

Modern potassium nitrate is produced industrially, for example by reacting sodium nitrate with potassium chloride, or from nitric acid and potassium hydroxide; the broader nitrogen supply chain relies on the Haber–Bosch fixation of atmospheric nitrogen.
