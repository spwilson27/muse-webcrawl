Reflective practice


The reflective cycle: experience, reflection, conceptualisation, experimentation.

Reflective practice is the ability to reflect on one's actions so as to take a critical stance or attitude towards one's own practice and that of one's peers, engaging in a process of continuous adaptation and learning. It is a core component of professional development in teaching, nursing, medicine, social work, and management.

Origins

The concept is commonly traced through John Dewey's account of reflective thought in How We Think (1933) to Donald Schön's influential book The Reflective Practitioner (1983). Schön distinguished reflection-in-action—thinking on one's feet while doing—from reflection-on-action—reviewing an experience afterwards—and contrasted both with the "technical rationality" of applying standard rules.

Models

Widely used frameworks include Gibbs' reflective cycle (description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan), Kolb's experiential learning cycle, and Johns' model of structured reflection. Reflective journals, portfolios, supervision, and critical-incident analysis are common vehicles for the practice.

Criticism

Critics note that reflection can become a formulaic, assessed exercise rather than genuine inquiry, that it tends to focus on the individual rather than organisational structures, and that evidence for its effect on outcomes is mixed.
