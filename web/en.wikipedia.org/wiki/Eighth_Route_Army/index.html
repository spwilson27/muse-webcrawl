Eighth Route Army

The Eighth Route Army (八路軍, Bālù-Jūn), officially known as the 18th Group Army of the National Revolutionary Army, was a group army under the command of the Chinese Communist Party, nominally within the structure of the Chinese National Revolutionary Army during the Second Sino-Japanese War. Created in 1937 when the Chinese Red Army was reorganised under the Second United Front against Japan, it grew from about 30,000 troops into a force of several hundred thousand by 1945, operating mainly as guerrillas behind Japanese lines in north China.

Formation

Following the Xi'an Incident of December 1936 and negotiations between the Communists and the Kuomintang, the Red Army's main forces in the north were redesignated the Eighth Route Army on 22 August 1937. Zhu De served as its commander and Peng Dehuai as deputy commander. It comprised three divisions — the 115th under Lin Biao, the 120th under He Long, and the 129th under Liu Bocheng — with Mao Zedong's political direction exercised through party committees. A second Communist formation in central China, the New Fourth Army, was created shortly afterwards.

Operations

Early in the war the army won a celebrated victory at the Battle of Pingxingguan in September 1937, where the 115th Division ambushed a Japanese column. Thereafter it concentrated on establishing base areas behind Japanese lines, notably the Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei (Jin-Cha-Ji) border region, mobilising the peasantry and waging guerrilla warfare. In 1940 Peng Dehuai launched the Hundred Regiments Offensive, the largest Communist offensive of the war, attacking railways and Japanese positions across north China; it provoked fierce Japanese retaliation under the "Three Alls" policy.

Legacy

After Japan's defeat in 1945, the Eighth Route Army formed the core of what became the People's Liberation Army during the resumed Chinese Civil War. The name "Eighth Route Army" (colloquially "Balu") remains a fond term in Chinese popular culture for Communist soldiers of the period.
