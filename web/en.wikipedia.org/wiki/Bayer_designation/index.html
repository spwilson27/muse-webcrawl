Bayer designation

A Bayer designation is a stellar designation in which a specific star is identified by a Greek or Latin letter followed by the genitive form of its constellation's Latin name. The original list of Bayer designations was published by the German astronomer Johann Bayer in his star atlas Uranometria of 1603.

The system

Bayer generally assigned the Greek letter alpha (α) to the brightest star of a constellation, beta (β) to the second brightest, and so on through the 24 letters of the Greek alphabet, then continued with Latin letters. Examples include Alpha Centauri, Betelgeuse (Alpha Orionis, though Rigel — Beta Orionis — is actually brighter) and Polaris (Alpha Ursae Minoris). In practice Bayer often ordered stars by position within the constellation's figure rather than strictly by brightness, and measuring inaccuracies produced many exceptions.

Extensions and use

When Greek letters ran out, Bayer used Latin letters up to Q; later astronomers extended the system, and variable stars received separate designations (such as R Leonis) when they lacked Bayer letters. Bayer designations remain the standard identifiers for bright stars, complementing Flamsteed numbers and modern catalogue numbers such as HD and HIP designations. Some Bayer letters now mark stars reassigned when constellation boundaries were redrawn — for example, Alpha and Beta Scorpii.
