Stop and frisk

Stop and frisk is a policing practice in which an officer briefly detains a person on the street, questions them, and may pat down ("frisk") their outer clothing for weapons. Under the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, the practice rests on the Supreme Court's decision in Terry v. Ohio (1968), which held that a stop and frisk is constitutional where an officer has reasonable suspicion—a lower standard than probable cause—that the person has committed, is committing, or is about to commit a crime, and reason to believe they may be armed.

New York City

The practice became most prominent, and most controversial, in New York City, where NYPD stops rose to a peak of about 686,000 in 2011, the great majority involving Black and Latino men and resulting in no arrest or summons. In Floyd v. City of New York (2013), a federal judge found the city's application of the policy violated the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments and appointed a monitor. Stops subsequently fell dramatically—to a few tens of thousands a year—while crime continued to decline, fuelling debate over the practice's effectiveness and its racial impact.

Criticism and defence

Critics describe stop and frisk as racial profiling that erodes community trust and produces very low rates of weapon recovery. Supporters argue it deters gun carrying and contributed to historic crime reductions. Similar powers exist elsewhere, for example "stop and search" under the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 in England and Wales.
