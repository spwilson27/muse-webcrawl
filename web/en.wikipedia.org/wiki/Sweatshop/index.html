Sweatshop

A garment factory floor

A sweatshop is a factory or workshop, especially in the garment industry, where workers are employed at very low wages, for long hours, and under poor or unsafe conditions. The term originated in 19th-century Britain and America for the "sweating system", in which middlemen subcontracted piecework to cramped workshops, squeezing wages at each layer.

History

Sweatshops were emblematic of early industrial cities: London's East End tailoring trades and New York's Lower East Side garment shops employed waves of immigrants, including children. The 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in New York, which killed 146 workers trapped behind locked doors, became a turning point for US labour regulation. Reform movements, trade unions such as the ILGWU, and legislation on minimum wages and hours pushed the worst conditions out of much of the industrialised world during the 20th century.

Globalisation

From the 1970s, apparel production shifted to low-wage countries in Asia, Latin America, and later Africa. Campaigns in the 1990s exposed sweatshop conditions behind major Western brands, leading to codes of conduct, factory audits, and the fair trade and anti-sweatshop movements. Disasters such as the 2013 Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh, which killed more than 1,100 garment workers, renewed scrutiny and produced the Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety. Economists debate sweatshops: critics stress exploitation, while some defenders argue such jobs, however harsh, may pay more than local alternatives and form a rung on the ladder of development — a position labour activists strongly contest.
