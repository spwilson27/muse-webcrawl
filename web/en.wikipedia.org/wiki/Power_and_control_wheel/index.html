The Power and Control Wheel is a diagram developed in 1984 by the Domestic Abuse Intervention Project (DAIP) in Duluth, Minnesota, as part of the approach that became known as the Duluth model of intervention in domestic violence. Derived from the reported experiences of women who had been abused by male partners, the wheel depicts the pattern of coercive behaviours used by an abuser to maintain power and control over an intimate partner.

The wheel: physical and sexual violence form the rim; the spokes are the tactics of control.

Structure

In the diagram, "power and control" sits at the hub. Around it, like spokes, are eight tactics:

  • Using intimidation
  • Using emotional abuse
  • Using isolation
  • Minimizing, denying and blaming
  • Using children
  • Using male privilege
  • Using economic abuse
  • Using coercion and threats

Physical and sexual violence form the outer rim: they may occur relatively infrequently but they reinforce the whole system, so that the non-violent tactics are backed by the credible threat of violence. A contrasting "Equality Wheel" describes the behaviours of a non-violent relationship, including negotiation and fairness, shared responsibility, honesty and accountability, and trust and support.

Use and adaptations

The wheel has been translated into many languages and adapted for other contexts, including teen dating relationships, immigrant and refugee communities, same-sex relationships, and elder abuse. It is used in batterer-intervention programmes, victim advocacy, and professional training.

Criticism

Critics have noted that the original wheel was based on the experiences of a specific sample of women with male partners and embeds assumptions about gender and privilege; some argue it underplays mutual or situational violence and psychological factors. Defenders reply that the wheel accurately captures the pattern of coercive control described by many survivors, while acknowledging that adaptations are needed for other populations.
