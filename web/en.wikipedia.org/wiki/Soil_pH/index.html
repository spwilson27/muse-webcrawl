Soil pH

Soil pH is a measure of the acidity or basicity (alkalinity) of a soil. Soil pH is a key characteristic that can be used for informative analysis both qualitative and quantitatively of soil characteristics. pH is defined as the negative logarithm (base 10) of the activity of hydronium ions (H+ or more precisely H3O+aq) in a solution. In soils, it is measured in a slurry of soil mixed with water (or a salt solution, such as 0.01 M CaCl2), and normally falls between 3 and 10, with 7 being neutral.

Classification

Soils are classed from ultra-acidic (below 3.5) through extremely alkaline (above 9). Acid soils are associated with aluminum and manganese toxicities and deficiencies of calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus; alkaline soils often suffer iron and micronutrient deficiencies and, when sodic, poor structure.

Determinants

Soil pH is controlled by parent material, climate (leaching of base cations under high rainfall acidifies soil), vegetation, fertilizer use (ammonium-based fertilizers acidify), and acid deposition. It also varies with depth and season.

Plant growth

Most crops grow best between pH 5.5 and 7.5. Some plants are specialists: blueberries, rhododendrons, and azaleas prefer acid soils, while alfalfa and asparagus tolerate alkalinity. Soil pH strongly affects nutrient availability — phosphorus availability peaks near pH 6.5, while molybdenum becomes less available in acid soils.

Adjustment

Acid soils are amended with lime (calcium carbonate, or dolomitic lime where magnesium is needed). Alkaline soils can be acidified with elemental sulfur, which soil bacteria oxidize to sulfuric acid, though strongly calcareous soils resist adjustment.
