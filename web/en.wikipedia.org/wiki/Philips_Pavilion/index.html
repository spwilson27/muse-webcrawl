Philips Pavilion

The Philips Pavilion was a temporary exhibition pavilion built for Expo 58, the Brussels World's Fair of 1958, by the Dutch electronics company Philips. It was designed by the office of Le Corbusier, with Iannis Xenakis playing a central role in its conception and detailed design. The building was demolished after the fair closed.

Design

The pavilion's form was generated from hyperbolic paraboloid surfaces — intersecting curved shells rising to sharp peaks, giving the structure a tent-like, sculptural appearance. Xenakis, who was both an architect in Le Corbusier's atelier and a composer, derived the geometry in part from the same ruled-surface ideas he had used in his orchestral work Metastaseis. The shell was constructed from prestressed concrete panels.

Poème électronique

Inside, visitors experienced the Poème électronique, an early multimedia spectacle combining film, coloured light, and spatialised electronic sound. The music was composed by Edgard Varèse — his last major work — with a brief interlude by Xenakis (Concret PH). Hundreds of loudspeakers arranged in "sound routes" along the interior walls moved the sound around the audience, controlled from a central console. The projected imagery, assembled by Le Corbusier and Philippe Agostini, presented a compressed history of humanity in about eight minutes. Around two million visitors passed through the pavilion during the exposition.

Legacy

The Philips Pavilion is regarded as a landmark in the history of electronic music, multimedia art, and experimental architecture. Much of the research behind it was carried out at the Philips Natuurkundig Laboratorium by engineers including Kid Baltan and Tom Dissevelt. Although the building no longer exists, reconstructions, models, and virtual recreations have been exhibited internationally.
