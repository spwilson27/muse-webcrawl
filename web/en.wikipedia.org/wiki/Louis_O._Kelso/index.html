Louis Orth Kelso (December 4, 1913 – February 17, 1991) was an American economist, corporate lawyer, and merchant banker whose ideas on widespread capital ownership led him to invent the Employee Stock Ownership Plan (ESOP) in the 1950s.

Ideas

Kelso argued that in a technologically advancing economy, capital — machines and structures — produces an ever-larger share of wealth, while labour's share declines. If ownership of capital remained concentrated, he believed, democracy and free markets would be undermined. His remedy was not redistribution of income but broadening the ownership of productive capital itself. With philosopher Mortimer Adler he set out the argument in The Capitalist Manifesto (1958) and The New Capitalists (1961).

The ESOP

Kelso designed the ESOP as a leveraged mechanism by which employees could acquire shares in their employing company, financed by credit repaid from the company's future earnings — capital acquisition without workers having to reduce their consumption. The first ESOP was arranged in 1956 for the employees of Peninsula Newspapers in California. With the support of Senator Russell Long, ESOPs received statutory recognition in the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA), and thousands of companies subsequently adopted them.
