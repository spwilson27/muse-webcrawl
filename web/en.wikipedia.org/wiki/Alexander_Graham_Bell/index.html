Alexander Graham Bell (3 March 1847 – 2 August 1922) was a Scottish-born inventor, scientist and engineer who is credited with patenting the first practical telephone. He founded the Bell Telephone Company in 1877 and co-founded the American Telephone and Telegraph Company (AT&T). His work grew out of a family tradition of elocution and the teaching of the deaf.

Early life

Bell was born in Edinburgh, Scotland, the son of Alexander Melville Bell, inventor of the "Visible Speech" system for teaching the deaf. His mother and his wife were both deaf, which profoundly shaped his career. After emigrating with his family to Canada in 1870, he moved to Boston, where he taught deaf students and became a professor of vocal physiology at Boston University.

Invention of the telephone

Working with his assistant Thomas Watson and funded by the families of his deaf pupils, Bell experimented with transmitting speech electrically. On 7 March 1876 he received US patent 174,465 for the telephone; three days later, on 10 March 1876, he transmitted the first intelligible sentence by telephone: "Mr. Watson, come here — I want to see you." The Bell Telephone Company was formed the following year, and Bell's patent survived fierce legal challenges to become one of the most valuable ever issued.

Later work

Bell went on to work on the photophone (transmitting speech on a beam of light), the metal detector, hydrofoils and early aviation — he was a founder of the Aerial Experiment Association, which flew some of Canada's first aircraft. He helped found the National Geographic Society and the journal Science. The unit of sound level, the bel (and the decibel), is named for him. He died at his Nova Scotia estate, Beinn Bhreagh, in 1922; at his burial, every telephone on the North American continent was silenced for one minute in his honour.
