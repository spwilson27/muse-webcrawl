Performing rights

Performing rights are the rights to perform copyrighted musical works (including songs with lyrics) in public. A performance in this sense includes live performance by musicians, playing a recording in a public place, broadcasting a work on radio or television, and transmitting it over cable or the internet. The owner of the performance right — typically the composer, songwriter, or music publisher — is entitled to licence these uses and receive royalties.

Legal basis

Under the United States Copyright Act, the copyright owner of a musical work holds the exclusive right "to perform the copyrighted work publicly". A performance is "public" if it occurs at a place open to the public, is transmitted to the public, or is received by a substantial number of people outside a normal circle of family and friends. Similar rights exist in other jurisdictions under national copyright laws and are recognized internationally through the Berne Convention. The performance right in a musical composition is distinct from rights in a sound recording; in the United States, sound recordings traditionally had no general public performance right for terrestrial radio, though digital audio transmissions are covered by a statutory licence.

Collecting societies

Because individual composers cannot negotiate with every broadcaster, venue, and business, they assign the administration of their performing rights to performing rights organizations (PROs), also called collecting societies. Major PROs include ASCAP, BMI, and SESAC in the United States; PRS for Music in the United Kingdom; SACEM in France; GEMA in Germany; and SOCAN in Canada. These organizations issue blanket licences to users, monitor performances through reports, sampling, and digital fingerprinting, collect licence fees, and distribute royalties to writers and publishers according to their distribution rules.

Licensing practice

Businesses that play music — radio and TV stations, streaming services, concert venues, bars, restaurants, shops, and fitness centres — generally obtain blanket licences from the PROs relevant in their country, paying fees scaled by factors such as audience size, revenue, or floor area. Failure to hold a licence exposes a business to copyright infringement suits. Exemptions exist in some jurisdictions for small establishments using ordinary home-style receiving equipment, and for certain religious, educational, and charitable uses.

Grand rights

Performances of "dramatic" works — operas, musicals, and ballets, where music is combined with staging, dialogue, and plot — are governed by so-called grand rights, which are not administered by PROs and must be licensed directly from the rights holders. PRO licences cover only "small rights": non-dramatic performances of individual songs or excerpts.
