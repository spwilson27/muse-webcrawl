Vowel reduction

In phonetics, vowel reduction is any of various changes in the acoustic quality of vowels as a result of changes in stress, sonority, duration, loudness, articulation, or position in the word. Unstressed vowels tend to become shorter, quieter and more centralised — in the extreme, they merge into the neutral vowel schwa [ə].

In English

English is a stress-timed language with extensive reduction: in most unstressed syllables, the vowel collapses to schwa or a syllabic consonant. Thus the first vowel of photograph differs from the reduced vowels in photography and photographic. Reduction is a major source of difficulty in English spelling, since the written vowel letters no longer correspond to the spoken sound.

In other languages

Russian has strong reduction (akanye and ikanye) in which unstressed /o/ and /a/ merge, and unstressed vowels shorten. Portuguese and Catalan show similar processes; in Catalan, unstressed vowels merge into a small set ([ə], [i], [u]). By contrast, languages such as Spanish and Finnish have little or no vowel reduction, so unstressed vowels retain their full quality.

Types

Linguists distinguish positional reduction (in unstressed syllables), reduction in fast or casual speech, and historical reduction, in which vowels in a language's history weakened or disappeared, as when final vowels were lost from Old to Middle English.
