Creative nonfiction

Image: A writer's desk with notebooks and a typewriter (placeholder)

Creative nonfiction (also called literary nonfiction or narrative nonfiction) is a genre of writing that uses literary styles and techniques to create factually accurate narratives. Writers of creative nonfiction, while remaining faithful to verifiable facts, present information in scenes, dialogue, characterisation and imagery rather than in the expository manner of standard journalism or academic writing. Lee Gutkind, founder of the journal Creative Nonfiction, has defined the genre as "true stories, well told".

Forms

The genre encompasses the personal essay and memoir, literary journalism (or New Journalism), the lyric essay, travel writing, nature writing, food writing, biography written with narrative techniques, and hybrid documentary forms. Its antecedents include Montaigne's essays, and it drew modern impetus from the New Journalism of the 1960s — writers such as Truman Capote (In Cold Blood, 1966), Norman Mailer, Joan Didion, Tom Wolfe and Gay Talese — and from earlier narrative reporters.

Characteristics and ethics

Creative nonfiction is distinguished by scene-by-scene construction, extended dialogue, first- or third-person narration with a distinct voice, and attention to the meaning of facts rather than bare reporting. Because the genre borrows fiction's tools, it raises ethical questions about reconstruction, composite characters, compressed chronology and invented detail. Most practitioners and editors hold that, unlike fiction, the genre forbids fabrication: the writer's licence lies in selection, structure and style, not in altering the facts. Controversies over embellished memoirs have periodically sharpened this debate.

Institutional presence

Creative nonfiction is now widely taught in MFA programmes, supported by dedicated journals and prizes, and common in magazines such as The New Yorker, Harper's and The Atlantic. Gutkind's journal, founded in 1993, and the Best American Essays series (begun 1986) helped consolidate it as the "fourth genre" alongside fiction, poetry and drama in creative-writing pedagogy.
