Unix-like

A Unix-like (sometimes referred to as UN*X or *nix) operating system is one that behaves in a manner similar to a Unix system, although it does not necessarily conform to or be certified to any version of the Single UNIX Specification. A Unix-like application is one that behaves like the corresponding Unix command or shell. Although the term also historically includes systems descended from the original AT&T Unix source code, it is commonly applied as well to independently developed systems that imitate its design, such as Linux.

Definition and categories

The Open Group holds the UNIX trademark and certifies products against the Single UNIX Specification; a system so certified may be called "Unix" (for example, Apple's macOS since Leopard, IBM AIX, HP-UX, and Oracle Solaris). Systems that descend from the Berkeley Software Distribution — FreeBSD, NetBSD, OpenBSD and DragonFly BSD — are genetic Unix descendants distributed as free software. Linux, by contrast, is a "functional" Unix-like system: its kernel, written by Linus Torvalds from 1991, was developed independently, while much of its userland derives from the GNU project begun by Richard Stallman in 1983.

Characteristics

Unix-like systems share much of the Unix philosophy: everything is a file; small, composable command-line tools chained by pipes; the shell as a programmable environment; hierarchical filesystems; and plain-text configuration. Most expose the POSIX application programming interface.

Examples

  • Free and open source: Linux distributions, FreeBSD, OpenBSD, NetBSD, illumos, Minix, Android (Linux kernel with a non-POSIX userland), ChromeOS.
  • Proprietary or certified: macOS, iOS (Darwin), AIX, HP-UX, Solaris, QNX, IRIX (historical).
