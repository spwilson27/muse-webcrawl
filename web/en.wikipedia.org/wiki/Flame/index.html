Flame

A flame (from Latin flamma) is the visible, gaseous part of a fire. It is caused by a highly exothermic chemical reaction, combustion, taking place in a thin zone. Some flames, such as the flame of a burning candle, are hot enough to have ionized gaseous components and can be considered a plasma. This subject is, however, still debated.

Very hot flames are hot enough to have ionized gaseous components of sufficient density to be considered plasma. Other flames, such as Bunsen burner flames, do not get as hot.

Flame types

Flames may be classified according to the method by which fuel and oxidizer are brought together. In a diffusion flame, fuel and oxidizer diffuse into each other; the flame occurs where they meet in suitable proportions. Candle flames and match flames are examples. In a premixed flame, the fuel and oxidizer are mixed before ignition, as in a Bunsen burner or a gas stove. Premixed flames generally burn faster, hotter, and with a shorter, more clearly defined flame.

Flame color and temperature

The color of a flame depends on several factors, most importantly the dominant emission and the temperature. Incomplete combustion produces yellow, soot-laden flames whose color comes from incandescent carbon particles. Complete combustion yields blue flames, in which excited molecular radicals such as CH and C2 emit light in the blue and green parts of the spectrum. Different chemical elements burn with characteristic colors — sodium gives intense yellow, copper gives green or blue, strontium gives red, and potassium gives lilac — a property exploited in the flame test of analytical chemistry and in fireworks.

Typical temperatures include about 600 °C for a match flame's cooler regions, around 1,000 °C in parts of a candle flame, and up to about 1,500 °C in a Bunsen burner flame. An oxyacetylene flame can exceed 3,000 °C.

Structure

A candle flame has a characteristic teardrop shape under gravity, driven by buoyant convection: hot gases rise and draw in fresh air at the base. In microgravity, flames instead form spheres, because there is no buoyant flow, and they tend to burn dimmer and bluer due to limited oxygen supply. A diffusion flame's luminous zone is shaped by the balance between fuel vaporization, mixing, and reaction rate.

Flames in religion and culture

Fire and flame have long carried symbolic weight, representing purification, divine presence, passion, and destruction alike. The Olympic flame, eternal flames at memorials, and votive candles all draw on this symbolism.
