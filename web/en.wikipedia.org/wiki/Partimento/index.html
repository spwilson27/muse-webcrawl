Partimento

A partimento (plural partimenti) is a sketch, often a single bass line with figures, used as the basis for an improvised or composed keyboard piece. Partimenti were the core teaching tool of the Neapolitan conservatories of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, where students learned counterpoint, harmony, melody and form by realising thousands of them at the keyboard.

Practice

A partimento gives the student only a bass line — sometimes figured, sometimes unfigured — from which a complete composition must be improvised or written out. In realising it, students applied a repertoire of learned schemata: the Rule of the Octave for harmonising scales, standard cadences, sequences, suspensions and imitative entries. Masters such as Francesco Durante, Leonardo Leo, Nicola Porpora and Giovanni Paisiello left large collections of partimenti; the most famous pedagogue, Fedele Fenaroli, codified the method in his Regole (1775).

Historical significance

The partimento tradition trained the composers of the galant and early Classical eras, from Pergolesi and Cimarosa to pupils who carried the method across Europe. It fell out of use in the nineteenth century as conservatory teaching shifted toward written harmony exercises and four-part chorale style. Since the late twentieth century, scholars such as Robert Gjerdingen — whose work on galant schemata draws heavily on partimenti — have revived interest, and partimento-based improvisation is again taught as a historically informed route into eighteenth-century compositional craft.
