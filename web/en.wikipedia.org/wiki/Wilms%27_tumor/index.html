Wilms' tumor, also called nephroblastoma, is a cancer of the kidneys that occurs mainly in children. Named after the German surgeon Max Wilms, who described it in 1899, it is the most common kidney cancer of childhood, typically appearing between the ages of three and four.

Signs and symptoms

The most common presentation is a painless abdominal mass noticed by a parent or on examination. Other features may include abdominal pain, haematuria (blood in the urine), fever and high blood pressure. Most cases involve one kidney, though around 5–10% are bilateral.

Causes and associations

Wilms' tumor arises from embryonic kidney tissue (nephrogenic rests). Mutations in the WT1 gene on chromosome 11 are implicated, and the tumor is associated with syndromes including WAGR syndrome, Denys–Drash syndrome and Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome.

Treatment and prognosis

Management combines surgery (usually nephrectomy) with chemotherapy and, in higher-stage disease, radiotherapy, following protocols developed by cooperative groups such as the Children's Oncology Group and SIOP. Outcomes have improved dramatically, with overall survival now exceeding 90% for localised disease.
