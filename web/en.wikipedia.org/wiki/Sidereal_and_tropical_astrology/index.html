Sidereal and tropical astrology

Sidereal and tropical are two different coordinate systems, or zodiacs, used in astrology to divide the ecliptic into twelve signs of thirty degrees each. The tropical zodiac, used by most Western astrologers, is anchored to the seasons: 0° Aries begins at the March equinox. The sidereal zodiac, used in Hindu (Vedic) astrology and by a minority of Western astrologers, is anchored to the fixed stars and the actual constellations.

The two zodiacs drift apart by about one degree every 72 years due to axial precession.

The difference: precession

Because of the precession of the equinoxes, the position of the March equinox point slowly shifts westward along the ecliptic relative to the stars, by roughly 1° every 72 years, or about 30° — a full sign — every 2,150 years. When the tropical zodiac was codified in the Hellenistic period, the two systems roughly coincided. Today they differ by about 24°, so most planets fall in different signs depending on which system is used.

Ayanamsa

The angular difference between the two zodiacs at a given date is called the ayanamsa (Sanskrit: ayanāṃśa). Several values are in use; the Lahiri ayanamsa, standardized by the Indian Calendar Reform Committee in the 1950s, is the most widely used in India, with a value of about 24°. In the modern West, the Fagan–Bradley ayanamsa underlies the sidereal school revived in the twentieth century by astrologers such as Cyril Fagan.

Usage

The tropical zodiac predominates in Western astrology and is defended by its users on the grounds that the signs derive their meaning from the seasonal cycle rather than the constellations. The sidereal zodiac is standard in Jyotiṣa (Vedic astrology) and is defended on the grounds that it preserves the original alignment of signs with the visible star groups. The debate between the two systems is one of the oldest technical controversies in astrology.
