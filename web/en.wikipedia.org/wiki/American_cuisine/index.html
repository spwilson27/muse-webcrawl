American cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An American diner meal with a burger, fries and a milkshake]

The cuisine of the United States reflects the nation's history of immigration and regional diversity. It combines Native American ingredients (corn, beans, squash, turkey, maple syrup) with the cooking of successive settlers — English, German, Dutch, African (through the forced migration of enslaved people), French, Italian, Mexican, Chinese and many others — producing both a shared national repertoire and proud regional cuisines.

Iconic foods include the Hamburger, hot dog, Fried chicken, barbecue, clam chowder, Thanksgiving turkey with pumpkin pie, Tex-Mex, Cajun and Creole cooking of Louisiana, soul food, New York pizza and deli culture, and the diner Breakfast of pancakes, eggs and bottomless coffee.

Regional cuisines

New England cooks seafood, baked beans and maple; the South barbecues pork, fries chicken and slow-cooks greens; Louisiana's Cajun and Creole kitchens use the "holy trinity" of onion, celery and bell pepper with rice, gumbo and jambalaya; the Southwest blends Mexican and cowboy traditions; the Pacific Northwest prizes salmon and coffee; Hawaii fuses Polynesian, Japanese, Filipino and American food into plate lunches and poke.

Industrial food

The US pioneered industrial food: canning, frozen dinners, breakfast cereals, processed cheese and Fast food chains, exported worldwide. Counter-currents — from James Beard to Alice Waters's California cuisine and the farm-to-table movement — reasserted regional and seasonal cooking.
