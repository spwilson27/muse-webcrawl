Nathan of Breslov

Nathan of Breslov
Born: Nathan Sternhartz, January 22, 1780, Nemyriv, Ukraine
Died: December 20, 1844, Breslov, Ukraine
Known for: Foremost disciple of Rebbe Nachman of Breslov; author/compiler of Breslov literature
Movement: Breslov Hasidism

Nathan of Breslov (Hebrew: נתן מברסלב), also known as Reb Noson or Rabbi Nathan Sternhartz (January 22, 1780 – December 20, 1844), was the chief disciple and scribe of Rebbe Nachman of Breslov, founder of the Breslov Hasidic movement. After his master's death in 1810, Nathan devoted his life to recording, publishing, and spreading Rebbe Nachman's teachings, effectively building the Breslov movement and keeping it alive without a successor rebbe.

Life

Born in Nemyriv to a wealthy merchant family, Nathan received a traditional education and was initially drawn to Hasidism through other courts before meeting Nachman of Breslov in 1802. He quickly became the Rebbe's closest follower, writing down his lessons, stories, and conversations. After Nachman's death, Nathan moved to Breslov, organized the annual Rosh Hashana pilgrimage (the kibbutz) to the Rebbe's grave in Uman, established a printing operation to publish Breslov books despite fierce opposition from other Hasidic groups, and in 1834 built the Breslov synagogue in Uman.

Works

Nathan edited and published Rebbe Nachman's teachings in works including Likutei Moharan (the Rebbe's magnum opus), Sefer HaMiddot, Sippurei Ma'asiyyot (the famed Tales), and Sichos HaRan and Chayei Moharan, which preserve biographical and conversational material. His own monumental work, Likutei Halakhot, is an eight-volume commentary linking the legal code Shulchan Aruch to Breslov teachings, and his Likutei Tefillot ("Collected Prayers") turned the Rebbe's lessons into personal prayers that remain central to Breslov devotion.

Legacy

Nathan is buried in Breslov. His insistence that the movement could continue through the Rebbe's teachings alone—without a living successor—made Breslov unique among Hasidic dynasties, and his writings remain the foundation of Breslov practice today.
