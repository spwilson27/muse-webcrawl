Phowa

Phowa (Tibetan: འཕོ་བ་, Wylie: 'pho ba; Sanskrit: saṃkrānti) is a tantric practice within Tibetan Buddhism, described as the transference of consciousness at the time of death. It is one of the Six Dharmas of Naropa, and its purpose is to eject the practitioner's consciousness from the body—traditionally through the crown of the head—into a pure land such as Sukhavati, the realm of the Buddha Amitabha, or to unite it with the dharmakaya, thereby avoiding an unfavourable rebirth in saṃsāra.

Practice

In the standard visualisation, the practitioner imagines the central channel running from the base of the spine to the crown, with the consciousness as a syllable or bindu at the heart and Amitabha seated above the head. With the forceful exclamation "hik!" and "ka," the consciousness is propelled upward and merged with the Buddha. Training in life—sometimes in intensive retreats—is said to produce physical signs, such as a softening or small opening at the crown into which a blade of kusha grass can be inserted, and a drop of fluid appearing there.

For the dying and the dead

Phowa may be performed for oneself at death, or by a lama on behalf of another person who is dying or recently deceased, guiding the consciousness out of the body. It is often recited together with readings from the Bardo Thodol (the so-called Tibetan Book of the Dead). Teachers emphasise that phowa is not a substitute for ethical conduct and meditation but a support at the critical moment of death.

Lineages

The practice is transmitted in all major schools of Tibetan Buddhism. The Drikung Kagyu school is especially known for its "Jaktse" phowa tradition and for the great phowa teachings (Phowa Chenmo) given periodically to large assemblies, a tradition associated with the Chungtsang Rinpoches. Marpa the Translator is credited with bringing the Six Dharmas, including phowa, from India to Tibet.
