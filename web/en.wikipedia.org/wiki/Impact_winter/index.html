Impact winter

Impact winter
CauseImpact of a large asteroid or comet
MechanismStratospheric dust and sulphate aerosols block sunlight
EffectsGlobal cooling, collapse of photosynthesis, mass extinction

An impact winter is a hypothesised period of prolonged global cooling caused by the impact of a large asteroid or comet on the Earth. If an impactor struck the Earth and threw sufficient quantities of dust, pulverised rock and sulphur-rich vapour into the stratosphere, the resulting aerosol layer would block sunlight for months to years, dropping surface temperatures, halting photosynthesis and collapsing food chains — an effect analogous to a volcanic winter or a nuclear winter, but potentially far more severe.

Mechanism

Unlike ordinary weather dust, fine material injected into the stratosphere is not quickly rained out, and can circle the globe within weeks. Sulphur compounds from vaporised target rock (as in the sulphur-rich sediments at Chicxulub) convert to sulphate aerosols that are especially effective at scattering sunlight. Models of the Cretaceous–Paleogene impact suggest global mean temperatures fell by more than 10 °C, land temperatures plunged below freezing for extended periods, and darkness sufficient to stop photosynthesis lasted for many months.

Evidence and examples

The impact winter concept was developed in the 1980s following the Alvarez hypothesis, which attributed the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago to a ~10 km impactor. The worldwide iridium layer, shocked quartz and the buried Chicxulub crater in Mexico support the scenario; climate simulations and the pattern of extinctions — devastating to photosynthesis-dependent food webs, less so to detritus-based ones — match its predictions. The smaller Toba super-eruption (~74,000 years ago) produced a comparable but volcanic "winter" and has been linked to a human population bottleneck.

Relation to other winters

Impact winter is one member of a family of sunlight-blocking catastrophes that includes volcanic winters (e.g. the Year Without a Summer, 1816, after Tambora) and nuclear winter. Because the hazard depends on impactor size and target composition, planetary defence programmes survey near-Earth objects large enough to cause such an event.
