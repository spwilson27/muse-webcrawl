In physical chemistry, the Faraday constant (denoted F) is the magnitude of electric charge per mole of electrons. It is named after the English scientist Michael Faraday. Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units, the Faraday constant has the exactly defined value F = 96 485.33212... C·mol−1, being the product of the elementary charge e (exactly 1.602176634×10−19 C) and the Avogadro constant NA (exactly 6.02214076×1023 mol−1).

Significance

The constant arises from Faraday's laws of electrolysis, which state that the amount of chemical change produced by an electric current is proportional to the charge passed, and that the masses of substances deposited or dissolved are proportional to their equivalent weights. One faraday of charge deposits one gram-equivalent of any substance during electrolysis — for example, about 107.87 g of silver from a silver nitrate solution.

Uses

The Faraday constant is central to electrochemistry: it converts between amounts of substance and charge in electrolysis, appears in the Nernst equation (through the combination RT/F, about 25.7 mV at room temperature), and relates the Gibbs energy change of a cell reaction to its potential via ΔG = −nFE. A related unit, the faraday, was formerly used as a unit of charge equal to this constant.
