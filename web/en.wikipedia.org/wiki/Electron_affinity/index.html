Electron affinity

Electron affinity
Symbol: Eea
Units: kJ/mol or eV
Largest: chlorine (~349 kJ/mol)
Related: ionisation energy, electronegativity

The electron affinity of an atom or molecule is the amount of energy released when an electron is added to a neutral atom in the gaseous state to form a negative ion. It quantifies how strongly an atom attracts an additional electron.

Trends

Electron affinity generally increases across a period of the periodic table, as the increasing nuclear charge pulls added electrons more strongly, and the halogens have the highest values — chlorine's is the largest of any element. Down a group the trend is weaker and less regular because added electrons enter shells farther from the nucleus. Noble gases and alkaline earth metals have near-zero or negative electron affinities because their electron shells are already stable.

Second electron affinity

Adding a second electron to an already negative ion requires energy input, since the incoming electron is repelled by the ion's charge; second electron affinities are therefore always endothermic.

Measurement

Electron affinities are measured by techniques such as photodetachment spectroscopy, in which laser light strips the extra electron from an anion and the threshold energy is determined.
