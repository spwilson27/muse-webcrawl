Sex offender registry

A sex offender registry is a system used by governments to track and monitor the activities, residence and movements of people convicted of sex offences after their release from custody. Registries exist in a number of countries, but they differ greatly in whether the information is available to the public or only to law enforcement.

United States

The modern American system grew from the Jacob Wetterling Act of 1994, which required states to maintain registries, and Megan's Law (1996), which required public notification. The federal Sex Offender Registration and Notification Act (SORNA), part of the Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act of 2006, created a national framework classifying offenders into three tiers by offence severity, with registration periods from 15 years to life. Registrants must periodically verify their address and report changes; failure to register is itself a criminal offence. State websites make much of the data publicly searchable.

Other countries

The United Kingdom maintains the Violent and Sex Offender Register (ViSOR), accessible only to police and probation services, though parents may request information under the Child Sex Offender Disclosure Scheme ("Sarah's Law"). Canada operates a national registry restricted to law enforcement. Australia and New Zealand maintain non-public registers, while South Korea and a few other jurisdictions make some offender information public.

Criticism

Critics argue that public registries impede rehabilitation, expose registrants and their families to vigilantism and homelessness (through residency restrictions), and capture many low-risk offenders, including juveniles; supporters credit them with aiding investigations and public awareness. Empirical research on their effect on recidivism is mixed.
