Onomasiology

Onomasiology (from Greek ὀνομάζω onomāzō, "to name", and -λογία -logia) is a branch of linguistics and semantics concerned with the naming of concepts: it begins from a concept or referent and investigates the various expressions by which that concept can be designated. In this respect it is the mirror image of semasiology, which begins from a word and investigates the range of meanings it can convey.

Scope and methods

Onomasiological research asks, for example, how speakers of different languages or dialects name the same object, plant, animal or idea, and what motivates the names they choose. It is closely connected with dialectology and linguistic geography: the great linguistic atlases compiled by the Wörter und Sachen ("words and things") school mapped competing designations for everyday concepts across regions. Onomasiology also studies processes of word formation and lexical change, since new concepts constantly demand new names, and old names shift to new concepts.

History

The discipline emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries within European linguistics; the term was popularized in German scholarship (Bezeichnungsgeschichte). In the later 20th century, onomasiology was revived within cognitive linguistics, most notably in the work of scholars such as Dirk Geeraerts, who contrasted semasiological change (changes in a word's meaning) with onomasiological change (changes in the set of names available for a concept), treating the latter as a measure of lexical salience and entrenchment.

Related areas

Onomasiology overlaps with onomastics (the study of proper names) only in part; it is primarily concerned with common nouns and naming strategies. It is also relevant to terminology science, lexicography and translation studies, where the mapping between concepts and their designations in different languages is a practical problem.
