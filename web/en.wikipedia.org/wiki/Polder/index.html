Polder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A polder (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈpɔldər]) is a low-lying tract of land that forms an artificial hydrological entity, enclosed by embankments known as dikes. The ground level in drained marshes subsides over time, so all polders eventually lie below the surrounding water level. Water in polders is managed artificially: drainage systems of ditches, canals, and pumping stations (historically windmills) remove excess water into surrounding waterways. Polders are most strongly associated with the Netherlands, where they make up a large share of the country's land area — roughly a fifth to a quarter of the territory has been reclaimed from the sea, lakes, and marshes.

Formation

Polders are created by enclosing a body of shallow water or marshland with dikes and then draining it. The three main types are land reclaimed from a sea or lake (such as the Dutch Noordoostpolder and Flevopolders in the former Zuiderzee), floodplains enclosed near rivers, and marshes enclosed and drained in place (the low-lying peat polders of the western Netherlands). After reclamation, soil — especially peat — oxidizes and compacts, a process called subsidence, so polders sink further below water level and require continuous drainage.

The Netherlands

Dutch polder building began in earnest in the Middle Ages, organized through local water boards (waterschappen), among the oldest democratic institutions in the country. From the 17th century, windmills drained lake polders such as the Beemster (1612) and Schermer. Steam, then diesel and electric pumping stations followed; the largest project was the enclosure and partial draining of the Zuiderzee under the Zuiderzee Works, followed by the Delta Works after the catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953. The Dutch saying "God created the Earth, but the Dutch created the Netherlands" reflects this history. The polder model has also entered political vocabulary as a term for consensus-based decision-making.

Elsewhere

Polders exist in many other countries, including Belgium (the Flemish polders along the coast), Germany, France (the Marais Poitevin), the United Kingdom (parts of the Fens and the Somerset Levels), Italy, Spain, Poland (the Żuławy Wiślane), Bangladesh, Guyana, and Japan, where they are called shinden; large modern examples include the vast empoldered delta lands of Bangladesh protected by coastal embankments.

Risks

Because polders lie below surrounding water levels, dike failure or extreme flooding can cause rapid, deep inundation. Sea-level rise and continued soil subsidence pose long-term challenges, and Dutch policy has shifted partly from drainage alone toward giving "room for the river" and managed water storage.
