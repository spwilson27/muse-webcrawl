Record label

A record label, or record company, is a brand or trademark of music recordings and music videos, or the company that owns it. Sometimes, a record label is also a publishing company that manages such brands and trademarks, coordinates the production, manufacture, distribution, marketing, promotion, and enforcement of copyright for sound recordings and music videos, while also conducting talent scouting and development of new artists (A&R), and maintaining contracts with recording artists and their managers.

The term "record label" derives from the circular label in the centre of a vinyl record which prominently displays the manufacturer's name, along with other information. Within the music industry, most recording artists have traditionally relied on record labels to broaden their consumer base, market their albums, and promote their singles on streaming services, radio, and television.

Major and independent labels

By the 2010s the market had consolidated into three major groups — Universal Music Group, Sony Music Entertainment and Warner Music Group — collectively called the "Big Three", which between them account for the large majority of global recorded-music revenue. Independent ("indie") labels operate outside these groups, often focusing on particular genres or scenes; historically influential independents include Sun Records, Motown (later acquired by Universal), Atlantic (within Warner), Sub Pop and XL Recordings. Many majors also own or distribute nominally independent imprints.

Functions

  • A&R (artists and repertoire) — scouting and signing talent, matching artists with songwriters and producers, overseeing recording.
  • Production and manufacturing — financing studio time and producing physical media and digital masters.
  • Marketing and promotion — publicity, radio and playlist promotion, music videos, advertising.
  • Distribution — delivering recordings to retailers and streaming platforms, historically through the label's own distribution arm.
  • Rights administration — licensing, royalty collection and copyright enforcement.

Contracts and criticism

Traditional recording contracts give the label ownership of the master recordings in exchange for advances and royalties to the artist; the length of copyright ownership and low royalty rates have drawn sustained criticism from artists, from Prince's 1990s dispute with Warner Bros. to Taylor Swift's re-recording of her early albums after the sale of her masters. Since the 2000s, "360 deals" have extended labels' participation to touring, merchandise and endorsements. Digital distribution has lowered barriers to entry, enabling self-releasing artists and label-services companies, though the majors retain dominant market share and catalogue.

Imprints and vanity labels

Large companies operate multiple imprints specialising by genre or territory. Successful artists are sometimes given vanity labels (e.g. the Beatles' Apple Records, Led Zeppelin's Swan Song) distributed by a major. Imprints frequently survive changes of corporate ownership as brands long after their founding companies disappear.
