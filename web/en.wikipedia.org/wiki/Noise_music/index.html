Noise music is a form of music that uses sounds normally considered noise — feedback, static, distortion, machine sounds, and unpitched or abrasive timbres — as its primary material. It challenges the conventional distinction between musical and non-musical sound, and is associated with avant-garde, industrial and experimental scenes worldwide.

Origins

The Italian Futurist Luigi Russolo's 1913 manifesto The Art of Noises argued that the industrial soundscape demanded a new music; he built mechanical "intonarumori" noise instruments. Later precedents include Edgard Varèse, John Cage's prepared piano and compositions for radio and tape, and musique concrète's spliced found sounds.

Development

In the 1960s–70s, the Velvet Underground's feedback, Lou Reed's Metal Machine Music (1975), and industrial groups such as Throbbing Gristle and SPK brought abrasive sound into rock contexts. Japan developed a distinctive, extreme scene — "Japanoise" — led by Merzbow (Masami Akita), Hijokaidan and Incapacitants, characterised by dense, high-volume walls of distortion circulated on cassette. Power electronics (Whitehouse) and later harsh noise wall (Vomir) pushed toward confrontation and stasis respectively.

Techniques and aesthetics

Noise performers commonly use feedback loops, circuit-bent electronics, contact microphones on scrap metal, shortwave radio, and chains of distortion pedals, often improvising live. Scholarship (e.g. Paul Hegarty's Noise/Music, Jacques Attali's Noise: The Political Economy of Music) reads noise both as aesthetic material and as a critique of order, commodification and the very category of "music".

Related scenes

Noise overlaps with industrial, drone, free improvisation, sound art and some extreme metal. Dedicated labels (RRRecords, Hospital Productions) and festivals sustain an international underground network.
