Chapel

A chapel is a Christian place of prayer and worship that is usually relatively small. The term has several senses: it may denote a place of worship within a larger building (such as a castle, palace, hospital, school, airport or prison), a side area of a large church containing an altar, or — particularly in Britain — a Nonconformist place of worship. Unlike a parish church, a chapel generally has no parish of its own and no resident parish priest.

Etymology

The word derives from the Latin cappella, diminutive of cappa (cloak), after the cloak of St Martin of Tours, preserved as a relic by the Frankish kings and carried into battle. The sanctuary where the relic was kept came to be called a cappella, and the priests who served it cappellani — the origin of the word chaplain.

Types of chapel

  • Private chapels in great houses, castles and palaces, such as the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris, built to house relics of the Passion.
  • Institutional chapels in colleges, hospitals, military bases, prisons, airports and cemeteries.
  • Side chapels within cathedrals and large churches, often dedicated to a saint or used for private prayer, including Lady chapels dedicated to the Virgin Mary.
  • Chapels of ease, built within a large parish to serve distant parishioners.
  • Nonconformist chapels — in Wales and parts of England, "chapel" became the generic term for the meeting houses of Methodist, Baptist, Congregational and other dissenting congregations.

Famous chapels

The Sistine Chapel in the Vatican, famous for Michelangelo's ceiling, is the chapel of the Apostolic Palace and the site of papal conclaves. The Sainte-Chapelle (1248) in Paris is a masterpiece of Rayonnant Gothic. King's College Chapel, Cambridge, is renowned for its fan vaulting and choral tradition.
