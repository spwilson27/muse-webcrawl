A fire brick, firebrick, fire clay brick, or refractory brick is a block of ceramic material used in lining furnaces, kilns, fireboxes and fireplaces. A refractory brick is built primarily to withstand high temperature, but also usually has a low thermal conductivity for greater energy efficiency.

Properties and grades

Fire bricks are made from fire clay containing mainly alumina and silica. Standard-duty fire bricks withstand temperatures up to about 1,650 °C, while high-alumina bricks — with alumina contents from roughly 50% to over 80% — serve at still higher temperatures and resist slag attack better. Dense fire bricks are strong and abrasion-resistant but conduct more heat; insulating fire bricks are porous, lightweight and have low thermal conductivity, making them suitable for backup insulation in kilns, forges and pizza ovens.

Unlike ordinary clay bricks, fire bricks do not readily spall under rapid temperature change, and their pale buff colour reflects their low iron content. They are laid with refractory mortar formulated to match their expansion and service temperature.

Uses

  • Lining of industrial furnaces, rotary kilns and incinerators
  • Fireboxes of boilers, locomotives and wood stoves
  • Domestic fireplaces, chimneys, pizza ovens and forges
  • Steel and glass industry vessels (special grades)

History

Refractory bricks became an industry in their own right during the Industrial Revolution, when centres such as Stourbridge in England and the Glenboig district of Scotland supplied fire bricks worldwide for steam boilers, gasworks and iron furnaces. Glenboig bricks were so renowned they were exported globally and stamped with the works' name.
