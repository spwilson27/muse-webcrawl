Capsaicinoid

Chili peppers owe their pungency to capsaicinoids. (Image placeholder)

Capsaicinoids are a class of chemical compounds produced as secondary metabolites by chili peppers of the genus Capsicum. They are responsible for the pungent ("hot") sensation of the fruit and act as a deterrent against mammals and fungal parasites, while birds — which disperse the seeds — are insensitive to them. The best-known capsaicinoid is capsaicin, which typically accounts for the majority of the capsaicinoid content of a pepper, followed by dihydrocapsaicin.

Chemistry and mechanism

Capsaicinoids share a common structural motif: a vanillyl group linked by an amide bond to a fatty acid chain of variable length and saturation. They exert their effect by binding to the TRPV1 receptor, a heat- and pain-sensitive ion channel in sensory neurons, which normally responds to temperatures above about 43 °C and to acidity; this is why capsaicin produces a burning sensation without actual thermal injury.

Uses

Beyond their role in food, capsaicinoids are used in topical analgesic creams for conditions such as arthritis and neuropathic pain, in pepper spray (oleoresin capsicum) for self-defence and law enforcement, and as additives in animal feed and antifouling paints. Oral "capsaicinoid" supplements are also marketed for weight management, based on their modest thermogenic effects.

The Scoville scale, devised by Wilbur Scoville in 1912, measures pepper pungency in terms of total capsaicinoid concentration; modern analysis uses high-performance liquid chromatography instead of the original organoleptic dilution test.
