Haikai

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Haikai (Japanese: 俳諧, "comic, unorthodox") is a broad category of Japanese poetry and poetic practice that flourished in the Edo period (1603–1868), distinguished from the aristocratic court tradition of waka and orthodox linked verse (renga) by its use of colloquial language, everyday and humorous subjects, and occasional Chinese vocabulary. The term is also used more loosely for the playful spirit informing several related genres.

Forms and history

The principal haikai genre was haikai no renga — comic linked verse composed in sequence by a group of poets — which developed in the sixteenth century under masters such as Yamazaki Sōkan and Arakida Moritake, whose collections (the Inu Tsukuba-shū, "Mongrel Tsukuba Collection") delighted in puns and vulgar wit. In the seventeenth century the Teimon school of Matsunaga Teitoku codified rules and wordplay, and the Danrin school of Nishiyama Sōin pushed toward freer, rapid-fire improvisation from everyday life.

Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694), originally a haikai master, transformed the genre by infusing it with the depth, stillness and aesthetic values of sabi and wabi, in works that reached fruition in his travel records such as Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Interior). Bashō prized the opening verse of a sequence, the hokku, and it was from this verse that Masaoka Shiki, in the Meiji era, carved out the independent modern poem known as the haiku. Haikai sensibility also fed the comic and satirical short poem senryū, the prose genre haibun, and haiga, the sketch-like paintings that accompany haikai texts.

Characteristics

Haikai poets deliberately mixed high and low registers, admitted Chinese-derived and slang words banned from court verse, and treated subjects from fleas and radishes to moon-viewing with the same formal care. The governing virtues included lightness (karumi), novelty, and the linked-verse art of shifting each new verse away from its predecessor while remaining connected — an aesthetic of constant change that Bashō compared to following "the creative" itself.
