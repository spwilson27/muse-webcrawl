Voltage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of a battery circuit labelled with voltage drops across components]
Voltage drives current around a circuit.

Voltage (also called electrical potential difference) is the difference in electric potential between two points: the work needed per unit charge to move a test charge between them, measured in volts (V), where 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb. It is named after Alessandro Volta, inventor of the voltaic pile (1800), the first battery.

Voltage is the 'pressure' of circuits: sources (batteries, generators, solar cells) maintain potential differences that drive current through resistances (Ohm's law, V = IR). In field terms it is the line integral of the Electric field; in quantum terms it couples directly to phase, and via the Josephson effect voltage is now defined by frequency and fundamental constants (the Josephson standard: 1 V ↔ 483.6 GHz). Voltages in nature range from microvolts across cell membranes' signalling to the hundreds of millions of volts in lightning. Household supplies (120 V or 230 V nominal) are a historical compromise of safety, efficiency and legacy.
