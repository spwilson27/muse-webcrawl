A United States magistrate judge is a judicial officer of the United States district courts, the trial courts of the federal judiciary. Magistrate judges are appointed by the district judges of a court, serve renewable eight-year terms (four years for part-time magistrate judges), and handle a wide variety of matters assigned by the Article III district judges.

Authority

Under the Federal Magistrates Act, magistrate judges may conduct preliminary proceedings in criminal cases — initial appearances, arraignments, detention hearings, and the issuance of search warrants and arrest warrants — and may try federal misdemeanor cases. In civil cases they conduct pretrial management, discovery, settlement conferences and evidentiary hearings, and prepare reports and recommendations on dispositive motions and habeas corpus petitions for the district judge's review.

With the consent of all parties, a magistrate judge may preside over an entire civil case through trial and entry of judgment, with appeal lying to the court of appeals as from a district judge's decision. A magistrate judge may not, without consent, try a felony case or finally decide dispositive motions.

History and selection

The office replaced the former system of United States commissioners, many of whom were not lawyers and were paid by fees. The Federal Magistrates Act of 1968 created the magistrate system; amendments in 1976 and 1979 greatly expanded its civil and criminal jurisdiction, and the Judicial Improvements Act of 1990 changed the title from "United States magistrate" to "United States magistrate judge." Candidates are screened by merit selection panels, and there are more than 570 authorized full-time magistrate judge positions nationwide.
