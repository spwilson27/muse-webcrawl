Clinical pastoral education

Clinical pastoral education (CPE) is education to teach pastoral care to clergy and others through supervised practice in clinical settings such as hospitals, hospices, prisons and other institutions. CPE is a method of learning pastoral care skills by engaging in ministry with people in crisis and reflecting on that practice, under supervision, in a peer group.

History

CPE began in the United States in the 1920s. The movement's principal founder was Anton Boisen, a Presbyterian minister who had himself experienced mental illness and believed that theological students should learn from "living human documents" by working with patients. Richard Cabot, a physician at Massachusetts General Hospital, and Russell Dicks were also influential in establishing supervised clinical training for ministry. The approach drew on the case study method and on experiential learning theory.

Method

A standard unit of CPE comprises 400 hours, combining direct ministry practice with group and individual supervision. Students present "verbatims" — written accounts of pastoral encounters — for critical reflection with peers and a certified supervisor. Learning goals typically include pastoral identity and formation, competence in listening and empathy, awareness of cultural and systemic dynamics, and the integration of theology with practice.

Accreditation and use

In the United States, CPE is accredited principally by ACPE: The Standard for Spiritual Care & Education. CPE training is required or expected for many hospital and hospice chaplains, and certification by bodies such as the Board of Chaplaincy Certification Inc. generally requires several units of CPE. Many seminaries and ordination processes also require at least one unit.
