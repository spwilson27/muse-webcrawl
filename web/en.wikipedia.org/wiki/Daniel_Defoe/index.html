Daniel Defoe (c. 1660 – 24 April 1731), born Daniel Foe, was an English writer, trader, journalist, pamphleteer and spy. He is most famous for his novel Robinson Crusoe (1719), often credited as the first novel in English and one of the most published books in history. A prolific and versatile writer, he produced more than three hundred works on topics from politics and crime to economics and geography.

Life

The son of a London tallow chandler and a Presbyterian Dissenter, Defoe was educated at a dissenting academy and entered trade, dealing in hosiery, woollens and wine. His business career was turbulent — he was bankrupted in 1692 and imprisoned for debt. He supported William III, wrote the celebrated poem The True-Born Englishman (1701), and in 1703 was pilloried and imprisoned for the satirical pamphlet The Shortest-Way with the Dissenters. Released through the intervention of Robert Harley, he became a government agent and propagandist, and founded the periodical The Review (1704–1713), a forerunner of the modern magazine. He worked in Scotland in support of the Anglo-Scottish Union of 1707.

The novels

Defoe turned to long fiction late in life. Robinson Crusoe (1719), inspired in part by the real castaway Alexander Selkirk, was an immediate success, followed by Captain Singleton (1720), Moll Flanders and A Journal of the Plague Year (both 1722), Colonel Jack (1722) and Roxana (1724). His pseudo-factual, plain style and attention to economic detail led later critics such as Ian Watt to credit him with founding the formal realism of the novel. Other major works include the three-volume Tour thro' the Whole Island of Great Britain (1724–1727) and The Complete English Tradesman (1726).
