Animal welfare

Free-range livestock are often cited as an example of improved animal welfare in agriculture.

Animal welfare is the well-being of non-human animals. Formal standards of animal welfare vary between contexts and are debated. Strong views exist about the extent to which animals should be treated humanely, ranging from concern for humane treatment in farming and experimentation to the position, associated with animal rights, that animals should not be used by humans at all.

History

Systematic concern for the welfare of animals used by humans appeared in Britain in the early 19th century. The first animal welfare legislation was the Cruel Treatment of Cattle Act 1822, often called Martin's Act after the Irish MP Richard Martin. The RSPCA was founded in 1824. In the 20th century, the publication of Ruth Harrison's Animal Machines (1964) prompted the British government's Brambell Report (1965), which led to the influential concept of the Five Freedoms.

The Five Freedoms

The Five Freedoms, developed by the UK Farm Animal Welfare Council, state that animals should have:

  • Freedom from hunger and thirst
  • Freedom from discomfort
  • Freedom from pain, injury or disease
  • Freedom to express normal behaviour
  • Freedom from fear and distress

Assessment

Animal welfare science uses indicators such as health, productivity, behaviour and physiology to assess how an animal is coping with its environment. The philosopher and animal welfare scientist Donald Broom defined welfare as the state of an animal as regards its attempts to cope with its environment. Welfare assessment frameworks, such as the Welfare Quality project in Europe, combine animal-based measures including body condition, lameness and social behaviour.

Legislation

Many countries regulate the treatment of animals in farming, transport, slaughter, research and entertainment. The European Union recognises animals as sentient beings under the Treaty of Lisbon (2009). In the United States, the Animal Welfare Act of 1966 covers animals in research, exhibition and transport, but excludes farm animals used for food. Britain's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 formally recognised animal sentience in law.

Criticism and debate

Welfarism is criticised by abolitionist animal rights advocates, such as Gary Francione, who argue that welfare reforms legitimise animal exploitation. Others, such as Peter Singer, argue from a utilitarian standpoint that reducing animal suffering is morally required regardless of the legal status of animals. Welfare standards are also criticised for relying on industry self-regulation and for differences between standards in intensive and extensive production systems.
