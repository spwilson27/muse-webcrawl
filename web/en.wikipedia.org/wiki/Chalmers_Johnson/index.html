Chalmers Johnson

Chalmers Ashby Johnson (August 6, 1931 – November 20, 2010) was an American political scientist and author. A professor at the University of California, San Diego, he specialised in the politics and economics of East Asia, particularly Japan. He is credited with coining the concept of the "developmental state" in his influential study of Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI). In later life he became a prominent critic of American foreign policy and military expansion, popularising the Central Intelligence Agency term "blowback" in a trilogy of books.

Life and career

Johnson was born in Phoenix, Arizona, and grew up in Texas and California. He served as a naval officer during the Korean War, an experience that took him to Japan. He earned his bachelor's degree in economics and his doctorate in political science from the University of California, Berkeley, where his dissertation concerned the Chinese Communist conquest of China. He taught at the University of California, Los Angeles, and then at UC San Diego, where he was a founding member of the Graduate School of International Relations and Pacific Studies. He also co-founded and served as president of the Japan Policy Research Institute.

The developmental state

Johnson's 1982 book MITI and the Japanese Miracle argued that Japan's rapid post-war industrial growth was guided by a "capitalist developmental state", in which an elite bureaucracy — above all MITI — set strategic priorities and steered private firms toward them. The book reshaped the study of East Asian political economy and influenced debates about industrial policy worldwide.

His earlier work included Peasant Nationalism and Communist Power (1962), which argued that Chinese Communist success owed much to nationalist resistance to Japanese invasion rather than to social revolution alone.

Criticism of American empire

After the Cold War, Johnson turned to criticising United States foreign policy. In Blowback: The Costs and Consequences of American Empire (2000), published shortly before the September 11 attacks, he argued that covert and imperial policies abroad would produce unintended retaliation against the United States. He continued the argument in The Sorrows of Empire (2004), about America's global network of military bases, and Nemesis: The Last Days of the American Republic (2006).

Death

Johnson died on November 20, 2010, at his home in Cardiff-by-the-Sea, California, after a long battle with rheumatoid arthritis. His final book, Dismantling the Empire, was published in 2010.
