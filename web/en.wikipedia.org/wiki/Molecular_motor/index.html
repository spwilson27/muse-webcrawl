Molecular motor

Molecular motors are biological molecular machines that convert chemical energy, usually from the hydrolysis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), into mechanical work. They are essential agents of movement in living organisms, responsible for intracellular transport, muscle contraction, cell division, DNA replication and the rotation of bacterial flagella.

Classes

The best-studied molecular motors are the cytoskeletal motor proteins: myosins, which move along actin filaments and power muscle contraction and cytokinesis; kinesins, which generally walk toward the plus end of microtubules and transport vesicles and organelles; and dyneins, which move toward the minus end and power the beating of cilia and flagella. Other motors include the rotary ATP synthase, which both synthesises and hydrolyses ATP as its central shaft rotates; helicases and polymerases that move along DNA and RNA; and the packaging motors of bacteriophages.

Mechanism

Motor proteins cycle through ATP binding, hydrolysis and product release, coupling each step to a conformational change — often described as a power stroke or a hand-over-hand ("walking") motion. Single kinesin molecules take 8-nanometre steps along a microtubule and can exert forces of several piconewtons. Brownian ratchet models explain how thermal fluctuations are rectified into directed motion.

Synthetic molecular motors

Chemists have built artificial molecular motors driven by light, chemical fuels or electric fields. The work of Jean-Pierre Sauvage, J. Fraser Stoddart and Ben Feringa on molecular machines was recognised by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
