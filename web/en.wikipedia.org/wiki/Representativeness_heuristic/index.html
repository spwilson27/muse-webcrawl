Representativeness heuristic

The representativeness heuristic is a cognitive shortcut used when making judgements about the probability of an event under uncertainty: people assess probability by how much one thing resembles, or is representative of, another. It was identified by psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in work beginning in the early 1970s and is one of the original heuristics in their heuristics-and-biases programme.

Associated biases

Reliance on representativeness produces several predictable errors:

  • Insensitivity to prior probabilities (base rate neglect): in the famous engineers-and-lawyers experiment, descriptions swamped statistical base rates in subjects' judgements.
  • Insensitivity to sample size: people expect small samples to mirror the population, fuelling the "law of small numbers" and the gambler's fallacy.
  • Misconceptions of chance: sequences such as H-T-H-T-T-H are judged more likely than H-H-H-T-T-T even though random processes produce both equally often.
  • Conjunction fallacy: in the Linda problem, most respondents rated "bank teller and feminist" as more probable than "bank teller", because the combined description seemed more representative.
  • Insensitivity to predictability and the illusion of validity: confidence tracks how coherent a description feels rather than how reliable the evidence is.

Criticism

Gerd Gigerenzer and others have argued that some effects shrink when probabilities are presented as natural frequencies, and that the heuristic is vaguely defined. Nevertheless, representativeness remains a standard explanation for stereotyping, clinical judgement errors and financial forecasting mistakes.
