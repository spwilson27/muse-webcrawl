Vegetative reproduction (also known as vegetative propagation, vegetative multiplication or cloning) is a form of asexual reproduction in plants in which a new individual grows from a fragment or specialized structure of the parent plant, without the production of seeds or spores. The offspring are genetically identical to the parent.

Natural mechanisms

Plants have evolved a range of specialized structures for vegetative reproduction. Rhizomes are horizontal underground stems that produce new shoots, as in ginger and many grasses; stolons or runners grow along the surface, as in strawberries; bulbs (onion), corms (crocus), tubers (potato) and bulbils store food and bud off new plants; and suckers arise from roots, as in aspen, whose clonal colonies — such as the Pando grove in Utah — are among the largest and oldest organisms on Earth.

Artificial propagation

Horticulturists and farmers exploit vegetative propagation to reproduce desirable cultivars that do not breed true from seed. Techniques include taking cuttings, grafting, layering, division and tissue culture (micropropagation). Bananas, seedless grapes, navel oranges and many potato varieties are maintained entirely by vegetative means.

Advantages and disadvantages

Vegetative reproduction is faster than growing from seed and preserves valued traits exactly, but the resulting genetic uniformity makes populations vulnerable to disease and environmental change — a factor in, for example, the susceptibility of clonally propagated banana cultivars to fungal pandemics.
