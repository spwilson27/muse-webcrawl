International trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

International trade is the exchange of capital, goods and services across international borders or territories. It is a major component of most economies' GDP and a defining feature of globalisation. Trade exists because countries differ — in resources, climate, technology and skills — and can gain by specialising according to comparative advantage, the principle (Smith, Ricardo) that even a country worse at producing everything gains by concentrating where its disadvantage is least and trading for the rest.

World trade grew faster than output for most of the period since 1945 under the liberalisation of the GATT and WTO, the container revolution, and the integration of China and the former Soviet bloc; its growth has levelled relative to GDP since about 2008 (“slowbalisation”). Composition has shifted from goods alone to services, data and global value chains in which a single product (a smartphone, an aircraft) embodies value added in dozens of countries. Trade policy — tariffs, quotas, subsidies, sanctions and industrial strategy — remains a central arena of politics, and trade deficits and job displacement (the “China shock” literature) fuel recurring protectionist pressure.

Institutions

The WTO (164 members as of the mid-2020s) sets and enforces the rules for most of world trade; regional agreements (EU single market, USMCA, RCEP, CPTPP) liberalise among subsets; export controls and sanctions have re-emerged as instruments of statecraft. Economists continue to find aggregate gains from trade with concentrated losses — the distributional problem that trade politics turns on.
