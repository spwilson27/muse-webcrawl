Black comedy, also known as dark comedy, gallows humour, or black humour, is a style of comedy that makes light of subject matter that is generally considered taboo, particularly subjects that are normally considered serious or painful to discuss — death, disease, war, violence, and human suffering. Writers and comedians often use it as a tool for exploring morbid issues, provoking discomfort and serious thought as well as amusement in their audience.

Origins and history

The term derives from the French humour noir, popularised by surrealist theorist André Breton, whose 1940 Anthology of Black Humor traced the mode back to Jonathan Swift. Elements of black comedy appear in Aristophanes and Chaucer, and in the "gallows humour" of condemned prisoners and soldiers. The genre flourished after the Second World War in the novels of Joseph Heller (Catch-22), Kurt Vonnegut (Slaughterhouse-Five), and Thomas Pynchon, and in films such as Dr. Strangelove (1964).

Characteristics

Black comedy typically juxtaposes morbid or horrific elements with farcical or deadpan delivery. It frequently targets institutions — the military, medicine, religion, bureaucracy — whose official solemnity is undercut by absurdity. The humour depends on the audience's recognition that the subject is grave; the shock of the contrast produces both laughter and unease.

Notable examples

  • Catch-22 (1961) — Joseph Heller's war novel
  • M*A*S*H (1970 film and television series)
  • Fargo (1996) and other Coen brothers films
