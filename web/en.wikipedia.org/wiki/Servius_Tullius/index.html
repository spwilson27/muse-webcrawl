Servius Tullius

King of Rome
Reign: c. 578 – 535 BC (traditional)
Predecessor: Tarquinius Priscus
Successor: Tarquinius Superbus

Servius Tullius was the legendary sixth king of Rome, reigning traditionally from about 578 to 535 BC. He succeeded Tarquinius Priscus and was in turn succeeded by Tarquinius Superbus, his son-in-law. Ancient tradition credited Servius with a series of major political and social reforms, above all the census and the organisation of the Roman people into classes and centuries, and with the first wall around the city.

Origins

Traditions about his birth conflicted. In the version told by Livy, Servius was the son of a slave woman named Ocrisia, a captive from Corniculum, raised in the palace of Tarquinius Priscus; a flame was said to have played around the child's head as he slept, a portent of greatness, and Queen Tanaquil protected and educated him. An alternative Etruscan tradition, reflected in the speech of the emperor Claudius and in Etruscan art such as the François Tomb, identified him with the Etruscan hero Mastarna, a companion of Caelius Vibenna.

Reforms

Servius was credited with dividing the Roman population by wealth into five property classes for military and political purposes, organising the comitia centuriata (centuriate assembly) around 193 centuries, and instituting the census, by which citizens and their property were registered — traditionally 80,000 men. He was also said to have reorganised the tribes: the four urban tribes and the rustic tribes of the surrounding territory. The "Servian Wall" of Rome was attributed to him, though the surviving circuit wall is now dated to the fourth century BC. Tradition also connected him with the temple of Diana on the Aventine, founded as a federal sanctuary of the Latins.

Death

According to the dramatic account in Livy, Servius was murdered through the ambition of his daughter Tullia and her husband Lucius Tarquinius (the future Tarquin the Proud): Tarquin seized the throne, and Servius was killed in the street — afterwards called the Vicus Sceleratus, the "Street of Crime" — and Tullia drove her carriage over her father's body.

Historicity

Modern scholars treat the details of the narrative as legendary, but many regard the "Servian" reforms as genuine institutional developments of the sixth century BC, even if their attribution to a single king is artificial. The census-based military organisation and Rome's expansion in this period fit the archaeology of archaic Rome's growth into a major city-state.
