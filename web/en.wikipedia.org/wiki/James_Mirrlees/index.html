Sir James Alexander Mirrlees (5 July 1936 – 29 August 2018) was a Scottish economist who shared the 1996 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with William Vickrey "for their fundamental contributions to the economic theory of incentives under asymmetric information". His 1971 paper on optimal income taxation founded the modern theory of tax design under private information.

Early life and education

Mirrlees was born in Minnigaff in southwest Scotland and educated at Douglas Academy and the University of Edinburgh, where he took a degree in mathematics and natural philosophy in 1957. He moved to Trinity College, Cambridge, completing his PhD in 1963 with a thesis on planning in economies with uncertain prospects. He was briefly a fellow at MIT before returning to Cambridge.

Contributions

In "An Exploration in the Theory of Optimum Income Taxation" (1971), Mirrlees modelled the problem facing a government that wishes to redistribute income but cannot observe citizens' earning ability. His analysis — the Mirrlees approach to mechanism design — showed how an optimal income tax schedule balances equity and efficiency and yielded the surprising result, within the model, of a zero marginal tax rate at the very top of the earnings distribution. The framework became foundational for public economics and contract theory.

Mirrlees also worked on growth theory, project appraisal with Ian Little (their OECD manual on cost–benefit analysis for developing countries), and the economics of insurance and welfare. He was professor of political economy at Cambridge from 1995 to 2003 and master of Trinity College, Cambridge, from 2003 until 2015, and later taught at the Chinese University of Hong Kong. He chaired the Mirrlees Review of the British tax system, published by the Institute for Fiscal Studies in 2011, which proposed a comprehensive reform of taxation.
