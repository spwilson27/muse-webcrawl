Củ Chi tunnels

The Củ Chi tunnels are an immense network of connecting tunnels located in the Củ Chi District of Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon), Vietnam, and are part of a much larger network of tunnels that underlie most of the country. The Củ Chi tunnels were the location of several military campaigns during the Vietnam War, and were the Viet Cong's base of operations for the Tết Offensive in 1968.

History

The tunnels were dug beginning in the late 1940s during the war against French colonial rule, and expanded greatly during the 1960s as the Viet Cong built an underground system stretching some 250 kilometres (160 mi) from the outskirts of Saigon to the Cambodian border. Located roughly 70 km (43 mi) northwest of Ho Chi Minh City, the district's hard clay soil made tunnelling possible with simple hand tools.

Structure

The network existed on multiple levels and contained living quarters, kitchens, hospitals, weapons caches, command centres, and storage areas, connected by narrow passages often less than a metre high. Booby traps, air vents disguised as termite mounds, and camouflaged trapdoors protected the system. Cooking smoke was vented through elaborate chambers to disperse it at a distance from the kitchens.

Countermeasures

American and Australian forces attempted to destroy the tunnels with bombing, defoliants, and gas. Volunteer soldiers known as "tunnel rats" entered the tunnels armed with a pistol, a knife, and a flashlight to search them. Large-scale B-52 bombing in Operation Crimp (1966) and later operations heavily cratered the area, but the network was never fully eliminated, and the base area around Củ Chi remained in Viet Cong hands.

Present day

The Vietnamese government has preserved about 75 miles (121 km) of the tunnels as a war memorial park, and two sections — at Bến Dược and Bến Đình — are open to tourists, who can crawl through enlarged, low-risk sections of the passages. The site is one of Vietnam's most visited historical attractions.
