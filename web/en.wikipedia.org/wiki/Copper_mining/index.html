Copper mining is the extraction of copper-bearing ores from the Earth for industrial use. Copper has been mined for thousands of years and remains one of the world's most important industrial metals, used in electrical wiring, electronics, construction, and renewable-energy technologies. World mine production is on the order of 22–26 million tonnes of contained copper per year, with Chile the largest producer.

Ore deposits

Most copper comes from porphyry copper deposits, large low-grade bodies associated with intrusive igneous rocks, where the principal ore mineral is chalcopyrite (CuFeS2). Other important deposit types include sediment-hosted stratiform deposits (as in the Central African Copperbelt), volcanogenic massive sulfides, iron oxide copper gold (IOCG) deposits such as Olympic Dam in Australia, and skarns. Oxidised near-surface ores contain minerals such as malachite and azurite.

Extraction and processing

Low-grade porphyry ores are usually mined in large open pits; higher-grade deposits may be mined underground. Sulfide ores are crushed, ground, and concentrated by froth flotation to produce a copper concentrate (typically 25–30% copper), which is then smelted and refined, often by electrorefining, to produce high-purity cathode copper. Oxide ores are commonly treated by heap leaching with dilute sulfuric acid followed by solvent extraction and electrowinning (SX-EW), which yields cathode copper directly.

History

Copper was among the first metals worked by humans, with evidence of mining in Anatolia, the Balkans, and the Great Lakes region of North America many thousands of years ago. Roman mines in Spain and Britain, medieval mines in Germany and Sweden (Falun), and the great nineteenth- and twentieth-century mines of Michigan, Montana, Chile, and Zambia trace the metal's central role in industrialisation.

Environmental issues

Copper mining generates large volumes of waste rock and tailings; sulfide waste can produce acid mine drainage. Modern operations face scrutiny over water use—particularly in arid regions such as northern Chile—and over tailings-dam safety.
