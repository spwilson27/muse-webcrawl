A cameo is a method of carving an object — usually a gem, shell, or layered stone — so that a raised (positive) relief design stands out against a background of a contrasting colour. Cameo is the converse of intaglio, in which the design is cut into the surface. Cameos are most often made into jewellery such as brooches, pendants, and rings, and have been carved since antiquity.

History

Antiquity

The art reached its first peak in the Hellenistic and Roman worlds. Masterpieces in layered sardonyx include the Gonzaga Cameo (3rd century BC), depicting Ptolemy II and Arsinoe II, and the Gemma Augustea, showing the emperor Augustus enthroned. Roman emperors wore large state cameos as emblems of authority, and many survive because medieval treasuries preserved them, often mounted in reliquaries and book covers.

Renaissance to the 19th century

Renaissance collectors revived cameo carving, and the art flourished again in the 18th and 19th centuries. Napoleon was an enthusiast who founded a school of gem engraving in Paris; his wife Joséphine's cameo parure remains in the Swedish royal collection. In Italy, carvers at Torre del Greco near Naples perfected cameos cut in marine shells (especially the helmet shell), which were cheaper than hardstone and became quintessential Victorian souvenirs of the Grand Tour. The finest hardstone workshops were in Idar-Oberstein, Germany.

Materials and technique

Ideal cameo material is banded: in sardonyx and agate, the carver cuts the design through a pale upper layer, leaving the darker layer as background. Shell cameos exploit the two layers of the shell — a white or pink relief over an orange-brown ground. Modern production also uses glass, resin, and machine (often ultrasonic) carving, while traditional hand-carving with steel tools, wheels, and abrasive dust continues in specialist workshops.
