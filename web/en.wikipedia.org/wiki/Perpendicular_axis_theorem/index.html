Perpendicular axis theorem

In physics, the perpendicular axis theorem states that the moment of inertia of a planar object about an axis perpendicular to its plane is equal to the sum of the moments of inertia about two perpendicular axes lying in the plane of the object and intersecting at a point where the perpendicular axis passes through it.

Iz = Ix + Iy

Derivation

Working in Cartesian coordinates, the moment of inertia of a planar body lying in the xy-plane about the z-axis is the integral of r² dm, where r² = x² + y². Splitting this into the integrals of x² dm and y² dm gives exactly the moments of inertia about the y-axis and the x-axis respectively, which establishes the result. The theorem holds only for planar (two-dimensional) objects, in contrast to the parallel axis theorem, which applies to bodies of any shape.

Example

For a uniform disc of mass M and radius R, the moments of inertia about two diameters are each (1/4)MR². By the perpendicular axis theorem, the moment of inertia about the central perpendicular axis is (1/4)MR² + (1/4)MR² = (1/2)MR², the standard result.
