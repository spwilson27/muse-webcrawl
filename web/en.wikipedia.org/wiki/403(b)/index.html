A 403(b) plan, formally a tax-sheltered annuity plan, is a retirement savings plan available in the United States to employees of public schools, employees of certain tax-exempt organisations under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, and certain ministers of religion. It is named after the section of the Internal Revenue Code that authorises it and functions in most respects like the better-known 401(k) plan offered by private employers.

How it works

Employees contribute a portion of salary, usually by payroll deduction, on a pre-tax basis (reducing current taxable income, with withdrawals taxed in retirement) or, if the plan allows, as after-tax Roth contributions with tax-free qualified withdrawals. Employers may also make contributions. Money is invested in annuity contracts issued by insurance companies or, since a 1974 change in the law, in mutual funds held in custodial accounts. Earnings grow tax-deferred until withdrawal, which is generally restricted before age 59½ except on separation from service, death, disability, or hardship.

Contribution limits

For 2024 the elective deferral limit was $23,000 for employees under 50, with an additional $7,500 catch-up for those 50 and older, plus a special "15 years of service" catch-up available to long-serving employees of eligible organisations. Combined employer and employee contributions are capped by the section 415 limit. Required minimum distributions generally begin at age 73.

Differences from 401(k)

Historically 403(b) plans were limited to annuity products and were lightly regulated; since Department of Labor regulations effective in 2009, most 403(b) plans are subject to ERISA-like requirements similar to 401(k) plans, though plans of public schools and churches often remain exempt from ERISA. 403(b) accounts can also be rolled over into IRAs or other employer plans upon separation from service.
