Ego

Ego (Latin for "I") may refer in psychology to the part of the psyche that mediates between instinctual drives and the demands of reality, and in ordinary language to a person's sense of self-importance. This article concerns the psychoanalytic concept developed by Sigmund Freud.

Freud's structural model: id, ego and super-ego.

Freud's structural model

In Freud's structural model of the psyche, set out in The Ego and the Id (1923), the mind comprises three agencies. The id is the wholly unconscious reservoir of instinctual drives operating on the pleasure principle. The ego develops out of the id through contact with the external world and operates on the reality principle, deferring gratification and negotiating compromises between drive, conscience and circumstance. The super-ego represents internalised parental and social prohibitions and ideals.

Functions

Freud and later ego psychologists such as Anna Freud and Heinz Hartmann attributed to the ego such functions as reality testing, perception, memory, judgement, impulse control, synthesis and the deployment of defence mechanisms — including repression, denial, projection and rationalisation — which manage anxiety arising from conflicts between the agencies. Anna Freud's The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936) systematised this catalogue.

Ego psychology and later views

Ego psychology, dominant in American psychoanalysis in the mid-twentieth century, emphasised the ego's autonomous, conflict-free capacities for adaptation. Erik Erikson built his theory of psychosocial development around the ego's growth across the lifespan. Critics within and beyond psychoanalysis have questioned the model's scientific testability, while the everyday sense of "ego" as vanity survives in words such as egotism and egocentric.
