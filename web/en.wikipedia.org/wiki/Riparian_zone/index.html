A riparian zone, or riparian area, is the interface between land and a river or stream — the strip of moist soil and vegetation immediately adjacent to the watercourse. The word derives from the Latin ripa, meaning river bank. Riparian zones are among the most ecologically productive and important habitats in many landscapes, often occupying a small fraction of the land area while supporting a disproportionate share of biodiversity.

Ecological functions

Riparian vegetation stabilizes banks against erosion with root networks, filters sediment, nutrients and pollutants from runoff before they reach the water, and shades streams, moderating temperature for fish and aquatic invertebrates. Fallen leaves and wood supply energy and habitat to the aquatic food web — large woody debris creates pools used by salmonids. Riparian corridors also connect fragmented habitats, allowing wildlife movement across agricultural and urban landscapes. Characteristic plants are hydrophilic species such as willows, alders, cottonwoods and sedges, and the zones grade into wetlands and floodplains.

Management and restoration

Because of their water-quality benefits, riparian buffer strips are a standard conservation practice in agriculture and forestry, often protected by setback regulations (for example in forest practice codes and agri-environment schemes). Degradation by grazing, channelization and development has made riparian restoration — fencing, replanting and reconnecting floodplains — a major focus of watershed management worldwide.
