New York School (art)

Years active1940s–1960s
LocationNew York City, United States
Major figuresJackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Mark Rothko, Arshile Gorky, Franz Kline, Robert Motherwell
InfluencesSurrealism, Cubism, European modernism

The New York School was an informal group of American poets, painters, dancers, and musicians active in New York City in the 1940s, 1950s, and 1960s. In visual art the term is closely associated with abstract expressionism, the movement that made New York, rather than Paris, the center of the Western art world after the Second World War.

Painting

The painters of the New York School — including Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Mark Rothko, Barnett Newman, Franz Kline, Arshile Gorky, Clyfford Still, Ad Reinhardt, and Robert Motherwell — developed large-scale, gestural or color-field abstraction influenced by Surrealist automatism and by European émigré artists who had fled to New York during the war. Many had worked for the WPA Federal Art Project in the 1930s and gathered around venues such as the Eighth Street Club and the Cedar Tavern. Critics Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg championed the movement, Rosenberg coining the term "action painting" in 1952. A second generation — including Joan Mitchell, Helen Frankenthaler, Grace Hartigan, and Larry Rivers — extended the idiom in the 1950s.

Poetry and other arts

The New York School of poetry centered on Frank O'Hara, John Ashbery, Kenneth Koch, and James Schuyler, whose conversational, urbane poems were closely tied to the painters' milieu; O'Hara worked at the Museum of Modern Art and collaborated with many artists. The term has also been applied to avant-garde composers around John Cage and to the Judson Dance Theater generation. The 1951 exhibition "9th Street Art Exhibition," organized by Leo Castelli, is often taken as the movement's public debut.
