Correspondence course

A correspondence course is a form of distance education in which instruction is delivered to students who are not physically present in a traditional classroom, historically by postal mail. Students receive printed lessons and assignments, complete them at their own pace, and return work to instructors for marking, communicating through letters. Correspondence study was the dominant form of distance education for more than a century before being largely absorbed into online learning.

History

The cheap, uniform postal service created by the British Penny Post of 1840 made teaching by mail practical. Caleb Phillips had advertised shorthand lessons by mail in Boston as early as 1728. In England, Isaac Pitman taught shorthand by correspondence from 1840, and in 1858 the University of London began external degrees open to students anywhere. Anna Eliot Ticknor's Society to Encourage Studies at Home (1873) served women in the United States, and the International Correspondence Schools of Scranton, Pennsylvania (founded 1891) enrolled millions of working adults studying technical and vocational subjects.

Development

Correspondence methods were adopted by universities, the military, and governments: Australia's School of the Air (1951) combined correspondence materials with two-way radio for children in the outback. In 1969 the United Kingdom founded the Open University, blending printed correspondence units with broadcast lectures and local tutorials. From the 1990s, email, the web, and later MOOCs (massive open online courses) replaced postal delivery, but print-based correspondence remains in use where internet access is limited and in prisons.

Characteristics

Correspondence courses typically feature self-paced study, modular printed materials written for independent learning, tutor feedback on assignments, and proctored examinations at local centers. Their strengths are flexibility and access for rural, working, or institutionalized learners; weaknesses include delayed feedback, isolation, and historically high dropout rates.
