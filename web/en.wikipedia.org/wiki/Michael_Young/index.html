Michael Young, Baron Young of Dartlington (9 August 1915 – 14 January 2002) was a British sociologist, social reformer, and politician, one of the most prolific social entrepreneurs of the twentieth century. He is best known for his 1958 satirical book The Rise of the Meritocracy, which coined the term meritocracy as a warning rather than a compliment, and for helping to draft the Labour Party's 1945 election manifesto, "Let Us Face the Future".

Life and career

Young was educated at Dartington Hall School in Devon — the progressive school run by his parents' friends Leonard and Dorothy Elmhirst — and studied economics at the London School of Economics, later qualifying as a barrister. During the Second World War he directed Political and Economic Planning (PEP), an influential think tank. From 1943 he headed the Labour Party's research department and was a principal author of the 1945 manifesto that underpinned the post-war welfare state.

In 1952 he co-founded the Institute of Community Studies in Bethnal Green, east London, and with Peter Willmott produced the classic study Family and Kinship in East London (1957), which documented the strength of working-class extended families and criticised housing policies that dispersed them. Their work helped establish British urban sociology.

The Rise of the Meritocracy

The Rise of the Meritocracy 1870–2033 (1958) is written as a retrospective history by a smug sociologist of the future, celebrating a society sorted entirely by IQ plus effort, in which a hereditary cognitive elite rules and the dispossessed eventually revolt; the narrator is killed in the uprising. Young intended the book as a dystopian satire, and he later expressed dismay that "meritocracy" had been adopted as a term of praise by politicians, protesting in a 2001 article that it was "good sense" to be appointed on merit but deeply corrosive for the meritorious to become a self-satisfied caste.

Social entrepreneur

Young founded or helped found an extraordinary range of institutions: the Consumers' Association and its magazine Which? (1957), the Advisory Centre for Education, the National Extension College, the Open University (whose concept he developed in the early 1960s and which Harold Wilson's government launched in 1969), the School for Social Entrepreneurs, and dozens of smaller ventures in health, education, and consumer rights. He was made a life peer in 1978.

Legacy

Young's work links research to action: he believed social science should be used to found institutions, not merely to describe society. His concept of meritocracy remains central to debates about education, inequality, and social mobility.
