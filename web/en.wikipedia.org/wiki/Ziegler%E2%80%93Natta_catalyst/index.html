Ziegler–Natta catalyst

A Ziegler–Natta catalyst, named after Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta, is a catalyst used in the synthesis of polymers of 1-alkenes (alpha-olefins). Two broad classes are recognised: heterogeneous supported catalysts based on titanium compounds combined with organoaluminium co-catalysts such as triethylaluminium, and homogeneous catalysts based on complexes of titanium, zirconium or hafnium, often metallocenes combined with methylaluminoxane.

History

In 1953 Karl Ziegler's group at the Max Planck Institute in Mülheim found that mixtures of titanium tetrachloride and organoaluminium compounds polymerised ethylene at low pressure to give high-density polyethylene. Giulio Natta in Milan extended the work to propylene, producing crystalline, stereoregular (isotactic) polypropylene. Ziegler and Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for these discoveries.

Significance

The catalysts made possible the mass production of high-density polyethylene and isotactic polypropylene — among the world's most produced plastics — as well as synthetic rubbers such as polybutadiene. Later generations of supported catalysts greatly increased activity and stereocontrol, and metallocene single-site catalysts allowed still finer control of polymer microstructure.
