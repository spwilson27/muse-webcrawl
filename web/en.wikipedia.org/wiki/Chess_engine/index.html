In computer chess, a chess engine is a computer program that analyses chess positions and generates a move or list of moves ranked by strength. The engine is typically a back end with a command-line interface, communicating with a graphical user interface (GUI) through a standard protocol such as the Universal Chess Interface (UCI) or the Chess Engine Communication Protocol (WinBoard). This separation lets different engines be combined with different interfaces and lets engines compete directly against one another.

Techniques

Traditional engines search the game tree using minimax with alpha–beta pruning, enhanced by techniques such as transposition tables, iterative deepening, quiescence search, null-move pruning, and late move reductions. Positions at the leaves are scored by an evaluation function weighing material, piece-square tables, pawn structure, king safety, mobility, and other features. Modern top engines have largely adopted efficiently updatable neural networks (NNUE) for evaluation, first popularised in shogi and integrated into Stockfish in 2020.

A second approach, pioneered by AlphaZero (2017) and the open-source Leela Chess Zero, uses deep neural networks trained by self-play reinforcement learning combined with Monte Carlo tree search. AlphaZero's victories over Stockfish demonstrated that learned evaluation plus MCTS could match or exceed classical alpha–beta engines.

Strength

Engines surpassed the best human players in the 2000s; Deep Blue's 1997 victory over Garry Kasparov was a landmark, and by the mid-2000s engines on commodity hardware defeated world-class grandmasters in matches. Today's strongest engines, such as Stockfish and Leela Chess Zero, are rated well above 3500 Elo on engine rating lists — far beyond any human player. Engine competitions include the Top Chess Engine Championship (TCEC) and the Computer Chess Championship (CCC).

Uses

Engines are used by players for preparation and post-game analysis, by correspondence chess players (subject to competition rules), in chess composition, and for solving endgames in conjunction with tablebases, which give perfect play for positions with few pieces.
