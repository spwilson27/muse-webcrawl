Yuri Andropov

Yuri Vladimirovich Andropov (15 June 1914 – 9 February 1984) was a Soviet politician who served as General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union from November 1982 until his death fifteen months later. He was previously the longest-serving chairman of the KGB (1967–1982) and, as ambassador to Hungary, played a central role in the Soviet suppression of the 1956 Hungarian uprising. His brief tenure as Soviet leader combined campaigns against corruption and absenteeism with an intensification of Cold War confrontation.

Early career and Hungary

Andropov rose through the Komsomol and party apparatus in Karelia during and after the Second World War. As ambassador to Hungary from 1954 to 1957 he witnessed and helped direct the response to the Hungarian Revolution of 1956, reporting to Moscow that intervention was essential; Soviet troops crushed the uprising in November 1956.

KGB chairman

Appointed head of the KGB in 1967, Andropov modernised the security service and systematised the suppression of dissent: dissidents such as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn were exiled, others confined to psychiatric hospitals or imprisoned. He also sponsored a generation of reform-minded younger officials, including Mikhail Gorbachev, his eventual successor-but-one.

Leadership

Succeeding Brezhnev in November 1982, Andropov launched an anti-corruption drive and a campaign for workplace discipline, and experimented cautiously with economic measures giving enterprises more autonomy. Abroad, his tenure saw the Soviet shoot-down of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 in September 1983, the NATO Able Archer exercise scare, the continued war in Afghanistan and the collapse of arms-control talks after the deployment of Pershing II missiles. In a celebrated exchange, American schoolgirl Samantha Smith wrote to him about nuclear war fears; his reply invited her to the USSR. Suffering from kidney failure, Andropov was hospitalised from August 1983 and died in February 1984.
