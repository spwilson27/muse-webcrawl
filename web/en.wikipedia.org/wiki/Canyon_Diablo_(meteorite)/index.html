Canyon Diablo (meteorite)

The Canyon Diablo meteorite comprises the many fragments of the asteroid that created Meteor Crater (also known as Barringer Crater) in Arizona, United States. The meteorites are named for Canyon Diablo, a canyon near the crater in Coconino County. The impact occurred roughly 50,000 years ago, when an iron asteroid estimated at about 50 metres (160 ft) across struck the Colorado Plateau at high speed, excavating a crater about 1.2 kilometres (0.75 mi) in diameter.

Composition

Canyon Diablo meteorites are iron meteorites composed chiefly of kamacite (an iron–nickel alloy) with taenite, troilite nodules, schreibersite, cohenite, and graphite. They are classified as coarse octahedrites of the IAB main group. Notably, some fragments contain microscopic diamonds — including the hexagonal form lonsdaleite — formed by the intense shock pressures of the impact. The mineral moissanite (silicon carbide) was first identified in nature in Canyon Diablo material by Henri Moissan in 1893, and the rare minerals krinovite and merrillite were also described from these meteorites.

History

Meteorite fragments around the crater were known to Native American peoples, who used the metal, long before scientific study. In 1891 the fragments came to scientific attention, and mining engineer Daniel M. Barringer later championed the theory that the crater was impact-produced, hoping to mine the buried iron mass; commercial drilling failed to find a large intact body because most of the asteroid vaporised on impact. The largest individual fragment recovered, known as the Holsinger Meteorite, weighs about 639 kg (1,409 lb) and is displayed at the Meteor Crater visitor centre.

Clair Patterson and the age of the Earth

In the 1950s, geochemist Clair Cameron Patterson measured lead isotope ratios in Canyon Diablo troilite — which contains very little uranium and therefore preserves the primordial isotopic composition of lead in the Solar System. Using these measurements, Patterson calculated the age of the Earth as approximately 4.55 billion years, a landmark result first presented in 1953 and published in 1956.
