The Ardeidae are the family of wading birds known as herons, egrets, and bitterns. About 72 species are recognized, distributed nearly worldwide in wetlands and shallow waters. Herons and egrets are long-legged, long-necked predators of fish and other aquatic animals, while bitterns are shorter-necked, secretive birds of reed beds. The distinction between "heron" and "egret" is conventional rather than biological — egrets are simply the species that develop ornamental breeding plumes and historically were hunted for them.

Anatomy and hunting

Ardeids possess a uniquely modified sixth cervical vertebra that lets the neck fold into an S-shape and strike forward with great speed, spearing or seizing prey with a dagger-like bill. Unlike storks and cranes, they fly with the neck drawn back. Specialized powder-down feathers, groomed with a serrated middle claw (pectinate claw), keep plumage free of fish slime. Many species hunt by standing motionless or walking slowly; a few — such as the black heron — canopy their wings to shade the water, and the cattle egret follows grazing animals to snatch flushed insects.

Subfamilies and genera

The family is usually divided into the typical herons and egrets (Ardeinae: Ardea, Egretta, Nycticorax and allies, including the night herons), the bitterns (Botaurinae: Botaurus, Ixobrychus), and the tiger herons (Tigrisomatinae) of the Neotropics. Familiar species include the grey heron, great egret, great blue heron, green heron, and Eurasian bittern.

Conservation

Most ardeids are widespread and adaptable, but several are threatened, including the white-bellied heron, Madagascar pond heron, and the Slaty egret, chiefly through wetland loss. Plume hunting in the late 19th century devastated egret populations and helped inspire the first bird-protection societies, including the Audubon movement.
