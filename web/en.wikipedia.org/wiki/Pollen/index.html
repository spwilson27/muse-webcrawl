Pollen

Pollen is a fine powder produced by the male reproductive structures of seed plants (the anthers of flowers and the male cones of gymnosperms). Each pollen grain contains the male gametophyte — a greatly reduced haploid generation — enclosed in a tough, resistant wall. Pollen is transferred to the female reproductive structures in the process of pollination, enabling fertilisation and seed production.

Pollen grains of several species under the scanning electron microscope.

Structure

A pollen grain consists of a vegetative (tube) cell and a generative cell that divides to form two sperm cells. The wall has two layers: the inner intine, of cellulose and pectin, and the outer exine, made of sporopollenin — one of the most chemically resistant biological materials known. The exine's sculptured patterns of spines, pores, and furrows are characteristic of each species and are used in identification.

Transfer

Wind-pollinated (anemophilous) plants produce vast quantities of small, smooth, lightweight pollen; animal-pollinated (zoophilous) plants produce stickier, ornamented grains carried by insects, birds, bats, and other animals. Self-incompatibility systems in many species prevent pollen from fertilising the plant that produced it.

Human relevance

Airborne pollen is a major cause of allergic rhinitis ("hay fever"), and pollen counts are routinely forecast in weather reports. Because sporopollenin resists decay, fossil pollen preserved in lake sediments and peat is a key record of past vegetation and climate, studied in the field of palynology. Pollen analysis (melissopalynology) is also used to verify the botanical origin of honey.
