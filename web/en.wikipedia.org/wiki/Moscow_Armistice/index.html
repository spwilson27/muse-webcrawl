Moscow Armistice

Moscow Armistice
Signed19 September 1944, Moscow
PartiesFinland; Soviet Union and United Kingdom (on behalf of the Allies)
EffectEnded the Continuation War; Finland broke relations with Germany

The Moscow Armistice was signed between Finland on one side and the Soviet Union and the United Kingdom — acting on behalf of the Allied powers — on the other side on 19 September 1944. It ended the Continuation War between Finland and the USSR, restoring the borders agreed in the Moscow Peace Treaty of 1940 with additional territorial and political concessions by Finland.

Finland ceded Petsamo and leased the Porkkala peninsula to the USSR under the armistice.

Background

Finland had fought the Continuation War alongside Germany since 1941 to recover territories lost in the Winter War. By the summer of 1944, the Soviet Vyborg–Petrozavodsk offensive had driven Finnish forces back, and Finland's president Risto Ryti resigned so that the country could escape his personal commitment to Germany. His successor, C. G. E. Mannerheim, opened negotiations in Moscow.

Terms

Finland was required to restore the 1940 borders and additionally cede Petsamo, giving the USSR a border with Norway and access to the Arctic; to lease the Porkkala peninsula near Helsinki to the USSR for 50 years as a naval base (it was returned early, in 1956); to pay war reparations of US$300 million in goods over six years (later extended to eight); to expel or intern all German troops on Finnish soil — which led to the Lapland War; to demobilise most of its army; and to legalise the previously banned Communist Party and prosecute those deemed responsible for the war, in the war-responsibility trials of 1945–46.

Aftermath

The armistice terms were confirmed in the Paris Peace Treaty of 1947. Compliance with the armistice, supervised by an Allied Control Commission dominated by the Soviet Union, set the framework for Finland's post-war position of neutrality between East and West, sometimes described by critics as "Finlandization".
