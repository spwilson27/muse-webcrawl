Joseph Haydn

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Joseph Haydn in old age (1791) by Thomas Hardy: the composer in a dark coat, his wig powdered, in London triumph]

Franz Joseph Haydn (31 March 1732 – 31 May 1809) was an Austrian composer of the Classical period, the central architect of the classical style. He was a principal developer of the string quartet and the symphony — 'father of the symphony' and 'father of the string quartet' are his conventional titles — and spent thirty years as court musician to the Esterházy princes before conquering London in the 1790s. His younger brother Michael, his friend Mozart and his pupil Beethoven complete his circle.

Haydn's output is numbered by the Hoboken catalogue at over a hundred symphonies (the named ones include the Farewell, No. 45; the Surprise, No. 94; the twelve London symphonies), 68 string quartets (the Sun, Russian, and Emperor quartets, whose slow movement became Austria's and then Germany's anthem), keyboard sonatas, the operas, and the late oratorios The Creation (1798) and The Seasons (1801).

Esterháza and isolation

A choirboy at St Stephen's, Vienna, dismissed when his voice broke, Haydn taught himself from Fux and made a decade of freelance poverty before entering Esterházy service in 1761. At remote Esterháza palace he was, as he said, cut off from the world and forced to become original (in his famous retrospective summary). Three decades of daily music-making with a first-class orchestra and opera house let him refine every genre he touched.

London and last years

Freed by Prince Nikolaus's death in 1790, Haydn accepted the impresario Salomon's invitation to London (1791–92, 1794–95): triumphs, an Oxford doctorate, and the example of Handel's oratorios spurred The Creation. Back in Vienna he taught Beethoven (the lessons famously soured), wrote the Emperor's Hymn (1797), and composed six late masses. He died in 1809 as French troops occupied Vienna — Napoleon, by tradition, posted a guard of honour at his house.
