Subpoena

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A subpoena (from Latin sub poena, "under penalty") is a written order issued by a government agency, most often a court, compelling a witness to testify (subpoena ad testificandum) or to produce evidence (subpoena duces tecum), under penalty of contempt for non-compliance. Subpoenas are the standard mechanism for obtaining testimony and documents from non-parties in civil and criminal proceedings, and a vital investigative tool of grand juries, administrative agencies and legislatures.

Legislative and contested subpoenas

Congressional subpoenas power US oversight — and trigger separation-of-powers litigation when aimed at presidents and their advisers (Trump v. Mazars, 2020, on congressional access to a president's financial records; contempt prosecutions of officials who defy committees, from the McCarthy era to the January 6 committee's referrals). Defences include privilege (executive, attorney–client, journalistic), relevance and burden objections, and the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination for testimony (not for pre-existing documents, in most cases). Enforcement through inherent contempt, civil suit or criminal referral determines subpoenas' practical strength in inter-branch conflict.
