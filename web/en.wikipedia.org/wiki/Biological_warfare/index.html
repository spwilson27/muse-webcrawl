Biological warfare

Biological warfare involves the use of infectious agents as weapons.

Biological warfare, also known as germ warfare, is the use of biological toxins or infectious agents such as bacteria, viruses, insects and fungi with the intent to kill, harm or incapacitate humans, animals or plants as an act of war. Biological weapons (often termed "bio-weapons" or "bio-agents") are living organisms or replicating entities. Entomological (insect) warfare is a subtype of biological warfare.

History

The use of disease as a weapon is ancient: Hittite texts describe driving infected people into enemy territory, and in 1346 Mongol forces besieging Caffa are said to have catapulted plague victims' corpses over the walls. In the First World War, Germany ran a covert programme to infect Allied livestock with anthrax and glanders. During the Second World War, Japan's Unit 731 conducted extensive human experiments and field attacks with plague and cholera in China, while the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union developed their own programmes; Britain tested anthrax on Gruinard Island, which remained contaminated for decades.

After the war, the US and Soviet programmes expanded during the Cold War. In 1969 President Richard Nixon unilaterally ended the US offensive programme. The 1979 Sverdlovsk anthrax leak from a Soviet facility killed at least 68 people and demonstrated that the USSR had continued its programme despite international commitments.

International law

The 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use of biological weapons in war. The Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), opened for signature in 1972 and in force since 1975, comprehensively prohibits the development, production, acquisition, stockpiling and use of biological and toxin weapons. Unlike the Chemical Weapons Convention, the BWC lacks a formal verification organisation, a weakness widely discussed by states parties.

Defence

Biodefence includes surveillance and detection systems, protective equipment, vaccines and antibiotics, and public-health response planning. The 2001 anthrax letter attacks in the United States, which killed five people, spurred major investment in biodefence infrastructure.
