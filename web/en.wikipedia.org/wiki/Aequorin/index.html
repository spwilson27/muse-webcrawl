Aequorin is a calcium-activated photoprotein isolated from the hydrozoan jellyfish Aequorea victoria. On binding calcium ions it emits blue light, a property that has made it a foundational tool for measuring intracellular calcium concentrations in cell biology.

Structure and mechanism

Aequorin consists of an apoprotein (apoaequorin, about 21 kDa) and a prosthetic group, the luciferin coelenterazine, bound together with molecular oxygen as a stable peroxide complex. When Ca²⁺ binds to the protein's three EF-hand sites, the protein undergoes a conformational change and oxidises coelenterazine to coelenteramide and carbon dioxide, releasing a photon of blue light. In the living jellyfish, the energy is transferred to the green fluorescent protein (GFP), shifting the emission to green.

Discovery and uses

Aequorin was isolated in 1961 by Osamu Shimomura at Friday Harbor, Washington, in work that later also yielded GFP and contributed to the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Because its light output depends on calcium concentration and it is consumed in the reaction, microinjected or genetically targeted aequorin became one of the first practical probes of free Ca²⁺ inside living cells, used notably in classic experiments on muscle contraction, fertilisation and neuronal signalling. Modern work typically expresses recombinant apoaequorin targeted to specific organelles, reconstituted with synthetic coelenterazine analogues.
