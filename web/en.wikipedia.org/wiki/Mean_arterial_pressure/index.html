Mean arterial pressure


An arterial pressure waveform over one cardiac cycle

The mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average arterial pressure throughout one cardiac cycle, systole, and diastole. MAP is influenced by cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, each of which is influenced by several variables. It is used clinically as an indicator of perfusion pressure to the body's organs.

Calculation

While MAP can be directly measured only by invasive monitoring, it is commonly estimated from systolic (SP) and diastolic pressure (DP):

MAP ≈ DP + ⅓(SP − DP)

The difference SP − DP is the pulse pressure. The ⅓ factor reflects that diastole normally lasts about twice as long as systole at resting heart rates; at high heart rates the weighting changes. True MAP is the area under the arterial pressure–time curve divided by the cycle duration.

Clinical significance

MAP is considered a better indicator of tissue perfusion than systolic blood pressure because it accounts for the full cardiac cycle. A MAP of at least 60 mmHg is generally considered necessary to maintain adequate perfusion of vital organs, and values of 65 mmHg or above are commonly targeted in the management of septic shock. Persistently low MAP is associated with organ dysfunction, while chronically elevated MAP occurs in hypertension.

Regulation

MAP is regulated by the baroreceptor reflex, the renin–angiotensin system, and local autoregulation in vascular beds such as the brain and kidneys, which maintain relatively constant blood flow across a range of pressures.
