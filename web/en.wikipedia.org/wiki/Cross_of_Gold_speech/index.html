Cross of Gold speech
DateJuly 9, 1896
VenueChicago Coliseum, Democratic National Convention
SpeakerWilliam Jennings Bryan
SubjectBimetallism; free silver; opposition to the gold standard

The Cross of Gold speech was delivered by William Jennings Bryan, a former United States Representative from Nebraska, at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago on July 9, 1896. In the address, Bryan supported bimetallism or "free silver", which he believed would bring the nation prosperity by inflating prices and easing debts, and he denounced the gold standard, concluding with the famous line: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold."

Background

The speech came amid the economic distress of the Panic of 1893 and a fierce national debate over the monetary standard. Farmers and debtors, concentrated in the South and West, blamed the gold standard and the repeal of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act for deflation and hardship, while financial interests in the East defended gold. Bryan, then 36, spoke in the platform debate of the convention.

The speech

Bryan argued that the Democratic Party should champion "the producing masses" against the "money power", framing the contest as one between two ideas of government and between the people and entrenched wealth. He closed by stretching out his arms in the pose of a crucifixion as he delivered the final sentence, holding the pose in silence before the hall erupted.

Impact

The speech electrified the convention; the next day Bryan won the Democratic presidential nomination, becoming the youngest major-party nominee in American history. He lost the 1896 election to William McKinley after a campaign remembered as a watershed in American politics, but the address is consistently ranked among the greatest American political speeches, noted for its religious imagery, rhetorical power and role in shifting the Democratic Party toward economic populism.
