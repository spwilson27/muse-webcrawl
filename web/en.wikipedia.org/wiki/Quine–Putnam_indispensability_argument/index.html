Quine–Putnam indispensability argument

The Quine–Putnam indispensability argument is an argument in the philosophy of mathematics for the existence of abstract mathematical objects such as numbers and sets. It takes its name from the philosophers Willard Van Orman Quine and Hilary Putnam, and is widely regarded as the most influential argument for mathematical platonism in contemporary analytic philosophy.

The argument

A standard formulation runs:

  1. Indispensability: We ought to have ontological commitment to all and only those entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
  2. Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories: physics and other sciences cannot be formulated without quantifying over numbers, functions, and sets.
  3. Therefore, we ought to have ontological commitment to mathematical entities.

The argument rests on two characteristic Quinean doctrines: confirmational holism, by which the mathematics used in a well-confirmed theory shares in the theory's confirmation, and naturalism, the view that philosophy should not stand above science but within it, so that the entities to believe in are those our best science posits. Quine's criterion of ontological commitment—"to be is to be the value of a bound variable"—underlies the claim that science, when regimented in first-order logic, quantifies over mathematical objects.

Reception and objections

Nominalists have resisted the argument in several ways. Hartry Field's Science Without Numbers (1980) attempted to show that mathematics is not, strictly, indispensable, by reformulating Newtonian physics without quantifying over mathematical entities, treating mathematics as a useful but dispensable shortcut. Others dispute confirmational holism: Penelope Maddy argued that ordinary scientific practice does not support mathematical realism, since scientists themselves are indifferent to the existence of the mathematical objects they use. Still others distinguish the indispensability of mathematics to explanation, prompting a related "explanatory indispensability argument" debated by Alan Baker, Mark Colyvan, and others. Fictionalists such as Field and Stephen Yablo accept the argument's premises in part but deny that scientific assertions aim at literal truth about abstracta.
