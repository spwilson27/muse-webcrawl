Bias–variance tradeoff

Expected error as a function of model complexity: bias falls and variance rises.

The bias–variance tradeoff is a central problem in supervised machine learning and statistics: models with low bias tend to have high variance and vice versa, so an optimal model must balance the two. The expected prediction error of a model can be decomposed into three parts: the square of the bias, the variance, and the irreducible noise.

Decomposition

For a target value y = f(x) + ε and a model f̂ trained on random datasets, the expected squared error decomposes as

E[(y − f̂(x))2] = (Bias[f̂(x)])2 + Var[f̂(x)] + σ2.

Interpretation

A simple model (such as linear regression applied to a curved relationship) underfits: it has high bias but its predictions are stable, i.e. low variance. A very flexible model (such as an unpruned decision tree) overfits: low bias on the training data but high variance, because its predictions swing wildly with the training sample. Model selection methods such as cross-validation, regularization, bagging, and ensembling all amount to managing this tradeoff.

Recent perspective

Research on deep neural networks has complicated the classical picture: heavily over-parameterised models can interpolate the training data yet still generalize well, a phenomenon studied under the name "double descent", in which test error falls again after the interpolation threshold.
