Pacta sunt servanda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pacta sunt servanda (Latin for "agreements must be kept") is a brocard and a fundamental principle of law: in contract law it expresses that agreements freely concluded are binding and must be performed in good faith; in international law it is the foundational rule of the law of treaties, codified in Article 26 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties — every treaty in force is binding upon the parties and must be performed by them in good faith.

Scope and limits

The principle's corollaries do as much work as the maxim: a party may not invoke its internal law to justify failure to perform (VCLT Article 27, with the narrow manifest-violation exception at conclusion); agreements are to be interpreted in good faith; and obligations end only as the law of treaties provides (performance, consent, material breach by the other side, impossibility, or the near-impossible rebus sic stantibus door). In private law the same maxim underlies freedom of contract's enforcement and its modern qualifications (good-faith duties, hardship and unconscionability doctrines that let courts adapt or dissolve contracts — functional cousins of rebus). Because there is no world executive, the international version's enforcement depends on reputation, retaliation, and institutions — making pacta sunt servanda at once international law's most recited principle and its most visibly strained.
