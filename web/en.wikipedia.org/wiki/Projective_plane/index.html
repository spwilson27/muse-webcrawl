Projective plane

In mathematics, a projective plane is a geometric structure that extends the concept of a plane in two ways: parallel lines are taken to meet at "points at infinity," and a "line at infinity" is added, so that any two distinct lines intersect in exactly one point and any two distinct points determine exactly one line. Projective planes are the fundamental objects of projective geometry, a subject motivated originally by perspective drawing and formalised in the 19th century.

Definition

An abstract projective plane is a set of points and a set of lines (subsets of points) satisfying three axioms:

  1. Any two distinct points lie on a unique line.
  2. Any two distinct lines intersect in a unique point.
  3. There exist four points, no three of which are collinear (a complete quadrangle).

The symmetry between the first two axioms illustrates duality: interchanging "point" and "line" in any theorem of projective geometry yields another valid theorem.

The real projective plane

The most familiar example is the real projective plane, obtained from the ordinary Euclidean plane by adding a point at infinity for each direction (each family of parallel lines) and collecting those points into a line at infinity. Algebraically, its points are the lines through the origin in three-dimensional space, and it can be described using homogeneous coordinates [x : y : z], not all zero, up to non-zero scalar multiples. Topologically it is a non-orientable surface, realisable as a sphere with antipodal points identified, or as a Möbius strip joined to a disc; it cannot be embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space without self-intersection (models such as the Roman surface and Boy's surface are immersions).

Finite projective planes

A finite projective plane has finitely many points; it is known that if it has n + 1 points on each line, then it has n2 + n + 1 points and the same number of lines, and n is called the order of the plane. The smallest example, of order 2, is the Fano plane with seven points and seven lines. Projective planes of order n exist whenever n is a prime power, constructed from finite fields; whether planes exist for other orders is a famous open problem. Orders 6 and 14 are excluded by the Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem, and order 10 was ruled out by an enormous computer search completed in 1989; order 12 remains open.

Applications

Projective geometry underlies computer vision and computer graphics, where perspective projection is naturally modelled by projective transformations, and finite projective planes appear in combinatorics, coding theory and the design of experiments.
