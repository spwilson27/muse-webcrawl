Gastric emptying

Gastric emptying is the process by which the contents of the stomach are moved into the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine. It is controlled by the stomach and duodenum through a combination of muscular contractions and hormonal and neural feedback, and its rate varies with the volume, consistency, caloric content and composition of a meal.

Physiology

After eating, the proximal stomach relaxes to accommodate food (receptive relaxation), while peristaltic contractions originating near the mid-stomach mix and grind solid food into a semi-liquid mixture called chyme. Small jets of chyme are squirted through the pylorus into the duodenum with each contraction. Liquids empty exponentially, with a half-time of roughly 10 to 30 minutes, whereas digestible solids show a lag phase followed by a roughly linear emptying over two to four hours.

Regulation

Feedback from the duodenum — triggered by acidity, fatty acids, amino acids and hyperosmolarity — slows emptying via the enterogastric reflex and hormones such as cholecystokinin, secretin and gastric inhibitory peptide. The hormone motilin accelerates emptying during fasting, when the migrating motor complex sweeps residual debris from the stomach.

Clinical significance

Abnormally delayed emptying in the absence of obstruction is called gastroparesis and is most often associated with diabetes mellitus; it causes nausea, vomiting, early satiety and bloating. Rapid emptying can occur after gastric surgery and contributes to dumping syndrome. Gastric emptying rate is commonly measured with scintigraphy using a radiolabelled meal, or with breath tests.
