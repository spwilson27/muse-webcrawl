Pick's disease is a rare neurodegenerative disease that causes progressive and irreversible dementia. It is one of the pathological causes of the clinical syndrome of frontotemporal dementia. Classically, the term refers to the specific pathology characterised by Pick bodies — spherical inclusions of the protein tau — and ballooned neurons (Pick cells) in the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain.

Pick bodies: round tau-positive inclusions in neurons of the dentate gyrus

History

The disease is named after the Czech psychiatrist and neuropathologist Arnold Pick, who in 1892 described a patient with severe language disturbance and lobar atrophy. In 1911, Alois Alzheimer described the characteristic spherical argyrophilic inclusions and swollen cells that later came to be called Pick bodies and Pick cells.

Signs and symptoms

Pick's disease typically presents between the ages of 40 and 60, earlier than Alzheimer's disease. Early symptoms are dominated by changes in personality and behaviour — disinhibition, apathy, loss of social awareness, hyperorality and repetitive behaviours — or by progressive language disturbance (progressive aphasia). Memory is relatively spared early on but deteriorates as the disease advances. The course is progressive over roughly 6–10 years, and there is no cure; treatment is supportive.

Pathophysiology

Pick's disease is a tauopathy: abnormal hyperphosphorylated tau protein, predominantly the 3-repeat (3R) tau isoform, aggregates within neurons to form Pick bodies. In contrast to the widespread cortical involvement of Alzheimer's disease, atrophy in Pick's disease is strikingly circumscribed, often with a "knife-edge" appearance of frontal and temporal gyri. A minority of cases are associated with mutations in the MAPT gene on chromosome 17, though most cases are sporadic.
