Science park

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A science park (also called a research park, technology park, technopark or science and technology park) is a purpose-built cluster of offices, laboratories and support facilities intended to promote the development and commercialisation of knowledge-based businesses. Science parks are usually associated with one or more universities or research institutes, and aim to encourage flows of knowledge and technology between academia and industry, to incubate start-up companies, and to attract research and development activity to a region.

Characteristics

According to the International Association of Science Parks and Areas of Innovation (IASP), a science park is an organisation managed by specialised professionals whose main aim is to increase the wealth of its community by promoting the culture of innovation and the competitiveness of its associated businesses and knowledge-based institutions. Common features include:

  • Formal links with a university, college or public research centre;
  • Incubation facilities and services for spin-out and start-up firms, such as shared laboratories, meeting space and business advice;
  • High-quality, often landscaped, low-density environments designed to appeal to mobile knowledge workers;
  • On-site research institutes, corporate R&D centres and technology-transfer offices.

History

The concept emerged in the United States in the 1950s. Stanford Industrial Park (now Stanford Research Park), established in 1951 on Stanford University land, hosted firms such as Varian Associates and Hewlett-Packard and became a nucleus of Silicon Valley. Research Triangle Park in North Carolina (1959) followed, anchored by three universities. In Europe, early examples include Cambridge Science Park, founded by Trinity College in 1970, and Sophia Antipolis in France. The model spread worldwide from the 1980s as governments adopted parks as instruments of regional development and innovation policy; China alone has built well over a hundred national-level science and technology parks, the best known being Zhongguancun in Beijing.

Effectiveness

Research on science parks is mixed. Firms on parks often report benefits from prestige, proximity to universities and networking, and some studies find higher survival and growth rates for on-park firms; others argue that parks frequently function as subsidised property developments, that innovation benefits are hard to attribute to the park itself, and that successful examples depend on a pre-existing research base and entrepreneurial culture. Science parks are nevertheless a near-universal element of regional innovation strategies, and umbrella bodies such as the IASP and the UK Science Park Association coordinate the sector.
