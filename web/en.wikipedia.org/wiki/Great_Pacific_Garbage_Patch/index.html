Great Pacific Garbage Patch

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch, also described as the Pacific trash vortex, is a garbage patch, a gyre of marine debris particles, in the central North Pacific Ocean. It is located roughly from 135°W to 155°W and 35°N to 42°N. The collection of plastic and floating trash originates from countries around the Pacific Rim, including countries in Asia and the Americas.

Formation

The patch is formed by the North Pacific Gyre, a system of rotating ocean currents created by wind patterns and the Coriolis effect. Debris entering the ocean from coastal regions is gradually drawn toward the gyre's relatively stationary center, where it accumulates. Contrary to popular depictions of a solid "island of trash," the patch consists largely of suspended microplastics—tiny fragments of photodegraded plastic, often invisible to the naked eye—distributed through the upper water column over an enormous area.

Composition and research

A 2018 study estimated the patch covered about 1.6 million square kilometres and contained at least 79,000 tonnes of ocean plastic in some 1.8 trillion pieces. Fishing gear, especially abandoned nets, made up a large share of the mass. The debris is primarily polyethylene and polypropylene from consumer products and fishing equipment.

Environmental impact

Marine animals can ingest or become entangled in the debris; microplastics enter food webs when eaten by small organisms. Chemicals from plastics may leach into the water, and floating debris can transport invasive species. Cleanup efforts, such as those by The Ocean Cleanup project, face the difficulty that most of the plastic is tiny and dispersed, and removing it without harming marine life is challenging. Prevention—reducing plastic entering the ocean from rivers and coasts—is widely considered the most effective response.
