Hellenistic culture

Hellenistic culture refers to the civilisation that emerged across the eastern Mediterranean and western Asia after the conquests of Alexander the Great (336–323 BC), blending Greek language, art and institutions with the older traditions of Egypt, Persia and Mesopotamia. The term "Hellenistic" was coined by the historian Johann Gustav Droysen in the 19th century.

Language

The spread of Koine ("common") Greek, based largely on the Attic dialect, gave the Hellenistic world a shared tongue for administration, commerce and literature from Sicily to Bactria. Koine became the language of the Septuagint translation of the Hebrew Bible and, later, of the New Testament.

Art and architecture

Hellenistic art is noted for its dynamism, emotional intensity and interest in realism and individual character. Famous works include the Winged Victory of Samothrace, the Venus de Milo, the Pergamon Altar with its gigantomachy frieze, and the Laocoön group. Cities were laid out on Hippodamian grids with agoras, stoas, gymnasia and theatres, while rulers founded royal libraries, most famously the Library of Alexandria.

Science and learning

The period produced Euclid's Elements, Archimedes' work in geometry and engineering, Eratosthenes' measurement of the Earth's circumference, Aristarchus' heliocentric proposal, and the anatomical researches of Herophilus and Erasistratus. Alexandria's Mouseion was the era's foremost research institution.

Religion and society

Traditional Olympian cults continued alongside ruler cults, mystery religions and syncretic deities such as Serapis, created under Ptolemy I to unite Greek and Egyptian worshippers. Cosmopolitan monarchies rested on Greek and Macedonian settler elites ruling large non-Greek populations, though intermarriage and cultural exchange were widespread.

Legacy

Hellenistic culture profoundly shaped Roman civilisation and provided the cultural medium — language, cities, philosophy — within which early Christianity spread.
