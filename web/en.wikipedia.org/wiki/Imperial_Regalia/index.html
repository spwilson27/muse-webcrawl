Imperial Regalia

The Imperial Regalia, also called the Imperial Insignia (German: Reichskleinodien), are the regalia of the Holy Roman Emperor. The most important parts are the Imperial Crown, the Imperial Orb, the Imperial Sceptre, the Holy Lance, and the Imperial Sword, together with ceremonial garments and reliquaries. For most of their history the regalia were kept in Nuremberg, were displayed publicly, and were carried to coronations at Aachen and later Frankfurt. Since 1800 they have been preserved in the Imperial Treasury of the Hofburg Palace in Vienna.

Components

  • Imperial Crown — an octagonal crown of gold plates joined by hinges, made probably in the late 10th or early 11th century, set with precious stones and enamel plaques and surmounted by an arch and cross.
  • Holy Lance — a winged lancehead containing, by tradition, a nail from the True Cross; among the most venerated objects of the Empire.
  • Imperial Sword — a ceremonial sword, made in the 12th century, with a scabbard decorated with enamel plaques of kings and emperors.
  • Imperial Orb and Sceptre — later medieval insignia of worldly dominion.
  • Coronation garments — including the Imperial Mantle, albs, dalmatic, and gloves, many made in the Norman-Sicilian royal workshops of Palermo in the 12th century.
  • Imperial Cross and reliquaries — containers for relics displayed alongside the insignia.

History

From 1424 the regalia were kept in the Church of the Holy Ghost in Nuremberg, guarded by the city council, and shown annually at the Heiltumsweisung (display of the relics). A replica set was kept at Aachen. During the Revolutionary Wars the treasure was moved for safety, ultimately reaching Vienna in 1800; Nuremberg's claim for its return was never honoured.

In 1938, after the Anschluss, Adolf Hitler ordered the regalia transferred back to Nuremberg. Near the end of the Second World War they were hidden in a bunker beneath Nuremberg Castle; American forces recovered them in 1945–46 amid the hunt dramatised in later accounts, and they were returned to Vienna in 1946, where they remain on public display in the Weltliche Schatzkammer (Secular Treasury).
