Golden Bull of 1356

The Golden Bull of 1356 (German: Goldene Bulle; Latin: Bulla Aurea) was a decree issued by the Imperial Diet at Nuremberg and Metz headed by the Emperor Charles IV that fixed, for a period of more than four hundred years, important aspects of the constitutional structure of the Holy Roman Empire. It takes its name from the golden seal (Latin: bulla aurea) appended to it.

Content

The bull regulated the election of the Holy Roman Emperor by seven prince-electors (Kurfürsten): the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne; the King of Bohemia; the Count Palatine of the Rhine; the Duke of Saxony; and the Margrave of Brandenburg. Elections were to be held at Frankfurt, by majority vote, ending disputed elections and papal claims to approve the result. The bull also confirmed the electors' sovereign rights within their territories, including privileges of mining, coinage, and tolls, and made their lands indivisible and subject to primogeniture.

Significance

The Golden Bull became the Empire's most important constitutional document, remaining in force until the Empire's dissolution in 1806. By entrenching the power of the electors, it shaped the Empire's decentralised character for centuries. Seven original copies survive; the copy held in Frankfurt is inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
