Titian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Flaying of Marsyas (c. 1570s), Titian's late, loose-brushed mythological scene, among his final works]

Tiziano Vecelli or Tiziano Vecellio (c. 1488/90 – 27 August 1576), known in English as Titian, was an Italian Renaissance painter of the Venetian school, recognised by his contemporaries as 'the sun amidst small stars'. Over a career of some seventy years he revolutionised oil painting with his expressive brushwork and colour, painting religious works, mythologies and portraits for popes, emperors and kings.

His masterpieces include the Assumption of the Virgin (1516–18) in the Frari, Venice; Bacchus and Ariadne (1520–23); the portrait of Charles V at Mühlberg (1548); and the late, sombre poesie painted for Philip II of Spain, among them Diana and Actaeon and The Death of Actaeon.

Venetian career

Trained under Giovanni Bellini alongside Giorgione, Titian first won fame with the vast Assumption altarpiece for Santa Maria Gloriosa dei Frari, whose swirling movement and glowing colour transformed Venetian painting. He worked for the courts of Ferrara, Mantua and Urbino, and from the 1530s became the favoured portraitist of the Emperor Charles V, who ennobled him as Count Palatine — legend says the emperor once stooped to pick up Titian's dropped brush.

Late style

In his eighties Titian developed a radically free technique, painting with fingers as much as brushes, leaving canvases such as The Flaying of Marsyas and the unfinished Pietà in a state of flickering, broken paint that anticipated Impressionism. He died of plague in Venice in 1576 and was buried in the Frari, beneath his own Pietà.
