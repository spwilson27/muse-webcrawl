Sonoluminescence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sonoluminescence is the emission of light from imploding bubbles in a liquid when excited by sound. The effect was first discovered in 1934 at the University of Cologne by H. Frenzel and H. Schultes, who observed that photographic film placed in an ultrasonic bath became fogged by tiny bright spots produced by acoustic cavitation bubbles.

Single-bubble sonoluminescence

In 1989, Felipe Gaitan and Lawrence Crum produced stable single-bubble sonoluminescence (SBSL), in which a single bubble trapped in an acoustic standing wave expands and collapses once per cycle, emitting a flash of light with each collapse. The flashes are extremely brief — on the order of tens to hundreds of picoseconds — yet remarkably stable and regular, and the emitted spectrum is continuous, extending into the ultraviolet.

Mechanism

During collapse the bubble's radius shrinks to a small fraction of its maximum size, compressing and heating the gas inside to temperatures estimated at thousands of kelvin — comparable to the surface of the Sun. The light is thought to arise from hot, partially ionised gas through processes such as bremsstrahlung and recombination radiation. Despite decades of study, the precise mechanism remains an active research topic.

Multi-bubble sonoluminescence

Multi-bubble sonoluminescence (MBSL) occurs when many cavitation bubbles collapse in a driven liquid. Its spectra show characteristic emission lines of excited species in the liquid, and it is of interest in sonochemistry.

Biological occurrence

Pistol shrimp (snapping shrimp) produce a cavitation bubble by snapping a specialised claw; its collapse generates a shock wave and a brief flash of light, a phenomenon sometimes called "shrimpoluminescence". The flash serves no known biological function and is a by-product of the stunning mechanism used to incapacitate prey.
