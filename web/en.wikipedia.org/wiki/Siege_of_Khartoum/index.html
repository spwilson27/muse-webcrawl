The Siege of Khartoum (13 March 1884 – 26 January 1885) was a decisive episode of the Mahdist War in Sudan. Forces of the self-proclaimed Mahdi, Muhammad Ahmad, besieged the Egyptian garrison of Khartoum, defended under the direction of Major-General Charles George Gordon, the British officer sent by London nominally to evacuate Egyptian troops and civilians. After a siege of 317 days the city fell to a night assault on 26 January 1885; Gordon was killed, two days before the advance guard of a British relief expedition reached Khartoum.

Background

Egypt had conquered Sudan in the 1820s, and resentment of Egyptian rule fuelled the revolt proclaimed by Muhammad Ahmad in 1881. Mahdist forces destroyed Egyptian armies at El Obeid and at the Battle of Shaykan in November 1883, where the expedition of Hicks Pasha was annihilated. The British government of William Gladstone, anxious to extricate Egypt from Sudan, sent Gordon—who had served as Governor-General of Sudan in the 1870s—to Khartoum in early 1884.

The siege

Gordon arrived on 18 February 1884 and, instead of evacuating, resolved to hold the city and "smash the Mahdi". Mahdist forces closed in from March, cutting the telegraph and eventually the river route. Gordon organised energetic defences: fortified lines at Omdurman across the Nile, armed steamers, mines and wire entanglements, and improvised paper currency to pay the garrison. Sorties and steamer raids delayed the besiegers, but food grew scarce from the autumn; by January 1885 the inhabitants were reduced to eating rats, dogs and gum arabic.

The relief expedition and the fall

Under public pressure, Gladstone authorised a relief expedition under General Garnet Wolseley in August 1884. It advanced slowly up the Nile and across the Bayuda desert, fighting at Abu Klea on 17 January 1885. On the night of 25–26 January, exploiting low Nile water, the Mahdists stormed the weak river defences at dawn. The city fell in hours; Gordon was killed, probably on the steps of the governor's palace, and his head was taken to the Mahdi. The garrison and thousands of inhabitants were killed or enslaved. When Sir Charles Wilson's advance party arrived by steamer on 28 January, it came under fire and withdrew.

Aftermath

The fall of Khartoum caused a political crisis in Britain: Queen Victoria publicly reproached Gladstone, whose government narrowly survived, and "Gordon's revenge" became a popular slogan. The Mahdi died in June 1885 and was succeeded by the Khalifa Abdallahi, whose state endured until Anglo-Egyptian forces under Kitchener destroyed it at the Battle of Omdurman in 1898. Gordon became an archetypal Victorian imperial martyr-hero, commemorated in statues and in the 1966 film Khartoum.
