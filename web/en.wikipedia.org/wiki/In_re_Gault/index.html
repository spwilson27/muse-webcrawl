In re Gault, 387 U.S. 1 (1967), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court holding that juveniles accused of crimes in delinquency proceedings are entitled to many of the same due process rights guaranteed to adults by the Fourteenth Amendment.

Background

In 1964, fifteen-year-old Gerald Gault of Arizona was taken into custody for allegedly making an obscene phone call to a neighbour. Neither Gault nor his parents received formal notice of the charges; the complainant never appeared in court, no transcript was made, and no witnesses were sworn. The juvenile court judge committed Gault to a state industrial school until he turned twenty-one — a potential six-year confinement for an act that, for an adult, carried a small fine or at most two months' imprisonment.

Decision

Writing for an 8–1 majority, Justice Abe Fortas held that juvenile proceedings must satisfy the essentials of due process: timely written notice of the charges, notice of the right to counsel (with appointed counsel for the indigent), the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, and the privilege against self-incrimination. The opinion famously observed that "the condition of being a boy does not justify a kangaroo court." The ruling transformed American juvenile justice, ending the era in which the informality of the juvenile court was assumed to excuse the absence of procedural safeguards.
