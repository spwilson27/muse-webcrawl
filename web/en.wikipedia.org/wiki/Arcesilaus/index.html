Arcesilaus (Greek: Ἀρκεσίλαος; 316/315 – c. 241/240 BC) was a Greek philosopher and the founder of the Middle Academy and of Academic scepticism. A pupil of Crantor and Theophrastus, he became head (scholarch) of the Platonic Academy in Athens around 264 BC and turned it away from dogmatic Platonism toward the Socratic practice of argument and counter-argument.

Philosophy

Arcesilaus wrote nothing; his philosophy is known through later sources, especially Cicero and Sextus Empiricus. His main target was the Stoic doctrine of the "cognitive impression" (phantasia katalêptikê), which the Stoics held to be a guaranteed criterion of truth. Arcesilaus argued that for any true impression there could be an indistinguishable false one, so no impression can guarantee its own truth.

From this he drew the practical conclusions of epoché (suspension of assent) and akatalêpsia (the inapprehensibility of things). Against the Stoic objection that suspension of judgement makes action impossible, he proposed that one can act reasonably by following the eulogon — the "plausible" or reasonable — without assenting to any proposition as true.

Method

Following the example of the Socrates of Plato's early dialogues, Arcesilaus taught dialectically: he would refute whatever thesis his interlocutor proposed, taking positions only for the sake of argument. Later Academics, especially Carneades, developed his scepticism further, and Arcesilaus' arguments strongly influenced the later Pyrrhonist tradition recorded by Sextus.

Life

Born at Pitane in Aeolis, Arcesilaus studied at Athens under the Peripatetic Theophrastus before joining the Academy. He was famed for his wit, eloquence, and independence — anecdotes tell of his quietly helping indigent friends while insisting the help remain anonymous. He died around 241/240 BC and was succeeded as scholarch by Lacydes.
