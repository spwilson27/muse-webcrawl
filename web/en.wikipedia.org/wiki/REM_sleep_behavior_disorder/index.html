Rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder or simply REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is a sleep disorder (a parasomnia) in which people act out their dreams. It involves abnormal behaviour during the sleep phase with rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. The major feature of RBD is loss of muscle atonia (i.e., the loss of paralysis) during otherwise intact REM sleep, resulting in dramatic dream-enacting behaviour, often involving shouting, punching, kicking, or leaping out of bed, which may cause injury to the sleeper or their bed partner.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes occur during REM sleep, typically in the second half of the night. Behaviours are usually consistent with the dream content: a person dreaming of being chased or attacked may shout, flail, or strike out. Upon awakening, the person is generally alert and can recall the dream, which distinguishes RBD from sleepwalking and night terrors, which arise from non-REM sleep and are associated with confusion and amnesia.

Causes

RBD results from dysfunction of the brainstem circuits, particularly in the pons and medulla, that normally suppress muscle activity during REM sleep. The idiopathic form of the disorder is strongly associated with the alpha-synucleinopathies: longitudinal studies show that a large majority of people with isolated RBD eventually develop Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, or multiple system atrophy, often many years later. RBD can also occur in narcolepsy and can be triggered or worsened by certain antidepressants.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires video polysomnography demonstrating REM sleep without atonia together with a history of dream-enacting behaviour, in accordance with the International Classification of Sleep Disorders criteria.

Treatment

Management focuses on safety: removing dangerous objects from the bedroom, padding the floor beside the bed, and sometimes sleeping separately from a partner. Pharmacological treatment commonly uses clonazepam at bedtime or high-dose melatonin, both of which reduce episode frequency in many patients.

History

The disorder was formally described in 1986 by Carlos Schenck, Mark Mahowald and colleagues at the Minnesota Regional Sleep Disorders Center.
