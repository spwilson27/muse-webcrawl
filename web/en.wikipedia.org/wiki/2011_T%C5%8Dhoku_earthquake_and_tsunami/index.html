2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami

Date11 March 2011, 14:46 JST
Magnitude9.0–9.1 Mw
EpicentreOff the Sanriku coast, Miyagi Prefecture
Casualties19,759 dead; 2,553 missing; 6,242 injured
Damage~US$235 billion — costliest natural disaster in history

The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami occurred at 14:46 JST on Friday 11 March 2011. The undersea megathrust earthquake, centred 72 km east of the Oshika Peninsula, had a magnitude of 9.0–9.1 — the most powerful ever recorded in Japan and the fourth most powerful in the world since modern record-keeping began. It triggered powerful tsunami waves reaching heights of up to 40.5 metres that travelled up to 10 km inland, and it caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster.

Earthquake

The quake resulted from thrust faulting on the subduction zone between the Pacific Plate and the Okhotsk Plate. The rupture, some 500 km long and 200 km wide, shifted the seabed by tens of metres; Honshu moved about 2.4 m eastward, and the Earth's axis shifted by an estimated 10–25 cm, shortening the day by about 1.8 microseconds.

Tsunami

Warnings were issued within minutes, but wave heights far exceeded the design assumptions of coastal defences in Miyagi, Iwate, and Fukushima prefectures. Entire towns — Rikuzentakata, Minamisanriku, Ōtsuchi — were largely destroyed. The wave overtopped the seawall at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant, flooding its emergency generators.

Casualties and damage

Japan's National Police Agency confirmed 19,759 deaths, with over 2,500 people still listed as missing. Over 120,000 buildings were destroyed and more than a million damaged. The World Bank estimated the economic cost at US$235 billion, making it the costliest natural disaster in world history.

Fukushima nuclear disaster

Loss of cooling at Fukushima Daiichi led to meltdowns in three reactors, hydrogen explosions, and the release of radioactive contamination, prompting the evacuation of more than 150,000 people. It was classified at Level 7 on the International Nuclear Event Scale, the only accident besides Chernobyl to receive that rating.

Aftermath

Japan shut down its nuclear fleet for review, restructured its nuclear regulation, raised seawalls and revised hazard maps nationwide, and embarked on a decade-long reconstruction of the Tōhoku coast. The disaster is commemorated annually on 11 March.
