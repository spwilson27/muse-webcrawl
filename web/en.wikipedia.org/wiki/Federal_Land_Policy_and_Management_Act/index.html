Federal Land Policy and Management Act

The Federal Land Policy and Management Act (FLPMA) is a United States federal law enacted in 1976 that is the primary statute governing the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) and the public lands it administers — roughly 245 million acres, mostly in the western states. FLPMA ended the era of disposal of the public domain, declaring that the public lands generally be retained in federal ownership, and directed that they be managed for "multiple use" and "sustained yield".

Provisions

The Act requires the BLM to inventory its lands and prepare land-use plans with public involvement; establishes procedures for the sale, exchange and withdrawal of public lands; authorises grazing permits and rights-of-way; and provides for the review of roadless areas for possible wilderness designation. It repealed many earlier homesteading and land-disposal statutes and consolidated the BLM's previously fragmented authorities into a single organic act.

History

FLPMA was passed after years of debate over the future of the public lands, informed by the work of the Public Land Law Review Commission. Its land-retention policy and planning requirements helped provoke the Sagebrush Rebellion, a movement by western interests seeking transfer of federal lands to the states. The Act remains the BLM's governing charter and a central statute in American public land law.
