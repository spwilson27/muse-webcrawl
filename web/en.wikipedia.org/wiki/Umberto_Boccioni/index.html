Umberto Boccioni (19 October 1882 – 17 August 1916) was an influential Italian painter and sculptor, the leading theorist and practitioner of Futurism. His work embodied the movement's celebration of speed, technology, dynamism, and the modern city, and his sculpture Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913) is one of the icons of 20th-century art — it appears on the Italian 20-cent euro coin.

Unique Forms of Continuity in Space (1913), Boccioni's most famous sculpture.

Life and work

Born in Reggio Calabria, Boccioni studied in Rome under the poster designer Giacomo Balla and travelled to Paris and Russia before settling in Milan. In 1910 he drafted, with Balla, Carlo Carrà, Luigi Russolo, and Gino Severini, the Manifesto of Futurist Painters and the Technical Manifesto of Futurist Painting, following Filippo Tommaso Marinetti's founding Futurist manifesto of 1909.

His mature paintings — The City Rises (1910–11), the States of Mind triptych (1911), and Elasticity (1912) — combined Divisionist technique with Cubist fragmentation to render movement, crowds, and industrial energy. In 1912 he published the Technical Manifesto of Futurist Sculpture, calling for sculpture to abandon traditional materials and express "plastic dynamism". Most of his sculptures were destroyed, but Unique Forms, Development of a Bottle in Space, and related works survive in plaster and posthumous bronze casts.

Death

Boccioni volunteered for the Italian army in the First World War. On 16 August 1916, during a cavalry exercise near Verona, he was thrown from his horse and trampled; he died the following day, aged 33.
