Burgess model (concentric zone model)
Proposed byErnest Burgess
Year1925
FieldUrban geography / urban sociology

The Burgess model, or concentric zone model, is one of the earliest theoretical models to explain urban social structures. It was created by sociologist Ernest Watson Burgess in 1925, based on observations of Chicago as part of the Chicago School of sociology. The model describes the city as a series of concentric rings expanding outward from a central business district, each ring characterised by different land uses and social groups.

The zones

Burgess identified five zones: (1) the central business district (CBD), the commercial and civic core; (2) the transitional zone, an area of deteriorating housing, rooming houses, factories and recent immigrants; (3) the zone of independent workers' homes, settled by second-generation immigrants; (4) the zone of better residences, middle-class single-family homes; and (5) the commuters' zone of suburbs and satellite cities. Burgess explained the pattern through ecological processes of invasion and succession, in which groups and land uses displaced one another as the city grew outward.

Criticism

The model has been criticised for being specific to early 20th-century American cities such as Chicago, for neglecting transport corridors (addressed by Homer Hoyt's sector model of 1939), multiple business centres (addressed by the Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei model of 1945), topography, and the effects of planning and gentrification. Despite these limits, it remains a foundational model in urban geography and is still taught as a baseline for analysing urban structure.
