Equant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The equant is a mathematical point used in the Ptolemaic system of astronomy, described by Claudius Ptolemy in the Almagest (2nd century AD). In Ptolemy's model, each planet moves on a small circle (an epicycle) whose center travels along a larger circle (the deferent). The center of the epicycle does not move uniformly as seen from the center of the deferent, nor from the Earth; instead, it moves with uniform angular velocity as seen from the equant — a point displaced from the deferent's center by the same distance, in the opposite direction, as the Earth is displaced. It is this device that let the Ptolemaic model reproduce the observed non-uniform speeds of the planets, in particular their varying apparent motion through the zodiac.

The equant point (●) lies opposite the Earth with respect to the center of the deferent (⊙ marks Earth in period diagrams).

History and controversy

The equant was ingenious but philosophically troubling. Ancient and medieval astronomy inherited from Greek natural philosophy the axiom that celestial motions must be composed of uniform circular motions. Motion uniform about an empty point off the center of the carrying sphere seemed to violate that principle: the deferent sphere would have to rotate at a variable rate about its own center. The equant thus became the focus of centuries of criticism. Islamic astronomers of the Maragha school — notably Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, whose "Tusi couple" generated linear motion from two circular motions, and later Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus — constructed models that matched Ptolemy's predictions as closely as possible while eliminating the equant.

Nicolaus Copernicus shared this distaste: in his Commentariolus and in De revolutionibus (1543), his objections to the equant — which he argued violated uniform circular motion — were a major motivation for reworking planetary theory, even though his own heliocentric models retained epicycles. The problem was finally dissolved by Johannes Kepler, who, in the Astronomia Nova (1609), abandoned uniform circular motion altogether and showed that planets move in ellipses, sweeping out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law). Strikingly, Ptolemy's equant construction approximates Keplerian motion quite well for nearly circular orbits: the equant point plays a role similar to the empty focus of an ellipse, and the model is sometimes described as a remarkably good numerical approximation to elliptical motion.
