Salic law (Latin: Lex Salica) is the ancient Frankish civil and criminal law code promulgated around 500 by Clovis I, king of the Salian Franks. One of the earliest written law codes of post-Roman Europe, it is famous above all for the succession principle later derived from it: the exclusion of women from inheriting the throne, invoked in France and elsewhere for centuries.

The code

The original code of 65 titles is a tariff of composition fines: it famously prices offences in solidi — so much for a stolen pig, so much for a murder, with a free Frank's wergild set at 200 solidi, double for a royal retainer — replacing blood feud with payment. It regulates inheritance of allodial land; its Title 59 (De alodis) provided that "no portion of the inheritance of Salic land shall come to a woman" — a rule about private landholding that later jurists stretched far beyond its original meaning. The code was repeatedly expanded under Merovingian and Carolingian kings, and its court procedure, at the mallus, preserves many Frankish legal terms as glosses.

The succession principle

When the direct Capetian line failed in 1316–1328, French jurists invoked the "Salic law" to bar Edward III of England — a claimant through his mother — and to justify the accession of Philip VI of Valois, a decision bound up with the outbreak of the Hundred Years' War. The doctrine became a "fundamental law" of the French monarchy and spread to other monarchies; Spain's Philip V introduced a version of it, Spain's ambiguous succession law helped ignite the Carlist Wars, and several European royal houses applied semi-Salic rules until modern reforms introduced absolute primogeniture (Sweden 1980, the United Kingdom 2013, and others).
