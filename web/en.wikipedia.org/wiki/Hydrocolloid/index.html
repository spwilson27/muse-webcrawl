Hydrocolloid

A hydrocolloid is a substance that forms a gel in the presence of water. Hydrocolloids are generally long-chain polymers — mostly polysaccharides and some proteins — that disperse in water to thicken, gel, stabilize, or emulsify mixtures. They are widely used in the food industry, medicine, and cosmetics.

Common hydrocolloids

Familiar examples include agar and carrageenan (from seaweed), alginate (from brown algae), pectin (from fruit), gelatin (from animal collagen), gum arabic, guar gum, xanthan gum, and carboxymethyl cellulose. In food products they appear as thickening and gelling agents in jams, desserts, sauces, dairy products, and confectionery.

Medical uses

Hydrocolloid dressings, introduced in the 1980s, are wound dressings containing gel-forming agents such as carboxymethylcellulose in an adhesive matrix. They maintain a moist wound environment, absorb exudate, and are used for pressure ulcers, leg ulcers, and minor burns. Hydrocolloid materials are also used for dental impressions, notably irreversible hydrocolloid (alginate).
