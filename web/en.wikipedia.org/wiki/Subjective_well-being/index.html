Subjective well-being

Subjective well-being (SWB)
ComponentsLife satisfaction; positive affect; low negative affect
Key researchersEd Diener, Daniel Kahneman
FieldPositive psychology, happiness economics

Subjective well-being (SWB) is a scientific term for how people experience and evaluate their lives and specific domains and activities in their lives. It is usually assessed by self-report and comprises three components: frequent positive affect, infrequent negative affect, and cognitive evaluations such as life satisfaction. The concept was developed and popularised by psychologist Ed Diener beginning in the 1980s.

Components

Life satisfaction is a global cognitive judgement of one's life, while the affective components concern the balance of pleasant and unpleasant emotions experienced in daily life. Instruments include the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS), developed by Diener and colleagues in 1985, and the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS).

Determinants

Research finds that SWB is influenced by temperament and genetics (twin studies suggest a substantial heritable set point), income (with diminishing returns, a pattern related to the Easterlin paradox), health, social relationships, employment, and cultural norms about expressing emotion. People tend to adapt to many life changes, though adaptation to unemployment, disability, and widowhood is often incomplete.

Measurement and policy

Large surveys such as the Gallup World Poll and the World Happiness Report use the Cantril ladder, asking respondents to rate their lives on a scale from 0 to 10. Since the 2000s, governments and the OECD have explored using SWB measures alongside GDP as indicators of national progress.

Criticism

Critics note measurement problems, including cultural differences in scale use, the focusing illusion, and the distinction between momentary experienced well-being and remembered, evaluative well-being emphasised by Daniel Kahneman.
