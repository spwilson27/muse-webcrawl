Ruffe

Ruffe
Scientific name:Gymnocephalus cernua
Family:Percidae
Order:Perciformes

The ruffe (Gymnocephalus cernua), also known as the Eurasian ruffe or pope, is a freshwater fish found in temperate regions of Europe and northern Asia. It has been introduced into the Great Lakes of North America, where it is considered an invasive species, reportedly in the ballast water of ships.

Description

The ruffe is a small fish, typically 12–20 cm long, with an olive-brown back, mottled sides and a spiny dorsal fin joined to the soft-rayed portion. It has a highly developed lateral line system with sensitive neuromasts that allow it to detect prey in darkness, giving it a competitive advantage over native perch species.

Distribution and habitat

Native to lakes and slow-flowing rivers of Europe and Siberia, the ruffe was first found in North America in the St. Louis River estuary near Duluth, Minnesota, in the mid-1980s, and has since spread through Lake Superior and into other Great Lakes.

Ecology

Ruffe feed mainly on benthic invertebrates, especially chironomid larvae. They mature early, spawn repeatedly in spring and summer, and have high fecundity, traits that have made them successful invaders. Control efforts in North America focus on preventing further spread via ballast water and bait buckets.
