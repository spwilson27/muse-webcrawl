The Alaska Native languages are the Indigenous languages of Alaska. Around twenty languages survive, belonging to four distinct groupings, and all are classified as endangered, though revitalisation efforts have grown since the late twentieth century.

Language families

The Eskimo–Aleut family includes the Inuit languages (Iñupiaq and its relatives), the Yupik languages — Central Alaskan Yup'ik is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in Alaska — and Unangam Tunuu (Aleut). The Na-Dene group includes the many Athabascan languages of the interior (such as Gwich'in, Dena'ina and Ahtna), as well as Eyak, whose last native speaker died in 2008, and Tlingit. Haida is a language isolate, and Tsimshian belongs to the Tsimshianic family.

Documentation and revitalisation

The Alaska Native Language Center at the University of Alaska Fairbanks, founded in 1972, has been the principal institution for documenting and teaching these languages. In 2014 Alaska recognised its twenty Indigenous languages as official languages of the state alongside English, a largely symbolic measure intended to support preservation. Immersion programmes and master–apprentice schemes aim to produce new generations of speakers.
