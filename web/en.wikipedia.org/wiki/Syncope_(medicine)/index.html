Syncope (medicine)

Syncope
Other namesFainting, blacking out, swooning
SpecialtyCardiology, neurology
SymptomsLoss of consciousness and muscle tone, rapid onset, short duration, spontaneous recovery
CausesVasovagal, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac arrhythmia, structural heart disease

Syncope, commonly known as fainting or passing out, is a loss of consciousness and muscle strength characterised by a fast onset, short duration, and spontaneous recovery. It is caused by a decrease in blood flow to the brain, typically from low blood pressure. Before fainting, there sometimes may be symptoms of lightheadedness, sweating, pale skin, blurred vision, nausea, vomiting, or feeling warm; a person may also feel agitated just before losing consciousness.

Causes

Causes are conventionally grouped into three categories. Reflex (neurally mediated) syncope includes the common vasovagal faint, triggered by pain, fear, the sight of blood, or prolonged standing. Orthostatic syncope results from a drop in blood pressure on standing, due to dehydration, medication, or autonomic failure. Cardiac syncope — caused by arrhythmias such as bradycardia or ventricular tachycardia, or by structural disease such as aortic stenosis — carries the worst prognosis and demands urgent evaluation.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on history, physical examination (including orthostatic blood pressures), and an electrocardiogram; tilt-table testing, echocardiography, and ambulatory rhythm monitoring are used when indicated. Most evaluation aims to distinguish benign reflex syncope from cardiac causes and to identify red flags such as syncope during exertion or while supine, family history of sudden death, or an abnormal ECG. Treatment targets the underlying cause: education and counter-pressure manoeuvres for vasovagal episodes, medication review for orthostatic cases, and pacemakers, catheter ablation, or defibrillators for cardiac causes. Syncope accounts for roughly 1% of emergency department visits.
