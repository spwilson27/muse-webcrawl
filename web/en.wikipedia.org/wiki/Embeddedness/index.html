Embeddedness

Embeddedness is the degree to which economic activity is constrained by non-economic institutions and social relations. The concept originates with Karl Polanyi and was elaborated in economic sociology by Mark Granovetter.

Polanyi

In The Great Transformation (1944) and in "The Economy as Instituted Process" (1957), Karl Polanyi argued that in pre-modern societies the economy is "embedded and enmeshed" in social, religious, and kinship institutions, and that the modern market economy represented an attempt — never fully successful — to "disembed" economic activity from society, treating land, labor, and money as commodities.

Granovetter

Mark Granovetter's influential 1985 paper "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness" argued that economic behavior is embedded in networks of interpersonal relations. Against both "undersocialized" accounts (rational, self-interested actors) and "oversocialized" accounts (actors wholly determined by norms), Granovetter held that concrete ongoing social relations generate trust and discourage malfeasance, shaping transactions between firms and within organizations.

Later developments

Subsequent scholars distinguished relational, structural, cognitive, cultural, and political forms of embeddedness, applying the idea to organizations, markets, migration, and globalization. The concept is central to the "new economic sociology."
