Discriminant of an algebraic number field

In mathematics, the discriminant of an algebraic number field is a fundamental invariant of the field, generalizing the discriminant of a quadratic form or of a polynomial. It is defined as the determinant of the matrix of traces of products of elements of an integral basis of the ring of integers of the field. Equivalently, up to sign, it is the square of the volume of the fundamental parallelepiped of the ring of integers embedded into Euclidean space through the field's embeddings.

Definition

Let K be an algebraic number field of degree n over the rationals, and let ω1, …, ωn be an integral basis of its ring of integers OK. The discriminant ΔK is the determinant of the matrix whose entries are the traces TrK/Q(ωiωj). It is a nonzero rational integer independent of the choice of integral basis, and its sign is (−1)r2, where r2 is the number of pairs of complex embeddings.

Ramification

The discriminant encodes ramification: a prime number p ramifies in K if and only if p divides ΔK. This theorem, due to Dedekind, makes the discriminant a central tool in algebraic number theory. Related quantities include the relative discriminant of an extension of fields and the different ideal. Minkowski's bound shows that every number field other than Q has discriminant of absolute value greater than 1, hence every nontrivial field is ramified somewhere; Hermite's theorem states that there are only finitely many number fields with a given discriminant.

Examples

For a quadratic field Q(√d) with squarefree d, the discriminant is d if d ≡ 1 (mod 4) and 4d otherwise. Cyclotomic fields and cubic fields have explicit discriminant formulas in terms of their defining data.
