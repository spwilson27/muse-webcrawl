Eli Whitney Jr. (December 8, 1765 – January 8, 1825) was an American inventor, widely known for inventing the cotton gin in 1793 and for promoting the concept of interchangeable parts in the manufacture of muskets. The cotton gin revolutionized the economy of the Southern United States — making short-staple cotton profitable, vastly expanding cotton cultivation, and, paradoxically, entrenching and enlarging the institution of slavery that supplied its labour.

The cotton gin

After graduating from Yale in 1792, Whitney travelled to Georgia to work as a tutor and found himself at Mulberry Grove, the plantation of Catharine Greene, widow of General Nathanael Greene. There he devised a machine that used a rotating cylinder of wire teeth and a brush to pull cotton fibres through a grid too fine for the seeds. He received a patent in March 1794 and formed a partnership with Phineas Miller to manufacture gins and process cotton for a fee. Rampant patent infringement and costly litigation consumed most of the profits, though Whitney's gin transformed world textile production: U.S. cotton exports soared, and the demand for enslaved labour in the Deep South rose with them.

Interchangeable parts and arms manufacture

In 1798 Whitney won a federal contract to produce 10,000 muskets for the U.S. government. At his armory in Whitneyville, near New Haven, Connecticut, he pioneered machine tools, division of labour, and the ideal of uniform, interchangeable components — the heart of the emerging "American system of manufactures". Although historians have shown that Whitney's actual parts were not as perfectly interchangeable as his celebrated demonstrations suggested, his promotion of the concept, and his milling-machine innovations, were highly influential.

Whitney died in New Haven in 1825. His son, Eli Whitney Jr., continued the armory and later built the Whitney waterworks for the city.
