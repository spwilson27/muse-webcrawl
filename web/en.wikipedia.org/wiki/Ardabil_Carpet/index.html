Ardabil Carpet

Ardabil Carpet
Date1539–40 (946 AH), Safavid Persia
Size10.51 m × 5.34 m (34 ft 6 in × 17 ft 6 in)
MaterialsWool pile on silk warp and weft; about 26 million knots
LocationVictoria and Albert Museum, London (twin in Los Angeles)

The Ardabil Carpet is one of the oldest dated Islamic carpets in the world and a masterpiece of Persian art. Woven in 1539–40 for the mosque of Shaykh Safi al-Din in Ardabil, in what is now north-western Iran, it is celebrated for its immense size, intricate medallion design, and a woven inscription that includes the name of its maker and the date of its completion.

Design

The carpet measures about 10.5 by 5.3 metres and contains roughly 26 million knots, at a density of about 530 knots per square inch. On a deep indigo field scattered with peonies and lotuses sits a huge central sunburst medallion in yellow, ringed by sixteen pendants; quartered medallions fill the corners. At each end appears a mosque lamp — the two lamps are deliberately woven at different sizes so that, viewed from either end, perspective makes them appear equal, and because a lamp shown "upside down" might seem irreverent. The border carries a Persian inscription in naskh script quoting the poet Hafiz, concluding: "I have no refuge in the world other than thy threshold. There is no protection for my head other than this door. The work of the slave of the threshold, Maqsud of Kashan, in the year 946."

History

The carpet was one of a pair commissioned under Shah Tahmasp I for the Ardabil shrine. Damaged by an earthquake in the nineteenth century, the carpets were sold abroad — reputedly to fund repairs to the mosque — and acquired in the 1890s by the carpet firm Ziegler & Co., which used parts of one to restore the other. The restored carpet was purchased for the Victoria and Albert Museum in London in 1893 after William Morris championed the acquisition, calling it a work of singular perfection. Its less complete twin is now in the Los Angeles County Museum of Art.

Display and influence

Since 2005 the London carpet has been displayed horizontally in a purpose-built glass case in the V&A's Jameel Gallery, lit briefly to limit fading. Its design has been widely reproduced on commercial carpets, and the carpet has become an emblem of Iranian cultural heritage.
