Nouvelle cuisine

Artful presentation is a hallmark of nouvelle cuisine

Nouvelle cuisine (French: [nuvɛl kɥizin]; "new cuisine") is an approach to cooking and food presentation in French cuisine. In contrast to cuisine classique, an older form of haute cuisine, nouvelle cuisine is characterized by lighter, more delicate dishes and an increased emphasis on presentation. It was popularized in the 1960s by the food critic Henri Gault, who invented the phrase, and his colleagues André Gayot and Christian Millau, in their Gault Millau restaurant guide.

Principles

Gault and Millau formulated ten points of the new style, including: refusing excessive complication; shortening cooking times for fish, seafood, game birds and vegetables to preserve natural flavour; using the freshest possible ingredients; abandoning heavy marinades and rich sauces in favour of fresh herbs, lemon juice and quality butter; shortening menus; avoiding fermented and hung meats; drawing on regional dishes; embracing new techniques and equipment (such as the microwave); attending to diners' dietary needs; and encouraging chefs to be inventive.

Chefs and influence

Leading practitioners included Paul Bocuse, Alain Chapel, Michel Guérard (who developed the even lighter cuisine minceur), Jean and Pierre Troisgros, and Roger Vergé, many of them trained in the tradition of Fernand Point. Nouvelle cuisine reshaped fine dining worldwide: small artfully arranged portions, sauces served beneath rather than over food, and menus built on seasonal market produce became the new standard, though critics derided tiny portions and undercooked vegetables, and by the 1980s the term itself fell out of fashion even as its principles were absorbed into modern cooking.
