Molecular symmetry

Molecular symmetry in chemistry describes the symmetry present in molecules and the classification of molecules according to their symmetry. Molecular symmetry is a fundamental concept in chemistry, as it can be used to predict or explain many of a molecule's chemical properties, such as whether or not it has a dipole moment, as well as its allowable spectroscopic transitions, chirality, and reactivity.

Symmetry operations and elements

A symmetry operation is an action that leaves the molecule in a configuration indistinguishable from, and superimposable on, the original. Each operation is associated with a symmetry element: a point, line or plane with respect to which the operation is carried out. The principal operations are:

  • Identity (E) — doing nothing; possessed by all molecules.
  • Rotation (Cn) — rotation by 360°/n about an axis.
  • Reflection (σ) — reflection through a plane (horizontal σh, vertical σv, or dihedral σd).
  • Inversion (i) — projection of every atom through a centre of symmetry to the equal opposite distance.
  • Improper rotation (Sn) — rotation followed by reflection through a plane perpendicular to the axis.

Point groups

The set of all symmetry operations of a molecule forms a mathematical group called its point group, since all elements pass through a single point. Common point groups include C1 (no symmetry except identity), Cs, C2v (for example water, H2O), C3v (ammonia, NH3), D2h, D6h (benzene), Td (methane, CH4), Oh (octahedral complexes such as SF6) and Ih (buckminsterfullerene, C60). Schoenflies notation is used for molecular point groups, while Hermann–Mauguin notation is used for crystals.

Character tables and group theory

Group theory provides the mathematical framework for exploiting symmetry. Each point group has a character table listing its symmetry operations, irreducible representations, and how functions such as atomic orbitals and vibrations transform. Applying representation theory allows chemists to construct symmetry-adapted linear combinations of atomic orbitals, to label molecular orbitals, and to determine selection rules.

Applications

  • Spectroscopy: symmetry determines how many vibrational modes are infrared- or Raman-active; for example, the mutual exclusion rule applies to molecules with a centre of inversion.
  • Polarity: a molecule can have a permanent dipole moment only in the point groups C1, Cs, Cn and Cnv.
  • Chirality: a molecule is chiral if it lacks any improper rotation axis Sn (which includes mirror planes and inversion centres as special cases).
  • Reactivity: orbital symmetry underlies the Woodward–Hoffmann rules governing pericyclic reactions.
