Pumped-storage hydroelectricity

Pumped-storage hydroelectricity (PSH), or pumped hydroelectric energy storage (PHES), is a type of hydroelectric energy storage used by electric power systems for load balancing. The method stores energy in the form of the gravitational potential energy of water pumped from a lower-elevation reservoir to a higher elevation. Low-cost surplus off-peak electric power is used to run the pumps; during periods of high demand, the stored water is released back through turbines to generate electricity.

Overview

A pumped-storage plant consists of two water reservoirs at different elevations connected by tunnels or penstocks, with reversible pump-turbines or separate pumps and turbines in a powerhouse. Round-trip efficiency is typically between 70 and 85 percent. Pumped storage is by far the largest form of grid energy storage in the world, accounting for the overwhelming majority of stored energy capacity, with well over 160 GW installed globally.

History

The first pumped-storage plants were built in Switzerland and Italy in the 1890s and 1900s, and the technology spread with the growth of large power grids. Notable facilities include the Bath County Pumped Storage Station in Virginia, the largest in the world with about 3,000 MW of capacity; Dinorwig Power Station in Wales, known as "Electric Mountain," capable of reaching full output in seconds; and the Fengning plant in China, the world's largest by capacity at about 3,600 MW.

Role in the grid

Pumped storage provides load balancing, frequency regulation, spinning reserve, and black-start capability. Its ability to absorb surplus generation has made it a natural complement to variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar, storing midday solar surpluses for evening demand peaks. New projects increasingly use seawater, abandoned mines, or off-river closed-loop reservoir pairs to reduce environmental impact.
