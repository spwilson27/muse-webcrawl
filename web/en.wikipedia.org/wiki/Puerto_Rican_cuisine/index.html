Puerto Rican cuisine


Mofongo, a signature Puerto Rican dish made from fried green plantains.

Puerto Rican cuisine has its roots in the cooking traditions and practices of Europe (Spain), Africa and the native Taínos. Beginning in the latter part of the 19th century, the cuisine of Puerto Rico has been influenced by the United States in the ingredients used in its preparation. Puerto Rican cuisine has transcended the boundaries of the island, and can be found in several countries outside the archipelago.

History

The native Taíno people relied on a diet of cassava (yuca), maize, peppers, and seafood gathered from coastal waters. Spanish colonists, arriving in the late 15th and 16th centuries, introduced beef, pork, rice, wheat, olive oil, garlic, onions, and spices, as well as cooking techniques from Spain. Enslaved Africans brought okra, taro (yautía), plantains, and the practice of frying foods. The blending of these three traditions—Taíno, Spanish, and African—forms the core of what is called cocina criolla.

Basic ingredients

The foundation of Puerto Rican cooking is sofrito, a puréed mixture of onions, garlic, sweet peppers (ají dulce), culantro (recao), and tomatoes that seasons countless dishes. Adobo and sazón are dry and blended seasonings used on meats. Plantains are central: green plantains are fried twice to make tostones, mashed with garlic and pork cracklings to make mofongo, and ripe plantains (maduros) are fried sweet. Rice and beans (arroz con habichuelas) accompany most meals, and arroz con gandules (rice with pigeon peas) is the national dish, traditionally served at Christmas.

Signature dishes

Other well-known dishes include lechón asado (spit-roasted pork), pasteles (a masa of green banana and root vegetables wrapped in banana leaf and boiled), alcapurrias (fried fritters), bacalaítos (codfish fritters), empanadillas (turnovers), asopao (a gumbo-like rice soup), and sancocho, a hearty stew. Desserts include tembleque (coconut pudding), arroz con dulce, flan, and coquito, a coconut-based eggnog-like drink served during the holidays.

Beverages

Puerto Rico is famous for its rum, and the piña colada—created in San Juan in 1954—was declared the national drink in 1978. Coffee, historically grown in the island's mountain regions, is also a staple.
