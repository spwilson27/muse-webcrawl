Meno

Meno (Greek: Μένων, Menōn) is a dialogue by Plato in which Socrates and the Thessalian aristocrat Meno discuss the nature of virtue (aretē) and whether it can be taught. Written in Plato's transitional or early middle period, the dialogue is famous for introducing the theory of recollection (anamnesis), for the "Meno paradox" about inquiry, and for Socrates' geometry lesson with an uneducated slave boy.

Summary

What is virtue?

Meno opens by asking whether virtue can be taught, is acquired by practice, or comes by nature. Socrates insists they must first know what virtue is. Meno's attempts at definition — a list of virtues for man, woman, child and slave, then "desiring fine things and being able to acquire them" — are tested and rejected, as every list presupposes the single form common to all virtues, and the offered definitions are shown to be circular or too broad.

Meno's paradox

Meno counters with a puzzle: one cannot search for what one already knows (no need) nor for what one does not know (one would not recognise it when found). Socrates replies with the doctrine that the soul is immortal and has learned all things before birth; learning is therefore recollection. To demonstrate, he questions Meno's slave boy — who has never studied geometry — and by questions alone leads him from a confident wrong answer to discovering how to double a square, using the diagonal. The boy, Socrates concludes, recovered true opinions from within himself.

Can virtue be taught?

Returning to the original question, Socrates argues hypothetically: if virtue were knowledge, it would be teachable; but there appear to be no teachers of virtue, and great men such as Themistocles and Pericles failed to teach virtue to their own sons. The dialogue ends in provisional puzzlement: virtue seems to come to those who have it "by divine dispensation", as true opinion rather than knowledge — unless someone could turn true opinion into understanding.

Themes and influence

The Meno bridges Plato's Socratic dialogues and his mature metaphysics: the distinction between knowledge and true opinion, and the theory of recollection anticipating the doctrine of Forms developed in the Phaedo and Republic. Its geometry scene remains a classic text in the philosophy of education and innatism debates.
