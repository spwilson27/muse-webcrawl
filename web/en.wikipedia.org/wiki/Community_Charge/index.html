The Community Charge, commonly known as the poll tax (or community charge), was a system of local taxation introduced by the government of Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher to replace domestic rates as a source of revenue for local government in Great Britain. It was a flat-rate, per-capita tax on every adult resident, set by the local authority, and was introduced in Scotland in 1989 and in England and Wales in 1990.

Rationale and design

The government argued that rates — levied on householders according to property values — were unfair because many users of local services contributed nothing, and that a charge payable by all adults would make local councils accountable to all voters. Under the charge, in principle, a duke and a dustman in the same area paid the same amount, subject to rebates of up to 80 per cent for those on low incomes; students paid 20 per cent, and some groups were exempt.

Opposition and abolition

The tax proved intensely unpopular. Critics attacked its regressiveness; large-scale non-payment campaigns, organised by Anti-Poll Tax Unions, saw millions refuse to pay, and councils struggled with collection. On 31 March 1990, a demonstration in London turned into one of the worst riots in the modern capital, with fighting in Trafalgar Square. The charge contributed significantly to Thatcher's downfall in November 1990. Her successor, John Major, abolished the tax; it was replaced in 1993 by the Council Tax, a property-based tax with a personal element. Northern Ireland never adopted the charge, retaining domestic rates.

Legacy

The Community Charge is frequently cited as one of the most politically damaging policies in modern British history and a classic case study in tax design: a levy whose perceived unfairness undermined its collectability and the government that introduced it.
