Ayyubid dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Citadel of Cairo, founded by Saladin, with the later mosque of Muhammad Ali]

The Ayyubid dynasty (1171–1260, in its various branches) was a Sunni Muslim dynasty of Kurdish origin founded by Saladin (Salah ad-Din al-Ayyubi, named for his father Ayyub), which overthrew the Fatimid Caliphate in Egypt and at its height ruled Egypt, Syria, the Hejaz, Yemen and Upper Mesopotamia. Conceived as a family confederation of appanages under a supreme sultan, the Ayyubid state fought the Crusader states to a standstill — Hattin (1187) and the recovery of Jerusalem, then the long duel of truces and campaigns with the Third, Fifth and Seventh Crusades — while its courts in Cairo and Damascus patronised madrasas, hospitals and architecture in stone. It was supplanted in Egypt in 1250 by its own mamluk slave-soldiers, though Ayyubid princes ruled Syrian enclaves for decades after (and in Hisn Kayfa until 1524).

Saladin's federation

Saladin's achievement was held together by his person: Egypt's revenues, Syria's cities and his family's ambitions balanced by his prestige. After his death (1193) his brother al-Adil reunified the realm by diplomacy and force, and al-Kamil of Egypt negotiated with Frederick II the Treaty of Jaffa (1229) that handed Jerusalem to the Crusaders by agreement for ten years — reviled by contemporaries, vindicated by its bloodlessness. The dynasty's later sultans leaned on mamluk regiments (the Bahriyya) bought as boys from the Kipchak steppe.

The mamluk succession

When Louis IX of France invaded Egypt (1249–1250, the Seventh Crusade), the dying sultan al-Salih's widow Shajar al-Durr and the Bahri mamluks destroyed the crusader army at Mansurah and captured the king — then murdered al-Salih's heir Turanshah and took power themselves (1250). Ayyubid branches persisted in Damascus (until the Mongols, 1260), Aleppo, Homs (until 1262) and Yemen; the name endured longest in the Anatolian principality of Hisn Kayfa. Cairo's Ayyubid citadel, madrasas and the dynasty's chancery style passed intact into the Mamluk sultanate that inherited its wars — against Mongols and Crusaders alike.
