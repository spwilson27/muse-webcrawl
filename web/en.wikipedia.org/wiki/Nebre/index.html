Nebre, also known as nebbe, is a common name applied in parts of the Caribbean and northern South America to wetland trees of the legume family, most often associated with Pterocarpus officinalis, a swamp-forest tree sometimes called the "dragonsblood tree" for the red sap that exudes from its cut bark. Local usage of the name varies, and in some areas it is applied to related swamp trees of the genus Pterocarpus and to mangrove associates with similar habitats.

Description

Trees called nebre are evergreen, medium to tall trees of flooded forests, recognizable by their broad buttressed trunks and spreading root systems adapted to waterlogged, anaerobic soils. The bark, when cut, yields a red resinous latex resembling blood, a feature shared with the related dragon's blood trees (Pterocarpus species such as P. draco relatives). The pinnate leaves and papilionaceous flowers are typical of the pea family, and the fruit is a flattened, winged indehiscent pod suited to dispersal by water.

Distribution and habitat

The swamp forests dominated by these trees occur along the Atlantic coasts of the Americas, from Central America and the Caribbean islands through the Guianas and Venezuela to northern Brazil. They occupy brackish and freshwater swamps behind mangrove belts, river floodplains and coastal lagoons, often forming nearly pure stands known locally as pterocarpus swamps.

Uses

The timber has been used locally for construction, posts and fuel, and the red sap was traditionally used as a dye, a varnish ingredient and in folk medicine as an astringent. Because swamp forests protect coastlines against erosion and storm surge and provide habitat for fish and birds, stands of nebre are of conservation interest, though many have been drained or cleared for agriculture and development.
