Hacksilver

Hacksilver (also spelled hack silver, or hacksilber in German contexts) is a term used to describe silver objects that were cut or broken into fragments in antiquity and the early Middle Ages, so that the metal could be used as a form of bullion-based currency. Fragments of jewellery, tableware, coins and ingots were chopped up and exchanged by weight rather than by face value, and balances with weights were used to measure out payments.

History

Hacksilver was widely used in the ancient Near East from at least the Bronze Age. Hoards of cut silver have been found in the Levant dating to around 2000 BC, and the practice is thought to have been an important medium of exchange before and alongside the earliest coinage. In the Hebrew Bible, silver is frequently described as being weighed out for payments, consistent with a hacksilver economy.

The practice persisted in northern Europe into the Viking Age (roughly the 8th to 11th centuries AD). Viking hoards across Scandinavia, the British Isles, Ireland and the Baltic region commonly contain a mixture of complete coins, ingots, arm-rings and hacksilver fragments, suggesting an economy in which silver circulated by weight, with dirhams from the Islamic world forming a large part of the supply in the earlier period.

Archaeology

Notable hacksilver-bearing finds include the Cuerdale Hoard in Lancashire (deposited around 905–910 AD), one of the largest Viking silver hoards known, and numerous hoards from Gotland. Cut marks, bending ("pecking") test marks and nicks on fragments are taken as evidence that the purity and value of pieces were regularly tested in transactions.
