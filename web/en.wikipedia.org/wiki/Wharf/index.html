Wharf

A wharf is a level platform, usually of stone, concrete or timber, built along the edge of a harbour or river bank so that ships can lie alongside to load and unload cargo and passengers. Unlike a pier, which projects out into the water, a wharf generally runs parallel to the shore; unlike a quay, the terms overlap greatly, though "wharf" often implies the whole structure including sheds and equipment, and is the usual term in North America.

Features

Wharves are typically equipped with cranes, warehouses, bollards, fenders and rail or road access. The raised area behind the berth is called the apron. Famous examples include London's historic wharves along the Thames (many now converted to housing and offices — "wharf" survives in district names such as Canary Wharf), and Fisherman's Wharf in San Francisco, now a major tourist district.

Etymology

The word derives from Old English hwearf, related to a verb meaning "to turn", probably referring to the bank or shore around which a river turns; the backronym "ware house at river front" is a folk etymology.
