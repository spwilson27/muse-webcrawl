Martial law in the United States

Martial law in the United States refers to times in United States history when a region or the whole country has been placed under military control. On a federal level, only the president has the power to impose martial law; in each state, the governor has the right to impose it within state boundaries. The concept has never been precisely defined in the US Constitution, and its scope has been shaped by statute and case law, notably the Supreme Court's decisions in Ex parte Milligan (1866) and Duncan v. Kahanamoku (1946), which limited the use of military trials where civilian courts are functioning.

Historical instances

Significant episodes include Andrew Jackson's declaration of martial law in New Orleans during the War of 1812, which he maintained after the battle until official word of peace arrived — for which he was fined for contempt by a federal judge. During the American Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln suspended habeas corpus and authorised martial law in areas of rebellion, a policy contested in Ex parte Merryman and reviewed after the war in Milligan, which held that civilians could not be tried by military commissions where the courts remained open.

Martial law was imposed repeatedly during Reconstruction, in labour conflicts such as the Pullman Strike era state declarations in Colorado (1903–04), and in San Francisco following the 1906 earthquake (in practice, if never formally declared). The Territory of Hawaii was placed under full martial law from December 1941 to October 1944 after the attack on Pearl Harbor, with military government, censorship and mass trials; the Supreme Court struck down the resulting military trials of civilians in Duncan v. Kahanamoku.

Modern framework

The Posse Comitatus Act of 1878 generally prohibits use of the federal Army and Air Force as domestic law enforcement, while the Insurrection Act provides exceptions allowing the president to deploy troops domestically. State governors routinely declare emergencies and use the National Guard, but formal declarations of martial law are now rare; the last widely recognised instances occurred during the civil rights era, such as in Cambridge, Maryland (1963), and during the 1992 Los Angeles riots the federal response proceeded under the Insurrection Act rather than martial law.
