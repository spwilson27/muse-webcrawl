Infant industry argument
Economic rationale for temporary protectionism
Associated with: Alexander Hamilton, Friedrich List

The infant industry argument holds that new domestic industries need temporary protection from established foreign competition until they mature, achieve economies of scale, and can compete on their own. It is one of the oldest arguments for tariffs and industrial policy.

History

The argument was articulated by Alexander Hamilton in his 1791 Report on Manufactures and developed by Friedrich List in the nineteenth century. John Stuart Mill accepted it as a limited exception to free trade, provided protection is temporary. Historically, the United States, Germany, Japan, and later South Korea and other East Asian economies sheltered industries during industrialisation.

Criticism

Critics argue that governments pick winners poorly, that "temporary" protection tends to become permanent as firms lobby to keep it, and that consumers pay higher prices in the meantime. Supporters reply that learning-by-doing and capital-market imperfections can make unprotected development impossible, pointing to successful East Asian industrial policy.
