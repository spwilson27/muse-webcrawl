The abyssal zone or abyssopelagic zone is a layer of the pelagic zone of the ocean. "Abyss" derives from the Greek word ἄβυσσος (abyssos), meaning "bottomless". At depths of 4,000 to 6,000 metres (13,000 to 20,000 ft), this zone remains in perpetual darkness. It covers 83% of the total area of the ocean and 60% of Earth's surface. The abyssal zone has temperatures around 2 to 3 °C (36 to 37 °F) and contains huge pressures of up to 76 megapascals (11,000 psi).

The zone below the abyssal zone is the sparsely inhabited hadal zone, found in the deep oceanic trenches. The abyssal zone lies above the hadal zone and below the more populous bathyal zone. The seafloor in this region, the abyssal plain, lies between 3,000 and 6,000 metres deep and covers over 50% of the Earth's surface, making it one of the least explored ecosystems on the planet.

Conditions

No sunlight penetrates to the abyssal zone, so photosynthesis is impossible and there are no plants; all primary production comes from chemosynthetic bacteria at hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, and from organic matter sinking from above. The water is extremely cold, highly pressurised, and has a high dissolved oxygen content because the water originated in polar regions. Salinity is nearly constant at about 35 parts per thousand. Currents are very slow, and the environment is remarkably stable — conditions change little over long timescales except for the slow rain of organic debris known as marine snow.

Life

Despite the crushing pressure, near-freezing temperatures and scarcity of food, the abyss is inhabited by a surprising diversity of organisms, many of them endemic to the deep sea. Animals are typically small, slow-growing and long-lived, with slow metabolisms adapted to food scarcity; many exhibit gigantism (as in giant isopods and amphipods) or, conversely, miniaturisation. Characteristic abyssal fauna include tripod fish, cusk-eels, rattails (grenadiers), sea cucumbers, brittle stars, giant isopods, xenophyophores and a wide variety of invertebrates that graze on marine snow and on the carcasses of large animals that sink from surface waters ("whale falls"). Bioluminescence is common among the fishes and invertebrates of the deep ocean, although it is less universal than in the mesopelagic zone above.

Exploration and human impact

The abyss was long thought lifeless until nineteenth-century cable-laying and the Challenger expedition (1872–1876) revealed abundant deep-sea fauna. Today the zone is studied with deep-towed cameras, autonomous underwater vehicles and remotely operated vehicles. Human interest in the abyss has grown because of the polymetallic (manganese) nodules scattered over abyssal plains, which contain nickel, cobalt, copper and manganese and are targets for proposed deep-sea mining — a prospect that raises concern among scientists about damage to poorly known ecosystems that may recover only over geological timescales.
