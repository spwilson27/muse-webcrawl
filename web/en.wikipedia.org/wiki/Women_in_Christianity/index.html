Women in Christianity

Women in Christianity concerns the roles and prominence of women within Christian traditions, historically and in the present day. Women have played significant roles in the life of the church as disciples, patrons, prophets, deaconesses, nuns, mystics, theologians and missionaries, though formal leadership and priestly ordination have been restricted to men in many traditions.

New Testament and early church

The Gospels depict women among Jesus's followers, including Mary Magdalene, described in John's Gospel as the first witness of the Resurrection. Paul's letters name women such as Phoebe, called a deacon, Priscilla, a teacher, and Junia, noted among the apostles. In the early centuries, orders of widows and deaconesses carried out charitable and liturgical functions, while female ascetics and martyrs were widely venerated.

Medieval and modern periods

Medieval abbesses such as Hilda of Whitby and Hildegard of Bingen wielded intellectual and administrative authority, and mystics including Julian of Norwich and Teresa of Ávila became influential theologians; Catherine of Siena and Teresa were later declared Doctors of the Church. From the nineteenth century, many Protestant denominations began ordaining women; the Church of England ordained its first women priests in 1994 and consecrated its first woman bishop in 2015, while the Roman Catholic Church and the Orthodox churches continue to reserve priestly ordination to men. Debate over texts such as 1 Timothy 2 continues between complementarian and egalitarian positions.
