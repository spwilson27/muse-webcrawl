Self-heating food packaging

Self-heating food packaging is active packaging that can heat foodstuffs without external heat sources or power, usually by an exothermic chemical reaction. Such packaging is standard in military field rations (such as the flameless ration heater in the United States MRE) and is also marketed for camping, emergency supplies, and convenience products such as self-heating cans of coffee or soup.

Technology

The most common chemistry uses the corrosion of a magnesium–iron alloy by salt water, producing magnesium hydroxide and hydrogen gas along with heat. Another system relies on the reaction of calcium oxide (quicklime) with water, producing calcium hydroxide. In a typical design, the user adds water or pulls a tab that mixes the reactants in a compartment surrounding the food tray; steam and heat then raise the food temperature by roughly 30–40 °C (about 100 °F) within 10–15 minutes.

History

Self-heating tins were patented in the late 19th century and were supplied to polar expeditions and armies in the Second World War. The modern flameless ration heater was developed by the U.S. Army Natick Soldier Research, Development and Engineering Center and introduced with the Meal, Ready-to-Eat in the early 1990s.

Safety and regulation

Because the magnesium reaction generates hydrogen gas, heaters carry warnings against use in confined spaces and are restricted on aircraft. Disposal and recyclability of the chemical compartment remain environmental concerns.
