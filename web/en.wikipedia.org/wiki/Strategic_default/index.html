Strategic default

A strategic default (or strategic foreclosure) is the decision by a borrower to stop paying a mortgage — even though they could afford the payments — because the value of the property has fallen below the amount owed on the loan (the borrower is "underwater" or in negative equity). It is a calculated financial decision rather than a default forced by hardship, and it attracted widespread attention during the United States housing crisis that began in 2007.

Causes and scale

Economists model the decision as the exercise of a put option: in U.S. states with non-recourse mortgage law, a borrower can effectively sell the house to the lender for the outstanding loan balance by defaulting. Studies of the foreclosure crisis estimated that a substantial minority of defaults — figures around a quarter were widely cited in research by economists at the Federal Reserve and universities — were strategic, and that borrowers became much more likely to walk away as negative equity deepened, particularly beyond about 10–15% of the property value.

Consequences and debate

Strategic default damages the borrower's credit score for years, may expose them to deficiency judgments in recourse states, and can make future borrowing and renting harder. Commentators debated its morality: financial advisers argued it could be rational wealth protection, while others (and lenders) characterised it as a breach of obligation that worsens neighbourhood decline and lender losses. In 2009–2010 the topic entered popular culture when homeowners publicly defended walking away, and financial institutions warned of stigma erosion — surveys suggested social disapproval was an important brake, and that defaults clustered when borrowers saw others defaulting.
