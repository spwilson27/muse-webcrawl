Jesuit missions among the Guaraní

The Jesuit missions among the Guaraní were a type of settlement for the Guaraní people ("Indians" or "Indios") in an area straddling the borders of present-day Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil, established by the Society of Jesus in the early 17th century during the Spanish colonial period. The missions, known as reducciones (reductions), gathered indigenous people into organized towns where they were Christianized and taught European trades, agriculture, music, and crafts.

History

The first Guaraní reductions were founded around 1609–1610 in the Paraná region, following the Spanish crown's authorization of Jesuit missionary activity in the province of Paraguay. In these towns, which at their height in the early 18th century numbered about thirty and held a population of roughly 140,000, the Guaraní lived under a system combining indigenous self-governance—each reduction had its own elected cabildo—with Jesuit supervision of religious and economic life.

The missions became famous in Europe for their communal organization, their orchestras and choirs, their printing presses (which produced books in Guaraní), and their prosperous workshops producing yerba mate, textiles, and carved stone churches of remarkable sophistication. The Jesuits also organized Guaraní militias, which defended the reductions against raids by Portuguese slave traders (the bandeirantes) from São Paulo; the victory of the Guaraní militia at the Battle of Mbororé in 1641 secured royal permission for the missions to bear arms.

Decline and expulsion

The Treaty of Madrid (1750) transferred seven missions east of the Uruguay River to Portuguese control, provoking the Guaraní War (1754–1756), in which the missions resisted relocation. In 1767, King Charles III of Spain ordered the expulsion of the Jesuits from all Spanish territories. The missions were handed over to civil and other religious administrators and declined rapidly; their populations dispersed, and the towns fell into ruin. The stone remains of missions such as San Ignacio Miní, La Santísima Trinidad de Paraná, and São Miguel das Missões are now UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The missions' story was popularized by the 1986 film The Mission.
