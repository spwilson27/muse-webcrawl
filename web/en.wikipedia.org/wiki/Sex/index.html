Sex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sex is the trait that determines whether a sexually reproducing organism produces male or female gametes. In anisogamous species, the sex that produces the large, non-motile gamete (ovum) is called female, and the sex that produces the small, motile gamete (sperm) is called male. An organism that produces both types of gamete is a hermaphrodite. Sexual reproduction, in which genetic material from two individuals combines to produce offspring, contrasts with asexual reproduction, in which offspring arise from a single parent.

Sex determination

The mechanism by which sex is determined varies widely among species. In mammals, sex is typically determined genetically by the XY sex-determination system: females usually have two X chromosomes and males one X and one Y chromosome. Birds use the ZW system, in which males are ZZ and females ZW. In many reptiles, the sex of offspring is determined by the temperature at which the eggs are incubated. Other systems include haplodiploidy in bees and ants, and environmental or social influences in some fish, in which individuals may change sex during their lifetime.

Sexual dimorphism

Males and females of a species often differ in physical characteristics beyond their reproductive organs, a phenomenon known as sexual dimorphism. Such differences may involve size, colouration, ornamentation and behaviour, and are commonly attributed to sexual selection. Sex differences in humans include chromosomal, hormonal and anatomical traits, and develop through the process of sexual differentiation before and after birth.

Evolution

Sexual reproduction is thought to have evolved in the last universal common ancestor of eukaryotes. Its persistence is an enduring puzzle in evolutionary biology, since asexual reproduction can be more efficient; proposed advantages of sex include the generation of genetic diversity, the purging of harmful mutations, and faster adaptation to parasites and changing environments (the Red Queen hypothesis).
