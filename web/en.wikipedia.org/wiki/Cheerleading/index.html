Cheerleading

A competitive cheerleading team performing a pyramid stunt

Cheerleading is an activity in which participants ("cheerleaders") cheer for their team as a form of encouragement and perform routines incorporating jumps, tumbling, dance and stunts. It originated in the United States as sideline support for school and college sports teams, and competitive cheerleading has since developed into a distinct athletic discipline practised worldwide.

History

Organised cheerleading is usually dated to 1898, when University of Minnesota student Johnny Campbell directed a crowd in a chant at a football game, becoming the first recognised cheerleader. Early cheerleading was male; women joined in large numbers from the 1920s and especially during the Second World War, and the activity became predominantly female. Lawrence Herkimer founded the National Cheerleaders Association in 1948 and popularised innovations such as the pom-pom and the "herkie" jump. From the 1980s, "All Star" club teams and televised competitions emphasised athletic routines rather than sideline cheering.

Elements

  • Stunts — bases lift or toss a flyer into the air, building single- or multi-level pyramids.
  • Tumbling — gymnastic passes such as round-offs, back handsprings and full twists.
  • Jumps, dance and motions — synchronised choreography judged on sharpness and difficulty.
  • Cheers and chants — crowd-leading segments, especially in school and college ("sideline") cheer.

Competition and governance

Competitive routines are typically two to two-and-a-half minutes long and are scored on difficulty, execution and choreography. Major events include the Cheerleading Worlds for club teams and national championships for school and university squads. The International Cheer Union received provisional recognition from the International Olympic Committee in 2016 and full recognition in 2021, a milestone in the campaign for cheerleading to be treated as a sport — a designation long contested in US Title IX litigation.

Safety

Stunting makes cheerleading one of the more hazardous school activities: falls from pyramids and baskets can cause catastrophic injuries, and studies have found cheerleading accounts for a large share of catastrophic injuries among US high school and college female athletes. Governing bodies have responded with rules limiting stunt heights and surfaces, certification for coaches, and restrictions on hard-surface performance.
