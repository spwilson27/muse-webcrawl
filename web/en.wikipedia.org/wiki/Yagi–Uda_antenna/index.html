Yagi–Uda antenna

A Yagi–Uda antenna, commonly known as a Yagi antenna, is a directional radio antenna consisting of a driven element (typically a folded dipole or simple dipole) plus one or more parasitic elements: a slightly longer reflector behind it and one or more slightly shorter directors in front of it. Coupling between the elements through mutual inductance focuses the radiation pattern into a beam in the direction of the directors, giving high gain and narrow beamwidth relative to its simplicity — the familiar rooftop television aerial, and widely used for VHF/UHF amateur radio, FM reception, radar, and radio astronomy.

History

The antenna was invented in 1926 by Shintaro Uda of Tohoku Imperial University, Japan, with a lesser role played by his colleague Hidetsugu Yagi; English-language literature credits both, commonly abbreviated to "Yagi". Its use spread worldwide during World War II, notably in airborne radar on Allied aircraft — ironically, Japan was slow to adopt the Japanese invention while Allied engineers independently rediscovered its design. Mass-produced rooftop versions became ubiquitous for terrestrial television reception.

Principle of operation

Only the driven element is fed. The reflector, cut about 5% longer than half a wavelength, re-radiates in phase to reinforce the forward signal, while directors, about 5% shorter, act as waveguides that increase directivity. Typical spacing is 0.1–0.25 wavelength between elements; adding directors raises gain and sharpens the beam at the cost of narrower bandwidth. Variants include the log-periodic dipole array (which uses all elements driven with graduated lengths) and loaded or shortened designs for HF bands.
