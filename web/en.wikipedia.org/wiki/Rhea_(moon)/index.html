Rhea (moon)

Rhea (/ˈriːə/) is the second-largest moon of Saturn and the ninth-largest moon in the Solar System, with a surface area that is comparable to the area of Australia. It is the second-smallest body in the Solar System for which precise measurements have confirmed a shape consistent with hydrostatic equilibrium, after the dwarf planet Ceres. Rhea was discovered in 1672 by Giovanni Domenico Cassini and named after the Titan Rhea of Greek mythology — see also Rhea (mythology).

Physical characteristics

Rhea is an icy body with a density of about 1.236 g/cm³, which indicates that it is composed of roughly three-quarters water ice and one-quarter rock. Unlike the larger moon Titan, Rhea has no appreciable atmosphere, though the Cassini spacecraft detected an extremely tenuous exosphere of oxygen and carbon dioxide in 2010 — the first oxygen exosphere found around an icy moon. Rhea's surface is heavily cratered and marked by bright, wispy streaks — cliffs of exposed ice formed by tectonic fractures — first seen by Voyager and later imaged in detail by Cassini.

Early Cassini gravity data suggested Rhea might have a relatively undifferentiated interior; later analyses have been debated. In 2008, a claim that Rhea possessed a faint ring system — which would have made it the first moon known to have rings — was widely reported, but subsequent imaging searches found no evidence of rings, and the claim is generally considered refuted.

Exploration

Rhea was imaged at a distance by Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 in 1980–81. The Cassini orbiter made several close flybys between 2005 and 2013, including a pass within about 70 km of the surface in March 2010 and a final close flyby in February 2013, returning detailed images and composition maps.
