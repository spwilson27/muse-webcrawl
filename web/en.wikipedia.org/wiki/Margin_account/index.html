Margin account

Illustration: Buying on margin (placeholder image)

A margin account is a brokerage account in which the broker lends the customer cash to purchase securities, using the account's holdings as collateral. The practice of buying securities with borrowed money is called buying on margin. It allows investors to leverage their capital — magnifying both gains and losses — and is essential for strategies such as short selling.

How it works

In the United States, the Federal Reserve's Regulation T sets the initial margin requirement, generally 50 percent: an investor may initially borrow up to half the purchase price of a stock. Exchanges and brokers impose maintenance margin requirements (commonly 25 percent or more of the position's value must be held as equity). The investor pays interest on the borrowed funds, and the broker may lend out securities held in the account (hypothecation).

Margin calls

If the value of the securities in the account falls so that the customer's equity drops below the maintenance requirement, the broker issues a margin call, requiring the customer to deposit additional cash or securities. If the customer fails to do so, the broker may liquidate positions without notice to restore the required margin. Forced liquidations during market crashes can accelerate declines, as occurred in the margin-fuelled crash of 1929, which prompted modern margin regulation.

Risks

Because losses are leveraged, investors can lose more than their initial deposit in a margin account — a risk that distinguishes it from a cash account, in which securities must be paid for in full. Margin borrowing is generally unsuitable for retirement accounts, and regulators and brokers require customers to sign margin agreements acknowledging the risks.
