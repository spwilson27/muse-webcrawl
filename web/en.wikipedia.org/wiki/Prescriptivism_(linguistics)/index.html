Prescriptivism (linguistics)

Linguistic prescriptivism, or prescriptive grammar, is the establishment of rules defining preferred usage of language. These rules may address such topics as spelling, pronunciation, vocabulary, morphology, syntax and semantics. Sometimes informed by linguistic purism, prescriptivism may suggest that certain usages are "incorrect", inconsistent, illogical, lacking communicative effect, or of low aesthetic value, even when they are common among fluent speakers.

History

Prescriptive grammar flourished in 18th-century England with works such as Robert Lowth's A Short Introduction to English Grammar (1762) and Samuel Johnson's dictionary (1755), which codified a standard modelled on educated usage. Rules such as the prohibition on split infinitives and sentence-ending prepositions were imported from Latin grammar.

Prescriptivism vs descriptivism

Modern linguists generally adopt a descriptive approach, aiming to describe how language is actually used rather than how it "should" be used. Prescriptivists respond that standards aid clear communication and are needed in education and formal writing. Many usage disputes — over "literally", singular "they", or split infinitives — sit at this boundary, and style guides increasingly accept once-condemned forms as usage shifts.

Institutions

Some languages have official regulators, such as the Académie française for French and the Real Academia Española for Spanish, which publish authoritative norms. English has no such body; authority rests with dictionaries, style manuals and publishers.
