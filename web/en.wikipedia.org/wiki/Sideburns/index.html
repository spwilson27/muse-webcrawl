Sideburns

Sideburns, sideboards, or side whiskers are strips of facial hair grown in front of the ears, extending from the hairline down the side of the face. They may be worn alone, connected to a moustache, or grown into full "mutton chops." The English word is an anagrammatic alteration of Burnsides, after the American Civil War general Ambrose Burnside, whose extravagant side whiskers joined to a moustache became famous.

Mutton-chop sideburns, popular in the 19th century.

History

Side whiskers became fashionable in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, notably among military men — hussars and other cavalrymen grew them into the broad style known as mutton chops. They reached a peak of popularity in the Victorian era, worn by statesmen, soldiers, and gentlemen, often combined with a clean-shaven chin or a full beard.

In the twentieth century sideburns became a marker of youth and popular culture. Elvis Presley's long sideburns in the 1950s made them a symbol of rock and roll rebellion; they remained prominent through the 1960s and 1970s — including the flared "lamb chop" styles worn by musicians and actors — before receding in the 1980s. Shorter, neatly trimmed sideburns have remained a standard element of men's hairstyles since, their length often following fashion cycles.

Variations

Styles range from short tabs ending at mid-ear, to long mutton chops flaring along the jaw, "friendly mutton chops" connected across the chin by a moustache, and the narrow strips worn in some subcultures. In Orthodox Judaism, the sidelocks called payot are a distinct religious practice and should not be confused with sideburns, though both involve hair in front of the ears.
