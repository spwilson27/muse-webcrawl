Hanno the Navigator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hanno the Navigator (Punic: 𐤇𐤍𐤀, Ḥannō) was a Carthaginian explorer of the fifth century BC, best known for his naval expedition along the western coast of Africa. His account, the Periplus of Hanno, survives in a Greek translation and is one of the earliest surviving descriptions of the West African coast.

The voyage

According to the Periplus, Hanno was commissioned by Carthage to sail beyond the Pillars of Heracles with a fleet of sixty ships and thousands of settlers to found or reinforce colonies along the African coast. He established settlements including Thymiaterion and reached, among other places, the island of Cerne, often identified with a site off modern Mauritania or Senegal.

Sailing further south, the expedition encountered rivers, hostile peoples, a coast of fire associated with a great mountain called the "Chariot of the Gods"—sometimes identified with Mount Cameroon—and hairy wild people whose name, translated from local interpreters, was given as "Gorillae". Skins of these creatures, said to have been brought back to Carthage, were sometimes claimed in antiquity to be those of gorillas, though they may have been another primate species.

Extent and legacy

Scholars debate how far Hanno reached: estimates range from southern Morocco to Sierra Leone, Gabon or Cameroon. Pliny the Elder and other classical authors preserved his fame; he is one of several Hannos in Carthaginian history and should not be confused with Hanno the Great. His voyage remained the outstanding feat of early Atlantic exploration for two thousand years.
