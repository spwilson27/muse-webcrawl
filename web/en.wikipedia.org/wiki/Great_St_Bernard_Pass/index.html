The Great St Bernard Pass (French: Col du Grand Saint-Bernard; Italian: Colle del Gran San Bernardo) is the third-highest road pass in Switzerland, at 2,469 metres, connecting Martigny in the canton of Valais with Aosta in Italy through the Pennine Alps. It is one of the oldest and most historically celebrated Alpine crossings, used since the Bronze Age and a principal route between northern Europe and Italy for Roman armies, medieval pilgrims, and Napoleon's army in 1800.

History

The Romans built a road and a temple to Jupiter Poeninus at the summit, from which the pass took its Latin name Mons Jovis. Around 1050 Saint Bernard of Menthon founded the hospice at the summit, staffed by Augustinian canons, to shelter travellers endangered by weather, avalanches, and brigands. The hospice's monks, aided by their famous St Bernard rescue dogs — of which Barry, credited with saving some forty lives in the early nineteenth century, is the most celebrated — made the pass's name synonymous with alpine rescue. In May 1800 Napoleon led his Army of the Reserve across the still snowbound pass with artillery to descend on the Austrians in Italy before the Battle of Marengo, an episode immortalised in Jacques-Louis David's painting of Bonaparte Crossing the Alps.

Modern route

The carriage road dates to 1905. Since 1964 the Great St Bernard Tunnel, one of the first road tunnels through the main Alpine chain, has carried through traffic below the pass, leaving the summit road — open only in summer — to tourists. The hospice still operates year-round, accessible in winter only on skis or by snowcat, and a museum and the dogs' kennels remain at the summit beside the small lake that marks the Italian–Swiss border.
