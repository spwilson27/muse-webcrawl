Daimyo

Daimyo
Japanese大名 (daimyō)
Literal meaning"Great name"
Period12th century – 1871
Abolished1871 (haihan-chiken)

Daimyo (Japanese: 大名, "great name") were powerful Japanese magnates, feudal lords who, from the 12th century to the Meiji Restoration in 1868, ruled most of Japan from their vast hereditary land holdings. They were subordinate to the shōgun and nominally to the emperor, and hired the samurai as their military retainers.

History

The term emerged in the Kamakura period for major landholders; the shugo daimyo of the Muromachi period governed provinces on behalf of the Ashikaga shogunate. During the Sengoku period (1467–1600), independent sengoku daimyo such as Oda Nobunaga, Takeda Shingen, and Uesugi Kenshin fought to unify Japan. Under the Tokugawa shogunate, daimyo were classified as shinpan (collateral houses), fudai (hereditary vassals), and tozama (outside lords), and by the size of their domains (han), measured in koku of rice; a lord needed at least 10,000 koku to qualify as a daimyo.

Edo period control

The shogunate controlled the daimyo through laws (the Buke shohatto), hostage and attendance requirements, and the sankin-kōtai system, which required daimyo to spend alternate years in Edo, leaving their families there as hostages. Daimyo processions to Edo became elaborate displays of wealth and status. Their castle towns (jōkamachi) were centres of administration, commerce, and culture.

Abolition

Following the Boshin War and the Meiji Restoration, the domains were returned to the emperor (1869) and abolished outright in 1871 (haihan-chiken), with daimyo pensioned off and later incorporated into the new kazoku peerage.
