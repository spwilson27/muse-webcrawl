Xunantunich

Xunantunich (pronounced shoo-NAHN-too-neech) is an ancient Maya archaeological site in western Belize, about 110 km (68 mi) west of Belize City, in the Cayo District near the village of San José Succotz. The name means "Stone Woman" in the Yucatec Maya language, after a local legend of a ghostly woman sighted at the site. The site flourished in the Late and Terminal Classic periods (c. 600–900 CE) as a regional civic-ceremonial centre in the upper Belize River valley, and its dominant structure, the pyramid known as El Castillo, rises about 40 m (130 ft), making it one of the tallest human-made structures in Belize.

History

Occupation began by the Middle Preclassic, but the monumental core dates mainly to the Late Classic. Xunantunich's history was intertwined with the larger nearby city of Naranjo, in present-day Guatemala: an inscription records that in 642 CE Naranjo's forces defeated Xunantunich, and the site's fortunes rose again in the late 8th century, when El Castillo's famous carved stucco frieze was created. In 2016 excavations at El Castillo uncovered one of the largest known Classic Maya royal tombs, containing the remains of a young adult male with jade and ceramic offerings, along with a hieroglyphic panel that shed light on the "snake dynasty" of Calakmul and its conflicts with Tikal. The site was largely abandoned by about 1000 CE.

The site

The core occupies a limestone ridge above the Mopan River and includes six plazas and some 26 temples and palaces. El Castillo (Structure A-6) is decorated on its eastern and western sides with friezes of stucco masks, astronomical symbols, and figures; the fragile originals are partly covered by a fibreglass replica for protection. Visitors reach the site by a hand-operated ferry across the Mopan River and a short uphill drive — one of Belize's most-visited Maya ruins.
