The Fuggerei is the world's oldest social housing complex still in use. It is a walled enclave within the city of Augsburg, Bavaria, Germany, founded in 1516 by the wealthy merchant and banker Jakob Fugger ("Jakob the Rich") of the Fugger family. Building took place between 1516 and 1523, and the settlement's charter was formalised in a deed of foundation in 1521.

Description

The complex consists of rows of two-storey terraced houses arranged along straight lanes, with around 67 houses containing 147 apartments, a church (St. Markus), a fountain, and administrative buildings. Gates are locked at night, a custom dating from the settlement's early years. Residents must be Catholic, of Augsburg origin or long residence, over 55 (originally), and poor through no fault of their own. The annual rent remains one Rhenish guilder — today about €0.88 — plus three daily prayers (the Lord's Prayer, Hail Mary, and the Nicene Creed) for the founder and his family, and a part-time contribution to the community.

History

The Fuggerei was heavily damaged by bombing during the Second World War — about a third of it was destroyed in a 1944 air raid — and was rebuilt in its original style afterwards. It remains administered by the Fugger family foundation (Fürstlich und Gräflich Fuggersche Stiftungs-Administration) and is financed in part by forestry income and admission fees paid by day visitors. It is frequently cited as a pioneering model of charitable social housing and a precursor of later workers' settlements.
