Swahili, also known by its local name Kiswahili, is a Bantu language originally spoken by the Swahili people of the East African coast. It is the national language of Tanzania and Kenya, an official language of Uganda and of the African Union, and a lingua franca across the African Great Lakes region, making it one of the most widely spoken languages in Africa.

Swahili has been written in both Arabic and Latin scripts

History

Swahili developed on the coast and islands of present-day Kenya and Tanzania through centuries of trade across the Indian Ocean. Although its grammar and core vocabulary are Bantu, it absorbed large amounts of vocabulary from Arabic (an estimated 30–40% of words, including swahili itself, from sawāḥil, "coasts"), and later from Persian, Portuguese, German, and English. Standard Swahili is based on the Kiunguja dialect of Zanzibar City. It was historically written in Arabic script for poetry and religious texts; the Latin alphabet was promoted under colonial administrations in the 20th century.

Grammar

Like other Bantu languages, Swahili is agglutinative, with verbs built up from prefixes and infixes marking subject, tense, object, and negation, and a system of roughly 15 noun classes that govern agreement throughout the sentence. It has five vowels and a syllable structure that is predominantly open, giving place names and loanword adaptations their characteristic shapes (e.g. shule "school", from German Schule).
