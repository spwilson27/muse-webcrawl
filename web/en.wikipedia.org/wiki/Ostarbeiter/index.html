Ostarbeiter

Ostarbeiter (German for "Eastern worker") was a Nazi German designation for foreign forced labourers gathered from occupied Central and Eastern Europe to work in Germany during World War II. Between 1942 and 1945, several million people — mainly from Ukraine, Poland, Belarus and Russia — were deported to the Reich for work in agriculture and industry; at its height the programme encompassed over 2.5 million Soviet civilians alone.

The "OST" badge Ostarbeiter were forced to wear

Recruitment and conditions

Under Fritz Sauckel, appointed General Plenipotentiary for Labour Deployment in 1942, recruitment in the occupied Soviet territories shifted rapidly from propaganda appeals to mass round-ups and deportation. Ostarbeiter were required to wear a blue-and-white "OST" badge, were subject to curfews, movement restrictions and severe punishments, and were paid far less than German or Western European workers, if at all. They lived in guarded camps and worked long hours in armaments factories, mines and farms; mortality from malnutrition, disease and air raids was high, and children born to Eastern workers were often taken to so-called "care centres" where many died.

Repatriation and aftermath

After the war, most surviving Ostarbeiter were repatriated to the Soviet Union, where many faced suspicion, filtration camps and stigma as suspected collaborators. Recognition and compensation came only decades later, principally through the German Foundation "Remembrance, Responsibility and Future" established in 2000, which made payments to former forced labourers including surviving Ostarbeiter.
