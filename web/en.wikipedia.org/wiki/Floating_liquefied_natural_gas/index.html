Floating liquefied natural gas

Floating liquefied natural gas (FLNG) is a technology that enables the development of offshore natural gas resources by performing the production, liquefaction, storage, and transfer of liquefied natural gas (LNG) — and in some designs LPG and condensate — entirely at sea aboard a floating facility, rather than in an onshore plant. An FLNG vessel moors above an offshore gas field, processes the gas, cools it to about −162 °C so it shrinks to roughly 1/600 of its volume, stores it in hull tanks, and offloads it to LNG carriers that come alongside.

Advantages and challenges

FLNG avoids the cost of long subsea pipelines to shore and large onshore construction, can reduce environmental footprint, and can make smaller or remote "stranded" gas fields economic; the vessel can in principle be redeployed when a field is exhausted. The challenges are substantial: fitting a full liquefaction plant onto a hull, safety in proximity of large inventories of LNG, motion effects on process equipment and storage, and ship-to-ship transfer in open water.

Projects

The first FLNG facility in operation was Petronas's PFLNG Satu, which began production offshore Malaysia in 2016–2017, followed by PFLNG Dua (2020). The largest is Shell's Prelude FLNG off north-western Australia — at 488 metres long and displacing around 600,000 tonnes, the largest floating facility ever built — which shipped its first LNG cargo in 2019. Converted-carrier FLNG units by Golar LNG (such as Hilli Episeyo off Cameroon and Gimi for the Tortue field) established a lower-cost conversion model, and additional units operate or are under development off Mozambique, Malaysia, and West Africa.
