Diabetes insipidus

Diabetes insipidus (DI) is a condition characterised by large amounts of dilute urine and increased thirst. The amount of urine produced can be nearly 20 litres per day. Reduction of fluid has little effect on the concentration of the urine. Complications may include dehydration or seizures.

Signs and symptoms

Excessive urination and extreme thirst are typical. The urine is dilute, with a specific gravity below 1.005, and the person may crave cold water. Nocturia — waking at night to urinate — is common.

Causes and types

There are four types. Central DI is due to a lack of the antidiuretic hormone vasopressin (ADH), often from damage to the hypothalamus or pituitary gland. Nephrogenic DI occurs when the kidneys do not respond properly to vasopressin. Dipsogenic DI is due to abnormal thirst mechanisms, and gestational DI occurs only during pregnancy.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis involves blood and urine tests and a water deprivation test. It must be distinguished from diabetes mellitus, with which it shares the name and the symptom of polyuria but not the mechanism: in diabetes mellitus urine contains glucose, whereas in DI it is tasteless — hence insipidus, Latin for tasteless.

Treatment

Central and gestational DI are treated with desmopressin, a synthetic analogue of vasopressin. Nephrogenic DI may be treated with thiazide diuretics and by addressing the underlying cause. Adequate fluid intake is essential in all forms.

Etymology

The word diabetes comes from Greek for a siphon or passing through; insipidus reflects the tasteless urine, in contrast to the sweet urine of diabetes mellitus.
