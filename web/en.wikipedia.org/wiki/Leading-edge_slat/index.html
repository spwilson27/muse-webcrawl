Leading-edge slat

Leading-edge slats are aerodynamic surfaces on the leading edge of an aircraft wing which, when deployed, allow the wing to operate at a higher angle of attack. A higher coefficient of lift is produced by the slat's own geometry and by the slot it creates, which energizes the boundary layer on the upper wing surface and delays the stall.

Operation

When extended, the slat moves forward and down, opening a slot between the slat and the wing. Air flows through the slot from the lower surface to the upper surface, keeping the airflow attached at angles of attack that would otherwise stall the wing. This lowers the stalling speed and improves low-speed handling during takeoff and landing.

Types

Slats may be fixed, as on light aircraft such as the Fieseler Storch; automatic, opening under aerodynamic forces at high angles of attack (an idea patented by Handley Page); or powered, driven by motors on transport aircraft as part of the high-lift system alongside trailing-edge flaps.
