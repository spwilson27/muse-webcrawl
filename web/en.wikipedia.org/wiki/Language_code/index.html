A language code is a code that assigns letters or numbers as identifiers or classifiers for languages. These codes are used in computing, library cataloguing, and linguistics to refer unambiguously to a natural language, language family, or variety, regardless of the language's name in any particular tongue (which varies: German is Deutsch in German, allemand in French).

Common schemes

ISO 639 is the principal international standard. Its parts include ISO 639-1 (two-letter codes for 184 major languages, e.g. en for English), ISO 639-2 (three-letter codes, including bibliographic and terminological variants, e.g. ger/deu), ISO 639-3 (three-letter codes intended to cover all known living and ancient languages, maintained by SIL International, with over 7,000 codes), and ISO 639-5 for language families.

IETF language tags, defined by BCP 47, build on ISO codes to form tags such as en-US (American English) or zh-Hant-TW (Traditional Chinese as used in Taiwan) by combining language, script, and region subtags. They are used in HTTP, HTML, and XML (the lang attribute) and in software localization. Other systems include the bibliographic codes of MARC, the Glottolog database's glottocodes, and the Linguist List codes.

Difficulties

Assigning codes raises classificatory problems: distinguishing languages from dialects, handling macrolanguages such as Arabic or Chinese (which group many varieties under one code), and accommodating extinct, constructed, and sign languages. Codes are periodically revised, merged, or retired as scholarship changes.
