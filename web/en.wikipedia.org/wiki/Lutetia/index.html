Lutetia (Latin: Lutetia Parisiorum) was a Gallo-Roman city on the site of modern Paris. Originally a settlement of the Celtic Parisii tribe, located around the Île de la Cité in the River Seine, it came under Roman control after Julius Caesar's conquest of Gaul in 52 BCE and developed into a prosperous provincial town.

The Arènes de Lutèce, a surviving monument of Roman Lutetia

Roman city

Under Roman rule the city expanded principally onto the Left Bank (the modern Latin Quarter), laid out on a grid around a forum on the hill of modern Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. Lutetia acquired the usual apparatus of a Roman town: baths (whose remains survive in the Thermes de Cluny), a theatre, an amphitheatre (the Arènes de Lutèce, seating perhaps 17,000), temples, aqueducts bringing water from the south, and an extensive necropolis. Its inhabitants worked in trade, river transport and crafts, and the boatmen's guild dedicated the Pillar of the Boatmen, now in the Musée de Cluny.

Late antiquity

In the troubled third century CE the Left Bank town was largely abandoned and the population retrenched behind walls on the Île de la Cité. By about 360 CE, when Julian was proclaimed emperor there, the name Paris, from the Parisii, was replacing Lutetia. The name Lutetium, for element 71, commemorates the city, and the asteroid 21 Lutetia also takes its name from it.
