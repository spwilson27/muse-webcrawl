Rosie the Riveter

Rosie the Riveter is an allegorical cultural icon in the United States who represents the women who worked in factories and shipyards during World War II, many of whom produced munitions and war supplies. These women sometimes took entirely new jobs, replacing the male workers who joined the military.

Origins

The figure emerged in 1942: the song "Rosie the Riveter", written by Redd Evans and John Jacob Loeb, celebrated women war workers, and Norman Rockwell's painting of a muscular riveter named Rosie appeared on the cover of the Saturday Evening Post in May 1943. Separately, the artist J. Howard Miller produced the famous "We Can Do It!" poster for Westinghouse Electric in 1943; although only briefly displayed during the war, that image became widely associated with Rosie the Riveter from the 1980s onward.

Several real women have been proposed as models or inspirations, including Geraldine Hoff Doyle, who worked briefly in a Michigan metal plant and resembled the woman in Miller's poster, and Naomi Parker Fraley, identified by later research as the woman in a 1942 photograph of a factory worker that likely inspired the poster.

Impact

Rosie embodied a government propaganda campaign that recruited millions of women into the industrial workforce; the number of working women in the United States rose from about 12 million to 20 million during the war. After the war many women were displaced from industrial jobs, but the experience is widely regarded as a turning point in women's employment.

Legacy

Rosie the Riveter remains a feminist and patriotic symbol, reproduced on posters, clothing and memorials. The Rosie the Riveter/World War II Home Front National Historical Park in Richmond, California, preserves the shipyards where thousands of women "Rosies" worked. In 2020 the surviving Rosies were collectively honoured with a Congressional Gold Medal.
