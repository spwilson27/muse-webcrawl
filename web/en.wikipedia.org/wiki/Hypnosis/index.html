Hypnosis

Hypnosis
Field: Psychology, psychotherapy
Key figures: James Braid, Franz Mesmer, Milton Erickson
Applications: Pain management, anxiety, irritable bowel syndrome, habit change

Hypnosis is a human condition involving focused attention (the selective attention/sustained concentration kind), reduced peripheral awareness, and an enhanced capacity to respond to suggestion. A person in this state is said to be hypnotized, and the process of inducing it is typically carried out by a hypnotist using verbal suggestion, though self-hypnosis is also practiced. The term derives from the Greek hypnos, "sleep," coined by the Scottish surgeon James Braid in the 1840s, who later regretted the name because hypnosis is not sleep.

History

Practices resembling hypnosis appear in ancient religious and healing rituals, but its modern history begins with Franz Anton Mesmer's "animal magnetism" in the 18th century. A royal commission including Benjamin Franklin debunked Mesmer's magnetic fluid theory in 1784, attributing effects to imagination. In the 1840s Braid reframed the phenomenon in physiological terms. Later, Jean-Martin Charcot and Hippolyte Bernheim debated whether hypnosis was pathological or a normal product of suggestion; Sigmund Freud briefly used hypnosis before abandoning it for free association. In the 20th century Milton H. Erickson developed indirect, permissive techniques, and hypnosis gained ground in medicine and dentistry.

Theories

Researchers divide broadly into "state" theorists, who view hypnosis as an altered state of consciousness with distinctive brain activity, and "non-state" (sociocognitive) theorists, who explain it through suggestion, expectation, role-playing, and focused attention without a special state. Neuroimaging studies show changes in the anterior cingulate cortex and in connectivity between executive-control and salience networks during hypnosis. Hypnotizability is a stable trait, measurable with scales such as the Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale; roughly 10–15% of people are highly hypnotizable.

Applications

Clinical hypnosis (hypnotherapy) has evidence supporting its use as an adjunct in pain management — including procedural pain and some chronic pain — anxiety, and gut-directed hypnotherapy for irritable bowel syndrome. It is also used for habit disorders and as an adjunct in psychotherapy. Claims of recovering accurate "repressed memories" under hypnosis are scientifically discredited, as hypnosis increases confidence in memories without increasing their accuracy. Stage hypnosis is entertainment and relies on audience selection, social pressure, and suggestion.

Myths

Popular depictions of hypnosis as mind control are inaccurate: a hypnotized person cannot ordinarily be made to act against their deeply held values, does not lose consciousness, and can resist suggestions. Nor is hypnosis sleep; EEG patterns resemble wakefulness.
