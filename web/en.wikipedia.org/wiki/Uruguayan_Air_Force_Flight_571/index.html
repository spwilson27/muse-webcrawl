Uruguayan Air Force Flight 571 was a chartered flight carrying the Old Christians rugby union team from Montevideo, Uruguay, to Santiago, Chile. On 13 October 1972 the Fairchild FH-227 struck a mountain ridge in the Andes in poor weather and crashed on a remote glacier in Argentina. Of the 45 people aboard, 29 ultimately died; the 16 survivors endured 72 days at altitude in extreme cold before two of them trekked out of the mountains and brought rescue on 22–23 December 1972. The ordeal is known as the Miracle of the Andes (El milagro de los Andes).

Crash and survival

Disoriented in cloud, the pilots began their descent believing they had crossed the Andes; the aircraft clipped a peak, lost both wings and its tail, and the fuselage slid down the glacier. Twelve people died in the crash and immediate aftermath. The survivors sheltered in the fuselage, melting snow for water and rationing tiny amounts of food. After about ten days they heard on a transistor radio that the search had been called off. Facing starvation, they made the collective decision to eat the flesh of the dead — a decision survivors have described in moral and religious terms that won broad public understanding, especially in Catholic Uruguay.

Rescue

An avalanche killed eight more on 29 October. When summer thaw approached, Nando Parrado and Roberto Canessa, fed for the journey, climbed from the glacier and trekked west for ten days, eventually reaching a Chilean huaso, Sergio Catalán, who rode for help and alerted authorities. Chilean helicopters reached the crash site on 22 December 1972; the last survivors were lifted out the following day.

Aftermath and legacy

The survivors' story was told in Piers Paul Read's Alive (1974) and its 1993 film adaptation, in Nando Parrado's memoir Miracle in the Andes (2006), and in the 2023 film Society of the Snow, which was widely acclaimed and nominated for the Academy Award for Best International Feature Film. The survivors founded the Fundación Viven and remain closely connected; memorials stand at the crash site, which is visited by annual expeditions.
