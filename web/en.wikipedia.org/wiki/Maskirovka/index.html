Maskirovka

Maskirovka (Russian: маскировка, lit. 'camouflage' or 'masking') is a Russian military doctrine of deception that developed from the 1920s onwards. The term has a broad definition including all measures, active and passive, designed to deceive the enemy about the presence, position, strength, readiness, and intentions of friendly forces. It encompasses camouflage, concealment, decoys, feints, disinformation, and denial, and in Russian usage extends beyond the battlefield to operational, strategic, and even political deception.

Development

The doctrine emerged from Red Army experience in the Russian Civil War and was formalised in the 1920s, with a school of maskirovka established in 1929. Soviet regulations of the 1930s and the 1944 Field Regulations treated deception as a responsibility at every level of command, integral to achieving surprise (vnezapnost).

Second World War

Maskirovka reached a high point of sophistication during the Second World War. Before Operation Uranus at Stalingrad (1942), the Soviets concealed the build-up of over a million men. In 1944, Operation Bagration was preceded by elaborate deceptions that persuaded German commanders the main Soviet summer offensive would fall in Ukraine rather than Belorussia, contributing to the destruction of Army Group Centre. At the Battle of Kursk (1943), maskirovka concealed vast defensive preparations. Techniques included dummy airfields and tank concentrations, false radio traffic, simulated troop movements by night, and strict concealment discipline.

Post-Cold War usage

Western commentators later applied the term to Russian operations such as the 2014 seizure of Crimea, where unmarked personnel — the "little green men" — and official denials created ambiguity during the takeover. Commentators dispute how closely such "hybrid" methods align with the classical military doctrine, but the word has entered general use for Russian state deception broadly.
