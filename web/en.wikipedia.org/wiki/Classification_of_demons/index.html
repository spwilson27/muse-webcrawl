Classification of demons

There have been many attempts throughout Christian history to classify demons — the fallen angels and evil spirits of Christian demonology — for the purpose of understanding them, whether in theological treatises, grimoires, or the literature of witchcraft. Mirroring the nine orders of angels in the hierarchy of Pseudo-Dionysius, demonologists proposed infernal hierarchies of princes, dukes, and legions, and systems that sorted demons by their habitat, their office, or the sin to which they tempt humans.

Classification by domain

The Byzantine theologian Michael Psellos, in his eleventh-century De operatione daemonum, divided demons into six types according to where they dwell: the lelurion of the upper air, aerial demons, terrestrial demons, aqueous demons, subterranean demons, and the light-fleeing lucifugus of the deepest hell. Similar schemes appear in later occult works, which assign demons to the elements or to the spheres of the planets.

Classification by office

The Lantern of Light (early fifteenth century) named seven princes of hell, each associated with a deadly sin, and this scheme was elaborated by Peter Binsfeld in 1589, whose list pairs the princes with the seven deadly sins: Lucifer (pride), Mammon (greed), Asmodeus (lust), Leviathan (envy), Beelzebub (gluttony), Satan (wrath), and Belphegor (sloth). Other grimoires, such as the Lesser Key of Solomon, catalogue demons by rank — kings, dukes, princes, marquises, counts, knights, and presidents — each commanding legions of lesser spirits.

Michaelis classification

The French inquisitor Sébastien Michaelis, in his Histoire admirable de la possession et conversion d'une pénitente (1613), based on the Aix-en-Provence possessions associated with Madeleine de Demandolx, set out a hierarchy of demons in three orders, reported by the demon Balberith during an exorcism. The first hierarchy comprises fallen seraphim, cherubim, and thrones — including Beelzebub, prince of demons, Leviathan, and Asmodeus; the second, fallen dominions, virtues, and powers, such as Mammon and Belphegor; and the third, fallen principalities, archangels, and angels, including Belial and Meresin. Each demon is opposed by a particular saint.

Other systems

Alphonso de Spina classified demons into ten kinds, including demons of fate, incubi and succubi, and demons that are pure hallucinations. King James VI and I's Daemonologie (1597) distinguished spirits by their modes of haunting and harm. Johann Weyer's sceptical Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1563) listed 69 demons with their ranks and offices while denying their power, and the classification of demons by month, zodiac sign, and direction appears in several magical handbooks. Modern occultists and popular culture have continued to rearrange these lists.
