Carnivalesque is a literary and cultural concept developed by the Russian philosopher and critic Mikhail Bakhtin in his study of François Rabelais, Rabelais and His World. Drawing on the medieval and Renaissance carnival, Bakhtin described a mode of experience and of literature in which established hierarchies, norms and official truths are temporarily suspended, inverted and mocked.

The concept

For Bakhtin, carnival was a "second life" of the people, organised on the basis of laughter. Its features include the free and familiar contact between people normally separated by rank; the crowning and subsequent decrowning of a carnival king, symbolising the joyful relativity of all authority; parody and profanation of the sacred and official; and the "grotesque body" — open, exaggerated, eating, drinking and excreting — set against the closed, finished body of classical aesthetics, a style Bakhtin called grotesque realism. In literature, the carnivalesque appears in genres of comic inversion and polyphony, from Rabelais and Dostoevsky to modern satire.

Influence and criticism

The concept has been widely used in literary criticism, film and media studies, and cultural theory to analyse texts and practices that subvert authority through humour and inversion. Critics have debated whether carnival genuinely challenged power or acted as a licensed "safety valve" that ultimately reinforced the social order — a question Bakhtin's account has been read as answering both ways.
