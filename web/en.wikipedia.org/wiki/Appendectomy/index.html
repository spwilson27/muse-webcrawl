Appendectomy

An appendectomy (known outside North America as appendicectomy) is the surgical removal of the vermiform appendix. It is most commonly performed as an emergency procedure to treat acute appendicitis, in order to prevent rupture and peritonitis.

Procedure

Appendectomy may be open, through a small incision in the right lower abdomen, or laparoscopic, using several small incisions and a camera. Laparoscopic surgery is associated with less postoperative pain and faster recovery, and allows inspection of the rest of the abdomen; the choice depends on the patient, the surgeon and the severity of disease. The appendix is detached at its base on the caecum and removed, and any infection in the abdominal cavity is washed out.

Recovery and risks

Most patients recover within days to a few weeks. Complications can include wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess, bleeding and ileus. If the appendix has already perforated, treatment is more complex and may involve drainage and antibiotics before or instead of immediate surgery.

Non-operative management

Antibiotics alone can treat some cases of uncomplicated appendicitis, but a substantial minority of patients later recur and need appendectomy, so management is individualised.
