A cargo airline (or air freight carrier) is an airline dedicated to the transport of cargo by air, operating dedicated freighter aircraft rather than carrying freight only in the belly holds of passenger flights. Some cargo airlines are subsidiaries of passenger groups, while others — notably the fleets of integrated express carriers FedEx and UPS — are among the largest airlines in the world by fleet size and freight tonne-kilometres.

Operations

All-cargo carriers fly scheduled trunk routes between major freight hubs, often at night to suit express delivery networks, and use hub-and-spoke sorting centres such as FedEx's Memphis superhub and UPS's Louisville Worldport. Cargo is carried in unit load devices (ULDs) and containers on the main deck of converted or factory-built freighters; large freighters such as the Boeing 747F feature nose or side cargo doors accommodating outsize loads.

Air freight accounts for only about 1% of world trade by volume but roughly a third by value, because it concentrates on high-value, perishable and time-sensitive goods: electronics, pharmaceuticals, fresh food, mail and e-commerce parcels. The industry is highly cyclical, expanding sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic when passenger belly capacity vanished.

History

Scheduled air cargo began with airmail in the 1910s–1920s. Post-war surplus aircraft launched independent freight carriers; dedicated jets such as the 747 freighter from the 1970s and the rise of overnight express services in the 1970s–80s transformed the business.
