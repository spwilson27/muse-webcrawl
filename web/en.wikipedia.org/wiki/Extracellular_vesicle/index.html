Extracellular vesicle

Extracellular vesicles imaged by electron microscopy.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer-delimited particles that are naturally released from almost all types of cells and, unlike cells themselves, cannot replicate. They carry cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (including mRNA and microRNA) from their cell of origin and mediate intercellular communication, delivering their contents to recipient cells locally and at a distance via body fluids such as blood, urine, and saliva.

Types

The term covers a heterogeneous family of particles distinguished by size and biogenesis:

  • Exosomes — small vesicles (roughly 30–150 nm) formed inside multivesicular bodies and released when these fuse with the plasma membrane.
  • Microvesicles (ectosomes) — larger vesicles (roughly 100–1,000 nm) shed directly by outward budding of the plasma membrane.
  • Apoptotic bodies — large particles released by dying cells.

Because isolation methods co-purify overlapping populations, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) recommends operational terms such as "small EVs" unless biogenesis is directly demonstrated.

Functions and applications

EVs participate in normal physiology — immune regulation, coagulation, stem-cell maintenance, and neuronal signalling — and in disease, including cancer metastasis, where tumour-derived vesicles help prepare pre-metastatic niches. Their stability in body fluids makes them promising sources of biomarkers for "liquid biopsy," and they are under investigation as natural drug-delivery vehicles and therapeutics in their own right.
