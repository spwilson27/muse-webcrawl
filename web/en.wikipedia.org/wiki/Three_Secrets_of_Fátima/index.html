The Three Secrets of Fátima (Portuguese: Os Três Segredos de Fátima) are a series of apocalyptic visions and prophecies said to have been given to three young Portuguese shepherds — Lúcia dos Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto — by an apparition of the Virgin Mary at Fátima on 13 July 1917. According to Lúcia, the Virgin requested that the secrets not be revealed until the proper time; the first two were written down in her memoirs in 1941, while the third, committed to paper in 1944, remained sealed by the Vatican until the year 2000.

The first secret

The first secret was a vision of Hell: Lúcia described "a vast sea of fire" beneath the earth, in which were plunged demons and the souls of the damned, amid shrieks of pain and despair. The vision, she said, was shown so that the seers would work for the salvation of sinners through devotion to the Immaculate Heart of Mary.

The second secret

The second secret announced that the First World War would end, but that a worse war would break out during the pontificate of Pius XI if people continued to offend God; it asked for the consecration of Russia to the Immaculate Heart and the Communion of Reparation on the First Saturdays, warning that otherwise Russia would "spread her errors throughout the world", while promising that in the end the Immaculate Heart would triumph and a period of peace would be granted to the world. A sign foretold was an exceptionally bright aurora visible across Europe on the night of 25–26 January 1938, which Lúcia took to be the promised warning.

The third secret

Lúcia wrote the third secret on 3 January 1944, after a period of illness in which her bishop ordered the record made; it was placed in a sealed envelope, passed to Rome in 1957 and read by Pope John XXIII in 1959, who chose not to publish it. Pope John Paul II ordered its release in 2000 during his visit to Fátima for the beatification of Francisco and Jacinta. The text describes an angel with a flaming sword, a steep mountain crowned by a cross, and a "bishop dressed in white" — understood as the Pope — passing among corpses and being killed by soldiers' bullets and arrows. The Vatican's official commentary, prepared by Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger (later Pope Benedict XVI), interpreted the vision symbolically, associating the fallen pope chiefly with the 1981 assassination attempt against John Paul II. Some commentators questioned whether the published text was complete, a claim the Vatican denied.
