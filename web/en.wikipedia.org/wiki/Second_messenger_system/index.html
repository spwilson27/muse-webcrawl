Second messenger system

A first messenger (hormone or neurotransmitter) binds a receptor, triggering production of intracellular second messengers.

Second messengers are intracellular signalling molecules released by a cell in response to exposure to extracellular signalling molecules, the first messengers. Second messengers trigger physiological changes at the cellular level such as proliferation, differentiation, migration, survival, and apoptosis. They are one of the components of intracellular signal transduction cascades.

Examples of second messenger molecules include cyclic AMP (cAMP), cyclic GMP (cGMP), inositol trisphosphate (IP3), diacylglycerol (DAG), and calcium ions (Ca2+). The cell releases second messenger molecules in response to hormones, neurotransmitters and other first messengers binding to membrane receptors — either G protein-coupled receptors or receptor tyrosine kinases.

Discovery

The concept was developed by Earl Wilbur Sutherland Jr., who in the late 1950s showed that epinephrine's stimulation of glycogen breakdown in liver cells was mediated by cyclic AMP formed inside the cell. Sutherland received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1971 for this work.

Mechanism and amplification

Because second messengers are generated enzymatically, a single activated receptor can produce many second messenger molecules, and each of these can activate enzymes (such as protein kinase A in the cAMP pathway) that phosphorylate many target proteins. This cascade produces signal amplification, speed, and the ability to integrate multiple inputs. Signal termination is achieved by enzymes that degrade the messenger — phosphodiesterases hydrolyse cAMP and cGMP, and pumps and exchangers return cytosolic Ca2+ to low resting levels.

Common pathways

  • cAMP system — adenylyl cyclase synthesises cAMP from ATP, typically under the control of Gs or Gi proteins; cAMP activates protein kinase A and EPAC.
  • Phosphoinositol system — phospholipase C cleaves PIP2 into IP3, which releases Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum, and DAG, which with Ca2+ activates protein kinase C.
  • cGMP system — guanylyl cyclase, activated for example by nitric oxide, produces cGMP.
  • Calcium as messenger — Ca2+ binds calmodulin and other sensor proteins to regulate contraction, secretion and metabolism.
