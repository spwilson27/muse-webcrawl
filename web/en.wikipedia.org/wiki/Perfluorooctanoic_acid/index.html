Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), also known informally as C8, is a perfluorinated carboxylic acid produced and used worldwide as an industrial surfactant and as a processing aid in the manufacture of fluoropolymers such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Teflon). It is one of the best-known PFAS "forever chemicals".

History and uses

PFOA was manufactured from the 1940s, principally by 3M and DuPont. It was used as a polymerisation aid in making PTFE and other fluoropolymers, and its salts were used in aqueous film-forming foams, coatings and stain-resistant treatments. 3M phased out production in 2000–2002; the US EPA's 2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program secured commitments from major producers to eliminate emissions and product content by 2015.

Environmental persistence and health

PFOA is extremely persistent in the environment, is globally distributed — it is detectable in human blood samples worldwide — and bioaccumulates. Its elimination half-life in humans is roughly 2–4 years. Epidemiological research, notably the C8 Science Panel studies of communities near DuPont's Washington Works plant in West Virginia, found probable links between PFOA exposure and kidney and testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, pregnancy-induced hypertension and high cholesterol. The IARC classified PFOA as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) in 2023.

Regulation

PFOA, its salts and related compounds were listed for elimination under the Stockholm Convention in 2019. The European Union restricted it under REACH and the POPs Regulation, and in 2024 the US EPA set an enforceable drinking-water standard (MCL) of 4.0 parts per trillion for PFOA. Litigation over contamination, including the case depicted in the film Dark Waters, led to settlements totalling billions of dollars.
