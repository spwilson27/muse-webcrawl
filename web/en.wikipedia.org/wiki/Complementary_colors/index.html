Complementary colors

Complementary colors are pairs of colors which, when combined or mixed, cancel each other out (lose hue) by producing a grayscale color like white or black. When placed next to each other, they create the strongest contrast and reinforce each other. In color models, complementary pairs lie directly opposite one another on the color wheel.

Color models

  • Traditional (RYB) model, used in painting: the primary colors are red, yellow, and blue, and the complementary pairs are red–green, yellow–purple, and blue–orange. Mixing complementary pigments yields browns and grays.
  • RGB model, used for light: complementary pairs are red–cyan, green–magenta, and blue–yellow. Combined at full intensity, complementary lights produce white.
  • Opponent process theory of vision holds that the human visual system encodes color in opposing channels: red vs. green, blue vs. yellow, and black vs. white — which is why we cannot perceive "reddish green".

Afterimages

If you stare at a saturated color for a time and then look at a white surface, you see an afterimage in the complementary color, an effect explained by fatigue of the retinal receptors and the opponent organisation of colour vision.

Use in art and design

Artists from Delacroix to the Impressionists and Post-Impressionists — notably Van Gogh, who juxtaposed blue and orange and yellow and violet — exploited complementary contrasts to make colours appear more vivid. Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast (1839) described how adjacent colours shift perception toward each other's complement. Complementary schemes remain a standard tool in graphic design, branding, and cinematography (for example, the orange-and-teal palette), because pairing complements balances warmth and coolness while maximising legibility and visual energy.
