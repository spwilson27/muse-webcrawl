Charles-Michel de l'Épée

Charles-Michel de l'Épée (24 November 1712 – 23 December 1789), often known as the Abbé de l'Épée, was a French Catholic priest and philanthropist who founded the first free public school for deaf people. He is commonly regarded as the "father of deaf education" and a founder of French Sign Language teaching.

Life

L'Épée was born in Versailles into a wealthy family; his father was an architect to King Louis XV. He studied theology and law, was ordained a priest, but was barred from full ministry because of his sympathy with Jansenism. Around 1760, in Paris, he encountered two deaf sisters who communicated with signs, and he took over their instruction. Learning the signs his pupils already used among themselves, he developed a system of "methodical signs" combining their natural sign language with invented signs representing French grammar, and began teaching deaf children of poor families at his own expense.

School for the deaf

In 1771 he formally opened his school in Paris, the Institution Nationale des Sourds-Muets, regarded as the first free public school for the deaf in the world. He defended the use of sign language against the oralist methods championed in Germany by Samuel Heinicke, insisting that sign was the deaf person's natural language and the proper medium of instruction. His public demonstration lessons attracted visitors from across Europe, and his methods spread through the many teachers he trained, including the Abbé Sicard, who succeeded him.

After l'Épée's death in 1789, the French National Assembly adopted his school as a national institution in 1791. Because his pupils carried his methods abroad — most notably through Laurent Clerc, who co-founded the first permanent American school for the deaf in Hartford in 1817 — French Sign Language strongly influenced American Sign Language and many other national sign languages.

Works and legacy

His principal works include Institution des sourds et muets, par la voie des signes méthodiques (1776) and the posthumously published La véritable manière d'instruire les sourds et muets (1784). In 1798 his remains were honoured during the Revolution. He is commemorated by a monument in the church of Saint-Roch in Paris and is celebrated in the deaf community worldwide as a benefactor who first demonstrated that deaf people could be educated through sign.
