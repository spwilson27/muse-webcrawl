Wood lathe

TypeMachine tool (woodworking)
Main partsHeadstock, tailstock, bed, tool rest
Used forWoodturning: bowls, spindles, furniture parts

A wood lathe is a machine tool used in woodturning. It rotates a workpiece about a horizontal axis while the operator presses hand-held cutting tools — gouges, chisels, scrapers and parting tools — against the spinning wood to shape it into objects with rotational symmetry, such as bowls, table legs, balusters, baseball bats and pens.

Construction

A typical lathe consists of a heavy bed, a headstock containing the motor and a rotating spindle fitted with a drive center, spur center or faceplate, and a tailstock with a live or dead center that supports the other end of the workpiece. A movable tool rest provides a fulcrum for the cutting tools. Key specifications are the "swing" (the largest diameter that can be turned over the bed) and the distance between centers. Modern lathes often have electronic variable-speed control; traditional machines used pulleys or treadles.

Techniques

Turning between centers is called spindle turning and produces long, thin objects. Faceplate turning, in which the workpiece is mounted on a faceplate or chuck at the headstock only, is used for bowls and platters. Work is typically finished with abrasives while still on the lathe, then coated with oil, wax or lacquer.

History

Lathes are among the oldest machine tools: turnery dates to ancient Egypt and Greece, and spring-pole and bow lathes were used through the medieval period. Treadle lathes powered by foot, then water, steam and finally electricity, progressively increased capability and precision.
