Hekhalot literature

Hekhalot literature (Hebrew: ספרות ההיכלות, "literature of the palaces") is a body of Jewish mystical texts composed roughly between late antiquity and the early Middle Ages (c. 3rd–9th centuries CE, with later redactions). It belongs to the earliest stratum of Jewish mysticism, related to the Merkabah (divine chariot) tradition. The texts describe the visions of rabbis—above all Rabbi Ishmael and Rabbi Akiva—who "descend" (or ascend) through the seven heavenly palaces (hekhalot) guarded by angels, reciting hymns, divine names and seals in order to pass, until they reach the throne of glory and behold God, or the angel Metatron, in the seventh palace.

Major texts

The corpus includes Hekhalot Rabbati (Greater Palaces), Hekhalot Zutarti (Lesser Palaces), Maaseh Merkabah (Work of the Chariot), the Book of Enoch known as 3 Enoch, Shi'ur Qomah (Measure of the Body), describing the immense dimensions of God's limbs, and Sefer Hekhalot. Surviving in often confused manuscripts, many of them recovered from the Cairo Geniza, the texts combine visionary narrative with adjurations for magical practices such as the summoning of the Prince of Torah (Sar ha-Torah) to gain miraculous learning.

Scholarship and significance

Modern study was founded by Gershom Scholem, who treated the Hekhalot texts as the earliest recoverable form of Jewish mysticism and a forerunner of medieval Kabbalah; later scholars including Peter Schäfer, Ithamar Gruenwald and Rachel Elior debated their dating, their relation to rabbinic literature and to Greco-Egyptian magical and Gnostic traditions. The literature is remarkable for its hymns, its daring anthropology of the divine throne-world, and its continuities with apocalyptic literature such as 1 Enoch and 2 Enoch.
