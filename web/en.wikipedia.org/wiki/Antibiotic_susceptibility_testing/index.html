Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing, or antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), is a laboratory procedure used to determine which antimicrobial agents are effective against a particular bacterial isolate from a patient. The results guide clinicians in choosing an appropriate antibiotic and are an essential tool in combating antimicrobial resistance.

Methods

The disk diffusion method (Kirby–Bauer test) places paper disks impregnated with antibiotics on an agar plate inoculated with the bacterium; after incubation, the diameters of the zones of inhibition around the disks are measured and compared with standard tables. Dilution methods determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) — the lowest antibiotic concentration that prevents visible growth — using broth or agar containing serial dilutions of the drug. Gradient diffusion strips (such as the Etest) combine the two approaches, reading the MIC from a scale where the inhibition ellipse intersects the strip. Automated systems and, increasingly, molecular and genomic methods can deliver faster results.

Interpretation

Results are reported using interpretive categories — typically "susceptible", "intermediate" (or "susceptible, increased exposure"), and "resistant" — defined by breakpoint standards maintained by organisations such as EUCAST and the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Breakpoints take into account MIC distributions, pharmacokinetics, and clinical outcome data.

Clinical role

AST supports antimicrobial stewardship by enabling de-escalation from broad-spectrum to targeted therapy, helps detect resistance mechanisms such as extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases, and provides surveillance data on resistance trends at hospital, national, and global levels.
