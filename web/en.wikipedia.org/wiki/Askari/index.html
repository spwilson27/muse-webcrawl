Askari

Askari
EtymologyArabic ʿaskarī, "soldier"; via Swahili and other languages
Used byGerman, British, French, Belgian, Italian, Portuguese and Spanish colonial forces; Ottoman forces
PeriodLate 19th century – mid 20th century

An askari was a local soldier serving in the armies of European colonial powers in Africa, particularly in the great colonial regiments raised during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The word comes from the Arabic ʿaskarī (soldier) and passed into Swahili, Somali, and numerous European military vocabularies.

German askaris

The term is most closely associated with the Schutztruppe of German East Africa, where askaris — recruited mainly from Sudanese, Swahili-speaking and other local peoples, and officered by Germans and local NCOs — formed the backbone of the colony's defence. During the First World War, General Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck's force of a few thousand askaris and carriers tied down vastly larger Allied forces in a celebrated guerrilla campaign that lasted until after the Armistice in November 1918. The loyalty and endurance of the East African askaris became central to the campaign's legend.

Other colonial forces

Comparable troops served across the continent: the King's African Rifles and West African Frontier Force in British service, the tirailleurs in French colonies, Belgian Force Publique askaris in the Congo, and Italian askaris recruited in Eritrea, Somalia and Libya, who fought in the Italo-Ethiopian wars. Askaris also served in the Middle East and in Ottoman service.

Legacy

After the colonial period, many askaris found their pensions and status contested; Germany made final pension payments to surviving East African veterans in the 1960s. The word survives in East Africa as a general term for a guard, watchman or policeman — in Swahili, askari commonly means a security guard — and in place and memorial names such as the Askari Monument in Dar es Salaam.
