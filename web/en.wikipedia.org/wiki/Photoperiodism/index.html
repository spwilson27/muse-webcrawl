Photoperiodism

Photoperiodism is the physiological reaction of organisms to the length of light and darkness, or day and night. It occurs in plants and animals and governs processes such as flowering, growth, and reproduction in response to changes in day length over the seasons.

Overview

Many flowering plants use a photoreceptor protein, such as phytochrome or cryptochrome, to sense seasonal changes in night length, or photoperiod, which they take as signals to flower. Plants are conventionally divided into short-day plants, which flower when nights exceed a critical length; long-day plants, which flower when nights are shorter than a critical length; and day-neutral plants, whose flowering is not governed by photoperiod.

Discovery

Photoperiodism was first described in 1920 by Wightman Garner and Harry Allard of the United States Department of Agriculture, who showed that the flowering of the Maryland Mammoth tobacco mutant and of Biloxi soybeans was controlled by the relative length of day and night rather than by temperature or nutrition.

In animals

In animals, photoperiod influences seasonal breeding, migration, hibernation, and changes in coat colour and thickness. The hormone melatonin, secreted by the pineal gland during darkness, acts as a physiological signal of night length in many vertebrates.
