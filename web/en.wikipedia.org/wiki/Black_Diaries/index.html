The Black Diaries are a set of diaries purportedly written by the Irish nationalist and former British consular official Roger Casement, covering periods in 1903 and 1910–1911. The diaries contain explicit accounts of homosexual encounters and were used by the British government in 1916 to undermine appeals for clemency after Casement was convicted of high treason for his role in seeking German support for Irish independence during the First World War. Casement was hanged at Pentonville Prison on 3 August 1916.

Background

Casement had won an international reputation for his consular reports exposing atrocities in the Congo Free State (1904) and against the Putumayo Indians in Peru (1910–1911). After his arrest in April 1916, following the failed landing of German arms at Banna Strand, British officials circulated selected extracts from diaries said to have been found among his papers. The material was shown discreetly to journalists, politicians, and church figures who might otherwise have campaigned for a reprieve.

Authenticity debate

The authenticity of the diaries has been contested for over a century. Supporters of Casement long argued that the diaries were forged by British intelligence, pointing to discrepancies in handwriting and the circumstances of their discovery. Others maintained they were genuine. A forensic examination commissioned in 2002, led by handwriting expert Audrey Giles, concluded that the diaries were written by Casement. The debate continues among historians, with figures such as W. J. McCormack arguing for forgery and others accepting the Giles findings.

The term "Black Diaries" distinguishes them from the so-called "White Diaries," Casement's official journals of his consular investigations, which document the abuses he exposed in the Congo and Peru.
