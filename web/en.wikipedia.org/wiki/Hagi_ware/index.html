Hagi ware

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hagi ware (萩焼, Hagi-yaki) is a type of Japanese pottery traditionally produced in the town of Hagi in Yamaguchi Prefecture. One of Japan's most respected tea wares, it is prized for its soft, earthy colours, rough texture and understated beauty closely aligned with the wabi-sabi aesthetic of the tea ceremony. A traditional saying ranks the schools of tea pottery: "First Raku, second Hagi, third Karatsu".

History

Hagi ware began after the Japanese invasions of Korea in the 1590s, when the Mōri clan brought captured Korean potters — notably the brothers Yi Jak-kwang (Lee Sukkwang) and Yi Gyeong — to Hagi and established kilns under clan patronage. Their Korean stoneware techniques gave rise to a ware originally fired as hard stoneware, which over the Edo period softened in body and palette into the characteristic Hagi style.

Characteristics

Hagi ware is made from a porous, coarse clay fired at relatively low temperatures (around 1180–1220 °C), yielding a soft, slightly absorbent body under a feldspathic glaze that ranges from milky white and cream to soft pink, orange and grey, often over a crackle network. Because the clay is not fully vitrified, a Hagi tea bowl subtly changes with use — tea seeps into the fine cracks and stains the surface, a celebrated transformation known as nanabake, "the seven transformations". Many tea bowls have a characteristic notch (kiri-kōdai) cut out of the foot ring, legendarily to let commoners sell wares otherwise reserved for the lord, or to allow bowls to be stacked.

The most famous variety is Oni-Hagi ("demon Hagi"), with bold, rough surfaces and dramatic white glaze flowing over black. Modern Hagi potters continue the tradition in numerous kilns around the castle town.
