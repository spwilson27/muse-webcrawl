Roadside attraction

Roadside attraction
Type: Tourist attraction
Origin: United States, early 20th century
Common forms: Novelty architecture, giant statues, museums, natural curiosities

A roadside attraction is a feature along the side of a road built or promoted to draw travellers, usually motorists, to stop, visit, and spend money. Roadside attractions became common in the United States and Canada with the spread of automobile travel in the early 20th century, flourishing along highways such as U.S. Route 66. They range from natural features and historic sites to frankly commercial novelties designed to catch the eye of passing drivers.

Types

Common types include novelty architecture (buildings shaped like the products they sell, a style sometimes called mimetic architecture), oversized statues and "world's largest" objects — such as giant balls of twine, oversized animals, and enormous furniture — animal farms and alligator parks, mystery spots and gravity hills where optical illusions occur, reptile gardens, fossil beds, and small museums devoted to eccentric subjects. In Australia, similar roadside landmarks are known collectively as "big things".

History

Roadside attractions grew with the American highway system in the 1920s and 1930s, when businesses competed for the attention of motorists travelling long distances. Entrepreneurs built eye-catching structures — tepee-shaped motels, dinosaur statues, and donut-shaped donut shops. The construction of the Interstate Highway System from the 1950s, which bypassed many small towns and older two-lane highways, caused many roadside attractions to decline or close, though some survived as nostalgic heritage sites, and the preservation of Route 66 brought renewed interest.

Cultural significance

Roadside attractions are regarded as a distinctive part of American folk culture and vernacular architecture, celebrated in photography, song, and travel writing. Many of the most famous, such as Wall Drug in South Dakota, the Corn Palace in Mitchell, South Dakota, and the Cadillac Ranch in Texas, remain popular tourist stops.
