The Siege of Ostend (1601–1604) was one of the longest and bloodiest sieges of the Eighty Years' War. Ostend, on the Flemish coast, was the last stronghold of the Dutch Republic in the southern Netherlands, supplied by sea and garrisoned by Dutch, English, Scottish and French troops. The Habsburg Army of Flanders under Archduke Albert invested the town in July 1601; it did not fall until September 1604, by which time the town was a ruin and the combined death toll was reckoned in the tens of thousands. Contemporaries called it the "great carnival of death".

Background

Ostend's garrison raided the Flemish countryside and threatened Spanish shipping. Archduke Albert, sovereign of the Spanish Netherlands with his wife Isabella, resolved to eliminate the enclave and arrived before the town on 5 July 1601 with some 12,000–20,000 men. The first Dutch-English commander, Sir Francis Vere, a celebrated veteran, feigned negotiations to gain time for reinforcements—the celebrated "Vere's ruse"—and held the opening assaults.

The siege

Because the sea gave the defenders an inexhaustible supply line, the siege became an attritional engineering duel. The Spanish built vast earthworks, dams and galleries; the defenders countered with new outworks, inundations and mines. A great Spanish assault in January 1602 was repulsed with heavy loss. Command of the garrison changed hands several times as governors were killed or superseded. In October 1603 the Genoese banker-general Ambrogio Spinola took command of the besiegers with fresh money and troops and prosecuted the siege methodically, contracting the perimeter redoubt by redoubt. The States General meanwhile ordered Maurice of Nassau to invade Flanders, but his failure at Sluis could not save Ostend; Sluis itself fell to Maurice in August 1604 in compensation.

Fall and aftermath

By September 1604 the surviving garrison under Daniel de Hertaing held only a fragment of the shattered old town, the rest having been eaten away by assaults and the sea. On 20 September terms were agreed; the garrison marched out with honours on 22 September and was transported to Dutch territory. Spain had won the ground but at ruinous cost in men and money, and the conquest of an empty ruin contributed to the exhaustion that led to the Twelve Years' Truce of 1609. Spinola's reputation, made at Ostend, carried him to command in Germany in the Thirty Years' War.
