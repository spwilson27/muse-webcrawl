The Monadology (French: La Monadologie) is one of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's best known works of his later philosophy. It is a short text which presents, in roughly ninety numbered paragraphs, a metaphysics of simple substances, or monads. Leibniz wrote the work in 1714 while living in Vienna; it was not published in French during his lifetime, and first appeared in a German translation in 1720.

Summary

The treatise opens with the definition of the monad as a simple substance, without parts, which enters into compounds. Because monads have no parts, they cannot be formed or destroyed by natural means; they can only begin by creation and end by annihilation. Monads have no "windows" through which anything could enter or depart (§7); they do not interact causally with one another. Each monad, however, mirrors the entire universe from its own point of view, and differs from every other monad in the distinctness of its perceptions.

The internal principle of change in a monad is called appetition, the striving by which a monad passes from one perception to another. Leibniz distinguishes bare monads, which have unconscious perceptions; souls, which have distinct perception accompanied by memory; and rational spirits, capable of apperception and knowledge of eternal truths. God is the primitive monad, the necessary being whose existence guarantees the harmony among all others.

Pre-established harmony

Since monads cannot influence each other, the agreement between the states of the soul and the body, and indeed among all substances, is explained by the doctrine of pre-established harmony: God created each monad with an internal programme such that its states correspond exactly with those of all the others, like clocks perfectly synchronised at creation.

The best of all possible worlds

Leibniz argues from the principle of sufficient reason that God, choosing among an infinity of possible worlds, necessarily created the best of all possible worlds — the one combining the greatest variety of phenomena with the greatest simplicity of laws. Apparent evils are permitted as necessary concomitants of this optimal whole.

Influence

The Monadology profoundly influenced later German philosophy, was popularised and later parodied — Voltaire satirised its optimism in Candide — and remains a central text in the study of rationalist metaphysics.
