Parkinson's disease dementia

Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) is a dementia that develops in the course of established Parkinson's disease. By convention, dementia arising at least a year after the onset of parkinsonian motor symptoms is classified as PDD, whereas dementia preceding or coinciding with motor symptoms suggests dementia with Lewy bodies — the so-called one-year rule.

Features

PDD typically impairs executive function, attention and visuospatial ability more than memory in its early stages, and is often accompanied by apathy, depression, hallucinations and fluctuating alertness. The underlying pathology involves Lewy bodies — aggregates of alpha-synuclein — spreading into cortical regions.

Epidemiology and course

A substantial proportion of people with Parkinson's disease develop dementia over the long term, with prevalence rising with age and disease duration. PDD greatly increases care needs and is a major determinant of nursing-home placement.

Management

Cholinesterase inhibitors such as rivastigmine may modestly improve cognition. Care also involves managing neuropsychiatric symptoms, avoiding drugs that worsen confusion, and supporting carers.
