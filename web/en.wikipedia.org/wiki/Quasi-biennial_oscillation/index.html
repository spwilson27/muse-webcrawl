Quasi-biennial oscillation

Monthly equatorial stratospheric winds: easterly and westerly regimes descend over time.

The quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO) is a quasiperiodic oscillation of the equatorial zonal wind between strong easterlies and westerlies in the tropical stratosphere, with a mean period of about 28 to 29 months. The alternating wind regimes develop at the top of the lower stratosphere and propagate downwards at roughly one kilometre per month until they dissipate at the tropical tropopause. The QBO was discovered in radiosonde data around 1960 by Richard Reed, Graeme Ebdon, and others, and its mechanism was explained in 1968–1970 by Richard Lindzen, James Holton, and Jule Charney's contemporaries: the oscillation is driven by atmospheric waves (Kelvin and mixed Rossby–gravity waves) propagating up from the troposphere.

Effects

The QBO modulates the stratospheric circulation far beyond the tropics. Its phase influences the strength of the winter polar vortex and the probability of sudden stratospheric warmings, the distribution of stratospheric ozone, the transport of volcanic aerosols, and — through downward coupling — patterns of surface weather including the North Atlantic Oscillation. Statistical studies also link the QBO to tropical cyclone activity and to the Madden–Julian oscillation. A secondary circulation induced by the QBO redistributes trace gases such as ozone, methane, and water vapour, producing observable oscillations in their concentrations.

2016 and 2020 disruptions

In 2016, for the first time in observations reaching back to 1953, the QBO's regular cycle was disrupted: an anomalous band of westerlies formed within the easterly phase, attributed to unusually strong wave forcing linked to the 2015–16 El Niño. A second disruption occurred in 2019–2020. These events have prompted research into how climate change may affect the oscillation.
