Mortgage insurance

Mortgage insurance (also known as mortgage guarantee and home-loan insurance) is an insurance policy that compensates lenders or investors in mortgage securities for losses due to the default of a mortgage loan. Mortgage insurance can be either public (government-provided) or private, and is a form of credit enhancement that shifts part of the lender's credit risk to an insurer. It is distinct from life or property insurance products that may share similar names.

How it works

Mortgage insurance is typically required when a borrower makes a down payment of less than 20% of the home's purchase price — that is, when the loan-to-value ratio exceeds 80%. The insurer guarantees a portion of the loan balance (often 20–30%), reducing the lender's loss if the borrower defaults and the property sells for less than the amount owed. Premiums may be paid by the borrower as a monthly charge, as a single up-front premium, or by the lender (in which case the cost is usually recovered through a higher interest rate).

United States

In the United States, two main systems exist:

  • Government insurance: The Federal Housing Administration (FHA), created in 1934, insures loans through its Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund, charging an up-front mortgage insurance premium (UFMIP) plus annual premiums. The Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and the Department of Agriculture (USDA) run related guarantee programmes.
  • Private mortgage insurance (PMI): Provided by private companies for conventional (non-government) loans. Under the Homeowners Protection Act of 1998, borrowers may request cancellation of PMI once the loan-to-value ratio falls to 80%, and PMI must terminate automatically at 78% LTV based on the original amortisation schedule.

Other countries

In Canada, mortgage default insurance from the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC) or private insurers is compulsory for mortgages with down payments under 20%. In Australia, lenders mortgage insurance (LMI) likewise protects the lender, with the premium usually borne by the borrower on high-LTV loans. Historically, mortgage insurance in several countries contracted sharply after the 2007–2008 financial crisis, when losses forced government rescues or conservatorship of major insurers.

Criticism

Critics observe that mortgage insurance protects the lender, not the borrower, who nonetheless usually pays for it, and that it can encourage lax underwriting by dulling lenders' incentives. Supporters counter that it expands access to home ownership for borrowers with small deposits and stabilises housing finance by standardising underwriting.
