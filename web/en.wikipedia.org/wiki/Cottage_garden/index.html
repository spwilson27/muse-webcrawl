Cottage garden

The cottage garden is a distinct style of garden that uses an informal design, dense plantings and a mixture of ornamental and edible plants. It originated in the modest gardens of British rural labourers, where flowers were grown alongside vegetables, herbs and fruit for household use.

Characteristics

Cottage gardens favour traditional materials, gravel or brick paths, and an abundant, seemingly artless profusion of hardy perennials and self-seeding annuals: roses, hollyhocks, foxgloves, lavender, delphiniums and sweet peas, often spilling over low fences and around the cottage door. Planting is dense and layered rather than arranged in formal beds.

History

Once a purely practical working-class plot, the style was romanticised in the Victorian era and championed by gardeners such as William Robinson and Gertrude Jekyll, whose informal, painterly planting influenced garden design worldwide. Artists' gardens, such as Monet's at Giverny, share its spirit. The style was revived again in the late 20th century as gardeners sought wildlife-friendly, chemical-free gardens.
