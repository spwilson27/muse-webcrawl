Feminist epistemology

Feminist epistemology is the branch of feminist philosophy and philosophy of science that studies how gender and social location affect concepts of knowledge, the knower, and practices of inquiry and justification. Emerging from the 1970s and 1980s, it challenges ideals of a view-from-nowhere objectivity, arguing that knowledge is socially situated.

Main approaches

Lorraine Code's taxonomy distinguishes three broad strands. Feminist empiricism holds that sexism and androcentric bias are correctable failures of scientific method itself: applying norms of evidence and reasoning properly will expose them. Feminist standpoint theory, drawing on Marxian ideas and developed by thinkers such as Nancy Hartsock, Sandra Harding and Patricia Hill Collins, argues that marginalised social positions can afford epistemic advantage — a standpoint achieved through critical reflection gives less partial views of social relations. Feminist postmodernism, associated with Donna Haraway's "situated knowledges", rejects any single standpoint and emphasises plural, partial perspectives.

Key concepts

Central notions include Harding's "strong objectivity" (maximising objectivity by critically examining the social situation of inquiry itself), Helen Longino's contextual empiricism (knowledge as validated through critical discursive interaction within diverse communities), Miranda Fricker's concept of epistemic injustice — testimonial and hermeneutical wrongs done to people in their capacity as knowers — and critiques of the ideal of the autonomous, disembodied knower.

Criticism and influence

Critics have worried that standpoint and postmodern approaches risk relativism or essentialism about "women's ways of knowing"; defenders reply that the approaches aim at better, not abandoned, standards of justification. Feminist epistemology has influenced science studies, social epistemology, and debates over diversity in research communities.
