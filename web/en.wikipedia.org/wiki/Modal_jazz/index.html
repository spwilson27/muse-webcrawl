Modal jazz

Miles Davis, whose Kind of Blue (1959) is the landmark modal jazz album

Modal jazz is a style of jazz that uses musical modes rather than chord progressions as the harmonic framework for improvisation. Where bebop and hard bop pieces move through rapid sequences of chords, modal compositions typically rest on one or two scales (modes) for extended periods, giving soloists greater melodic freedom and creating a spacious, meditative character.

Origins and key recordings

The approach was theorised by pianist George Russell in his 1953 treatise Lydian Chromatic Concept of Tonal Organization. Miles Davis, who had worked with Russell's ideas, applied them on the title track of Milestones (1958) and on Kind of Blue (1959), whose pieces — "So What", "Flamenco Sketches" and others — are built on Dorian and other modes with minimal chord changes. Pianist Bill Evans was central to shaping the album's harmonic language.

John Coltrane took modal jazz further in the 1960s with recordings such as My Favorite Things and A Love Supreme, combining static modal vamps with intense, searching improvisation. Other important modal works include Herbie Hancock's "Maiden Voyage" and McCoy Tyner's recordings with Coltrane's quartet.

Influence

Modal techniques fed into jazz fusion, and the modal vamp became a staple of funk, rock and later electronic music. The loose, scale-based approach also influenced minimalism and much subsequent improvisational practice.
