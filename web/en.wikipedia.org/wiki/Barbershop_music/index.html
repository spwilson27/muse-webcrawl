Typical forcesFour voices: tenor, lead, baritone, bass

Barbershop music is a style of unaccompanied vocal harmony, usually sung by four parts — tenor, lead, baritone and bass — characterized by consonant four-part chords for every melody note (so-called "barbershop seventh" chords), a strong homophonic texture and an emphasis on "ringing" chords whose overtones reinforce one another. It is performed by quartets and choruses, both amateur and professional.

History

The style grew out of late 19th- and early 20th-century American popular song, vaudeville and African-American singing traditions. A revival movement in 1938 led to the founding of the Society for the Preservation and Encouragement of Barber Shop Quartet Singing in America (now the Barbershop Harmony Society), followed by organizations for women such as Sweet Adelines International (1945) and Harmony, Incorporated.

Characteristics

A characteristic feature of barbershop arrangement is the frequent use of dominant seventh and major seventh sonorities and close-position voicing designed to maximize sympathetic overtones. Songs typically use simple, emotionally direct lyrics from the Tin Pan Alley era and are often performed with choreographed visual presentation in contests judged on music, performance and singing.
