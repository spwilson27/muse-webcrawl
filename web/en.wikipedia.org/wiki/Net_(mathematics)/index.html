Net (mathematics)

In mathematics, more specifically in general topology and related branches, a net or Moore–Smith sequence is a generalisation of the notion of a sequence. Whereas a sequence is a function from the natural numbers, a net is a function from any directed set. Nets allow the concepts of convergence, limits, and continuity to be extended from metric spaces to arbitrary topological spaces, where sequences are insufficient to describe the topology.

Definition

A directed set is a set A equipped with a preorder ≤ such that every two elements have an upper bound. A net in a set X is a function from a directed set A to X. A net x• in a topological space converges to a point x if, for every neighbourhood U of x, the net is eventually in U: there exists an index a such that xb ∈ U for all b ≥ a.

History and motivation

Nets were introduced by E. H. Moore and H. L. Smith in 1922. They resolve a fundamental inadequacy of sequences: in a general topological space, a point can lie in the closure of a set, or a function can fail to be continuous at a point, without any sequence witnessing it — this holds only in first-countable spaces. Nets (and the related notion of filters, developed by Henri Cartan) restore the sequential characterisations: a set is closed if and only if it contains the limits of all convergent nets in it, and compactness can be characterised by the statement that every net has a convergent subnet.

Subnets and cluster points

The correct notion of subnet is subtler than for subsequences; the definition by Willard or Kelley ensures the fundamental theorem that a space is compact if and only if every net has a convergent subnet, paralleling the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem. The still more general concept of an ultranet corresponds to an ultrafilter and converges in every compact Hausdorff space containing its range.
