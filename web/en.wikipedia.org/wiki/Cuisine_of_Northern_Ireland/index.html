Cuisine of Northern Ireland


The Ulster fry, the best-known dish of Northern Ireland

The cuisine of Northern Ireland shares much with the wider Irish cuisine tradition, built around potatoes, breads, pork, beef and dairy, but has distinctive regional specialities of its own, most famously the Ulster fry. In recent decades Belfast and Derry have developed notable restaurant scenes, and local produce such as Lough Neagh eels, Comber potatoes and Armagh apples are celebrated.

Traditional dishes

  • Ulster fry — a cooked breakfast of bacon, eggs, sausages, black and white pudding, tomatoes and mushrooms, distinguished by the inclusion of soda bread and potato bread (fadge) fried alongside the other items.
  • Soda bread — a quick bread leavened with bicarbonate of soda, traditionally cooked on a griddle and cut into farls.
  • Potato bread (fadge) — flat cakes of mashed potato and flour, grilled or fried.
  • Champ — mashed potatoes with scallions (spring onions), butter and milk.
  • Pastie — a battered, deep-fried patty of minced pork, onion and potato, sold in fish and chip shops.
  • Vegetable roll — seasoned sliced beef formed into a roll with vegetables, sliced and fried.
  • Dulse — dried red seaweed eaten as a snack, notably sold at the Ould Lammas Fair in Ballycastle.
  • Yellowman — a chewy honeycomb toffee traditionally sold at fairs.

Produce and drink

Northern Ireland is known for grass-fed beef and lamb, pork products, and seafood including Strangford Lough oysters and Lough Neagh eels and pollan. The Old Bushmills Distillery in County Antrim produces Irish whiskey, and local brewing and craft cider (from County Armagh, the "Orchard County") have revived in recent decades. Traybakes — tray-baked sweet slices such as fifteens — are a staple of home baking and church and community gatherings.
