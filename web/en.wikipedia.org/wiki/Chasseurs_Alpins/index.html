The Chasseurs Alpins are the elite mountain infantry of the French Army. Trained for combat in mountainous and extreme terrain and in severe weather, they are recognisable by their wide dark-blue beret, nicknamed the tarte ("pie"), and by their distinctive dark-blue dress uniform.

History

France created its first mountain troops in 1888, converting twelve battalions of chasseurs à pied into bataillons alpins de chasseurs à pied to counter the Italian Alpini, who guarded the Alpine frontier between the two countries. The units were stationed in the Alps and the Vosges and trained in skiing, climbing and high-altitude bivouacking.

During the First World War the Chasseurs Alpins fought with particular distinction in the Vosges mountains, in battles such as Hartmannswillerkopf, earning from the Germans the nickname Schwarze Teufel ("Black Devils"), which in French became les diables bleus. In the Second World War they fought in Norway in 1940, in the defence of the Alps against Italy, and later with the Free French forces in the liberation of France and the advance into Austria.

Modern organisation

Today the mountain troops are grouped principally in the 27th Mountain Infantry Brigade (27e brigade d'infanterie de montagne), headquartered at Varces near Grenoble. It includes the 7th, 13th and 27th battalions of Chasseurs Alpins, the 93rd Mountain Artillery Regiment and the 2nd Foreign Engineer Regiment, among other units. Chasseurs Alpins train at the High Mountain Military School (École militaire de haute montagne) at Chamonix and specialise in winter warfare, ski-mounted operations and combat at altitude.

Traditions

The Chasseurs' identity is marked by strong traditions: the oversized beret, the hunting horn (cor de chasse) insignia, the fanfare bands, the patron saint Saint Bernard of Menthon, and the annual gathering at the Sidi-Brahim commemoration. Their motto is associated with the cry "Vivre libre ou mourir" and the famous order of the chasseurs at Sidi Brahim in 1845.
