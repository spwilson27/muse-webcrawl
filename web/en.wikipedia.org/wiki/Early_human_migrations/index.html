Early human migrations

Early human migrations are the earliest expansions of hominins and modern humans across the continents. Beginning with Homo erectus roughly two million years ago, successive waves of hominins left Africa and settled Eurasia; much later, anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) spread worldwide, reaching Australia by about 65,000 years ago and the Americas by at least 15,000–20,000 years ago.

Routes of the major dispersals of modern humans out of Africa.

Early hominin expansions

Homo erectus was the first hominin to leave Africa, reaching the Caucasus (Dmanisi, Georgia) by about 1.8 million years ago and Java and China soon after. Later, the ancestors of Neanderthals and Denisovans moved into Eurasia and evolved there in relative isolation, while other archaic populations spread across Africa.

The Out of Africa expansion of modern humans

Homo sapiens evolved in Africa by about 300,000 years ago. There were several early excursions into the Levant and Arabia—fossils at Misliya and Apidima date to roughly 180,000–210,000 years ago—but the main successful dispersal occurred around 60,000–70,000 years ago. Descendants of this population spread rapidly along the coasts of South Asia to Southeast Asia and Australia, and into Europe by about 45,000 years ago, interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans along the way.

Peopling of the Americas and the Pacific

Humans entered the Americas from Siberia via Beringia, probably by a coastal route, and spread throughout both continents by the end of the last Ice Age. The final great chapter was the Austronesian expansion, which carried seafaring settlers across the Pacific, reaching even remote islands such as Hawai'i, Rapa Nui and New Zealand within the last few thousand years.
