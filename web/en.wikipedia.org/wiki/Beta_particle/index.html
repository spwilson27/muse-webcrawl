A beta particle, also called a beta ray or beta radiation (symbol β), is a high-energy, high-speed electron (β−) or positron (β+) emitted by atomic nuclei during beta decay, a type of radioactive decay.

Beta decay

In β− decay, a neutron in the nucleus converts into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino; the daughter nucleus has an atomic number one higher. In β+ decay, a proton converts into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron neutrino. Because the decay shares energy with the nearly undetectable neutrino, beta particles emerge with a continuous range of energies up to a maximum — a puzzle resolved by Wolfgang Pauli's neutrino hypothesis in 1930. Henri Becquerel's experiments of 1896 and Ernest Rutherford's subsequent classification separated radioactive emissions into alpha, beta and gamma rays; J. J. Thomson showed beta particles were identical to electrons.

Properties and safety

Beta particles are ionising radiation of intermediate penetrating power: they travel a few metres in air and are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium or plastic, though they can burn skin and are hazardous inside the body. Their emission is exploited in radiocarbon dating (which detects β− decay of carbon-14), in nuclear medicine — positron emission tomography (PET) images the annihilation radiation from β+ emitters — and in industrial thickness gauges. When energetic beta particles decelerate in dense material they produce secondary X-rays (bremsstrahlung), so shielding is normally made from low-density materials.
