Hayyim ben Joseph Vital (Hebrew: חיים ויטאל; 1542–1620) was a rabbi and kabbalist in Safed, Ottoman Galilee, and the foremost disciple of Isaac Luria (known as the Ari), the founder of modern Kabbalah. Vital's writings, above all the Etz Hayyim ("Tree of Life"), are the principal source for Lurianic Kabbalah and the channel through which Luria's teachings spread throughout the Jewish world.

Life

Born in Safed to a family of Spanish exile origin, Vital studied Talmud and Kabbalah, including under Moses Cordovero. After Cordovero's death in 1570, Vital became the leading student of Isaac Luria, who arrived in Safed that year; in the roughly twenty months before Luria's death in 1572, Vital recorded his master's oral teachings on the Zohar, creation, the sefirot, tzimtzum (divine contraction), and the "shattering of the vessels," as well as techniques of mystical prayer and soul-diagnosis.

Writings

Vital guarded his manuscripts jealously, and they circulated slowly; portions were copied by his son Samuel and arranged into the "Eight Gates" (Shemonah She'arim), covering topics from the Gate of Verses to the Gate of Reincarnations (Sha'ar HaGilgulim). The Etz Hayyim, printed in the 18th century, expounds Lurianic cosmology. Vital also wrote Sefer HaHezyonot ("Book of Visions"), a remarkable autobiographical diary recording his dreams, aspirations, and messianic ambitions, including his belief that he himself might be the Messiah ben Joseph.

Later life and influence

Vital served as a rabbi in Jerusalem (1577–1585) and later settled in Damascus, where he headed a community until his death in 1620. His redaction of Luria's teachings became the authoritative version of Lurianic Kabbalah in Europe, shaping Hasidism and Jewish mysticism generally.
