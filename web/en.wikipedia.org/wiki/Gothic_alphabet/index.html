The Gothic alphabet is the script used to write the Gothic language, an extinct East Germanic language. It was devised in the fourth century AD, traditionally credited to Bishop Ulfilas (Wulfila), who created it in order to translate the Bible into Gothic for the Christianisation of the Goths.

Origins

The alphabet consists of 27 letters, most of which were adapted from Greek uncial forms, supplemented by characters drawn from Latin and possibly runic sources for sounds Greek lacked. Like Greek, the letters also served as numerals, each letter carrying an assigned numeric value.

Surviving texts

The principal surviving corpus is Ulfilas's Bible translation, best preserved in the Codex Argenteus, written in silver and gold ink on purple parchment, now held in Uppsala, Sweden. Other fragments include the Codex Carolinus and the Skeireins, a commentary on the Gospel of John. The script fell out of use as the Gothic kingdoms disappeared in the early medieval period.

The Gothic alphabet is included in the Unicode standard in the block U+10330–U+1034F.
