Total institution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A total institution is a place of residence and work where a large number of like-situated individuals, cut off from the wider society for an appreciable period of time, together lead an enclosed, formally administered round of life. The term was popularised by the sociologist Erving Goffman in his 1961 book Asylums: Essays on the Social Situation of Mental Patients and Other Inmates.

Characteristics

Goffman identified several defining features. All aspects of life — sleeping, working, and recreation — are conducted in the same place and under a single authority. Activities are carried on in the immediate company of a large batch of others, all treated alike and required to do the same thing together. Each phase of the day is tightly scheduled, and the various enforced activities are brought together into a single rational plan purportedly designed to fulfil the official aims of the institution.

Examples

Goffman grouped total institutions into five rough categories: institutions caring for persons incapable of self-care (orphanages, nursing homes); places for persons who are a threat to society though unintentionally so (mental hospitals, tuberculosis sanitaria); institutions organised to protect the community against intentional dangers (prisons, prisoner-of-war camps); institutions established to pursue a work-like task (army barracks, boarding schools, ships); and retreats from the world (monasteries, convents, abbeys).

Mortification of the self

On entry, inmates typically undergo what Goffman called the "mortification of the self": admission procedures such as being stripped, searched, photographed, and issued standard clothing strip away the individual's previous identity and supports for the self. In response, inmates develop an "underlife" of secondary adjustments — unofficial practices that get around the organisation's assumptions about what the inmate should be.
