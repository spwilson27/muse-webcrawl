Extrapyramidal system

In anatomy, the extrapyramidal system is a part of the motor system of the brain involved in the refinement and modulation of movement. It is distinguished from the pyramidal system, whose fibres pass through the pyramids of the medulla. The extrapyramidal system consists of a network of nuclei and pathways, principally the basal ganglia and their connections with the brainstem, including the rubrospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal and tectospinal tracts.

Function

Whereas the pyramidal (corticospinal) system is chiefly responsible for direct voluntary control of movement, especially of the distal limbs, the extrapyramidal system regulates posture, muscle tone, and the automatic, background adjustments that make voluntary movement smooth. It is heavily involved in starting, stopping and scaling movements, and in procedural motor learning.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the extrapyramidal system produces movement disorders: hypokinetic disorders such as the rigidity and bradykinesia of Parkinson's disease, and hyperkinetic disorders such as chorea in Huntington's disease, dystonia and tremor. The term is also familiar in pharmacology: "extrapyramidal symptoms" (EPS) are drug-induced movement abnormalities — including acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism and tardive dyskinesia — classically associated with antipsychotic drugs that block dopamine receptors.
