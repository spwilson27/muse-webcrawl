Arabization (Arabic: taʿrīb) is the process by which non-Arab populations adopt the Arabic language and Arab culture, or by which Arabic influence expands in a society. It describes both the gradual historical Arabization of the Levant, Egypt, Mesopotamia and North Africa after the early Islamic conquests, and deliberate modern government policies promoting Arabic.

Historical Arabization

Following the 7th-century conquests, Arabic spread first as the language of administration — notably under the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik, who made it the official chancery language — and over centuries became the mother tongue of most inhabitants, displacing Aramaic, Coptic, Greek and Berber languages in many areas, a process intertwined with Islamization and intermarriage.

Modern policies

In the 20th century, Arab nationalist governments in countries such as Algeria, Sudan, Iraq and Syria pursued Arabization of education and administration, policies sometimes criticised for marginalising Kurdish, Berber (Amazigh) and other minority languages and identities.
