Staircase lock

A staircase lock is a flight of canal locks in which the gates of one chamber serve also as the gates of the next, so that the chambers are arranged directly one above another with no intervening pound. A boat passes from one chamber straight into the next, ascending or descending "stairs" of water. The arrangement saves land and construction cost where a steep rise must be climbed, but it is slow and wasteful of water, and boats travelling in opposite directions cannot pass each other within the flight.

Operation

In a conventional flight of locks, each chamber is separated by a short reach of canal, allowing slack water in which levels can be adjusted. In a staircase, the upper gates of a chamber are also the lower gates of the chamber above; opening them puts the boat directly into the next chamber. Going up, each chamber is filled in turn from the one above; going down, the chambers are emptied in sequence. Careful sequencing is essential, and most staircases are worked by lock keepers or experienced crews following posted rules.

Examples

Famous examples in Britain include the Bingley Five Rise on the Leeds and Liverpool Canal, which lifts boats 18 metres through five chambers and opened in 1774; the Foxton Locks on the Grand Union Canal in Leicestershire, two staircases of five separated by a passing pound; the Caen Hill flight at Devizes on the Kennet and Avon Canal, whose main flight of 16 locks forms two staircases; and the Neptune's Staircase at Banavie on the Caledonian Canal in Scotland, a staircase of eight built by Thomas Telford. The lock staircases of the Canal du Midi in France, such as the Fonserannes locks near Béziers, are among the oldest.
