Ocean heat content

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ocean heat content (OHC) is the total thermal energy stored in the world's oceans, usually computed as the integral of temperature anomalies over a layer of the ocean (commonly the upper 700 m or 2,000 m) multiplied by the density and specific heat capacity of seawater. Because the ocean has absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat trapped by human-caused greenhouse gas emissions since the 1970s, ocean heat content is one of the clearest indicators of global warming and of Earth's energy imbalance.

Measurement

Historically, ocean temperature was measured by ships using buckets, engine-intake sensors, bathythermographs and research-vessel profiles. Since the early 2000s the Argo programme of autonomous profiling floats has provided near-global coverage of the upper 2,000 m, greatly improving estimates. Earlier records require corrections for systematic biases, notably in expendable bathythermograph (XBT) fall-rate errors.

Trends

Analyses by agencies such as NOAA and research groups show a sustained increase in ocean heat content since the mid-twentieth century, accelerating in recent decades, with successive record highs in the 2010s and 2020s. The warming is observed in all major ocean basins, with the Southern Ocean and Atlantic accounting for a large share of the heat uptake. Increased ocean heat content drives thermal expansion of seawater — a major contributor to sea level rise — and intensifies marine heatwaves, coral bleaching and the energy available to tropical storms.

Earth's energy budget

Ocean heat uptake is the dominant term in Earth's energy inventory: inventories attribute roughly 90 percent of accumulated energy to the oceans, with the remainder warming the land, melting ice and heating the atmosphere. The rate of ocean warming therefore provides an independent estimate of the planetary energy imbalance measured by satellites.
