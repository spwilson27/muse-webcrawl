Mediation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mediation is a structured, interactive process where an impartial third party, the mediator, assists disputing parties in resolving conflict through specialised communication and negotiation techniques. Unlike arbitration or litigation, mediation is non-binding unless the parties reach and formalise a settlement; the mediator has no power to impose an outcome. All participants are encouraged to actively participate, and the process is usually confidential.

Use

Mediation is used in family disputes (its largest modern field, especially divorce and custody), commercial contracts, workplace and community conflicts, environmental and land disputes, and international peace processes — where states, the UN and regional bodies mediate between governments and armed groups (with famous successes from Camp David's follow-on diplomacy to Mozambique and Colombia negotiations, and many failures). Court-annexed mediation is mandatory or default in many jurisdictions as a cheaper, faster alternative that preserves relationships; enforceability of international commercial settlements was strengthened by the Singapore Convention (2019).

Styles and debate

Practitioners distinguish facilitative, evaluative and transformative approaches; debates concern power imbalances (mediation between unequal parties), mediator neutrality in rights cases, and the privatisation of justice — balanced against consistently high settlement rates and party satisfaction in evaluations.
