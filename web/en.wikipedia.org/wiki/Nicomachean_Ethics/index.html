The Nicomachean Ethics is the name normally given to Aristotle's best-known work on ethics — ten books based on his lectures at the Lyceum in Athens. The title probably derives from its dedication to, or editing by, Aristotle's son Nicomachus. Together with the Eudemian Ethics and the Politics, it forms the core of Aristotle's practical philosophy and is regarded as one of the most important works in the history of moral philosophy.

Summary

Aristotle argues that every action aims at some good, and that the highest human good is eudaimonia — flourishing or living well — achieved by exercising the rational part of the soul excellently over a complete life. Virtue of character arises from habit: a virtue such as courage is a settled disposition lying in a mean between two vices, excess and deficiency (recklessness and cowardice), determined by practical wisdom (phronesis). The work also contains celebrated discussions of pleasure, friendship (philia), justice, weakness of will (akrasia), and the contemplative life, which the final book presents as the highest form of happiness.

Influence

Preserved through Hellenistic, Islamic and medieval Christian scholarship, the Ethics was a central text of the medieval university curriculum and profoundly shaped Thomas Aquinas. In the twentieth century it became the founding text of revived virtue ethics, especially after G. E. M. Anscombe's 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy".
