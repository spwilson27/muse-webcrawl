Unilateral declaration of independence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A unilateral declaration of independence (UDI) is a formal proclamation by a territory or political movement asserting its independence and statehood without the consent of the sovereign state from which it is separating, and without a negotiated settlement. The term came to prominence with Rhodesia's Unilateral Declaration of Independence on 11 November 1965, when Ian Smith's white-minority government declared independence from the United Kingdom rather than accept majority rule — the first such act against Britain since the American Declaration of Independence of 1776.

International law

International law neither expressly grants nor generally prohibits declarations of independence. In its 2010 advisory opinion on Kosovo, the International Court of Justice held that general international law contains no prohibition on such declarations, while deliberately declining to rule on any right of secession or on the legal consequences of recognition. In practice, a UDI's effectiveness depends on recognition by other states and on the new entity's control of its territory. Some declarations followed armed conflict or referendums; others, like Biafra (1967), Katanga (1960), and the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus (1983), achieved little or no recognition. The United Nations Security Council has declared certain UDIs invalid — notably Rhodesia's and Northern Cyprus's — where they conflicted with prohibitions on racial discrimination or the use of force.

Examples

Historically significant UDIs include those of the United States (1776), many Latin American states in the early 19th century, Israel (1948), Bangladesh's proclamation during its 1971 liberation war, Kosovo (2008), and Catalonia's short-lived declaration (2017). Rhodesia's UDI led to mandatory UN sanctions and a fifteen-year liberation war, ending with the Lancaster House Agreement and the internationally recognised independence of Zimbabwe in 1980.
