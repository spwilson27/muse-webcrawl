Clarendon Commission

The Clarendon Commission was a royal commission established in 1861 to investigate the state of the nine leading English public schools. Chaired by the Earl of Clarendon, it was appointed following concern about discipline and the quality and narrowness of the classical curriculum in the great schools. Its report, published in 1864, led to the Public Schools Act 1868, which reformed the governance of seven of the schools.

The commission investigated nine great schools, including Eton, Harrow and Rugby.

Schools investigated

The nine schools were Eton College, Charterhouse, Harrow, Rugby, Shrewsbury, Westminster, Winchester, Merchant Taylors' and St Paul's. The commission examined their finances, governance, teaching (overwhelmingly Latin and Greek), discipline — including fagging and flogging — and living conditions for boys.

Report and consequences

The commission praised much about the schools' moral influence and the character they produced, but criticised their narrow curriculum, weak teaching of modern subjects such as mathematics, science, modern languages and history, and defects in governance and financial management. The resulting Public Schools Act 1868 created new governing bodies for seven of the schools (Merchant Taylors' and St Paul's, as day schools, were excluded) and empowered them to make new statutes. A separate commission under Lord Taunton then investigated the endowed grammar schools, feeding into the Endowed Schools Act 1869.

Legacy

The Clarendon Commission is a landmark in the history of English education: it began the modernisation of the great public schools, widened their curricula, and established the precedent for parliamentary oversight while preserving the schools' independence.
