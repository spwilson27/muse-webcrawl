Asklepieion

The sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus

An asklepieion (Ancient Greek: Ἀσκληπιεῖον; Latin: aesculapium) was a healing temple in ancient Greece and Rome dedicated to Asclepius, the god of medicine. The sick travelled to these sanctuaries seeking cures through ritual, dreams and the ministrations of priest-physicians. More than 300 asklepieia are known across the classical world.

Cult and healing

Patients underwent purification, made offerings and sacrifices, and then slept in a special dormitory called the abaton or enkoimeterion. This practice, known as incubation, aimed to induce a healing dream in which Asclepius himself, or his sacred animals — snakes and dogs — would appear and cure the patient, or reveal a treatment the priests could interpret. Votive offerings and inscribed testimonials (iamata) recorded miraculous recoveries.

Major sanctuaries

The most famous asklepieion was at Epidaurus in the Argolid, which included a celebrated theatre, a stadium, baths, a gymnasium and a round building called the Tholos. Other major centres stood on the island of Kos, associated with Hippocrates, and at Pergamon in Asia Minor, where the physician Galen trained and practised in the 2nd century AD.

Legacy

The asklepieia combined religious ritual with increasingly empirical care — diet, exercise, bathing, massage and herbal remedies — and are often seen as forerunners of the hospital. The cult flourished into late antiquity before declining with the spread of Christianity.
