March law (Anglo-Scottish border)

March law (Latin: leges marchiarum) was the body of law, custom, and administrative practice governing the Anglo-Scottish border—the "Marches"—from the late medieval period until the Union of the Crowns in 1603. It existed alongside, and partly outside, the common law of England and Scots law, reflecting the border's character as a frontier zone of chronic raiding and intermittent warfare.

Administration

Each kingdom divided its side of the border into East, Middle, and West Marches, each administered by a Warden of the Marches. Wardens held courts, pursued cross-border raiders, and met their opposite numbers on appointed "days of truce" (days of march) at traditional meeting places, where bills of complaint from both sides were heard and offenders delivered or compensation ordered.

Customary practices

Distinctive institutions included the "hot trod," a legal form of cross-border pursuit undertaken within six days of a raid, with horn and hound and hue and cry, and its slower counterpart the "cold trod." Offences were described in a special vocabulary; a bill could be "fouled" (proven) or "cleared." Cross-border feuds, blackmail (protection rents), and reiving by the great Border surnames persisted despite the system.

Abolition

After 1603, James VI and I sought to pacify the region, renaming the Marches the "Middle Shires," abolishing march law, and suppressing the reiving families through commissions, executions, and transportations.
