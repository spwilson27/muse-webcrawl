Operating license

An operating license is a formal permission, usually issued by a government agency or regulatory authority, that allows an individual or organization to carry out a particular regulated activity or operate a facility or business. Operating licenses are distinct from general business registration: they certify that the holder has met specific safety, technical, professional, or environmental standards required for the activity in question.

Purpose

Operating licenses serve to protect public health, safety, and welfare by ensuring that operators of potentially hazardous or sensitive activities — such as nuclear power plants, airlines, hospitals, broadcasting stations, and commercial transportation services — meet minimum standards before beginning operations and continue to meet them over time.

Examples

  • Nuclear facilities: In the United States, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission issues operating licenses for commercial nuclear power reactors, typically for an initial term of 40 years, with possible renewal.
  • Aviation: Airlines require an air operator's certificate (AOC) to conduct commercial air transport operations.
  • Broadcasting: Radio and television stations operate under broadcast licenses granted by national regulators such as the FCC.
  • Healthcare: Hospitals and clinics commonly require facility operating licenses in addition to the professional licenses of their staff.

Conditions and renewal

Operating licenses are usually time-limited and subject to conditions, inspections, reporting requirements, and renewal. A license may be suspended or revoked if the holder fails to comply with the applicable regulations.
