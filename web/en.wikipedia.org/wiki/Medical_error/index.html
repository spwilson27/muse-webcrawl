Medical error


The "Swiss cheese model" of how system failures align to cause harm

A medical error is a preventable adverse effect of care ("iatrogenesis"), whether or not it is evident or harmful to the patient. This might include an inaccurate or incomplete diagnosis or treatment of a disease, injury, syndrome, behaviour, infection, or other ailment. Medical errors include medication errors, surgical errors, diagnostic errors, and failures of equipment or communication.

Scale of the problem

The US Institute of Medicine report To Err Is Human (1999) estimated that between 44,000 and 98,000 deaths per year in American hospitals resulted from preventable medical errors, galvanising the patient safety movement. Later studies have produced widely varying estimates: a 2016 analysis suggesting medical error might contribute to over 250,000 deaths annually in the United States was influential but methodologically contested.

Causes

Research following James Reason's organisational accident model treats most errors as failures of systems rather than of individuals: latent conditions such as poor design, inadequate staffing, fatigue, and ambiguous labelling line up with active failures at the "sharp end" of care. Common contributing factors include communication breakdowns during handoffs, look-alike or sound-alike medications, and interruptions during prescribing or administration.

Responses

Responses emphasise a "just culture," incident reporting and root-cause analysis (as in the WHO's patient safety programme), and engineered defences such as computerised physician order entry, barcode medication administration, surgical checklists, and standardised handoff tools. The WHO surgical safety checklist was shown in a 2009 study to reduce major complications and deaths substantially.
