Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta

The Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta, or California Delta, is a vast inverted river delta and estuary in Northern California. It is formed at the western edge of the Central Valley by the confluence of the Sacramento River (flowing from the north) and the San Joaquin River (flowing from the south), and drains westward through Suisun Bay into San Francisco Bay and ultimately the Pacific Ocean. The Delta covers about 1,150 square miles (3,000 km²) and includes more than 1,000 miles (1,600 km) of waterways.

Formation and reclamation

The Delta formed as sea levels rose after the last glacial period, slowing the rivers and causing sediment to accumulate as tidal marsh. Beginning in the mid-nineteenth century, settlers and farmers built levees to reclaim the marshland into "islands" for agriculture, protected by roughly 1,100 miles (1,800 km) of levees. Oxidation of the peat soils has caused many islands to subside well below sea level, increasing flood risk.

Water supply

The Delta is the hub of California's water supply system. The State Water Project and the federal Central Valley Project pump water from the southern Delta into aqueducts serving agriculture in the San Joaquin Valley and urban areas in Southern California and the Bay Area, supplying water to tens of millions of people and millions of acres of farmland. Export pumping has major effects on the estuary's ecology and salinity.

Ecology

The Delta is one of the largest estuaries on the west coast of the Americas and supports hundreds of species of fish, birds and wildlife, including the threatened Delta smelt and runs of Chinook salmon. Invasive species, water diversions, pollution and levee failures are ongoing management concerns.
