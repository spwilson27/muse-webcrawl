Laodicea on the Lycus

Laodicea on the Lycus
Greek: Λαοδίκεια πρὸς τῷ Λύκῳ
Location: near Eskihisar, Denizli Province, Turkey
Founded: 3rd century BC by Antiochus II
Region: Phrygia, Anatolia

Laodicea on the Lycus (Greek: Λαοδίκεια πρὸς τῷ Λύκῳ; Latin: Laodicea ad Lycum) was an ancient city in Phrygia, in southwestern Anatolia, situated on a plateau between the Lycus (Çürüksu) valley and its tributaries, about 6 km north of modern Denizli in Turkey and near the famous hot springs of Hierapolis (Pamukkale). It was one of the most prosperous cities of Roman Asia and is known to Christians as one of the Seven Churches of Asia.

History

The city was founded by the Seleucid king Antiochus II in the mid-third century BC and named for his wife Laodice, on or near the site of earlier settlements called Diospolis and Rhoas. It passed to the kingdom of Pergamon and then, in 133 BC, to Rome. Under Roman rule Laodicea became a great commercial and financial centre: it lay on the trade route from Ephesus into the interior, was famous for its banking, for a glossy black wool and the garments made from it, for its medical school and its eye salve (the "Phrygian powder"), and for wealthy citizens such as the banker-bishop Zeno. Cicero cashed bills of exchange there, and the city minted its own coinage.

Laodicea was repeatedly damaged by earthquakes—the region is highly seismic—and rebuilt; after the great earthquake of AD 60, the citizens famously rebuilt the city from their own resources without imperial aid, a point of pride noted by Tacitus. The city continued to flourish in the Byzantine period before declining after invasions and further earthquakes in the seventh century and beyond; the population gradually shifted to nearby Denizli.

Christianity

Laodicea had a large Jewish population and an early Christian community, to which Paul's Epistle to the Colossians refers (an "epistle from Laodicea" is also mentioned). In the Book of Revelation (3:14–22), the Laodicean church is rebuked for being "lukewarm—neither hot nor cold", an image often linked to the city's tepid aqueduct water, in contrast to the hot springs of Hierapolis and the cold waters of Colossae. The Council of Laodicea, held in the fourth century, issued sixty canons, including an early listing of the books of the Bible.

Archaeology

Excavations since 2003, led by Pamukkale University, have uncovered one of the largest stadiums in Anatolia, two theatres, temples, colonnaded streets, an elaborate water-distribution system, baths and a monumental church—the Church of Laodicea—regarded as one of the seven churches of Revelation. The site remains under active excavation and conservation.
