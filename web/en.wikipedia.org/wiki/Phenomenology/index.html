Phenomenology is a philosophical movement founded by Edmund Husserl around 1900 that studies the structures of experience as experienced from the first-person point of view. Its method — 'back to the things themselves' — brackets assumptions about the external world (epoché) to describe how phenomena appear to consciousness, whose essential mark is intentionality: consciousness is always consciousness of something.

Development

Husserl's student Martin Heidegger transformed phenomenology into fundamental ontology in Being and Time (1927), analysing human existence (Dasein) through care, anxiety and being-toward-death. Maurice Merleau-Ponty centred the lived body; Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir carried it into existentialism and ethics. It remains influential in philosophy of mind, cognitive science (embodied approaches) and qualitative research methods.
