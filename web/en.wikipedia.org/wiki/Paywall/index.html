Paywall

A paywall is a method of restricting access to content, with a purchase or a paid subscription, especially news. Beginning in the mid-2010s, newspapers including The New York Times and The Wall Street Journal started implementing paywalls on their websites as a way to increase revenue after years of decline in paid print readership and, initially, rapidly rising ad revenue.

Types

Hard paywalls allow little or no content to be viewed without a subscription. Metered paywalls permit a set number of free articles per period (often per month) before requiring payment — the model adopted by The New York Times in 2011. Freemium models keep some content free while charging for premium material. Dynamic or personalised paywalls vary the offer and the meter according to reader behaviour and predicted propensity to subscribe.

History

The Wall Street Journal was among the first major newspapers to charge for online access, from 1996. Most publishers initially favoured free access supported by advertising, but the decline of digital advertising rates and the dominance of large platforms pushed the industry toward reader revenue in the 2010s. The New York Times metered model, launched in March 2011, was widely imitated after proving commercially successful.

Reception

Paywalls have been credited with restoring sustainable funding for journalism and encouraging quality over click-driven volume. Critics argue they restrict access to information, create "information inequality" between subscribers and others, and may push readers toward free, lower-quality or partisan sources. Academic journals' paywalls have prompted the open access movement and controversy over sites such as Sci-Hub.
