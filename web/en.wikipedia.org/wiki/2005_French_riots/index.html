The 2005 French riots were three weeks of civil unrest in the suburbs (banlieues) of Paris and other French cities in the autumn of 2005. They were the most extensive rioting in France since May 1968 and triggered the first declaration of a state of emergency under a 1955 law originally passed during the Algerian War.

Trigger

On 27 October 2005, two teenagers, Zyed Benna (17) and Bouna Traoré (15), were electrocuted in an electricity substation in Clichy-sous-Bois while hiding after encountering police; a third was seriously injured. Rumours and anger over the deaths, and remarks by Interior Minister Nicolas Sarkozy, who had days earlier vowed to clean a housing estate "with a Kärcher" and referred to rioters as racaille ("scum"), ignited unrest that spread from Seine-Saint-Denis to hundreds of communes across France.

Course and response

Nightly violence peaked around 6–7 November, when over 1,400 vehicles were torched in a single night. The government of Jacques Chirac and Dominique de Villepin declared a state of emergency on 8 November, empowering prefects to impose curfews. Violence subsided by mid-November. Around 9,000 vehicles, schools, gymnasiums and businesses were burned, thousands were arrested and hundreds of police injured; remarkably, deaths directly caused by the rioting itself were very few.

Causes and legacy

Commentators pointed to high youth unemployment, discrimination against residents of immigrant origin, poor housing in post-war housing estates (cités), and fraught relations with police identity checks. The riots prompted lasting debate about integration, secularism and policing in France, and periodic later unrest — including the nationwide riots after the 2023 police shooting of Nahel Merzouk — is often compared to 2005.
