Synthetic-aperture radar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Synthetic-aperture radar (SAR) is a form of radar that is used to create two-dimensional images or three-dimensional reconstructions of objects, such as landscapes. SAR uses the motion of the radar antenna over a target region to provide finer spatial resolution than conventional stationary beam-scanning radars. It is typically mounted on a moving platform, such as an aircraft or spacecraft, and works day or night and through cloud cover because it actively illuminates the target with microwaves.

Principle

As the platform moves, the antenna's successive positions along the flight path are combined coherently — as if they formed a single, very large ("synthetic") aperture — greatly improving azimuth resolution far beyond what the physical antenna size would allow. Range resolution is achieved by pulse compression. The recorded phase history is processed (historically by optical means, now digitally) to form the image. Doppler shifts of returns from points ahead of and behind the platform are what make the synthesis possible.

Applications

  • Earth observation: mapping terrain, monitoring deforestation, sea ice, oil spills, floods, and land subsidence
  • Interferometric SAR (InSAR): measuring centimetre-scale ground deformation from earthquakes, volcanoes, and glaciers by comparing phases of repeat images
  • Military reconnaissance: imaging through weather and darkness
  • Planetary science: radar mapping of Venus (Magellan) and Titan (Cassini) beneath opaque clouds

Notable civilian SAR satellites include the ESA Sentinel-1 constellation, Germany's TerraSAR-X, and Canada's RADARSAT series.

History

The principle was developed in the early 1950s by Carl Wiley at Goodyear Aircraft and pursued in classified military programmes; the first operational airborne SAR systems appeared in the 1960s, and Seasat (1978) was the first civilian spaceborne SAR.
