Gas turbine

TypeInternal combustion engine (continuous combustion)
Thermodynamic cycleBrayton cycle
Main componentsCompressor, combustion chamber, turbine
ApplicationsJet engines, power generation, marine propulsion, mechanical drive

A gas turbine is a type of continuous-flow internal combustion engine. In its simplest form it consists of an upstream rotating gas compressor coupled to a downstream turbine, with a combustion chamber between them. Energy is added by burning fuel in compressed air; the expanding hot gases pass through the turbine, producing shaft power or thrust. The working cycle is the Brayton cycle.

Operation

Air is drawn in and compressed to many times atmospheric pressure, mixed with fuel and burned at roughly constant pressure, then expanded through one or more turbine stages. Part of the turbine's output drives the compressor; the remainder is available as useful work — as exhaust jet thrust in a turbojet, as shaft power in a turboshaft or industrial engine, or driving a fan in a turbofan. Large industrial turbines can exceed 60% efficiency in combined cycle with a steam bottoming plant.

History

Early patents date to John Barber in 1791. Practical engines emerged in the 1930s–40s through the independent work of Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany; the first jet aircraft flew in 1939. After the Second World War gas turbines transformed aviation and became central to electricity generation, gas compression, and naval propulsion.

Advantages and disadvantages

Gas turbines offer very high power-to-weight ratios, smooth operation and multi-fuel capability, but are less efficient than reciprocating engines at small scale and part load, and their hot-section components require advanced alloys and cooling.
