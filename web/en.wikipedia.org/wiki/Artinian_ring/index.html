In abstract algebra, an Artinian ring (also called an Artin ring) is a ring that satisfies the descending chain condition on ideals: every descending chain of ideals R ⊇ I1 ⊇ I2 ⊇ ⋯ eventually stabilises. Equivalently, every non-empty set of ideals has a minimal element. Such rings are named after Emil Artin, who pioneered their study in the 1920s. A ring that is Artinian as a module over itself is the natural counterpart of a Noetherian ring, which satisfies the ascending chain condition.

Examples

Every finite ring is Artinian. Fields and finite-dimensional algebras over a field — including matrix rings over a field and quotient rings of polynomial rings such as Z/nZ — are Artinian. More generally, any finitely generated module over an Artinian ring is Artinian. The ring of integers Z is Noetherian but not Artinian, since the chain (2) ⊃ (4) ⊃ (8) ⊃ ⋯ never stabilises; localising changes the picture — an Artinian ring's structure is in many ways simpler and more rigid than a general Noetherian ring's.

Properties

Every Artinian ring is Noetherian, a non-trivial consequence of the Akizuki–Hopkins–Levitzki theorem, and is a finite product of Artinian local rings. In a (commutative) Artinian ring the nilradical is nilpotent, every prime ideal is maximal, and there are only finitely many maximal ideals. A commutative ring is Artinian if and only if it is Noetherian of Krull dimension zero. Noncommutative Artinian rings are central to the theory of semisimple modules: by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem, a ring is semisimple Artinian precisely when it is a finite product of matrix rings over division rings.

Artinian modules

The definition extends to modules: an Artinian module satisfies the descending chain condition on submodules. A ring is Artinian if and only if it is Artinian as a module over itself. Artinian modules have finite length only when they are also Noetherian; finitely generated modules over Artinian rings have both properties.
