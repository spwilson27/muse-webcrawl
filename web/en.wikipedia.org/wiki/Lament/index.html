Lament

Lament
DefinitionA passionate expression of grief or sorrow, in speech, song or poetry
Related formsElegy, dirge, threnody, keen, planctus

A lament or lamentation is a passionate expression of grief, often in music, poetry, or song form. Laments constitute some of the oldest surviving literature: city laments from ancient Sumer mourned the destruction of Ur and other cities more than 4,000 years ago, and the biblical Book of Lamentations mourns the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BCE.

In ancient literature

In Homer's Iliad, formal laments (threnoi and antiphonal gooi) are performed over the bodies of Patroclus and Hector by women and professional singers. Greek tragedy incorporated the kommos, a sung lament exchanged between actor and chorus. Hebrew psalms of lament — more than a third of the Psalter — follow a characteristic movement from complaint and petition toward trust and praise, a structure scholars such as Claus Westermann and Walter Brueggemann have analysed as fundamental to Israel's prayer.

Folk traditions

Ritual lament by women survived for millennia in many cultures: Irish and Scottish caoineadh (keening), Finnish-Karelian itkuvirsi, Greek moirologia, and Balkan funeral laments. Professional mourners improvised verses praising the deceased and voicing communal grief; church and state authorities periodically tried to suppress the practice as unruly, yet it persisted into the 20th century and has been extensively recorded by ethnomusicologists.

In music

The lament became a musical archetype: the descending chromatic tetrachord known as the lament bass underlies works from Monteverdi's Lamento d'Arianna (1608) and Purcell's "When I am laid in earth" (Dido's Lament) to countless later pieces. Scholars such as Ellen Rosand and Susan McClary have traced how the lament gave early opera its most emotionally powerful moments, typically assigned to female voices.
