Pyotr Kapitsa

Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa
Born: 9 July 1894, Kronstadt, Russian Empire
Died: 8 April 1984, Moscow, USSR
Known for: Superfluidity, low-temperature physics
Award: Nobel Prize in Physics (1978)

Pyotr Leonidovich Kapitsa (1894–1984) was a leading Soviet physicist who founded the Institute for Physical Problems in Moscow and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1978 for his basic inventions and discoveries in the area of low-temperature physics.

Career

After studying in Petrograd, Kapitsa moved to the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge in 1921, working under Ernest Rutherford, where he developed methods for producing very strong magnetic fields and became deputy director of magnetic research. In 1934, while visiting the Soviet Union, he was prevented from returning to England and was required to establish his institute in Moscow — an episode that caused a celebrated dispute over his equipment, which Rutherford eventually helped transfer.

Superfluidity

In 1937–38 Kapitsa discovered that liquid helium-4, cooled below the lambda point (2.17 K), loses all viscosity and flows without friction — a phenomenon he named superfluidity. At the same time he developed efficient turbo-expanders for liquefying air and gases, work of major industrial importance. After a period of house arrest following a dispute with the authorities over the atomic bomb project, he was reinstated and led his institute until his death.
