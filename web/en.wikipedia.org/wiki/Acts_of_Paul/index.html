Acts of Paul

The Acts of Paul is one of the major works in the collection of the New Testament apocrypha, written probably around AD 160–190. It narrates, in episodic form, the missionary travels, preaching, miracles and martyrdom of the apostle Paul of Tarsus, in a style resembling a Hellenistic romance.

[Image: Saint Paul preaching]

Contents

The work survives only in fragments in Greek, Coptic and other versions, from which scholars reconstruct episodes including Paul in Damascus, Antioch, Iconium, Athens, Corinth, Ephesus (where he baptises a lion and faces wild beasts), Philippi and Rome, culminating in his beheading under Nero. The best-known section is the Acts of Paul and Thecla, the story of Thecla of Iconium, a young noblewoman converted by Paul's preaching of chastity who miraculously escapes martyrdom several times; Thecla became one of the most venerated female saints of early Christianity.

Authorship and reception

Tertullian reported that the work was composed by a presbyter in Asia who was deposed after confessing he wrote it "out of love for Paul." The Acts of Paul strongly advocates asceticism and celibacy and gives prominent roles to women, features that generated both popularity and ecclesiastical controversy. The correspondence of Paul and Seneca and the so-called Third Epistle to the Corinthians were transmitted as parts of the collection.
