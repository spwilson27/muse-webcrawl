Quorum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A quorum is the minimum number of members of a deliberative assembly (a legislature, board, society or meeting) that must be present at any of its meetings to make the proceedings of that meeting valid. The word is Latin, from the genitive plural of qui ("who"), from the opening formula of commissions in medieval English law.

Practice

A legislature's quorum is typically a proportion of its total membership set by constitution or standing orders — the US Constitution requires a majority of each House; many parliaments require a third or fewer, and the House of Commons conducts most business with far lower effective attendance (a quorum of 40 is callable). Quorum rules protect decisions' legitimacy and the minority's right not to be ambushed, and are weaponised in return: quorum calls stall business, and quorum-busting walkouts — legislators fleeing the jurisdiction to prevent a vote, a recurring tactic in US state legislatures (Texas Democrats' flights in 2003 and 2021 are famous instances; Oregon walkouts prompted a constitutional response barring re-election after unexcused absences) — exploit sergeants-at-arms' powers to compel attendance, which often end at the state line. Corporate and voluntary-association law applies the same logic to boards and AGMs.
