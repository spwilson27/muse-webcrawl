Ancestral Puebloans

RegionFour Corners region, Southwestern United States
Periodc. 100 BCE – 1600 CE
Major sitesChaco Canyon, Mesa Verde, Canyon de Chelly, Bandelier
DescendantsModern Pueblo peoples (Hopi, Zuni, Rio Grande Pueblos)
Former nameAnasazi (term now generally avoided)

The Ancestral Puebloans, also known by the older term Anasazi, were an ancient Native American culture centred on the present-day Four Corners area of the United States, comprising southern Utah, northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, and southwestern Colorado. They are known for their distinctive pottery and for monumental architecture — great houses, cliff dwellings, and ceremonial great kivas — and they are the ancestors of the modern Pueblo peoples.

Cliff Palace at Mesa Verde, the largest cliff dwelling in North America.

Chronology

Archaeologists divide the sequence into Basket Maker and Pueblo periods (the Pecos Classification). Early Ancestral Puebloans were farmers of maize, beans, and squash living in pithouses; by the Pueblo II–III periods (900–1300 CE) they built the great houses of Chaco Canyon — including Pueblo Bonito, with hundreds of rooms — and later the cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde. Chaco was connected by a network of engineered roads to outlier communities across the San Juan Basin.

Migration and legacy

During the late 13th century, amid prolonged drought (the "Great Drought" of 1276–1299), conflict, and social change, the Four Corners region was largely depopulated as communities migrated south and east to the Hopi mesas, Zuni, Acoma, and the Rio Grande valley, where their descendants live today. Pueblo oral traditions recount these migrations and ancestral sites. The term "Anasazi", from a Navajo word often translated "ancient ones" or "ancient enemies", fell out of favour in consultation with Pueblo communities, who prefer "Ancestral Puebloans".

Culture

Ancestral Puebloan society produced fine black-on-white pottery, cotton textiles, turquoise jewellery and inlay, and sophisticated dry-land and irrigation farming. Ceremonial life centred on kivas — round, subterranean or semi-subterranean chambers — whose modern counterparts remain in use in Pueblo communities.
