Longitudinal study

Research typeObservational study
Key featureRepeated observations over time

A longitudinal study (or longitudinal survey, or panel study) is a research design that involves repeated observations of the same variables (e.g., people) over short or long periods of time — often many decades. It is a type of observational study, although it can also be structured as a randomised experiment by assigning treatments over time.

Background

Longitudinal studies are used in medicine to uncover predictors of certain diseases, in psychology to study developmental trends across the life span, and in social science to quantify change in behaviours and attitudes. Unlike cross-sectional studies, which sample different individuals at a single point in time, longitudinal research follows the same subjects and can therefore establish sequences of events and separate the effects of ageing from those of cohort membership.

Types

  • Panel study — the same individuals are surveyed at multiple waves.
  • Cohort study — a group sharing a defining characteristic (such as birth year or exposure) is followed over time.
  • Retrospective study — data on past events are assembled, often from records, to construct a longitudinal picture after the fact.

Advantages and disadvantages

Longitudinal studies can detect changes within individuals, establish temporal order, and control for stable between-person differences. However, they are expensive and time-consuming, suffer from attrition as participants drop out, and may be affected by practice effects and changes in measurement over long periods.

Notable examples

  • The Framingham Heart Study, begun in 1948, which identified major cardiovascular risk factors.
  • The British birth cohort studies, including the 1946 National Survey of Health and Development and the 1970 British Cohort Study.
  • The Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study in New Zealand.
  • The Up Series documentaries, following British children filmed every seven years since 1964.
