Trompe-l'œil

Trompe-l'œil is an artistic term for a highly realistic optical illusion, in which a painted image is rendered in such detail that the viewer mistakes it for a real object or scene. The term derives from the French trompe ("deceive") and l'œil ("the eye") and came into common use in the 19th century.

History

Illusionistic painting has roots in ancient Greece and Rome: Pliny the Elder famously tells of the contest between Zeuxis, who painted grapes so realistically that birds tried to peck them, and Parrhasius, who painted a curtain that Zeuxis himself tried to draw aside. Roman wall painting at Pompeii used illusionistic architecture, and Renaissance masters such as Mantegna and Correggio used quadratura to dissolve ceilings into fictive architecture. The technique flourished in Dutch Golden Age still life — as in Samuel Dirksz van Hoogstraten's letter racks — and in 19th-century American still life with artists such as William Harnett and John F. Peto.

Modern usage

Today, trompe-l'œil is used in murals, architecture, set design, and street art, where painted façades and pavement scenes create convincing illusions of depth and objects from a fixed viewpoint.
