Hard science fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hard SF emphasises scientifically plausible technology and natural laws.

Hard science fiction is a category of science fiction characterised by concern for scientific accuracy and logic. The term was first used in print by P. Schuyler Miller in 1957, in a review in Astounding Science Fiction, for stories in which established science is treated rigorously and extrapolations remain plausible. Hard SF typically foregrounds the natural sciences — physics, astronomy, chemistry, engineering — and the consequences of a single speculative premise explored with care.

Characteristics

There is no sharp boundary between hard and "soft" science fiction; the distinction is a spectrum. Hard SF stories avoid, or carefully justify, devices such as faster-than-light travel and psychic powers, and devote attention to technical detail: orbital mechanics, life support, radiation exposure and materials limits. Plot often arises directly from the scientific problem itself — solving an engineering or astrophysical puzzle is a classic hard SF narrative shape.

History

Early exemplars include Jules Verne's meticulously researched voyages and Hugo Gernsback's "scientifiction". The genre's golden-age centre was John W. Campbell's Astounding, with authors such as Robert A. Heinlein, Isaac Asimov, Hal Clement (Mission of Gravity, 1954) and Poul Anderson. Later leading practitioners include Arthur C. Clarke, Larry Niven (Ringworld, 1970), Gregory Benford, Greg Egan, Kim Stanley Robinson (Mars trilogy) and Andy Weir (The Martian, 2011). Hard SF in film includes 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) and The Martian (2015).

Criticism

Critics have argued that the label privileges a particular style of story — technology-centred, often at the expense of characterisation — and that "accuracy" dates quickly as science advances. Defenders reply that constraint is itself an aesthetic choice: the pleasure of hard SF lies in watching a consistent world unfold from its premises.
