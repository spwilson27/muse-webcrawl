Maramureș (Romanian: Maramureș; Hungarian: Máramaros; Ukrainian: Мармарощина) is a historical and geographical region in north-western Romania and western Ukraine, lying in the Carpathian Mountains along the upper Tisza valley. The region is celebrated for its preserved traditional village life, wooden architecture, and distinctive folk culture.

Geography and division

The Maramureș Depression is enclosed by mountains — the Rodna, Țibleș, Gutâi, and Maramureș ranges — which long isolated the region and helped preserve its customs. Following the redrawing of borders after the First World War, the historical county was divided along the Tisza River: roughly the southern two-thirds went to Romania (today Maramureș County, plus parts of neighbouring counties) and the northern third to what is now Ukraine's Zakarpattia Oblast.

History

In the Middle Ages Maramureș was organised under voivodes and knezes of the Romanian population within the Kingdom of Hungary; its voivodes, notably Bogdan of Cuhea, played a founding role in the establishment of the Principality of Moldavia in the fourteenth century. The region's noblemen were progressively integrated into the Hungarian nobility while village communities retained strong customary institutions. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, large Jewish communities developed in towns such as Sighet; most were destroyed in the Holocaust.

Culture

Maramureș is famous for its wooden churches — some ninety churches built of oak logs between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, eight of which are inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List — and for its monumental carved wooden gates, which mark the entrance to peasant homesteads. Other landmarks include the Merry Cemetery at Săpânța, whose brightly painted crosses carry witty epitaphs, and the narrow-gauge forestry railway of the Vaser Valley. Traditional dress, music, and festivals such as the winter customs remain living parts of local life, making the region a byword in Romania for an unbroken peasant civilisation.
