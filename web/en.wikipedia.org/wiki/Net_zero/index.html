Net zero (or net-zero emissions) is a state in which the greenhouse gases released into the atmosphere by human activity are balanced by removals of greenhouse gases from the atmosphere over a specified period. Achieving net zero at the global level is the central long-term goal of international climate policy: the IPCC has concluded that global carbon dioxide emissions must reach net zero around 2050 to limit warming to 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels, with net zero for all greenhouse gases following later in the century.

The concept applies at the level of countries, companies, cities and products. A growing number of states have written net zero targets into law, and thousands of companies have adopted voluntary pledges, commonly for the year 2050, 2040 or 2030.

Definitions and scope

Net zero is closely related to, but distinct from, carbon neutrality, which historically referred only to carbon dioxide and could be achieved largely through purchased offsets. Net zero standards — such as the Science Based Targets initiative's Corporate Net-Zero Standard (2021) — generally require deep reductions in emissions across all scopes (direct emissions, purchased energy, and supply-chain emissions) before residual emissions are neutralised by removals. Under the Paris Agreement, parties aim to achieve "a balance between anthropogenic emissions by sources and removals by sinks of greenhouse gases in the second half of this century."

Pathways

Modelled pathways to net zero combine rapid decarbonisation of electricity generation (wind, solar, hydro, nuclear and geothermal), electrification of transport, heating and industry, improvements in energy efficiency, reductions in non-CO2 gases such as methane, changes in land use and agriculture, and carbon dioxide removal through afforestation, soil carbon, bioenergy with carbon capture (BECCS) and direct air capture. Residual emissions from sectors such as aviation, cement and parts of agriculture are expected to be the hardest to eliminate.

Criticism

Net zero pledges have been criticised for vagueness, heavy reliance on future removals and offsets of questionable quality, and long target dates that defer near-term cuts. Climate scientists and campaigners argue that credible plans require interim targets and transparent accounting. The term has also been the subject of political debate in several countries over the costs and speed of the transition.
