The history of Suriname spans several thousand years of Indigenous settlement, followed by European colonisation, a plantation economy built on enslaved African labour, indentured immigration from Asia, and finally independence from the Netherlands in 1975. Suriname is the smallest independent country in South America and one of its most ethnically diverse.

Indigenous peoples and early contact

Before European arrival, the coastal plains and interior forests were inhabited by Arawak-speaking peoples, among them the Lokono, and by Carib-speaking groups such as the Kalina, as well as smaller peoples including the Akurio and Trio in the far south. The first sustained European contact came in the late 16th century, when Dutch, English, French and Spanish traders explored the "Wild Coast" of the Guianas in search of the legendary El Dorado.

English and Dutch colonisation

An English colony was founded in 1650 by Francis Willoughby, Lord Willoughby of Parham, using enslaved Africans to work sugar plantations along the Suriname River. In February 1667, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War, a Dutch squadron under Abraham Crijnssen captured the colony. The Treaty of Breda (1667) confirmed Dutch possession of Suriname in exchange for New Amsterdam — present-day New York — an exchange later recalled as the Dutch trading Manhattan for a sugar colony.

The plantation economy

Under the Society of Suriname, chartered in 1683, the colony developed into a plantation economy producing sugar, coffee, cocoa and cotton. Enslaved Africans were imported in large numbers under brutal conditions; many escaped into the interior, forming Maroon communities — including the Ndyuka, Saramaka, Paramaka, Aluku, Kwinti and Matawai — which waged long guerrilla wars against the colonial state before treaties in the 1760s recognised their freedom and territory. Jewish settlers, many fleeing persecution in Brazil and elsewhere, founded Jodensavanne, one of the oldest Jewish agricultural communities in the Americas.

Abolition and indentured labour

Slavery was abolished on 1 July 1863, though the formerly enslaved were required to work under state supervision for a further ten years. To replace them, plantation owners recruited indentured labourers: Hindustanis from British India (from 1873, arriving on ships such as the Lalla Rookh), Javanese from the Dutch East Indies (from 1890), and Chinese labourers. These migrations created the multi-ethnic society — Creole, Hindustani, Javanese, Maroon, Indigenous, Chinese, Lebanese and European — that characterises Suriname today.

Twentieth century and autonomy

Bauxite mining, begun during the First World War era, replaced agriculture as the economic mainstay. During the Second World War, United States forces helped guard the bauxite mines vital to Allied aircraft production. Suriname gained autonomy in internal affairs in 1954 as a constituent country of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. Full independence followed on 25 November 1975, with Johan Ferrier as the first president and Henck Arron as prime minister; roughly a third of the population emigrated to the Netherlands around independence.

Military period and return to democracy

On 25 February 1980, a coup led by Sergeant Dési Bouterse overthrew the government. The regime is remembered for the December murders of 1982, in which fifteen prominent critics were killed, and for the Interior War (1986–1992) against the Jungle Commando of Ronnie Brunswijk. Civilian rule was restored after elections in 1987 and a new constitution in 1987. Bouterse later returned to power democratically, serving as president from 2010 to 2020, despite a conviction in Suriname for his role in the 1982 murders and a separate drug-trafficking conviction in the Netherlands.

Recent history

In the 21st century Suriname has alternated governments between the National Party alliance and Bouterse's National Democratic Party; Chandrikapersad "Chan" Santokhi became president in 2020. Major offshore oil discoveries by TotalEnergies and partners in Block 58, announced from 2020 onward, raised expectations of a petroleum boom, with a final investment decision taken in 2024.
