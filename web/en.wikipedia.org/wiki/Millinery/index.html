Millinery

Millinery is the design, manufacture and sale of hats and other headwear. A person engaged in this trade is called a milliner or hatter. Historically, the term milliner derived from the city of Milan, Italy, from where fine straw and other fashionable goods were imported to England in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; early milliners sold a variety of haberdashery and accessories as well as hats.

History

By the eighteenth century, millinery had become a predominantly female trade in Britain and France, one of the few skilled occupations open to women. Milliners made and trimmed hats and bonnets and sold ribbons, laces, gloves and other trimmings. The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were the heyday of the elaborate women's hat, when no respectable outfit was complete without one; workshops used wooden hat blocks, steam, felt, straw braid and feathers.

The decline of everyday hat-wearing from the 1960s greatly reduced the trade, but millinery survives as a craft for formal occasions — weddings, racing events such as Royal Ascot, and haute couture — and contemporary designers have revived sculptural and avant-garde forms.

Techniques and materials

Traditional millinery materials include felt, straw, sinamay, buckram, silk and velvet, trimmed with ribbons, veiling, feathers and artificial flowers. Techniques include blocking (shaping material over a wooden form with steam), wiring brims, stitching straw braid, and hand-finishing trims. Notable milliners include Rose Bertin, milliner to Marie Antoinette, and modern designers such as Philip Treacy and Stephen Jones.
