Pizza in the United States developed primarily in the early 20th century in Italian-American immigrant communities, and since then has spread throughout the population and become a hallmark of American cuisine. The first pizzeria in the United States is generally credited to Lombardi's in New York City, opened in 1905 by Gennaro Lombardi.

History

Italian immigrants brought pizza with them from Naples, but the dish remained largely within immigrant communities until after World War II, when returning servicemen who had been stationed in Italy created a broader demand. The postwar decades saw the rise of major chains — Domino's, Pizza Hut, Little Caesars, and Papa John's — and home delivery became a defining feature of American pizza culture. The United States now has tens of thousands of pizzerias and one of the highest per-capita pizza consumption rates in the world.

Regional styles

New York-style pizza is a thin, foldable, hand-tossed crust with a crisp edge, derived from Neapolitan pizza. Chicago-style pizza is famous for deep-dish, baked in a pan with a thick crust and chunky tomato sauce on top. Other styles include Detroit-style (rectangular, brick-cheese, baked in steel pans), New Haven-style "apizza" (thin, charred, coal-fired), St. Louis-style (cracker-thin crust with Provel cheese), and California-style (inventive toppings popularized in the 1980s).

Toppings

Pepperoni is the most popular topping in the United States, followed by sausage, mushrooms, and extra cheese. Hawaiian pizza (ham and pineapple), barbecue chicken, and buffalo chicken are distinctly North American creations.
