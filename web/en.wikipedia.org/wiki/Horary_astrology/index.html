Horary astrology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A traditional horary chart

Horary astrology is an ancient branch of horoscopic astrology in which an astrologer attempts to answer a question by constructing a horoscope for the exact time and place at which the question was received and understood by the astrologer. The word horary comes from the Latin hora, "hour". Together with electional astrology — choosing a propitious time to begin an undertaking — it belongs to the category of interrogational astrology.

Approach

The chart is judged by assigning the querent (the person asking) to the ruler of the Ascendant, and the matter asked about (the quesited) to the ruler of the house governing the topic: the second house for money and lost objects, the seventh for relationships and opponents, the tenth for career, and so on. The astrologer examines the condition of the significators — their dignity, aspects, and whether an applying aspect perfects between them — together with the position and aspects of the Moon, which co-signifies the querent and the unfolding of events. Traditional technique also considers "considerations before judgment", such as a very early or late Ascendant degree or a void-of-course Moon, historically taken as warnings about the chart's validity.

History

Horary techniques were systematised in Hellenistic and Persian-Arabic astrology, transmitted through works such as those of Dorotheus of Sidon and Masha'allah, and codified in Latin medieval and Renaissance practice. Its most famous English exponent was William Lilly, whose Christian Astrology (1647) remains the standard text, notable for its many worked examples, including questions about lost property, battles of the English Civil War, and the famous "Fish stolen" chart. The practice declined with astrology's fall from respectability in the eighteenth century but was revived by twentieth-century traditionalists.
