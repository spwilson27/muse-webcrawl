The greater flamingo (Phoenicopterus roseus) is the most widespread and largest species of the flamingo family, found in parts of Africa, the Indian subcontinent, the Middle East and southern Europe (notably the Camargue in France, Spain, and also Italy, the Balkans and Anatolia). Until about 2008 it was treated as conspecific with the American (Caribbean) flamingo, which is now recognised as a separate species, Phoenicopterus ruber.

Description

The greater flamingo averages 110–150 cm tall and 2–4 kg in weight; exceptional males may reach 187 cm. The plumage is mostly pinkish-white, with red wing coverts and black flight feathers. The bill is pink with a black tip, and the legs are entirely pink. Colour derives from carotenoid pigments in its food — small crustaceans, blue-green algae, insect larvae and seeds — which are filtered from the water with the bill held upside-down; birds deprived of carotenoids fade to white.

Behaviour

Highly gregarious, greater flamingos feed, display and breed in flocks that can number thousands. They inhabit shallow saline or alkaline lagoons, salt pans, estuaries and large lakes. Group mating displays involve synchronised head-flagging, wing-salutes and marching. A single chalky-white egg is laid on a mud mound; both parents incubate for about a month and feed the chick "crop milk". Wild birds commonly live 30–40 years, and over 60 in captivity.

Conservation

The species is evaluated as Least Concern, with the world population estimated in the hundreds of thousands and broadly increasing; it is the state bird of the Indian state of Andhra Pradesh. Local threats include water-level changes at breeding lagoons, disturbance, lead poisoning and collisions with power lines.
