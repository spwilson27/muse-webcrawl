Miscibility

Miscibility is the property of two substances to mix in all proportions, forming a homogeneous mixture. The term is most often applied to liquids but also applies to solids and gases. Water and ethanol, for example, are miscible in all proportions, whereas oil and water are said to be immiscible.

Organic compounds

Among organic compounds, miscibility with water is largely determined by polarity and hydrogen bonding: short-chain alcohols are fully miscible with water, while hydrocarbons are essentially immiscible. The informal rule "like dissolves like" summarizes the tendency of similar substances to be miscible.

Miscibility gap

Some systems are only partially miscible: two liquids may dissolve in each other to a limited extent, forming two coexisting phases. The range of compositions over which a mixture separates into two phases is called a miscibility gap; phenol and water exhibit one below about 66 °C. In metallurgy, miscibility gaps also occur in solid solutions and liquid alloys.

Determination

Miscibility is typically determined by measuring phase diagrams as a function of temperature and composition, and by observing whether mixtures remain single-phase.
