Physical dependence

Physical dependence is a state that develops from chronic use of a drug or other substance, characterised by tolerance and the appearance of a withdrawal syndrome when use is abruptly reduced or stopped. It results from physiological adaptations — such as receptor downregulation and changes in neurotransmitter systems — as the body compensates for the substance's repeated presence.

Definition

Physical dependence is a normal pharmacological phenomenon and is not, by itself, the same as addiction, which involves compulsive drug-seeking despite harm. Patients taking prescribed opioids, antidepressants or corticosteroids can become physically dependent without being addicted. Modern diagnostic manuals (DSM-5) accordingly treat tolerance and withdrawal occurring under appropriate medical supervision as insufficient for a substance use disorder diagnosis.

Mechanism

Repeated exposure leads the nervous system to counter-adapt: receptor numbers and sensitivity fall, endogenous ligand production may decrease, and opposing physiological processes are recruited. When the substance is removed, these adaptations are unopposed, producing withdrawal symptoms that are often the opposite of the drug's acute effects (for example, anxiety and insomnia after sedative withdrawal).

Substances

Substances classically producing physical dependence include alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines and other sedative-hypnotics, nicotine and caffeine; abrupt discontinuation of some antidepressants, beta blockers and corticosteroids can also cause discontinuation syndromes. Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be medically dangerous and occasionally fatal.

Management

Management generally involves gradual dose reduction (tapering), substitution with a longer-acting agent of the same class, and symptomatic treatment. Medically supervised detoxification may be required for severe dependence.
