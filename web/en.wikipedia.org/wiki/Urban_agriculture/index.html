Urban agriculture

Community gardens are the most visible form of urban agriculture

Urban agriculture is the practice of cultivating, processing, and distributing food within or around urban areas. It encompasses community gardens, allotments, rooftop farms, vertical farms, backyard and balcony gardening, aquaponics and hydroponics, urban beekeeping, and the raising of small livestock, and is practised in cities on every continent for subsistence, income, recreation, and community building.

Scale and significance

Estimates by the United Nations Development Programme have suggested that hundreds of millions of urban residents participate in food production, and that urban agriculture supplies a significant share of the food consumed in many cities of the developing world — in some cases the majority of fresh vegetables. In wealthy countries, urban agriculture surged with wartime "victory gardens", declined post-war, and revived from the 1970s onward through the community gardening movement and, more recently, interest in local food systems and climate resilience. Havana's organopónicos, developed after the collapse of Soviet support in the 1990s, are a frequently cited model of intensive urban food production.

Benefits and challenges

Advocates cite improved food security and nutrition, shorter supply chains and lower transport emissions, productive reuse of vacant land and organic waste, stormwater absorption, mitigation of the urban heat island, and social benefits of gardening. Critics and researchers note constraints: scarce and expensive land, contaminated soils requiring testing and remediation, water costs, zoning restrictions, and the limited share of staple calories that cities can realistically produce. Emerging controlled-environment agriculture — LED-lit vertical farms growing leafy greens — addresses land and season constraints but faces high energy costs.
