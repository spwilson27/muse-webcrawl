Submarine warfare

Submarine warfare is one of the four divisions of underwater warfare, the others being anti-submarine warfare, mine warfare, and underwater diversions. It centres on the operations of submarines against enemy shipping and naval vessels, and in the modern era also land targets struck with cruise and ballistic missiles.

History

Early combat submarines — from the Turtle (1775) and the Confederate H. L. Hunley, which sank the USS Housatonic in 1864 — proved the concept. Submarine warfare came of age in the First World War, when German U-boats waged commerce raiding against Allied shipping; Germany's policy of unrestricted submarine warfare (1915 and again from 1917) sank millions of tons of shipping but outraged neutral opinion and, after the sinking of the Lusitania and the Zimmermann Telegram, helped bring the United States into the war.

In the Second World War the Battle of the Atlantic saw Admiral Dönitz's "wolfpack" tactics nearly sever Britain's supply lines before Allied convoys, air cover, radar, and codebreaking turned the tide in 1943. In the Pacific, U.S. submarines devastated Japanese merchant shipping and the Imperial Japanese Navy. Countermeasures define anti-submarine warfare: convoy, sonar (ASDIC), depth charges, escort carriers, and intelligence.

Modern era

Nuclear-powered attack submarines hunt enemy submarines and ships, while ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) form the most survivable leg of the nuclear triad, their deterrent patrols a central feature of Cold War and contemporary strategy.
