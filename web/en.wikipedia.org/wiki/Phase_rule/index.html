Phase rule

In thermodynamics, the phase rule (or Gibbs' phase rule) is a general principle governing systems in thermodynamic equilibrium, formulated by Josiah Willard Gibbs in his work On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances (1875–1878). It states that the number of degrees of freedom F of a system at equilibrium equals the number of components C minus the number of coexisting phases P plus two (for temperature and pressure):

F = C − P + 2

Derivation and examples

The rule follows by counting intensive variables (temperature, pressure, and the composition of each phase) and subtracting the constraints imposed by the equality of chemical potentials of each component across all phases. For a pure substance (C = 1) with a single phase, F = 2: both temperature and pressure can vary independently. Where two phases coexist — e.g. liquid and vapour along the vaporisation curve of a phase diagram — F = 1, so choosing the temperature fixes the vapour pressure. At the triple point (P = 3) F = 0: the state is invariant.[1]

Condensed systems and variants

For systems studied at constant pressure, the "reduced" phase rule F = C − P + 1 applies, as used in metallurgical binary diagrams. The phase rule underlies petrology (mineral assemblages), alloy design, and chemical engineering separations, and explains why invariant points and univariant curves constrain how phase assemblages can change with conditions.
