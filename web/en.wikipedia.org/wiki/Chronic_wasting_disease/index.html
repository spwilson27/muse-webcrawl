Chronic wasting disease

Chronic wasting disease (CWD) is a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy — a prion disease — affecting deer, elk, moose, and other cervids. It is invariably fatal, causing progressive neurodegeneration, behavioural changes, and severe weight loss ("wasting"). First recognized in captive mule deer in Colorado in the late 1960s, it has since spread through wild and farmed cervid populations in North America and has been detected in South Korea and Scandinavia.

Cause and transmission

CWD is caused by misfolded prion proteins (PrPCWD) that convert normal cellular prion protein into the abnormal form, which accumulates in the brain and lymphoid tissues. Infected animals shed prions in saliva, urine, faeces, and decomposing carcasses; prions persist in soil for years and can bind to soil particles, providing a long-lived environmental reservoir. Transmission occurs through direct contact and environmental exposure.

Signs

After an incubation period of at least 16 months, affected animals show progressive weight loss, listlessness, excessive salivation and thirst, stumbling, tremors, and a characteristic wide stance and lowered head. The disease progresses over weeks to months and always ends in death; there is no treatment or vaccine.

Distribution and management

CWD has been reported in the majority of U.S. states with significant cervid populations and several Canadian provinces; in 2016 it was found in reindeer and moose in Norway, the first detection in Europe. Management relies on surveillance testing of hunter-harvested animals, restrictions on moving live cervids and carcasses, and targeted culling in some areas.

Human health

No cases of CWD transmission to humans have been documented, but because related prion diseases such as BSE ("mad cow disease") did cross to humans as variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, public-health agencies recommend not eating meat from animals that test positive for CWD and handling carcasses with precautions.
