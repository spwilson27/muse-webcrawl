Pacific bluefin tuna

The Pacific bluefin tuna (Thunnus orientalis) is a species of tuna found in the North Pacific Ocean and, in smaller numbers, in the South Pacific. Together with the Atlantic and southern bluefin tunas, it is one of the three bluefin species. It is a large, fast-swimming predator prized for sushi and sashimi, and has been heavily fished throughout its range.

Description

Pacific bluefin can reach 3 metres in length and over 450 kg in weight, though most caught today are far smaller. Like other bluefins, it has a robust, torpedo-shaped body, dark metallic blue above and silvery below, and can maintain body temperatures above ambient water through a countercurrent heat-exchange system (rete mirabile), allowing it to forage in cold temperate waters.

Distribution and life history

Spawning occurs in the western North Pacific, mainly in the East China Sea and the Sea of Japan, in spring and summer. Many juveniles migrate across the Pacific to feed off the west coast of North America before returning west to spawn. Some fish also occur in the South Pacific off Australia and New Zealand.

Fishery and conservation

The species supports major purse-seine and longline fisheries, much of the catch going to Japan, where bluefin commands the highest prices of any fish at auction. Decades of intense fishing reduced the spawning stock to a small fraction of its unfished level by the 2010s, prompting international management measures through the Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission and the Inter-American Tropical Tuna Commission. Stock assessments in the 2020s indicated the population had begun to rebuild ahead of schedule, reaching interim recovery targets, though conservation groups continue to urge caution.
