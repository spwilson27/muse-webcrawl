Middle Platonism

Middle Platonism is the modern name for the stage of development of Platonism from roughly 80 BC, when Antiochus of Ascalon rejected the scepticism of the New Academy, until the emergence of Neoplatonism in the 3rd century AD with Plotinus. Middle Platonists revived dogmatic interpretation of Plato's dialogues, systematized his doctrines, and absorbed elements from Aristotelianism, Stoicism, and Pythagoreanism, while arguing against rival schools.

Doctrines

Middle Platonists commonly posited a hierarchy of principles: a supreme God or first intellect, the Platonic Forms — often reinterpreted as the thoughts of God — and a lower world soul or demiurgic power mediating between the intelligible and material worlds, drawing heavily on Plato's Timaeus. Thinkers such as Numenius distinguished a first God, at rest, from a second, active demiurge. Ethics centred on "likeness to God" (homoiōsis theōi), drawn from the Theaetetus, as the goal of life. Several Middle Platonists, notably Plutarch, grappled with the problem of evil, some positing an irrational or malevolent soul in matter.

Texts and influence

Important surviving works include Plutarch's philosophical essays, the Handbook of Platonism (Didaskalikos) attributed to Alcinous, Apuleius' On Plato, and the allegorical writings of Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish thinker whose synthesis of Plato with biblical exegesis deeply influenced early Christian theology. Middle Platonism shaped the philosophical vocabulary of the Church Fathers and provided the immediate background against which Plotinus developed Neoplatonism.
