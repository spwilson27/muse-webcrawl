Black Square (Russian: Чёрный квадрат) is a 1915 oil painting by Russian artist Kazimir Malevich. It consists of a black square on a white ground and is one of the most famous works of modern art and a founding icon of the Suprematist movement. Malevich painted at least four versions of the work between 1915 and the early 1930s; the first version is held by the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow.

History

Malevich first conceived the black square in 1913 as part of a backdrop design for the Futurist opera Victory over the Sun. The painted version was unveiled at the 0.10 exhibition of Futurist art in Petrograd in December 1915, where it was hung high across a corner of the room — the position traditionally reserved for religious icons in Russian homes — a gesture that announced the work as an icon of a new artistic age. At the same exhibition Malevich launched Suprematism with a manifesto declaring the square the "zero point of painting," from which a purely abstract art of feeling would begin.

Analysis

For Malevich, the square represented pure feeling and the end of the object-bound art of the past: "the visual phenomena of the objective world are, in themselves, meaningless; the significant thing is feeling." The heavily cracked surface (craquelure) of the first version reveals layers of earlier paint beneath, and X-ray analysis has shown that Malevich painted the square over two earlier compositions, including a Cubo-Futurist work.

Versions and legacy

Malevich returned to the motif repeatedly: a 1923 version and a version from around 1929 are also in Russian state collections, and a smaller version dated about 1930 accompanied his coffin — the square was displayed at his funeral in 1935 and marked his grave. Black Square has become one of the most frequently cited, reproduced, and parodied images in modern art, widely regarded as the point of departure for abstract painting.
