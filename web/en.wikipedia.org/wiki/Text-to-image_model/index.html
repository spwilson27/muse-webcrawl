Text-to-image model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An example of an image synthesized by a text-to-image model from a natural-language prompt]

A text-to-image model is a machine learning model which takes a natural language description as input and produces one or more images matching that description. Such models became widely available to the public in the early 2020s and are typically based on diffusion processes operating in a compressed latent space, guided by text encodings from large language–vision models.

History

Early attempts in the mid-2010s used generative adversarial networks to produce small, blurry images from captions. OpenAI's DALL-E (2021) demonstrated that transformer models trained on image–text pairs could generate coherent illustrations. In 2022, diffusion-based systems — DALL-E 2, Google's Imagen, and the open-source Stable Diffusion — brought photorealistic quality and public accessibility, followed by Midjourney and later models such as DALL-E 3, Flux, and Imagen 3.

Architecture

Most modern systems combine a text encoder, such as CLIP or a T5-style language model, with a generative backbone — usually a diffusion model (often a U-Net or diffusion transformer) that iteratively denoises random noise into an image conditioned on the text embedding. Latent diffusion performs this process in the latent space of a variational autoencoder, greatly reducing computation.

Uses and concerns

Text-to-image models are used in illustration, design, advertising, entertainment, and prototyping. They have also raised controversies over training-data copyright, the displacement of artists, deepfakes, bias in generated imagery, and the energy cost of training and inference.
