Liang Bua is a limestone cave on the island of Flores in Indonesia, near the town of Ruteng in Manggarai Regency. The cave, whose name means "cool cave" in the local Manggarai language, became world famous in 2003 as the discovery site of Homo floresiensis, the diminutive extinct hominin popularly known as the "Hobbit".

Discovery

The cave was investigated from the 1950s onward by the Dutch missionary and archaeologist Theodor Verhoeven and later by Indonesian archaeologists. In September 2003, a joint Indonesian–Australian team led by Raden Panji Soejono and Mike Morwood recovered a small, nearly complete hominin skeleton (specimen LB1) from Pleistocene deposits some six metres below the cave floor. Announced in the journal Nature in 2004, the skeleton belonged to an individual only about one metre tall with a brain volume of roughly 380–420 cm³, comparable to that of a chimpanzee.

Homo floresiensis

Further excavation recovered remains of at least nine to fourteen individuals, along with stone tools and the bones of extinct animals including the dwarf elephant relative Stegodon, giant rats, and Komodo dragons. Dating revisions published in 2016 placed the hominin remains between about 100,000 and 60,000 years old, older than initially reported. Debates over whether the fossils represent a distinct species or pathological modern humans have largely settled in favour of a separate species, likely descended from an early dispersal of Homo erectus (or a more primitive relative) that underwent island dwarfism.

The cave today

Liang Bua is now a visitor site with a small museum nearby displaying replicas of the finds. Ongoing excavations continue to explore the cave's deep stratigraphy, which spans the last 190,000 years.
