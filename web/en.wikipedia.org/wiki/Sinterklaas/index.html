Sinterklaas (Dutch pronunciation: [sɪntərˈklaːs]) is a legendary figure based on Saint Nicholas, the patron saint of children. The feast of Sinterklaas celebrates the name day of Saint Nicholas on 6 December, but in the Netherlands the main festivities take place on the evening of 5 December, known as pakjesavond ("presents evening"). Sinterklaas is the primary source of the popular Christmas icon Santa Claus.

Description

Sinterklaas is an elderly, stately and serious man with white hair and a long, full beard. He wears a long red cape over a traditional white bishop's alb and a red mitre, and he carries a long ceremonial shepherd's staff with a fancy curled top. He rides a white horse named Amerigo (in recent years renamed Ozosnel after criticism of the original name). In his big red book, Sinterklaas keeps a record of whether each child has been good or naughty in the past year.

He is traditionally assisted by helpers called Pieten (singular: Piet), historically depicted as Zwarte Piet ("Black Pete"), a figure whose appearance has become the subject of sustained public debate in the Netherlands and Belgium since the 2010s; many celebrations now use soot-smudged "roetveegpieten" instead.

The celebration

Arrival (intocht)

Sinterklaas traditionally arrives in mid-November by steamboat (stoomboot) from Spain, where he is said to reside. His arrival, the intocht, is celebrated in towns and cities across the country with parades, and the national arrival is broadcast live on television. Children welcome him by singing traditional Sinterklaas songs.

Schoen zetten and pakjesavond

Between the arrival and 5 December, children put out a shoe (schoen zetten) by the fireplace or door, often with a carrot or hay for the horse and a drawing for Sinterklaas, and find small treats such as chocolate letters, pepernoten, kruidnoten, marzipan and speculaas in the morning. On pakjesavond, gifts are exchanged, frequently accompanied by humorous, often rhyming poems (Sinterklaasgedichten) and creatively disguised presents called surprises.

Origins and history

The figure derives from Saint Nicholas of Myra, a 4th-century Greek bishop famed for secret gift-giving, such as dowries left in shoes. The Dutch tradition developed through the Middle Ages and the early modern period; after the Protestant Reformation, civic authorities attempted to suppress the feast, but it persisted as a domestic celebration. In the 19th century the schoolteacher Jan Schenkman's 1850 book Sint Nikolaas en zijn Knecht helped standardise the modern imagery, including the steamboat arrival from Spain and the Moorish servant.

Dutch emigrants carried the tradition to New Amsterdam, where "Sinterklaas" evolved phonetically into "Santa Claus" in the newly independent United States, eventually merging with Father Christmas traditions.

Controversy

Since the early 2010s the character of Zwarte Piet has drawn protests and counter-protests, with critics calling the traditional blackface portrayal a racist stereotype and defenders framing it as an innocent children's tradition. Municipalities, broadcasters and retailers have increasingly adopted chimney-soot ("roetveeg") Piets, while the debate remains a recurring feature of Dutch public life each November.
