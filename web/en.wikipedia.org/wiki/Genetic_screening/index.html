Genetic screening

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic screening is the process of testing a population or an individual for the presence of particular genetic variants, mutations, or chromosomal abnormalities, typically in order to identify people who have, are at risk of developing, or may pass on a genetic disorder. Screening differs from diagnostic genetic testing in that it is usually offered to people without symptoms or a specific clinical indication, on the basis of membership in a population group.

Types

Newborn screening programs test infants shortly after birth for a panel of treatable inherited conditions, beginning with phenylketonuria (PKU) in the 1960s following Robert Guthrie's bacterial inhibition assay; most jurisdictions now screen for dozens of metabolic and endocrine disorders. Carrier screening identifies individuals who carry a recessive variant, such as those for Tay–Sachs disease, cystic fibrosis, or sickle cell disease. Prenatal screening estimates the likelihood of fetal chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome, while preimplantation genetic testing screens embryos created by in vitro fertilization.

Population programs

Well-known population programs include premarital carrier screening for Tay–Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jewish communities, which reduced births of affected children dramatically, and thalassemia screening programs in Cyprus, Sardinia, and other Mediterranean populations, which substantially lowered the incidence of thalassemia major.

Ethical issues

Genetic screening raises questions about informed consent, privacy, the psychological effects of risk information, potential discrimination (partly addressed by laws such as the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act in the United States), and the historical legacy of eugenics, which makes coercive screening programs especially controversial.
