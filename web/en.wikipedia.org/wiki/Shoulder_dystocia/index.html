Shoulder dystocia

Shoulder dystocia is an obstetric emergency that occurs when, after delivery of the fetal head, the baby's anterior shoulder becomes stuck behind the mother's pubic bone, or the posterior shoulder is obstructed by the sacral promontory, preventing delivery of the body with normal gentle traction. It is often recognized by retraction of the delivered head against the perineum ("turtle sign").

Risk factors and complications

Risk factors include fetal macrosomia, maternal diabetes, obesity, post-term pregnancy, and prior shoulder dystocia, though most cases occur in normal-weight babies and are unpredictable. Complications for the baby include brachial plexus injury (such as Erb's palsy), clavicle or humerus fracture, hypoxia, and death; complications for the mother include postpartum hemorrhage and severe perineal tears.

Management

Management follows drill-based protocols (mnemonic HELPERR or ALARMER). First-line maneuvers are the McRoberts maneuver (hyperflexing the mother's thighs) combined with suprapubic pressure. Internal maneuvers include delivery of the posterior arm, rotational maneuvers (Rubin, Woods screw), and the Gaskin maneuver (all-fours position). Last-resort measures include the Zavanelli maneuver (pushing the head back for cesarean delivery) and deliberate clavicle fracture. Occurrence is roughly 0.2–3% of vaginal births.
