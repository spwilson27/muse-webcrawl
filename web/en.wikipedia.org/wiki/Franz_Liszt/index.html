Franz Liszt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Franz Liszt at the piano in old age, photographed in Weimar: the abbé in black with his long white hair and famous profile]

Franz Liszt (22 October 1811 – 31 July 1886) was a Hungarian composer, virtuoso pianist and conductor, the most celebrated pianist of the nineteenth century and a radical innovator of form. He invented the symphonic poem and the solo recital, developed thematic transformation, pushed harmony toward atonality in his last piano pieces, and as Weimar's Kapellmeister premiered the new music of his age — Wagner's Lohengrin among it.

His works include the Hungarian Rhapsodies, the Transcendental Études, the Piano Sonata in B minor (1853), the Années de pèlerinage, two piano concertos, the Faust and Dante symphonies, and a mass of transcriptions through which he spread other composers' music across Europe.

The virtuoso years

A child prodigy taught by Czerny and (in composition) Salieri, Liszt heard Paganini in 1832 and resolved to do for the piano what the violinist had done for his instrument. The touring years 1839–47 unleashed 'Lisztomania' (Heine's coinage) on Europe: swooning audiences, gloves and cigar-ends fought over, and a technique — octaves, leaps, orchestral masses of sound — that redefined the piano. His generosity was as famous: he taught unpaid and championed everyone from Berlioz to the young Grieg.

Weimar and the abbé

Settling in Weimar (1848–61) with Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein, he turned conductor-composer, then, thwarted in his hope to marry her, moved between Rome, Weimar and Budapest — the 'vie trifurquée' — taking minor orders in 1865 (hence 'Abbé Liszt'). Wagner, who eloped with and married Liszt's daughter Cosima, was his son-in-law and his cause. He died at Bayreuth in 1886 during the festival.
