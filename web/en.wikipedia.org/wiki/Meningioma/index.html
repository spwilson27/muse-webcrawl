Meningioma, also known as meningeal tumor, is typically a slow-growing tumor that forms from the meninges, the membranous layers surrounding the brain and spinal cord. Symptoms depend on the location and occur as a result of the tumor pressing on nearby tissue. Many cases occur for no specific reason. Occasionally they may occur in families with neurofibromatosis type 2. Risk factors include exposure to ionizing radiation, such as during radiation therapy.

Meningiomas are the most common primary tumors of the central nervous system, accounting for roughly a third of all primary brain and spinal tumors. They arise from arachnoid cap cells of the arachnoid villi in the meninges. Most are benign (WHO grade I), though a minority are atypical (grade II) or malignant/anaplastic (grade III).

Signs and symptoms

Small tumors are often found incidentally on imaging performed for other reasons and may cause no symptoms. Larger tumors can cause headaches, seizures, focal neurological deficits such as limb weakness, vision or hearing changes, and personality changes, depending on the site of compression.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically by medical imaging, such as contrast-enhanced MRI, where meningiomas appear as well-circumscribed, dural-based masses often showing a "dural tail". Biopsy and histopathological grading confirm the diagnosis.

Treatment

If symptoms are minor, periodic monitoring with imaging may be all that is required. Symptomatic or enlarging tumors are usually removed surgically; complete resection including the dural attachment is often curative. Radiation therapy, including stereotactic radiosurgery, may be used for inoperable, residual, or recurrent tumors.

Epidemiology

Meningiomas occur more commonly in women and in older adults. The term was coined by Harvey Cushing in 1922.
