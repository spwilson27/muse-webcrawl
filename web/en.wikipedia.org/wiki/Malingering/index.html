Malingering

Malingering is the deliberate fabrication, feigning, or gross exaggeration of physical or psychological symptoms for a secondary, external gain — such as avoiding work or military duty, obtaining financial compensation or drugs, escaping criminal prosecution, or securing admission to hospital. The word derives from the French malingre, "sickly."

Classification

Malingering is not classified as a mental disorder in the DSM-5; it appears as a "V code" condition that may be a focus of clinical attention. It is distinguished from factitious disorder, in which symptoms are produced to assume the sick role without obvious external incentives, and from somatic symptom and conversion disorders, in which symptoms are not intentionally produced. Clinicians may suspect malingering when there is a marked discrepancy between claimed disability and objective findings, lack of cooperation with evaluation, a medico-legal context, or antisocial personality features — though the DSM cautions these indicators are not proof.

Detection

Assessment relies on careful history, collateral records, observation of inconsistencies, and validated psychological instruments containing symptom-validity and performance-validity scales, such as the MMPI-2 validity indicators, the Structured Interview of Reported Symptoms (SIRS), and stand-alone effort tests used in neuropsychology. No single test is definitive, and falsely accusing a genuinely ill patient carries serious ethical and clinical risks, so the diagnosis is made cautiously.

Contexts

Malingering is a concern in military medicine (where the term originated), workers' compensation and disability evaluations, forensic psychiatry, emergency medicine, and prison health care. Its prevalence in medico-legal settings has been estimated at substantial rates in some surveys, though precise figures are contested.
