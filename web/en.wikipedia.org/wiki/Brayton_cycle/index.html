The Brayton cycle is a thermodynamic cycle that describes the workings of a constant-pressure heat engine. It is the basis of the gas turbine engine and the air-breathing jet engine. The cycle is named after the American engineer George Brayton, who developed a piston engine using it in the 1870s; the modern version, applied to continuous-flow turbomachinery, is also called the Joule cycle.

Processes

The ideal Brayton cycle consists of four processes:

  1. Isentropic compression – ambient air is drawn into a compressor and pressurised.
  2. Isobaric heat addition – the compressed air passes through a combustion chamber where fuel is burned at constant pressure.
  3. Isentropic expansion – the hot, pressurised gas expands through a turbine, producing work.
  4. Isobaric heat rejection – the exhaust gas is discharged to the atmosphere.

The thermal efficiency of the ideal cycle depends only on the pressure ratio rp and the ratio of specific heats γ:

η = 1 − rp−(γ−1)/γ

Applications

Brayton-cycle gas turbines are used for aircraft propulsion, marine propulsion, and stationary power generation. Efficiency can be improved with regeneration (using exhaust heat to preheat compressed air), intercooling, reheat, and by combining the gas turbine with a steam Rankine cycle in a combined cycle plant, which can exceed 60% efficiency.

History

John Barber patented a gas turbine concept in 1791, and George Brayton's "Ready Motor" of 1872 ran on the cycle with separate piston compressor and expander. The first practical gas turbines in the early 20th century realised the continuous-flow version of the cycle.
