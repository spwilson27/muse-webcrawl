The Elder Futhark (also called the Elder Fuþark, Germanic Futhark or Scandinavian Futhark) is the oldest form of the runic alphabets. It was used by Germanic-speaking peoples during the Migration Period for the Northwest Germanic dialects, including Proto-Norse. Its 24 characters are traditionally arranged in three groups of eight called ættir (families). Inscriptions are found on artefacts such as jewellery, amulets, tools, weapons and runestones across Europe, from roughly the 2nd to the 8th century AD.

Origins and use

The script probably developed in the 1st century AD from an Old Italic alphabet (such as Raetic or Etruscan) through contact in the Alpine region. The oldest known inscriptions, such as that on the Vimose comb (c. 160 AD), come from Denmark and northern Germany. The name "futhark" is an acronym of the first six runes: f, u, þ, a, r, k. Around the 8th century the Elder Futhark fell out of use in Scandinavia, where it developed into the simplified 16-rune Younger Futhark, while in England it expanded into the Anglo-Saxon futhorc. The complete row is preserved on objects such as the Kylver Stone from Gotland.
