Thirteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

Ratified6 December 1865

The Thirteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution abolished slavery and involuntary servitude throughout the United States, except as punishment for a crime. It was passed by Congress on 31 January 1865 and ratified on 6 December 1865, months after the end of the Civil War and Abraham Lincoln's assassination.

Text

Section 1: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction." Section 2 gives Congress power to enforce the article by appropriate legislation.

History and effects

Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1863) had freed enslaved people only in rebellious states and rested on war powers; the amendment settled the question permanently and nationwide, freeing some four million people. The punishment exception was later exploited through convict leasing and Black Codes in the South. The amendment was the first of the three Reconstruction Amendments, followed by the Fourteenth (citizenship, equal protection) and Fifteenth (voting rights). It built on the escape networks of the Underground Railroad and the constitutional theory of The Federalist Papers.
