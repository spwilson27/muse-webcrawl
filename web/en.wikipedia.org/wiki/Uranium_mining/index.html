Uranium mining is the extraction of uranium ore from the ground for use in nuclear power generation and, historically, nuclear weapons. The mined ore is milled to produce uranium oxide concentrate known as yellowcake, the feedstock for conversion and enrichment. World production has in recent decades been led by Kazakhstan, followed by Canada, Namibia and Australia.

Methods

Uranium is extracted by open-pit mining for shallow deposits, by conventional underground mining for deep high-grade ores such as those of Canada's Athabasca Basin (Cigar Lake and McArthur River are among the world's richest), and increasingly by in-situ leaching (ISL), in which an oxidising solution is pumped through the ore body underground and the uranium-bearing fluid recovered from wells — the dominant method in Kazakhstan. Olympic Dam in South Australia produces uranium as a co-product of copper mining.

History

Uranium was mined in Bohemia for radium production before the Second World War; large-scale mining began with the wartime and Cold War weapons programmes, notably in the Belgian Congo (Shinkolobwe), Canada, the United States (Colorado Plateau, Grants district) and the Soviet Union. Commercial demand for reactor fuel expanded the industry in the 1970s, followed by long price slumps, a speculative peak in 2007, and a further decline after the 2011 Fukushima accident.

Health and environment

Uranium mining exposes workers to radon gas and radioactive dust; lung cancer among early underground miners, including Navajo miners in the American Southwest and East German Wismut miners, is a well-documented occupational tragedy. Mill tailings retain most of the ore's radioactivity, principally radium-226 and thorium-230, and require long-term containment. Modern operations are regulated for radiation protection, groundwater restoration and site remediation.
