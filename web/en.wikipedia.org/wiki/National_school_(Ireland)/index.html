National school (Ireland)

A national school (Irish: scoil náisiúnta) is a type of primary school in the Republic of Ireland. The national school system was established in 1831 by a letter from the Chief Secretary for Ireland, Edward Stanley, which set out a framework for a non-denominational but state-funded system of elementary education, administered by Commissioners of National Education. Although intended to be religiously neutral, in practice most national schools came to be managed by local clergy and acquired a denominational ethos.

History

Before 1831, elementary education in Ireland was provided largely by hedge schools and by charitable and religious societies such as the Kildare Place Society. Stanley's letter of 1831 proposed schools in which children of all faiths would learn literacy, numeracy and moral subjects together, with separate religious instruction provided by clergy of the different denominations. The Board of Commissioners of National Education supervised the building of schools, the training of teachers and the approval of textbooks.

The system expanded rapidly through the nineteenth century and, by the time of Irish independence in 1922, national schools formed the backbone of primary education in the new state. The system survived largely intact in the Republic of Ireland, where the state continues to fund teacher salaries and much of the running costs while local boards of management, usually chaired by the parish priest or another patron nominee, operate the schools.

Organisation

National schools are state-funded but not state-owned: the land and buildings are typically held by a patron, most often the local Catholic bishop, a Church of Ireland authority, or a multi-denominational body such as Educate Together. Each school has a board of management, an in-school management team led by the principal, and follows the national primary curriculum set by the Department of Education. Irish (Gaeilge) is a compulsory subject in most national schools.
