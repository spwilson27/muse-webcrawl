Sunscreen

Sunscreen protects skin against ultraviolet radiation

Sunscreen, also known as sunblock or sun cream, is a topical product for the skin that helps protect against sunburn and skin cancer by absorbing or reflecting ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Sunscreens are rated by sun protection factor (SPF), which measures protection against UVB; "broad-spectrum" products also protect against UVA. Dermatological organisations recommend regular use, alongside shade and protective clothing, to reduce photoaging and the incidence of melanoma and other skin cancers.

How it works

Organic (chemical) filters such as avobenzone and octinoxate absorb UV photons and dissipate the energy as heat. Inorganic (mineral) filters — zinc oxide and titanium dioxide — absorb and scatter UV; modern micronised forms are transparent rather than leaving a white cast. An SPF 30 sunscreen, applied at the tested thickness of 2 mg/cm², transmits about 1/30 of erythemal UVB, blocking roughly 97%; SPF 50 blocks about 98%. Because users typically apply far less than the tested amount, real-world protection is substantially lower, so generous application and reapplication every two hours (and after swimming) are advised.

History and regulation

Early commercial sunscreens appeared in the 1930s–40s: chemist Franz Greiter developed "Gletscher Crème" (1938) and later introduced the SPF concept in 1962, while Benjamin Green marketed Red Vet Pet, a red veterinary-petroleum-based product used by US airmen, which became Coppertone. Sunscreens are regulated as over-the-counter drugs in the United States and as cosmetics in the European Union, which approves a wider range of modern filters.

Debates

Research confirms that regular sunscreen use reduces squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma risk. Concerns studied include systemic absorption of some organic filters (with the FDA requesting further safety data, while continuing to recommend use), effects on vitamin D synthesis (considered minor in practice), and environmental impacts: oxybenzone and octinoxate have been banned in Hawaii and some other jurisdictions over coral bleaching concerns, though the scale of sunscreen's role remains debated.
