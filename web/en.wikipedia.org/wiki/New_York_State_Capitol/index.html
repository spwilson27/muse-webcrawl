The New York State Capitol is the seat of the New York State government, located in Albany, the state capital. Completed in 1899 after 32 years of construction, the massive granite building houses the New York State Legislature — the Senate and Assembly — and the ceremonial offices of the Governor. It is renowned for its eclectic architecture, elaborate stone carving, and famous interior staircases, and forms the centerpiece of the Empire State Plaza complex.

Construction

Built between 1867 and 1899 at a cost of about $25 million — making it one of the most expensive government buildings of its time — the Capitol passed through the hands of several architectural teams. Thomas Fuller designed the initial Renaissance-classical scheme; Leopold Eidlitz and Henry Hobson Richardson reworked it in a Romanesque vein, producing the building's distinctive south front and the richly carved Assembly and Senate staircases; Isaac G. Perry completed the building, adding its granite dormers and central tower design, which was ultimately never built. Because of shifting designs, the building famously has no central dome.

Features

Interior highlights include the "Million Dollar Staircase" (the Great Western Staircase), adorned with hundreds of carved stone portraits of famous New Yorkers and ordinary citizens; the Senate Chamber, decorated with red granite, mahogany, and onyx; the Assembly Chamber, once distinguished by a vast sandstone vaulted ceiling that was later concealed for structural reasons; and the War Room, with murals tracing New York's military history. A fire in 1911 destroyed the State Library and parts of the western wing, prompting reconstruction. Exterior carving on the east front includes elaborate floral and figurative stonework by many hands.

The Capitol was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1979, and extensive restorations in the 21st century have repaired its roof, skylights, and chambers.
