Nick Bostrom (born 1973) is a Swedish philosopher at the University of Oxford known for his work on existential risk, artificial intelligence, and the far future of humanity. He founded and directed Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute (FHI) from 2005 until its closure in 2024, and co-founded the World Transhumanist Association (now Humanity+). His book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies (2014) brought the problem of controlling a future machine superintelligence to a mass audience and is widely credited with shaping the modern AI-safety field.

Existential risk and the simulation argument

Bostrom systematised the concept of existential risk — risks that could destroy humanity's long-term potential — and the methodology of prioritising them. His 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" presented the simulation argument: a trilemma holding that at least one of three propositions must be true — civilisations almost never reach a posthuman stage; posthuman civilisations almost never run many simulations of their ancestors; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. His earlier work, Anthropic Bias (2002), developed observation-selection theory, including the doomsday argument and related paradoxes of self-location.

Superintelligence

Superintelligence argued that if machine intelligence ever decisively surpassed human intelligence, the outcome could be determined by the goals instilled in it — goals that would not automatically include human welfare (the "orthogonality thesis" and instrumental convergence) — and that solving the control/alignment problem in advance is therefore urgent. The book influenced researchers and funders across the AI industry and helped seed the modern alignment research agenda; it also attracted criticism from those who judge such scenarios speculative relative to nearer-term harms. His later Deep Utopia (2024) asks what human life would mean in a world where AI had solved its material problems.

Controversy

In 2023, a racist email Bostrom had written as a student in 1996 resurfaced; he apologised for it, and Oxford investigated and issued a statement; the episode, and FHI's closure the following year amid an administrative dispute reported at the time, form part of his recent biography. His work remains central to longtermism and to debates about the governance of advanced AI.
