Twelve Tables

The Laws of the Twelve Tables (Latin: Leges Duodecim Tabularum) was the legislation that stood at the foundation of Roman law. Formally promulgated in 449 BC, the Tables consolidated earlier traditions and formed the centrepiece of the constitution of the Roman Republic, remaining in force in principle for centuries.

Background

The Tables arose from the Conflict of the Orders, the struggle between patricians and plebeians. Plebeians demanded that the customary law, administered and interpreted by patrician priests, be written down and published so it could not be applied arbitrarily. According to Livy, a commission of ten men, the Decemviri, produced ten tables in 451 BC and two more in 450 BC; the laws were inscribed on bronze (or ivory) tablets and displayed in the Forum. The originals were destroyed when the Gauls sacked Rome in 387 BC, and the text survives only in quotations by later authors.

Contents

The provisions covered legal procedure (summons, trial, enforcement of judgments), debt — including harsh remedies for defaulting debtors — family law and paternal power (patria potestas), inheritance and guardianship, property and torts such as theft and damage, public and sacred law, and criminal penalties. A notorious clause forbade intermarriage between patricians and plebeians, repealed within a few years. Roman schoolboys were still memorising the Tables in Cicero's youth, and Cicero called them the source of all public and private law.
