Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act

The Tariff Act of 1930, commonly known as the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act, was a United States federal statute that implemented protectionist trade policies in the United States. Sponsored by Senator Reed Smoot and Representative Willis C. Hawley, it was signed into law by President Herbert Hoover on June 17, 1930. The act raised US tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods.

Background

Following World War I, American farmers suffered from overproduction and falling prices, and manufacturers sought protection from foreign competition. In his 1928 campaign Hoover promised tariff relief for agriculture, but the bill that emerged from Congress extended increases across the economy. A petition signed by 1,028 economists urged Hoover to veto the bill, and industrialist Henry Ford personally lobbied against it, but the bill passed and Hoover signed it.

Impact

The act raised the average tariff rate on dutiable imports to historically high levels — estimates vary, but effective rates rose substantially, and deflation during the Depression further raised the real burden of specific duties. US trading partners retaliated: Canada, then the largest US trading partner, imposed new tariffs, and dozens of countries raised barriers. World trade collapsed: global trade fell by roughly two-thirds in value between 1929 and 1934, and US imports and exports each fell by more than half.

Assessment

Many economists argue that Smoot–Hawley worsened and prolonged the Great Depression by deepening the fall in international trade and provoking retaliation, though there is debate about the magnitude of its direct effect relative to the collapse in demand and the gold standard. The act became a symbol of "beggar-thy-neighbour" policies. In response, Congress passed the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934, authorizing the president to negotiate bilateral tariff reductions, beginning the long US shift toward trade liberalization that culminated in the GATT and WTO systems.
