Economic integration is the process by which different countries reduce or eliminate barriers to trade and coordinate their economic policies, so that goods, services, capital and (at deeper levels) labour move between them more freely. It is the economic side of regionalism, and its most advanced example is the European Union, whose single market and monetary union for eurozone members go far beyond a simple trade agreement.

Stages of integration

The economist Béla Balassa (1961) set out the standard sequence of deepening integration:

  • Preferential trading area — reduced (but not eliminated) tariffs for member countries.
  • Free trade area — no tariffs or quotas between members, but each keeps its own external tariffs (e.g. USMCA, EFTA).
  • Customs union — free trade plus a common external tariff (e.g. Mercosur, the EU–Turkey customs union).
  • Common (single) market — a customs union plus free movement of capital and labour and harmonised regulation (the EU single market; the EEA).
  • Economic union — a common market with harmonised fiscal and economic policies; an economic and monetary union adds a shared currency and central bank (the eurozone).
  • Total economic integration — unification of monetary, fiscal and social policies under supranational institutions, verging on political union.

Theory

Jacob Viner's theory of customs unions (1950) distinguishes trade creation (shifting production to more efficient member suppliers, which raises welfare) from trade diversion (shifting it away from more efficient outsiders, which lowers it). Later theory emphasises economies of scale, competition effects, investment creation and the credibility that integration gives to domestic policy reform. Integration also has costs: members surrender independent trade and, at deeper stages, monetary and fiscal policy, and regions within countries may gain or lose unevenly.

Examples and trends

Beyond the EU, major schemes include ASEAN and its free trade area in Southeast Asia, the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA, trading from 2021, the largest free trade area by membership), the Pacific Alliance and Mercosur in Latin America, and the Eurasian Economic Union. Britain's departure from the EU ("Brexit", 2020) is the most prominent modern case of deliberate disintegration. Global value chains have also produced "deep" bilateral and mega-regional agreements, such as the CPTPP, covering services, investment and regulation rather than tariffs alone.
