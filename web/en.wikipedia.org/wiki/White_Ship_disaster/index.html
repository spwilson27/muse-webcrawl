White Ship disaster
Date:25 November 1120
Location:English Channel, off Barfleur, Normandy
Vessel:Blanche-Nef ("White Ship"), captain Thomas FitzStephen
Deaths:~300, including William Adelin, only legitimate son of Henry I
Survivor:One (Berold, a butcher of Rouen, by tradition)

The White Ship disaster was the wreck of the Blanche-Nef on the night of 25 November 1120 in the English Channel near Barfleur. Among the roughly 300 who drowned was William Adelin, the only legitimate son and heir of King Henry I of England — a loss that precipitated a succession crisis and, ultimately, the civil war known as the Anarchy.

The voyage

Henry I had sailed ahead to England after a successful campaign in Normandy. The White Ship — a fine new vessel offered by Thomas FitzStephen, whose father had steered William the Conqueror's ship in 1066 — was to carry the prince William, his half-siblings Richard of Lincoln and Matilda of Perche, and much of the young Anglo-Norman nobility. Chroniclers report that the passengers distributed wine freely and that the crew and travellers were merry and drunk; priests who came to bless the ship were laughed away.

The wreck

Setting off at night in an apparent race to overtake the king's ship, the White Ship struck a submerged rock (the Quillebœuf rock) off Barfleur. Prince William was got away in a small boat but turned back at the cries of his half-sister Matilda; the overloaded boat was swamped. Only one man — a butcher from Rouen who had come aboard to collect a debt — survived by clinging to the mast, according to tradition.

Consequences

Henry I, who supposedly never smiled again, remarried but produced no further male heir. On his death in 1135 the throne was seized by Stephen of Blois, while Henry's daughter, the Empress Matilda, contested it through 19 years of civil war — the Anarchy — resolved only when her son became Henry II in 1154.
