Gustafson's law

Gustafson's law (also known as Gustafson–Barsis's law) is a law in computer engineering, formulated by John L. Gustafson and Edwin H. Barsis in 1988, that gives the theoretical scaled speedup obtainable from parallel computing when the problem size grows with the number of processors. It is usually contrasted with the more pessimistic Amdahl's law.

Formulation

Gustafson observed that in practice, when more computing power becomes available, users scale up the size of their problems to keep execution time roughly constant. If s is the fraction of the (enlarged) computation that must be performed serially and P is the number of processors, the scaled speedup is:

S = P + (1 − P)·s

Because the serial fraction applies to the fixed execution time rather than to a fixed problem size, the speedup grows almost linearly with the number of processors, even when the serial portion is substantial.

Interpretation

Amdahl's law assumes a fixed problem size, so that the serial fraction ultimately caps the speedup. Gustafson's law rests on the empirical observation — drawn from experiments at Sandia National Laboratories, where speedups above 1,000 were obtained on a 1,024-processor hypercube — that problem size tends to scale with machine size. The two laws are mathematically consistent; they differ in what is held constant. Gustafson's perspective underlies the notion of weak scaling used to benchmark supercomputers, as opposed to the strong scaling of a fixed workload.
