Modern naval tactics

A modern carrier strike group operating as an integrated task force

Modern naval tactics concern the employment of naval forces in battle since the mid-20th century, an era in which the gun-armed battleship has been displaced by aircraft carriers, guided missiles, nuclear submarines and networked sensors. Tactical engagements now typically occur at ranges of tens or hundreds of kilometres, often beyond the horizon of either combatant.

Key developments

The decisive weapon of the Second World War at sea was the carrier aircraft, which sank fleets without opposing ships sighting each other. After 1945 the anti-ship missile transformed tactics again: even small fast attack craft could threaten major warships, as shown by the sinking of the Israeli destroyer Eilat in 1967. Defences evolved in layers — airborne early warning, area-defence missiles on ships such as Aegis cruisers, point-defence guns and electronic decoys.

Principles

Modern doctrine emphasises detection and identification before being detected, concentration of missile fire ("massed fires") to saturate defences, and the integration of surface ships, submarines, aircraft and satellites into a single sensor-and-shooter network. Exercises such as RIMPAC rehearse coalition task-group operations, while anti-access/area-denial strategies aim to keep hostile fleets at a distance with land-based missiles and aircraft.
