Ewing sarcoma

SpecialtyOncology
SymptomsPain and swelling near the tumor, sometimes fever

Ewing sarcoma is a type of cancer that forms in bone or soft tissue. It is the second most common primary bone cancer in children and adolescents, after osteosarcoma. The tumor was described by American pathologist James Ewing in 1921. Ewing sarcoma most often arises in the long bones of the legs and arms, the pelvis, and the chest wall, but it can occur in almost any bone or soft tissue.

Signs and symptoms

The most common symptom is pain at the site of the tumor, often with swelling or a palpable mass. Symptoms may be present for months before diagnosis and can mimic infection or sports injury. Some patients have systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and fatigue, which are more common in metastatic disease.

Cause and genetics

Ewing sarcoma is driven by a characteristic chromosomal translocation. In about 85% of cases, the EWSR1 gene on chromosome 22 fuses with the FLI1 gene on chromosome 11 — t(11;22)(q24;q12) — creating an abnormal fusion transcription factor, EWS-FLI1, that drives tumor growth. Other cases involve related fusions between EWSR1 (or rarely FUS) and other members of the ETS transcription factor family, such as ERG. The disease is not inherited and has no known environmental cause; it occurs most frequently in people of European ancestry and is rare in African and East Asian populations.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on imaging (radiographs often show a destructive bone lesion with an "onion-skin" periosteal reaction), followed by biopsy. Under the microscope the tumor consists of sheets of small, round, uniform cells. Immunohistochemistry typically shows strong membranous CD99 expression, and molecular testing confirms the EWSR1 rearrangement. Staging includes imaging of the chest, bone scan or PET scan, and bone marrow sampling in some protocols, since metastases most often spread to the lungs, bone, and bone marrow.

Treatment

Treatment is multimodal and usually begins with multi-agent chemotherapy — commonly alternating cycles of vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide with ifosfamide and etoposide — followed by local control of the primary tumor with surgery and/or radiation therapy, and further chemotherapy. Nearly all patients receive chemotherapy because microscopic metastatic disease is assumed to be present even when scans show localized tumors.

Prognosis

Prognosis depends strongly on whether the disease has metastasized. Long-term survival for localized Ewing sarcoma is roughly 70%, while metastatic disease carries a considerably poorer outlook, particularly with bone or bone marrow metastases. Larger tumor volume, pelvic primary sites, and poor response to initial chemotherapy are adverse prognostic factors.
