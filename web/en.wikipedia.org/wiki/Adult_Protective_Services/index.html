Adult Protective Services (APS) are social services provided to abused, neglected or exploited older adults and adults with significant disabilities in the United States. APS programs investigate reports of abuse, neglect (including self-neglect) and financial exploitation, assess the adult's needs and risk, and arrange services intended to protect the adult while respecting their right to self-determination wherever possible.

Functions

APS agencies typically operate a reporting hotline, screen referrals, and send caseworkers to interview the adult and others involved. Where maltreatment is substantiated, workers may arrange in-home support, medical or mental-health evaluation, legal intervention (such as guardianship or protective orders), emergency shelter or assistance with benefits. Most states designate professionals such as doctors, nurses, police officers and bankers as mandatory reporters of suspected elder abuse.

History

Federal funding for protective services for adults began under amendments to the Social Security Act in the 1970s, and the Elder Justice Act of 2010 was the first comprehensive federal legislation addressing elder abuse, although APS remains primarily a state-administered system. The Older Americans Act supports related elder-rights programs, including the Long-Term Care Ombudsman.

Challenges

APS programs commonly report rising caseloads — especially financial exploitation cases — alongside flat budgets and workforce shortages. Balancing intervention with the autonomy of competent adults, and serving adults in facilities versus community settings, are recurring policy issues.
