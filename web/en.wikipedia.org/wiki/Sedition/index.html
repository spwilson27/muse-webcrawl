Sedition

Sedition laws have historically been used against political opposition movements.

Sedition is overt conduct, such as speech and organisation, that tends toward rebellion against the established order. Sedition often includes subversion of a constitution and incitement of discontent toward, or insurrection against, established authority. It is related to but distinct from treason: treason generally involves overt acts of war against or aid to an enemy of the state, whereas sedition typically covers speech, publication, and conspiracy aimed at stirring resistance. Laws against seditious libel — statements bringing the government into hatred or contempt — were a central instrument of censorship in early modern England and its colonies.

History

English common law punished seditious libel from the seventeenth century, and truth was long no defence ("the greater the truth, the greater the libel"). The American Sedition Act of 1798, under which opposition newspaper editors were jailed, provoked a famous backlash and helped define American free-speech traditions; it was allowed to expire in 1801. Britain prosecuted sedition sporadically into the twentieth century but abolished the offences of sedition and seditious libel in England and Wales in 2009.

United States law

The federal crime of seditious conspiracy (18 U.S.C. § 2384) punishes conspiring to overthrow the government by force or to oppose its authority by force; it carries up to 20 years' imprisonment and was used against, among others, Puerto Rican nationalists in the 1930s, Sheikh Omar Abdel-Rahman in 1995, and leaders of the Oath Keepers and Proud Boys convicted for their roles in the January 6 Capitol attack. The Smith Act of 1940, which criminalised advocacy of violent overthrow, was used against Communist Party leaders in the 1950s before the Supreme Court narrowed it in Yates v. United States (1957) and later protected advocacy short of incitement to imminent lawless action in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969).

Other jurisdictions

Sedition laws inherited from colonial rule remain controversial in many countries: India's Penal Code section 124A, dating from 1870, was used against figures from Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Mahatma Gandhi to modern journalists and protesters, and was held in abeyance by India's Supreme Court in 2022 pending review. Hong Kong's colonial-era sedition provisions were revived in prosecutions after 2020, and sedition offences continue to exist, under various names, in numerous common-law and civil-law states.
