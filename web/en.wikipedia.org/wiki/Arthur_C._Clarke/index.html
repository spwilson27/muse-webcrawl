Arthur C. Clarke

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arthur C. Clarke
Born16 December 1917, Minehead, England
Died19 March 2008, Colombo, Sri Lanka
Known for2001: A Space Odyssey; geostationary relay concept
HonoursKnight Bachelor (2000)
Clarke in Colombo.

Sir Arthur Charles Clarke (1917–2008) was a British science-fiction writer and futurist whose name is inseparable from spaceflight's popular imagination. In a 1945 paper he proposed relaying communications from satellites in what is now called the geostationary or "Clarke" orbit — the foundation of the global satellite industry — and he co-wrote the screenplay of 2001: A Space Odyssey with Stanley Kubrick.

A wartime radar officer and chairman of the British Interplanetary Society, Clarke wrote more than a hundred books of fiction and popular science, formulated the famous "Clarke's laws" (the third: any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic), and promoted the space elevator concept. He spent his later decades in Sri Lanka, diving and writing, and covered the Apollo missions for American television alongside Walter Cronkite.

Legacy in spaceflight

Beyond fiction, Clarke's 1945 geostationary proposal — published when rockets had barely reached space, using the V-2 rocket as his reference point — is considered one of the most consequential technical ideas of the century; the geostationary belt is formally named the Clarke Orbit by the International Astronomical Union. His undersea and space advocacy made him a bridge between the engineering community chronicled in this crawl and the public it served.
