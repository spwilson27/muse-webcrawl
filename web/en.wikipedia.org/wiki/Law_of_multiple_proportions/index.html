Law of multiple proportions

Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide illustrate the law of multiple proportions

In chemistry, the law of multiple proportions states that if two elements form more than one compound, then the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in a ratio of small whole numbers. The law, along with the law of definite proportions, forms the basis of stoichiometry.

History

The law was formulated by John Dalton, who presented it in connection with his atomic theory in the first decade of the nineteenth century (published in A New System of Chemical Philosophy, 1808). For example, carbon and oxygen form two compounds: in carbon monoxide, 12 grams of carbon combine with 16 grams of oxygen, while in carbon dioxide, 12 grams of carbon combine with 32 grams of oxygen. The masses of oxygen (16 and 32) stand in the simple ratio 1:2. Similarly, the hydrogen in water and hydrogen peroxide combining with a fixed mass of oxygen stands in a 2:1 ratio.

Significance

The law provided early experimental support for Dalton's atomic theory — the idea that elements consist of discrete atoms combining in simple integer ratios — and helped establish the atomic weights of the elements. Deviations occur in non-stoichiometric compounds, where composition varies continuously, and in very large molecules such as polymers, where the "small whole number" ratio is no longer readily apparent.
