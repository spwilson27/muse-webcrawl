Richard Meier

Richard Meier (born October 12, 1934) is an American abstract artist and architect whose geometric, white buildings — in the tradition of Le Corbusier — made him a leading figure of late modernism. A member of the "New York Five", he received the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 1984, then its youngest laureate, and designed the Getty Center in Los Angeles, one of the largest architectural commissions of the twentieth century.

Career

Meier graduated from Cornell University in 1957, worked briefly with Marcel Breuer and at Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, and established his own practice in 1963. His early houses — the Smith House (1967) and Douglas House (1973) — in brilliant white enamelled panels and glass, reworked Corbusian themes of ramps, pilotis, and free facades. With Peter Eisenman, Michael Graves, Charles Gwathmey, and John Hejduk he formed the "New York Five" (the "Whites"), featured in a landmark 1969 MoMA meeting and 1972 book.

Major works

The High Museum of Art in Atlanta (1983) launched a series of museum commissions: the Museum for Applied Arts in Frankfurt, the Barcelona Museum of Contemporary Art (1995), and the Ara Pacis Museum in Rome (2006), his first building in Rome's historic centre since the Fascist era — and a controversial one. His crowning work, the Getty Center (1997), arranges travertine-clad pavilions for the J. Paul Getty Trust along a Brentwood hilltop, visited by more than a million people a year.

Style

Meier's lifelong devotion to whiteness — "white is the most wonderful colour", he said, because within it all the colours of light can be seen — and to clear geometry, rational grids, and the play of light and shadow, made his work instantly recognisable. In 2018 he stepped back from day-to-day leadership of his firm amid allegations of sexual harassment reported by The New York Times, which he disputed while apologising to anyone offended; the practice was renamed Meier Partners in 2021.
