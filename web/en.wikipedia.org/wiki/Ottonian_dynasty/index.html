Ottonian dynasty

Ottonian dynasty
Country: East Francia / Kingdom of Germany, Kingdom of Italy, Holy Roman Empire
Founded: 919 (Henry I elected king); imperial from 962
Founder: Henry the Fowler
Final ruler: Henry II (died 1024)
Estate: Saxony

The Ottonian dynasty (German: Ottonen) was a Saxon dynasty of German monarchs (919–1024), named after three of its kings and Holy Roman Emperors, all named Otto — especially its first emperor, Otto I. The family, also known as the Saxon dynasty, succeeded the Conradines and preceded the Salian dynasty. Its rule is generally regarded as the first dynasty of the Holy Roman Empire.

Origins

The Ottonians were a family of the Saxon nobility, counts in the Harzgau, who rose to prominence in the 9th century; their semi-legendary founder was Liudolf (died 866), count and duke of eastern Saxony. His descendant Henry the Fowler, Duke of Saxony, was elected King of East Francia in 919, establishing the family's royal line.

Rulers

  • Henry I the Fowler (r. 919–936), king of East Francia; fortified the realm against the Magyars and Slavs and designated his son as successor, replacing elective fragmentation with hereditary succession in practice.
  • Otto I the Great (r. 936–973), emperor from 962; defeated the Magyars at Lechfeld (955) and founded the Empire.
  • Otto II (r. 973–983), emperor; married the Byzantine princess Theophanu; died at 28 after defeat by the Saracens in southern Italy.
  • Otto III (r. 983–1002), emperor from 996; pursued the programme of renovatio imperii Romanorum ("renewal of the Roman Empire") and promoted the Christianisation of Poland and Hungary.
  • Henry II (r. 1002–1024), emperor from 1014; the last Ottonian; later canonised as Saint Henry.

Characteristics of Ottonian rule

The Ottonian kings governed through itinerant kingship, moving between royal palaces (Pfalzen), and through the imperial church system (Reichskirche), endowing bishops with counties and immunities. Their period saw the Ottonian Renaissance in art and architecture — illuminated manuscripts from Reichenau, the bronze doors and column of Bernward of Hildesheim, and the first great Romanesque churches — as well as the monastic and literary work of figures such as Hrotsvitha of Gandersheim and Widukind of Corvey, whose Res gestae Saxonicae chronicles the dynasty.

End of the dynasty

Henry II died childless in 1024. The princes elected Conrad II, beginning the Salian dynasty. Ottonian power had nonetheless created durable structures: the imperial title, the stem duchies' integration into the realm, and an eastern policy that bound Poland, Bohemia and Hungary to the Empire.
