The Ethiopian–Adal war was a military conflict between the Christian Ethiopian Empire and the Muslim Adal Sultanate from 1529 to 1543. Led by Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmed Gragn", the left-handed), Adal's forces, reinforced with Ottoman muskets and artillery, conquered most of the Ethiopian highlands and came close to destroying the empire. The tide turned with the arrival of a Portuguese expedition under Cristóvão da Gama in 1541. Ahmad was killed at the Battle of Wayna Daga in February 1543, and Adal power collapsed soon afterwards.

Background

Relations between the Christian highland kingdom and the Muslim sultanates of the lowlands had been hostile for centuries, punctuated by trade and tribute disputes. In the 1520s Imam Ahmad, having seized control of Adal from Harar, refused tribute and defeated an Ethiopian army at Shimbra Kure in 1529, opening the way to all-out invasion.

Course of the war

Between 1531 and 1535 Ahmad's armies overran most of Ethiopia, burning churches and monasteries, deporting or massacring inhabitants, and forcing Emperor Lebna Dengel to flee from refuge to refuge until his death in 1540. At its height the conquest controlled perhaps three-quarters of the empire. Adal's success rested on cavalry, archers and an increasing number of Ottoman-supplied matchlockmen. In 1541, 400 Portuguese musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama, son of Vasco da Gama, landed at Massawa to aid the new emperor, Gelawdewos. Da Gama was captured and executed after the Battle of Wofla in August 1542, but the surviving Portuguese joined Gelawdewos's army. On 21 February 1543 at Wayna Daga, near Lake Tana, the combined force defeated the Adal army and Ahmad was shot dead, reputedly by a Portuguese musketeer.

Aftermath

Without Ahmad, Adal disintegrated into infighting and never again threatened the highlands, though the war left Ethiopia devastated and depopulated in many districts. The weakened state of both sides opened the way for the great Oromo migrations of the later sixteenth century, which reshaped the demography of the Horn of Africa. The Portuguese intervention also began Ethiopia's fateful entanglement with Catholic Jesuit missions, which led to civil war in the 1620s–30s.
