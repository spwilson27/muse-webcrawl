Technical diving

FieldUnderwater diving
TypeAdvanced recreational / professional diving
Key featuresMixed gas breathing, staged decompression, overhead environments
AgenciesTDI, IANTD, GUE, NAUI Tec, PADI TecRec

Technical diving (also known as tech diving) is a form of scuba diving that exceeds the generally accepted limits of recreational diving. It typically involves diving beyond a depth of about 40 metres (130 feet), mandatory staged decompression stops, the use of breathing gas mixtures other than air — such as helium-based trimix or enriched-air nitrox — and penetration into overhead environments such as caves and wrecks where a direct ascent to the surface is impossible.

Because these dives carry greater risk than recreational dives, technical diving requires specialised training, redundant equipment, careful gas planning, and disciplined team procedures. The discipline emerged in the late 1980s and 1990s as cave divers and deep wreck divers in the United States developed techniques to explore sites inaccessible to ordinary recreational methods.

Definition and scope

There is no single universal definition, but technical diving is commonly understood to include one or more of the following:

  • Diving deeper than the recreational limit of 40 m (130 ft)
  • Incurring a mandatory decompression obligation that prevents a direct ascent
  • Using multiple gas mixtures during a single dive, including helium-based mixes to reduce narcosis and oxygen toxicity
  • Penetrating an overhead environment — a cave, cavern, wreck, or ice ceiling — beyond the point where natural light is available
  • Using closed-circuit rebreathers or other advanced life-support equipment

Equipment

Technical divers typically carry redundant life-support systems. A common configuration is twin independent or manifolded cylinders (doubles) worn on the back, with additional stage or decompression cylinders slung at the side. Rebreather divers use closed- or semi-closed-circuit apparatus that recycles exhaled gas. Other characteristic items include backplate-and-wing buoyancy systems, long-hose regulators for gas sharing, backup lights, reels and guidelines for overhead penetration, and dive computers or tables supporting multi-gas decompression planning.

Risks

The principal hazards include decompression sickness from omitted or inadequate decompression, central nervous system oxygen toxicity from breathing high partial pressures of oxygen at depth, nitrogen narcosis, gas supply exhaustion — particularly serious under a physical or "virtual" decompression ceiling — and silt-outs or entanglement in caves and wrecks. These risks are managed through training, redundancy, conservative planning, and team diving protocols.

History

The roots of technical diving lie in the cave-diving community of Florida and the wreck-diving community of the US Northeast in the 1970s and 1980s. Explorers such as Sheck Exley systematised cave-diving safety procedures, while wreck divers pushed to deep sites such as the Andrea Doria. The term "technical diving" was popularised in the early 1990s by the magazine aquaCorps, and training agencies including IANTD (founded 1985) and TDI (founded 1994) formalised curricula. High-profile fatal accidents in the early 1990s prompted reforms in training standards and equipment configuration, including the standardised "Doing It Right" (DIR) approach developed by the Woodville Karst Plain Project and later Global Underwater Explorers (GUE).

Training

Technical training is progressive: entry-level courses cover advanced nitrox and decompression procedures, followed by trimix courses for deeper diving, and specialist certifications for cave, wreck penetration, and rebreather diving. Prerequisites generally include substantial recreational experience, rescue training, and demonstrated buoyancy control.
