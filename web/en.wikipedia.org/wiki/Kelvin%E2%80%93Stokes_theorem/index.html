Kelvin–Stokes theorem

The Kelvin–Stokes theorem, also known as simply the Stokes theorem (after George Gabriel Stokes), is a statement in vector calculus and differential geometry about the integration of differential forms on manifolds. In its classical form, it relates the surface integral of the curl of a vector field over a surface Σ to the line integral of the vector field around the boundary curve ∂Σ of the surface:

∬Σ (∇ × F) · dΣ = ∮∂Σ F · dr

History

The theorem was first communicated by Lord Kelvin (William Thomson) to Stokes in a letter dated 2 July 1850, and Stokes set it as a problem on the Smith's Prize examination of 1854 — hence the joint name. It is a three-dimensional special case of the generalised Stokes theorem, which equates the integral of a differential form over the boundary of a manifold with the integral of its exterior derivative over the whole manifold.

Interpretation

Physically, the theorem says that the circulation of a vector field around a closed loop equals the total flux of its curl through any surface bounded by the loop. In fluid dynamics it connects the circulation around a closed curve to the vorticity enclosed by it. In electromagnetism, the integral forms of Maxwell's equations — Faraday's law of induction and Ampère's law — are precisely statements of the Kelvin–Stokes theorem applied to the electric and magnetic fields.

Related results

The Kelvin–Stokes theorem is closely related to Green's theorem, which is its two-dimensional form in the plane, and to the divergence theorem. All three are unified by the generalised Stokes theorem, which also contains the fundamental theorem of calculus as its one-dimensional case.
