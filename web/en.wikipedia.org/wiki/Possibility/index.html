Possibility

Possibility is the condition or fact of being possible: a possible event, statement or state of affairs is one that could be true or could occur, as opposed to one that is actual (true in fact) or necessary (unable to be false) or impossible (unable to be true). Possibility is a central concept in metaphysics, logic and the philosophy of language.

Kinds of possibility

  • Logical possibility — freedom from contradiction. A proposition is logically possible if it could be true in some logically coherent scenario; this is the broadest sense of possibility.
  • Metaphysical possibility — possibility given the natures or essences of things; whether something could really be the case, beyond mere conceivability.
  • Nomological (physical) possibility — possibility consistent with the actual laws of nature; faster-than-light travel, for instance, may be logically possible but physically impossible.
  • Epistemic possibility — possibility relative to what someone knows; something is epistemically possible for a person if it is not ruled out by their knowledge.

Modal logic

In modal logic, possibility is represented by the diamond operator (◇): ◇p means "possibly p", and is defined in terms of necessity (□) as ◇p = ¬□¬p. The standard semantics, developed by Saul Kripke, interprets these claims using "possible worlds": a proposition is possible if it is true at some possible world, and necessary if true at all of them.

Possible worlds

Philosophers disagree about the status of merely possible things. Modal realists such as David Lewis held that possible worlds are as real as the actual world; others, such as "actualists", treat talk of possibilities as talk about representations, properties or fictions. In metaphysics, debates about possibility connect to questions about free will, causation, counterfactual reasoning and the nature of properties and essences.
