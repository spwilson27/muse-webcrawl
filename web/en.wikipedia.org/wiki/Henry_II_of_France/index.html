Henry II (French: Henri II; 31 March 1519 – 10 July 1559) was King of France from 1547 until his death in 1559. The second son of Francis I and Duchess Claude of Brittany, he became heir to the throne on the death of his elder brother Francis in 1536. As a child he spent over four years as a hostage in Spain, exchanged for his father's freedom after Francis I's capture at the Battle of Pavia — an experience contemporaries believed left a lasting mark on his character.

Marriage and court

In 1533, at the age of 14, Henry married Catherine de' Medici, the Florentine heiress of the Medici banking dynasty. The marriage was long overshadowed at court by the king's devotion to his mistress, Diane de Poitiers, who exercised great influence over him from his accession until his death and was his dominant favourite. Catherine eventually bore Henry ten children, three of whom — Francis II, Charles IX, and Henry III — became kings of France, in a succession of short and troubled reigns.

Reign

Henry pursued his father's wars against the Habsburgs. In 1552, allied with German Protestant princes by the Treaty of Chambord, he seized the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Toul, and Verdun in Lorraine. He also intervened in Italy; the French defeat at the Battle of St. Quentin (1557) was offset the following year by the Duke of Guise's capture of Calais, England's last possession in France. The resulting Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) ended the Italian Wars: France retained Calais and the Three Bishoprics but renounced its claims in Italy.

Domestically, Henry intensified the persecution of French Protestants (Huguenots), creating the chambre ardente to try heresy cases and issuing the Edict of Châteaubriant (1551); the repression foreshadowed the Wars of Religion that broke out three years after his death. He continued his father's patronage of Renaissance architecture and built the Château d'Anet for Diane de Poitiers.

Death

On 30 June 1559, during a tournament held in Paris to celebrate the Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis and the marriage of his daughter Elisabeth to Philip II of Spain, Henry was wounded when the lance of Gabriel de Montgomery, captain of his Scottish Guard, splintered and drove fragments through his visor into his eye and brain. Despite the attentions of the royal surgeons Ambroise Paré and Andreas Vesalius, the king died in agony eleven days later, on 10 July 1559, aged 40. His death fulfilled, in popular legend, a prophecy of Nostradamus, and it ended the practice of royal jousting in France. He was succeeded by his sickly 15-year-old son, Francis II.
