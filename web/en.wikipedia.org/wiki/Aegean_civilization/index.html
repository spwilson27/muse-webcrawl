Aegean civilization

Aegean civilization is a general term for the Bronze Age civilizations of Greece around the Aegean Sea. The term covers three overlapping but distinct cultures: the Minoan civilization of Crete, the Mycenaean civilization of mainland Greece, and the Cycladic civilization of the Aegean islands. These cultures flourished from roughly 3000 to 1100 BC.

Minoan civilization

The Minoans, named after the legendary King Minos, built great palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, and Malia. Their writing systems, Linear A (still undeciphered) and Cretan hieroglyphs, are among the earliest in Europe. Minoan art is noted for its naturalistic frescoes of marine life, bull-leaping, and processions. The eruption of Thera (Santorini), around 1600 BC, is often linked to the civilization's decline.

Mycenaean civilization

The Mycenaeans dominated mainland Greece from about 1600 BC. Their society, centred on fortified palaces such as Mycenae, Tiryns, and Pylos, is recorded in Linear B, an early form of Greek deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952. The Mycenaean world of warrior kings forms the historical backdrop to Homer's Iliad and Odyssey.

Collapse

Around 1200–1100 BC the Aegean palatial system collapsed as part of the wider Late Bronze Age collapse, ushering in the Greek Dark Ages.
