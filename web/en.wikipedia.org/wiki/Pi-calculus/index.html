Pi calculus

In the π-calculus, processes communicate by passing channel names, allowing the communication topology itself to change.

In theoretical computer science, the π-calculus (or pi calculus) is a process calculus originally developed by Robin Milner, Joachim Parrow and David Walker, as a continuation of work on the process calculus CCS (Calculus of Communicating Systems). The aim of the π-calculus is to be able to describe computations whose network of interconnections changes as the computation proceeds — so-called mobile processes.

Overview

The central feature of the π-calculus is the passing of channel names as data along channels themselves. A process can send a channel name to another process, which can then use that channel to communicate with processes it could not reach before. This gives the calculus the ability to model systems with dynamically evolving communication structure, such as mobile telephone networks, distributed systems and even the internal behaviour of cells.

Language elements

The π-calculus has a deliberately small set of constructs: processes may perform input and output on named channels, run in parallel composition, be replicated (creating copies that run indefinitely), create fresh restricted channel names, or terminate. Two processes are equivalent when they are bisimilar under the calculus's behavioural equivalence. Despite its minimalism, the π-calculus is Turing complete and can encode lambda calculus computations.

Influence

The π-calculus has influenced the design of concurrent programming languages and modelling frameworks (including the join calculus and languages such as Pict and occam-π), as well as models of business processes and biological systems. Milner's Turing Award citation recognised his work on process calculi including CCS and the π-calculus.
