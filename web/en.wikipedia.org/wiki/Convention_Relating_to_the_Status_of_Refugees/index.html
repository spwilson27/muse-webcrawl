Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, also known as the 1951 Refugee Convention or the Geneva Convention of 28 July 1951, is a United Nations multilateral treaty that defines who a refugee is, sets out the rights of individuals granted asylum, and the responsibilities of nations that grant it. Originally limited to Europeans displaced before 1951, it was universalised by the 1967 Protocol; about 149 states are party to one or both instruments.

Definition and core right

A refugee is a person outside their country owing to a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion, who is unable or (owing to such fear) unwilling to avail themselves of that country's protection. The Convention's core guarantee is non-refoulement (Article 33): no expulsion or return to a place where life or freedom would be threatened on those grounds — with narrow exceptions — a principle since recognised as customary law and extended by human-rights law (CAT, ECHR) to torture and inhuman treatment generally. Parties undertake a schedule of rights (work, welfare, documentation, travel documents, access to courts) scaled by the refugee's level of attachment to the host state. Regional instruments broaden the definition (the OAU Convention 1969 and Cartagena Declaration 1984 include flight from generalised violence and massive rights violations) — amendments by interpretation and parallel law, since reopening the 1951 text is politically radioactive.
