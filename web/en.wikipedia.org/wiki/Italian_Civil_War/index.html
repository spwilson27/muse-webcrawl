The Italian Civil War (Italian: Guerra civile italiana) was a civil war in the Kingdom of Italy fought during the Italian campaign of the Second World War between the Italian Co-belligerent Army and the Italian resistance movement on one side, and the collaborationist Italian Social Republic (RSI) and its Republican Armed Forces on the other, with German forces backing the latter. It lasted from the announcement of the Armistice of Cassibile on 8 September 1943 until the surrender of the RSI and German forces in Italy on 29 April 1945.

Background

On 25 July 1943, following the Allied invasion of Sicily, King Victor Emmanuel III dismissed and arrested Benito Mussolini, appointing Marshal Pietro Badoglio to head the government. Badoglio secretly negotiated an armistice with the Allies, announced on 8 September 1943. Germany immediately launched Operation Achse, disarming Italian units and occupying northern and central Italy. Mussolini was rescued by German paratroopers in the Gran Sasso raid and installed as head of the RSI, the "Republic of Salò", while the King and Badoglio fled south and Italy declared war on Germany on 13 October 1943.

The Resistance and the CLN

In German-occupied Italy, the National Liberation Committee (CLN) — uniting Communists, Socialists, Christian Democrats, Liberals and others — organised a large partisan movement, strongest in the northern mountains and cities. Partisan formations included the Communist Garibaldi Brigades, the Justice and Liberty brigades of the Action Party, and autonomous and Catholic formations. At its peak in the spring of 1945 the resistance numbered well over 200,000 fighters. The conflict had the character of a three-sided war: a patriotic war against the German occupier, a civil war between Italians, and in some areas a class war.

Reprisals and atrocities

The war was marked by brutal German reprisals against civilians, such as the Ardeatine massacre in Rome (335 victims, March 1944) and the Sant'Anna di Stazzema and Marzabotto massacres, as well as by RSI anti-partisan operations and postwar reprisal killings of Fascists. Estimates of partisan deaths are around 45,000; thousands of RSI servicemen were killed during and after the war's end.

End of the war

In April 1945, as the Allied spring offensive broke through, the CLN proclaimed a general insurrection; partisans liberated Genoa, Milan and Turin before Allied troops arrived. Mussolini was captured and executed by partisans on 28 April 1945 near Lake Como. German forces in Italy signed a surrender at Caserta effective 2 May 1945. The civil war's legacy — commemorated each 25 April on Liberation Day — remains politically contested in Italy, notably since the historian Claudio Pavone's influential 1991 study framed the Resistance explicitly as a civil war.
