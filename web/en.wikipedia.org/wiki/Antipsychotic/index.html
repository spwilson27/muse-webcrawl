Antipsychotic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Antipsychotics, previously known as neuroleptics and major tranquilizers, are a class of psychotropic medication primarily used to manage psychosis (including delusions, hallucinations, paranoia or disordered thought), principally in schizophrenia but also in a range of other psychotic disorders. They are also the mainstay of treatment for bipolar disorder during manic episodes, and are used adjunctively in several other conditions.

Generations

First-generation (typical) antipsychotics, beginning with chlorpromazine in 1952, act mainly as dopamine D2 receptor antagonists. Haloperidol is a widely used example. They are effective against positive symptoms but carry a risk of extrapyramidal side effects, including tardive dyskinesia.

Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics, beginning with clozapine and including risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, and aripiprazole, combine dopamine blockade with serotonin receptor effects. They tend to cause fewer movement disorders but more metabolic side effects such as weight gain and diabetes. Clozapine remains uniquely effective for treatment-resistant schizophrenia but requires blood monitoring because of the risk of agranulocytosis.

History and controversy

The introduction of chlorpromazine in the 1950s transformed psychiatric care and contributed to deinstitutionalisation. Antipsychotics remain debated: long-term use is associated with significant adverse effects, and critics question their use in some non-psychotic conditions, such as behavioural symptoms of dementia, where they increase mortality risk in elderly patients.
