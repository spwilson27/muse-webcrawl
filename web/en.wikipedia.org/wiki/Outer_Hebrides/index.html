The Outer Hebrides (Scottish Gaelic: Na h-Eileanan Siar, "the Western Isles") are an island chain off the west coast of mainland Scotland. The islands form part of the archipelago of the Hebrides, separated from the Scottish mainland and from the Inner Hebrides by the waters of the Minch, the Little Minch, and the Sea of the Hebrides. The chain stretches about 210 km (130 mi) from the Butt of Lewis in the north to Barra Head in the south, and comprises some 119 islands, of which around 15 are inhabited.

Geography and islands

The largest island is Lewis and Harris (a single island divided by a range of hills), followed by South Uist, North Uist, Benbecula, and Barra. The east coasts are deeply indented sea-loch shores of ancient Lewisian gneiss, among the oldest rocks in Europe, while the west coasts are fringed with machair — fertile, low-lying shell-sand plains famous for wildflowers and corncrakes. Many of the islands are now joined by causeways, notably in the Uists. Stornoway on Lewis is the main town, with ferry and air links to the mainland; other ferries serve Tarbert (Harris), Lochmaddy, Lochboisdale, and Castlebay (Barra), whose airport lands on the beach at Traigh Mhor.

History and culture

The islands were settled in prehistory — the Callanish Standing Stones on Lewis date from about 2900 BC — and later came under Norse control as part of the Kingdom of the Isles before passing to Scotland by the Treaty of Perth (1266). Gaelic culture remains exceptionally strong: the Outer Hebrides have the highest proportion of Scottish Gaelic speakers in Scotland, and the local authority Comhairle nan Eilean Siar promotes bilingualism. Religious observance distinguishes Presbyterian north (Lewis, Harris, North Uist) from Catholic south (South Uist, Barra — "the last bastion of Catholicism" in Scotland). Traditional industries include crofting, fishing, and Harris tweed, woven by islanders in their homes and protected by law and the Orb trademark since 1909.
