Phytase

A phytase is a type of phosphatase enzyme that catalyses the hydrolysis of phytic acid (myo-inositol hexakisphosphate), an indigestible organic form of phosphorus found in many plant tissues, especially seeds and grains, releasing a usable form of inorganic phosphorus. Phytases also occur in rumen microorganisms, fungi and some plant roots, enabling animals and plants to access phosphate otherwise locked away as phytate.

Phytase stepwise dephosphorylates phytic acid.

Use in animal feed

Monogastric animals such as pigs and poultry lack sufficient endogenous phytase, so much of the phosphorus in cereal-based feed passes through them undigested. Adding microbial phytase — commercially produced mainly from fungi such as Aspergillus niger or from bacterial genes expressed in fermentation hosts — releases this phosphorus, reducing the need for supplemental inorganic phosphate and cutting phosphorus excretion, a major cause of water eutrophication near intensive livestock operations. Phytase is among the most widely used feed enzymes in the world.

Classes

Phytases are classified by the carbon position on the inositol ring at which hydrolysis begins (3-phytases, 6-phytases, 5-phytases) and by catalytic mechanism, including histidine acid phytases and β-propeller phytases.

Human nutrition

Phytate in food chelates iron, zinc and calcium and can reduce their absorption; traditional processes such as soaking, germination and fermentation activate grain phytases and lower phytate, improving mineral availability.
