Rabindranath Tagore

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Rabindranath Tagore in the 1920s: the white-bearded poet in a long robe, seated in the garden of his Santiniketan school]

Rabindranath Tagore (7 May 1861 – 7 August 1941) was a Bengali poet, writer, composer, philosopher and painter who reshaped Bengali literature and music, and in 1913 became the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. His poetry in the English Gitanjali (Song Offerings, 1912), introduced by W. B. Yeats, made him a sage-celebrity across Europe and America; at home he is 'Gurudev', the author of two national anthems — India's and Bangladesh's — and of some two thousand songs, the Rabindra Sangeet.

Tagore founded the experimental school at Santiniketan (1901) that grew into Visva-Bharati University (1921), wrote the novels Gora (1910) and The Home and the World (1916), the dance-dramas and the short stories ('Kabuliwala' among them) that founded the modern Bengali short story, and took up painting at 60 with thousands of ink fantasies.

Life and thought

Youngest child of the reformer Debendranath Tagore and grandson of 'Prince' Dwarkanath, Tagore grew up in the Jorasanko house amid Bengal's renaissance. He supported Indian self-rule yet opposed narrow nationalism — debating Gandhi (who called him the Great Sentinel) on the spinning wheel and the Bihar earthquake — and renounced his knighthood in 1919 after the Amritsar massacre. His educational ideal, learning under trees in freedom, drew students and teachers worldwide to Santiniketan.

Legacy

Tagore's standing in the West faded after the thirties even as translations (notably by William Radice) revived it; in Bengal his supremacy is total and undiminished — his songs sung at weddings, protests and monsoon evenings, his face the culture's very emblem.
