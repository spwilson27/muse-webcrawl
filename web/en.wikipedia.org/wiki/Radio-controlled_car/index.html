Radio-controlled car

A radio-controlled car (or RC car) is a miniature model car, van or truck that can be controlled from a distance using a specialized transmitter or remote control. RC cars can be powered by various sources, the most common being electric motors powered by rechargeable battery packs, and internal combustion "nitro" engines using a special glow-fuel blend.

Types

Toy-grade RC cars are inexpensive, ready-to-run vehicles sold in general retail stores, typically with fixed features and limited performance. Hobby-grade cars are faster, more durable, and repairable, with replaceable parts; they are sold as ready-to-run models or as kits requiring assembly. Common scales include 1:10, 1:8, 1:12, and 1:16. Cars are built for on-road use (touring cars, drift cars) or off-road use (buggies, stadium trucks, monster trucks, and rock crawlers), with two-wheel or four-wheel drive.

Power systems

Electric cars historically used brushed motors and nickel-based batteries; modern hobby-grade cars predominantly use brushless motors and lithium polymer (LiPo) batteries, giving much higher speeds — top racing models exceed 100 km/h (60 mph). Nitro cars use small glow engines (typically 2.1–4.6 cc), while a small number of large-scale cars use gasoline two-stroke engines.

Racing

Organized RC car racing is sanctioned by bodies such as IFMAR internationally and ROAR in North America, with world championships in classes including 1:8 off-road, 1:10 touring car, and 1:12 pan car. Radio systems originally used 27 MHz and 75 MHz frequencies with interchangeable crystals, but have largely been replaced by 2.4 GHz spread-spectrum equipment that automatically avoids interference.
