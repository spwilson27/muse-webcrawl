Concertina

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A concertina is a free-reed musical instrument, like the various accordions and the harmonica. It consists of expanding and contracting bellows, with buttons (or keys) on both ends, unlike accordion buttons which are on the front of the instrument. The concertina was developed independently in both England and Germany in the 1820s–1830s; the English version was invented by Sir Charles Wheatstone (patented 1829, and in improved form 1844), while Carl Friedrich Uhlig introduced the German concertina in 1834.

Types

  • English concertina — fully chromatic, unisonoric (same note on push and pull), with buttons arranged in four columns; used for melody and classical playing.
  • Anglo concertina — bisonoric (different notes on push and pull), usually with 20 or 30 buttons in curved rows; the staple of Irish traditional music and Morris dancing.
  • Duet concertina — unisonoric systems (Maccann, Crane, Hayden) with melody on the right hand and accompaniment on the left.
  • German concertina / chemnitzer and bandoneon — larger square-built relatives; the bandoneon became central to Argentine tango.

Construction and sound

Pressing a button opens a pallet admitting air from the bellows to a steel (or brass) reed. English concertinas typically have hexagonal or octagonal ends, fretwork-cut end plates, and individual reeds mounted on a reed pan; German instruments often use long reed plates shared with accordions. Quality instruments by makers such as Wheatstone and Jeffries are prized antiques.

Use

The concertina was popular in 19th-century parlours, music halls, and Salvation Army bands, spread through maritime trade to become central in Irish traditional music (County Clare is a stronghold), English folk, South African boeremusiek, and Zulu maskandi. It survives in folk revivals and in the work of players such as Noel Hill.
