In geometry, the incircle of a triangle is the largest circle that fits inside the triangle; it touches (is tangent to) all three sides. Its centre, the incentre, is the point where the triangle's three internal angle bisectors meet. Closely related are the three excircles: an excircle lies outside the triangle and is tangent to one side and to the extensions of the other two. Excircles and the incircle together form a classic configuration of triangle geometry studied since Euclid.

Incircle

Let the triangle have side lengths a, b, c, semiperimeter s = (a + b + c)/2 and area Δ. The inradius is given by the elegant formula r = Δ / s. The points where the incircle touches the sides split each side into two tangent lengths: the touchpoint on side a divides it into segments of length s − b and s − c. The incentre is equidistant from all three sides, and its barycentric coordinates are a : b : c. The contact triangle formed by the three touchpoints is the pedal triangle of the incentre.

Excircles

The excircle opposite vertex A is tangent externally to side a and to the extensions of sides b and c; its centre, the A-excentre, is the intersection of the external bisectors at B and C with the internal bisector at A. Its radius is ra = Δ / (s − a), with analogous formulae for rb and rc. Among the many identities linking these quantities are 1/r = 1/ra + 1/rb + 1/rc and ra + rb + rc = 4R + r, where R is the circumradius. The three excentres form the excentral triangle, for which the original triangle is the orthic triangle, and the incentre is its orthocentre.

Related points and circles

The line from a vertex to the touchpoint of the opposite excircle passes through the incircle touchpoint direction associated with the Nagel point; the Gergonne point is the concurrency point of the cevians to the incircle touchpoints. The nine-point circle passes through the midpoints of the segments joining the incentre to the excentres, and the Spieker circle — the incircle of the medial triangle — is centred at the Spieker centre. Incircles generalise to any tangential polygon, a polygon all of whose sides are tangent to a single circle.
