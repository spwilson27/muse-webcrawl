Digitoxin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digitoxin is a purified cardiac glycoside, similar in structure and effects to digoxin (which it predates as a drug). It is extracted from the leaves of the foxglove plant, Digitalis purpurea. Digitoxin increases the force of contraction of the heart muscle (positive inotropic effect) and slows conduction through the atrioventricular node; it has been used in the treatment of heart failure and certain cardiac arrhythmias, although digoxin has largely replaced it in most countries. Unlike digoxin, digitoxin is eliminated mainly by the liver rather than the kidneys, and it has a much longer half-life.

Pharmacology

Like other cardiac glycosides, digitoxin inhibits the sodium–potassium ATPase pump in cardiac cell membranes. The resulting rise in intracellular sodium reduces sodium–calcium exchange, increasing intracellular calcium available to the contractile apparatus and strengthening contraction. Increased vagal tone slows the heart rate and AV nodal conduction, useful in controlling ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation. Digitoxin is highly protein-bound, undergoes hepatic metabolism, and its long half-life (around a week) means steady state takes weeks to achieve — and toxicity takes correspondingly long to resolve.

Medical use and decline

William Withering described the use of foxglove for "dropsy" in 1785, and purified digitoxin was a standard heart-failure drug through much of the 20th century. It has since fallen out of use in most of the world because its very long duration makes dosing and management of toxicity difficult; digoxin, with predominantly renal excretion and a shorter half-life, is preferred where a glycoside is indicated at all. Modern heart-failure therapy relies mainly on other drug classes, so glycosides are now second- or third-line agents.

Toxicity

Digitoxin has a narrow therapeutic index. Overdose causes nausea, vomiting, visual disturbances (including yellow-tinted vision, xanthopsia), confusion, and potentially fatal arrhythmias. Risk is increased by low potassium or magnesium. Severe poisoning from any cardiac glycoside can be treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab), which also bind digitoxin, together with correction of electrolytes and management of arrhythmias. Digitoxin has also featured in criminal cases: the American serial killer Charles Cullen was convicted of murders committed partly with digoxin and related drugs, and digitoxin itself has a long literary and forensic history as a poison.
