Brodie helmet

The Brodie helmet is a steel combat helmet designed and patented in London in 1915 by John Leopold Brodie. A modified form became standard issue in the British Army during the First World War, and it was also adopted by the United States Army as the M1917 helmet. Its wide, shallow brim earned it nicknames such as the "tin hat", "shrapnel helmet", "Tommy helmet" and, among American troops, the "doughboy helmet".

Background

The outbreak of the First World War found the British Army without a steel helmet. Trench warfare produced a large proportion of head wounds from shell fragments and shrapnel, prompting France to introduce the Adrian helmet in 1915. Brodie, a British inventor, offered his design to the War Office; it was manufactured from a single sheet of pressed steel, which made it strong and cheap to produce.

Design

The helmet consisted of a shallow bowl with a wide brim, a leather lining and a chinstrap. Later marks (Mark I) used Hadfield steel with a higher manganese content for greater resistance. The non-reflective, textured finish reduced glare. Weighing about 1.2 pounds (0.54 kg), it protected mainly against airburst shrapnel falling from above rather than direct bullet strikes.

Service

The Brodie entered British service in late 1915 and was general issue by the Battle of the Somme in 1916. The United States adopted it in 1917 as the M1917 and produced millions before developing the deeper M1917A1 and eventually replacing it with the M1 helmet in 1941. British and Commonwealth forces used the Mark II Brodie through the Second World War.
