Secularization

Many former churches in Western countries have been converted to secular uses such as bookshops, housing and cultural venues.

Secularization (or secularisation) is the process by which a society or institution moves away from religious values and institutions toward non-religious ones — in the broadest sense, the historic transformation in which religion's social and cultural significance declines. As a term in sociology, it has been used both descriptively, for the measurable decline of religious practice and authority, and normatively, whether celebrated as liberation from superstition or lamented as cultural loss.

Meanings

The word derives from the Latin saeculum ("world", "age"). Its earliest legal sense — used at the Peace of Westphalia in 1648 and during the revolutionary era — meant the transfer of church property to civil ownership or control, as in the secularization of monastic lands. In nineteenth-century thought it broadened into the idea of emancipation of social life from ecclesiastical tutelage. Sociologists distinguish several dimensions: the structural differentiation of society into separate religious and secular spheres; the decline of religious beliefs and practices among individuals; and the privatisation of religion, its retreat from public life.

The secularization thesis

The "secularization thesis" — that modernisation necessarily and irreversibly erodes religion — was a near-consensus among classical sociologists from Comte, Marx, Durkheim and Weber to many mid-twentieth-century theorists. From the 1960s the thesis was increasingly challenged: religion remained vigorous in the United States and much of the developing world, new religious movements flourished, and some scholars, including the early proponent Peter Berger, recanted. Charles Taylor's A Secular Age (2007) reframed the question as a change in the conditions of belief. José Casanova argued that only the differentiation component of the thesis is defensible, and coined "deprivatisation" for religion's re-entry into public life. Empirically, regular religious practice has fallen sharply in Western Europe, Canada, Australia and, more recently, the United States, with the rise of the religiously unaffiliated ("nones"), while sub-Saharan Africa, parts of Asia and the Muslim world remain highly religious.

Secularization as policy

In political contexts, secularization has also named deliberate state programmes to reduce religion's public power, such as the French law of 1905 separating churches and state (laïcité), the reforms of Atatürk in Turkey, and the anti-religious campaigns of communist states.
