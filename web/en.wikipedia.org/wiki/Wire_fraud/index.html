Wire fraud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wire fraud is a United States federal crime (18 U.S.C. § 1343) consisting of devising or intending to devise a scheme or artifice to defraud, and transmitting or causing the transmission of a writing, signal or sound by wire, radio or television communication in interstate or foreign commerce for the purpose of executing the scheme. Together with mail fraud (§ 1341, the older statute, from 1872), it is among the most frequently charged federal fraud offences — the default vehicle for prosecuting everything from telemarketing and investment scams to corporate and public corruption, historically extended by the “honest services” doctrine (§ 1346, enacted 1988) and then narrowed by the Supreme Court (notably Skilling v. United States, 2010, confining honest-services fraud to bribery and kickback schemes).

Elements require a scheme to defraud of money or property (the Court's property-focused readings struck again in Ciminelli v. United States, 2023, rejecting the “right to control” theory), material misrepresentation or omission, and a wire transmission in furtherance (which need not itself be false — a routine email or call suffices, making jurisdiction nearly automatic in modern commerce). Maximum penalties reach twenty years (thirty where a financial institution is affected or a major disaster is exploited). RICO predicates include wire fraud, compounding its reach.
