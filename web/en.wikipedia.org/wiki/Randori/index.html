Randori

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Japanese乱取り
Literally"taking the chaos" / free practice
Used inJudo, aikido, jujitsu

Randori (乱取り) is a term used in Japanese martial arts to describe free-style practice or sparring, most famously in judo. Literally "taking the chaos" — seizing the disorderly — randori contrasts with kata, the pre-arranged forms. It was central to the teaching method of judo's founder, Kanō Jigorō, who emphasized that techniques drilled in kata come alive when practised against a resisting, unpredictable partner.

Practice

In judo randori, two practitioners grip each other's judogi and attempt throws, pins, chokes, and armlocks (the latter categories depending on level and rules) while defending and countering in continuous motion. Unlike competition (shiai), randori is a training exercise: the emphasis falls on learning, experimentation, and mutual benefit rather than victory, and partners adjust intensity to each other's ability. Sessions vary in form — standing (tachi-waza) randori, groundwork (ne-waza) randori, and slower "French randori" for developing technique.

In other arts

In aikido, randori usually refers to an exercise in which a defender handles multiple attackers in succession or simultaneously. Related free-practice formats exist in Brazilian jiu-jitsu ("rolling") and other grappling arts, reflecting the same pedagogical principle: skills are consolidated by applying them under live, variable resistance.
