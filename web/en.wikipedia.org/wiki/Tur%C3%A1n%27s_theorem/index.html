Turán's theorem

In graph theory, Turán's theorem bounds the number of edges that can be included in an undirected graph that does not contain a complete graph of a given size as a subgraph. It is one of the central results of extremal graph theory and was proved by Hungarian mathematician Pál Turán in 1941.

Statement

Let G be an undirected graph on n vertices that contains no (r + 1)-clique. Then the number of edges of G is at most (1 − 1/r)·n²/2. The extremal graph achieving the bound is the Turán graph T(n, r): a complete r-partite graph on n vertices whose parts are as equal in size as possible. The triangle-free case (r = 2) is Mantel's theorem, which says a triangle-free graph has at most ⌊n²/4⌋ edges.

Significance

Turán's theorem founded the field of extremal graph theory and inspired the Erdős–Stone theorem and Turán-type problems for other forbidden configurations, as well as applications in theoretical computer science and additive combinatorics.
