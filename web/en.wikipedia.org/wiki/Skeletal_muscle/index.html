Skeletal muscle (also called voluntary muscle) is one of the three major types of muscle tissue in vertebrates, alongside cardiac muscle and smooth muscle. It is composed of bundles of long, cylindrical, multinucleated cells called muscle fibres, formed during development by the fusion of myoblasts, which show a banded (striated) appearance under the microscope. Attached to bones by tendons, skeletal muscles produce voluntary movement, maintain posture, stabilise joints and generate heat; in humans there are over 600 named skeletal muscles, making up roughly 30–40% of body mass.

Structure

A muscle is organised hierarchically: individual fibres are wrapped in endomysium, grouped into fascicles by perimysium, and the whole muscle is sheathed in epimysium. Each fibre contains myofibrils divided into repeating contractile units, sarcomeres, bounded by Z-discs and built from interdigitating thick (myosin) and thin (actin, tropomyosin, troponin) filaments. A specialised calcium store, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, surrounds the myofibrils, and transverse (T) tubules carry the electrical signal into the fibre's interior.

Contraction

Contraction is triggered when a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, causing an action potential that releases Ca²⁺ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binding to troponin exposes binding sites on actin, allowing myosin heads to form cross-bridges and pull the filaments past one another (the sliding filament theory), shortening the sarcomere. Muscles are recruited in motor units and graded by unit recruitment and firing rate. Fibres vary from slow oxidative (type I, fatigue-resistant, postural) to fast glycolytic (type IIx, powerful but fatigable).

Functions beyond movement

Skeletal muscle is also a metabolic and endocrine organ: it is the body's main site of glucose uptake and amino-acid reserve, contributes most resting heat production (augmented by shivering), and secretes signalling molecules called myokines during exercise. After injury, satellite cells on the fibre surface mediate repair and hypertrophy; muscle mass declines with age (sarcopenia) and in diseases such as the muscular dystrophies, in which mutations of structural proteins — most famously dystrophin in Duchenne muscular dystrophy — progressively destroy fibres.
