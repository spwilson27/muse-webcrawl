Tokugawa Ieyasu (1543–1616) was the founder and first shōgun of the Tokugawa shogunate, which ruled Japan from 1603 until 1868. The last of the "three unifiers" of Japan after Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, Ieyasu is celebrated in Japanese tradition for the patience with which he outlived and outmanoeuvred his rivals.

Early life and rise

Born Matsudaira Takechiyo in Okazaki Castle, Ieyasu spent much of his childhood as a hostage, first of the Oda and then of the Imagawa clan. After the Imagawa defeat at Okehazama (1560) he allied with Nobunaga, took the name Tokugawa Ieyasu, survived disaster at Mikatagahara (1573), and steadily enlarged his domains. After Nobunaga's assassination in 1582 he became one of the great powers of Japan, and in 1590 Hideyoshi transferred him to the Kantō region, where he made Edo (modern Tokyo) his seat.

Sekigahara and the shogunate

After Hideyoshi's death in 1598, Ieyasu defeated the western coalition of Ishida Mitsunari at the Battle of Sekigahara on 21 October 1600, the decisive battle of the age. In 1603 he received the title of shōgun and established his government (bakufu) at Edo. He retired nominally in 1605 in favour of his son Hidetada, securing the succession, but continued to rule from Sunpu.

Final years and legacy

The last threat vanished with the sieges of Osaka Castle (1614–15), in which the Toyotomi line was destroyed and Hideyoshi's heir Hideyori killed. Ieyasu died in 1616 and was enshrined at Nikkō as the deity Tōshō Daigongen. His regime — the sakoku edicts, the alternate-attendance system and the strict regulation of the daimyo — bequeathed Japan more than 250 years of peace.
