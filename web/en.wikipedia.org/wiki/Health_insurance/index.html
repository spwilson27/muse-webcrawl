Health insurance

Health insurance is insurance that covers the whole or part of the cost of a person's medical treatment.

Health insurance is a type of insurance coverage that pays for medical, surgical, and sometimes dental and prescription-drug expenses incurred by the insured. It can reimburse the insured for expenses from illness or injury, or pay the care provider directly. Health insurance is often provided as part of an employee benefits package or through government programmes.

How it works

Policyholders pay a premium; the insurer pools risk across many members. Common cost-sharing features include deductibles (an amount paid before coverage begins), copayments, and coinsurance. Policies define networks of approved providers, exclusions, and annual or lifetime limits. Two classic economic problems afflict health insurance markets: adverse selection, in which sicker people are likelier to buy coverage, and moral hazard, in which insured people consume more care because its marginal price to them is low.

Systems around the world

Models range from tax-funded national health services (the "Beveridge model" of the United Kingdom), to social health insurance funds financed by payroll contributions (the "Bismarck model" of Germany and France), to the mixed system of the United States, where coverage comes from employers, private markets, and public programmes such as Medicare and Medicaid. The Affordable Care Act of 2010 reformed U.S. markets with guaranteed issue, subsidies and (originally) an individual mandate. Universal coverage — the goal that all residents have access to needed care without financial hardship — is a stated aim of the World Health Organization.
