Nonstandard analysis

Nonstandard analysis is a branch of mathematics that reformulates calculus using a rigorous treatment of infinitesimals. Instead of the epsilon–delta limits of standard analysis, it works within the hyperreal number system *R, an ordered field that extends the real numbers with numbers that are smaller in absolute value than every positive real ("infinitesimals") and numbers larger than every real ("infinite" numbers).

History

Infinitesimals were central to the calculus of Leibniz and Newton but were displaced in the nineteenth century by the rigorous limit methods of Cauchy and Weierstrass. Abraham Robinson put infinitesimals on a sound logical footing in 1961, using model theory — specifically the compactness theorem and ultrapower constructions — to build the hyperreals and prove the transfer principle, which guarantees that first-order statements true of the reals remain true of the hyperreals. His 1966 book Non-standard Analysis presented the theory to a wide audience.

Core ideas

Every finite hyperreal is infinitely close to a unique real number, its standard part. Derivatives become ratios of infinitesimal differences followed by taking the standard part, and integrals become (hyperfinite) sums. Edward Nelson's Internal Set Theory (1977) offered an alternative, purely axiomatic route: ordinary ZFC set theory plus a new predicate "standard." Nonstandard methods have been applied in probability (Loeb measures), mathematical economics, and physics.

Criticism and reception

Reception was mixed: Errett Bishop famously criticized nonstandard analysis for obscuring computational meaning, while others praised its intuitive fidelity to the original infinitesimal calculus. Textbook treatments such as H. Jerome Keisler's Elementary Calculus: An Infinitesimal Approach (1976) showed the approach could be taught at undergraduate level.
