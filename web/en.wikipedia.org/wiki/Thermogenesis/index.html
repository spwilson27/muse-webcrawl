Thermogenesis

Thermogenesis is the process of heat production in organisms. It occurs in all warm-blooded animals and also in a few species of thermogenic plants such as the voodoo lily and the eastern skunk cabbage. In mammals, thermogenesis is a principal means of maintaining body temperature (thermoregulation) and accounts for a substantial fraction of daily energy expenditure. Heat is produced both as an unavoidable by-product of metabolism and by dedicated mechanisms that generate heat on purpose.

Types

Shivering thermogenesis is the rapid, involuntary contraction of skeletal muscles; inefficient contractions convert chemical energy largely to heat. Non-shivering thermogenesis occurs chiefly in brown adipose tissue (brown fat), whose mitochondria contain uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1). Activated by noradrenaline from the sympathetic nervous system, UCP1 short-circuits the proton gradient of oxidative phosphorylation so that the energy of fuel oxidation is released as heat instead of being stored as ATP. Brown fat is abundant in small mammals, hibernators and human infants; adult humans retain deposits in the neck and upper chest whose activity varies with cold exposure. A further component, diet-induced thermogenesis (the thermic effect of food), is the energy cost of digestion, absorption and storage of nutrients — roughly 10% of energy intake on a mixed diet, highest for protein. Exercise and ordinary muscle activity, including fidgeting (NEAT), also contribute.

Regulation

The hypothalamus integrates skin and core temperature signals and adjusts heat production and heat loss to defend a set-point. Falling temperature triggers sympathetic activation of brown fat, cutaneous vasoconstriction and, if needed, shivering; thyroid hormones raise basal metabolic rate over longer timescales. Fever is a regulated raising of the set-point, driven by prostaglandin E2, during which thermogenesis (and the sensation of chills) continues until the new set-point is reached.

Significance

Thermogenesis enabled endothermy — the maintenance of a high, stable body temperature independent of the environment — with profound consequences for animal activity, distribution and evolution. Its impairment contributes to hypothermia; defects and variation in thermogenic capacity are studied in relation to obesity and metabolic disease, and pharmacological activation of brown fat is an active research target. In thermogenic plants, heat volatilises odours that attract pollinators and can melt snow around the inflorescence.
