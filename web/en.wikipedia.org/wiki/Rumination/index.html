Rumination

Rumination is the process by which ruminant animals regurgitate previously swallowed, partially fermented food from the rumen back into the mouth, chew it again (chewing the cud), and swallow it for further digestion. By extension, the word is also used in psychology for the repetitive, passive focus on negative thoughts and feelings.

In animals

Ruminants such as cattle, sheep, goats, deer and giraffes have a specialised multi-chambered stomach. Plant material is first fermented by microbes in the rumen; the coarse fraction is then returned to the mouth as a bolus (the cud) and re-chewed, reducing particle size and exposing more surface area to microbial enzymes. Cattle typically spend up to eight hours a day ruminating. The process allows ruminants to extract energy from cellulose-rich forage that monogastric animals cannot digest efficiently.

In psychology

In response styles theory, developed by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, rumination is defined as repetitively and passively focusing on symptoms of distress and on their possible causes and consequences, without moving into active problem-solving. Ruminative response styles are associated with the onset and longer duration of depressive episodes and with anxiety disorders. Research distinguishes a maladaptive brooding component from a more neutral reflective pondering component. Treatments such as rumination-focused cognitive behavioural therapy aim to shift patients from abstract, evaluative thinking toward concrete, process-focused thinking.
