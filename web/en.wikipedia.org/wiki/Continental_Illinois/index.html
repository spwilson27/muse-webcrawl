Continental Illinois

Continental Illinois National Bank and Trust Company was at one time the seventh-largest commercial bank in the United States, headquartered in Chicago, Illinois. It is remembered chiefly for its failure and rescue in 1984 — then the largest bank failure in U.S. history — which popularised the phrase "too big to fail". Continental Illinois was acquired by BankAmerica in 1994.

History

The bank's roots go back to mid-19th-century Chicago institutions, taking the Continental Illinois name from a 1910 merger. Through the 20th century it grew into a major wholesale lender to corporations and the energy industry.

Failure and rescue (1984)

Continental had aggressively purchased oil and gas loan participations originated by Penn Square Bank of Oklahoma City. When Penn Square failed in July 1982, Continental was left with over a billion dollars of bad energy loans, compounded by troubled loans in steel, agriculture and Latin America. In May 1984 rumours about its condition triggered an electronic bank run by institutional depositors, who withdrew billions within days.

Fearing systemic consequences, the FDIC, the Federal Reserve and the Treasury organised a rescue: the FDIC guaranteed all depositors and general creditors, injected capital (eventually taking an 80% ownership stake and replacing management), and the Fed lent through the discount window. In congressional testimony, Comptroller of the Currency C. Todd Conover acknowledged that the largest banks would not be allowed to fail — the origin of the modern "too big to fail" debate. The intervention cost the FDIC roughly $1.1 billion.

Aftermath

The government sold its stake in a 1991 public offering. In 1994 BankAmerica acquired the bank for about $1.9 billion, and the Continental name disappeared from Chicago banking.
