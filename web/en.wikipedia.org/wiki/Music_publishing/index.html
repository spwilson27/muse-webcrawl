Music publishing

Music publishing is the business of creating, acquiring, protecting, and promoting musical compositions, and of collecting the income generated when compositions are performed, recorded, broadcast, streamed, or otherwise used. It concerns the rights in the song itself — melody and lyrics — as distinct from the music recording business, which concerns rights in specific sound recordings ("masters").

Printed sheet music was the original form of the publishing trade.

Origins

The term derives from the original business of printing and selling sheet music, which became practical after the invention of music printing in the early 16th century — Ottaviano Petrucci's Harmonice Musices Odhecaton (1501) was the first book of polyphonic music printed with movable type. For centuries, publication — making music available in print — was the principal means of distributing compositions, and publishers such as Henle, Schott, and Ricordi shaped the classical repertoire.

Rights and income streams

Copyright in a composition generates several distinct income streams that publishing manages:

  • Performance royalties — paid when a work is broadcast, streamed, or performed publicly; collected by performing rights organisations such as ASCAP, BMI, and PRS.
  • Mechanical royalties — paid for the reproduction of a work in recordings, historically per record sold and now per stream or download.
  • Synchronisation fees — negotiated licences for pairing music with film, television, advertisements, and video games.
  • Print income — sheet music and folios, now a small part of the business.

Publishing deals

Songwriters may sign full publishing deals (assigning copyright in exchange for royalties and advances), co-publishing deals (sharing ownership), or administration deals (retaining ownership while paying a fee for collection and licensing services). Self-publishing by artist-writers has become increasingly common with digital distribution and online royalty collection services.

Developments

Streaming transformed the economics and administration of publishing, requiring global databases of works and recordings, and prompting reforms such as the U.S. Music Modernization Act of 2018, which created a blanket mechanical licence for streaming services. The 2020s saw a boom in catalogue investment, with famous songbooks sold for hundreds of millions of dollars.
