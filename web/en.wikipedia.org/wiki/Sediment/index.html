Sediment

Sediment is a naturally occurring material that is broken down by processes of weathering and erosion, and is subsequently transported by the action of wind, water, or ice or by the force of gravity acting on the particles. For example, sand and silt can be carried in suspension in river water and on reaching the sea bed are deposited by sedimentation; if buried, they may eventually become sandstone and siltstone (sedimentary rocks) after lithification.

Classification

Sediment is commonly classified by grain size, from finest to coarsest: clay, silt, sand, gravel, cobbles and boulders, following scales such as the Udden–Wentworth scale. By origin, sediments may be clastic (fragments of pre-existing rock and mineral), chemical (precipitated from solution, such as evaporites and oolites) or biogenic (accumulations of shells, skeletons and plant material, such as limestone and coal).

Transport and deposition

Sediment transport is governed by the balance between flow energy and particle size, classically summarised by the Hjulström curve. Particles move by traction (rolling and sliding), saltation (bouncing) and suspension, and accumulate where energy falls — in river floodplains, lakes, estuaries, deltas and the deep ocean. The study of sediment and sedimentary rocks is called sedimentology.

Environmental significance

Sediments are both a habitat and a record: they support benthic ecosystems, archive past environments and climate in their layers, and can carry adsorbed pollutants, nutrients and contaminants into waterways. Excess sediment from erosion is itself a major form of water pollution, clouding water, smothering habitats and filling reservoirs and navigation channels.
