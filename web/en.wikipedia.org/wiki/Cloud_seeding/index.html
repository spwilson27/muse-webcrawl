Cloud seeding

Cloud seeding is a weather modification technique that aims to change the amount or type of precipitation falling from clouds by dispersing substances — most commonly silver iodide, potassium iodide, or dry ice — that serve as cloud condensation or ice nuclei. It is used to increase rainfall or snowfall, suppress hail, and dissipate fog.

[Image: An aircraft releasing seeding flares into a cloud system]

History

The technique grew from 1940s research at General Electric, where Vincent Schaefer demonstrated dry-ice seeding (1946) and Bernard Vonnegut identified silver iodide as an effective ice nucleus. Military applications included Operation Popeye, a U.S. effort to extend the monsoon over the Ho Chi Minh Trail during the Vietnam War, which prompted the 1977 ENMOD treaty banning hostile environmental modification.

Methods

Seeding agents are released from aircraft or ground-based generators into clouds judged suitable — typically supercooled clouds containing abundant liquid water below freezing. The introduced nuclei encourage ice crystals to form and grow at the expense of droplets (the Bergeron process) until they fall as precipitation. Hygroscopic seeding with salts is used in warm clouds in tropical regions.

Use and effectiveness

More than fifty countries operate weather modification programmes. China runs the world's largest, and the United Arab Emirates funds extensive rainfall-enhancement research for its arid climate. Snowpack augmentation programmes operate across the western United States. Scientific assessments, including by the U.S. National Research Council and the World Meteorological Organization, conclude that effects are plausible and locally measurable in favourable winter orographic clouds — the SNOWIE field campaign (2017) produced the first unambiguous radar evidence of seeding-induced snowfall — but that large, reliable increases in rainfall remain difficult to prove statistically.
