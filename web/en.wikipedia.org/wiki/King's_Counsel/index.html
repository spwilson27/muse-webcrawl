King's Counsel

In the United Kingdom and several Commonwealth realms, a King's Counsel (postnominal KC; during the reign of a queen, Queen's Counsel, QC) is a senior lawyer appointed by letters patent to be one of "His Majesty's Counsel learned in the law". The status — often called "taking silk", after the silk gown KCs wear — is a mark of eminence within the legal profession, awarded to experienced barristers and solicitor-advocates who have demonstrated excellence in advocacy.

[Image: a barrister wearing the silk gown and full-bottomed wig of a King's Counsel]

Appointment

In England and Wales, appointments are made by the monarch on the advice of the Lord Chancellor, following an open competition administered since 2005 by an independent selection panel (King's Counsel Appointments). Successful applicants — typically lawyers of at least ten years' standing, assessed against competencies including integrity, advocacy, and understanding of the law — attend a ceremony at Westminster Hall where they take their seats "within the Bar" of the court. KCs historically undertook not to appear against the Crown without permission, a restriction long obsolete. They command higher fees and are usually instructed in the most complex and serious cases, leading a junior barrister.

Other jurisdictions

The rank exists across Commonwealth jurisdictions including Scotland (appointed on the recommendation of the Lord Justice General), Canada (federal and provincial appointments), Australia, New Zealand, and parts of the Caribbean; several republics that abolished the title replaced it with equivalents such as Senior Counsel or Senior Advocate. The title automatically changed from Queen's Counsel to King's Counsel across the Commonwealth realms upon the accession of Charles III in September 2022.
