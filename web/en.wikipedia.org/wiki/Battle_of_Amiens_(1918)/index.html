Battle of Amiens (1918)

The Battle of Amiens, also known as the Third Battle of Picardy, was the opening phase of the Allied offensive which began on 8 August 1918, later known as the Hundred Days Offensive, that ultimately led to the end of the First World War. Allied forces advanced over 11 kilometres (7 mi) on the first day, one of the greatest advances of the war, with Henry Rawlinson's British Fourth Army playing the decisive role. German general Erich Ludendorff called 8 August "the black day of the German Army".

Background

By the summer of 1918 the German spring offensives had exhausted the German Army, while the arrival of American troops was tipping the manpower balance to the Allies. After the Allied success at the Battle of Hamel in July, planners under Field Marshal Haig prepared a surprise attack east of Amiens, spearheaded by the Australian and Canadian Corps, with massed tanks and aircraft and deliberately minimal preliminary bombardment to preserve surprise.

Battle

The attack began at 04:20 on 8 August under a dense creeping barrage, with over 500 tanks supporting the infantry. The Australian and Canadian Corps smashed through the German Second Army's defenses, advancing up to 11–13 km and capturing thousands of prisoners; many German units surrendered without a fight, shocking the German high command. French forces attacked on the southern flank. By 11 August effective resistance and stiffening German reinforcements slowed the advance, and Haig shifted the effort northward toward the subsequent Battle of Albert.

Significance

Amiens demonstrated mature Allied combined-arms methods — infantry, tanks, artillery and air power coordinated at scale — and marked a collapse in German morale from which the army never recovered. The battle opened the Hundred Days Offensive, a series of Allied victories that culminated in the Armistice of 11 November 1918.
