The Painted Grey Ware culture (PGW) is an Iron Age archaeological culture of the western Gangetic plain and the Ghaggar-Hakra valley in the northern Indian subcontinent, conventionally dated from about 1200 to 600 BCE. It is named after its characteristic pottery: a fine, smooth, grey ware painted with simple geometric designs in black. The culture is often associated by scholars — with varying degrees of confidence — with the later Vedic period and the world described in the early Sanskrit texts and the Mahabharata.

Characteristics

Painted Grey Ware is a fine, wheel-made pottery, well fired to an ash-grey colour, typically in the forms of bowls and dishes decorated with simple painted lines, circles, dots, and swastika-like motifs. It appears to have been a deluxe tableware used alongside coarser red and black wares. Settlements of the period were mostly villages of wattle-and-daub and mud-brick houses; agriculture based on rice, wheat, and barley, and the keeping of cattle, sheep, goats, and horses underpinned the economy. Iron objects — arrowheads, spearheads, sickles, and tools — become increasingly common through the period. Terracotta figurines, beads, and occasional evidence of craft specialisation are found, and at some late sites there are hints of fortifications and proto-urban planning.

Distribution and chronology

More than 650 PGW sites have been recorded, concentrated in present-day Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh, and the adjacent Punjab and Rajasthan. The excavations at Hastinapura by B. B. Lal in the 1950s first defined the culture stratigraphically; Lal controversially linked a flood deposit at the site to the Mahabharata tradition of the Kuru capital being abandoned after a flood. At Bhagwanpura, PGW levels overlap with late Harappan material, suggesting some continuity of population.

Historical interpretation

Many archaeologists associate the PGW culture with the early Indo-Aryan-speaking peoples of the Kuru-Panchala realm and the composition of the later Vedic texts. Others caution that pottery styles alone cannot be equated with languages or peoples. The culture grades into the Northern Black Polished Ware culture around 600 BCE, the material signature of the subcontinent's "second urbanisation", with its cities, coinage, and early historic kingdoms.
