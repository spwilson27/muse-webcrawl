Prosecutor's fallacy

The prosecutor's fallacy is a fallacy of statistical reasoning that occurs when the probability of the evidence given innocence is confused with the probability of innocence given the evidence — in symbols, mistaking P(E | innocent) for P(innocent | E). It takes its name from legal arguments in which a small probability that a matching piece of evidence (such as a DNA profile or blood type) would occur in an innocent person is presented as though it were the probability that the defendant is innocent. The two quantities can differ enormously because the second depends also on the prior probability of guilt and on how many people could have produced the evidence — the point made rigorously by Bayes' theorem.

Transposing the conditional: P(E|I) is not P(I|E)

Example

Suppose a DNA profile found at a crime scene matches one person in a million, and the defendant matches. It is fallacious to conclude that the probability the defendant is innocent is one in a million: in a population of, say, 30 million potential suspects, about 30 people would be expected to match, so without further evidence the probability that a randomly selected matching person is the source — and hence that the defendant specifically is — is far lower than the headline figure suggests. Conversely, the defense attorney's fallacy errs the other way, arguing that the existence of several possible matches in a large population makes the match evidence worthless, ignoring that the defendant is not a randomly chosen person (he may have been identified by independent evidence, and the match narrows the field dramatically).

Notable cases

The fallacy has figured in prominent miscarriage-of-justice controversies. In the British case of Sally Clark, a mother convicted in 1999 of murdering her two infant sons, an expert witness asserted that the probability of two cot deaths in one family was 1 in 73 million by squaring the probability of one — committing both the prosecutor's fallacy and an unjustified independence assumption; her conviction was quashed on appeal in 2003. Statistical evidence debates also marked the trials of O. J. Simpson, and the Dutch case of nurse Lucia de Berk, whose conviction was overturned in 2010 after flawed statistical arguments about her presence at incidents were discredited. In People v. Collins (California, 1968), an appellate court overturned a conviction obtained by multiplying probabilities of individual characteristics as if they were independent.
