Warsaw Ghetto Uprising
Date:19 April – 16 May 1943
Location:Warsaw Ghetto, German-occupied Poland
Combatants:Jewish resistance (ŻOB, ŻZW) vs. SS and German forces
Commanders:Mordechai Anielewicz; Jürgen Stroop
Result:German victory; ghetto destroyed
Casualties:~13,000 Jews killed in the ghetto; survivors deported to camps

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising was the 1943 act of Jewish resistance in the Warsaw Ghetto in German-occupied Poland during World War II, opposing Nazi Germany's final effort to transport the remaining ghetto population to extermination camps. It was the largest single revolt by Jews during the war.

Background

The Warsaw Ghetto, established in 1940, held more than 400,000 Jews at its peak in appalling conditions. During the Grossaktion Warsaw in the summer of 1942, roughly a quarter of a million ghetto residents were deported to Treblinka and murdered. Those who remained understood that deportation meant death, and resistance organisations resolved to fight.

Resistance organisations

The Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB), uniting Zionist youth movements, Bundists and communists, was commanded by Mordechai Anielewicz. The separate Jewish Military Union (ŻZW), linked to the Revisionist Zionist Betar movement, fought around Muranowski Square, where two flags — Polish and Jewish — were raised. Weapons were smuggled in with help from the Polish underground, though supplies remained meagre.

The uprising

When German forces entered the ghetto on 19 April 1943 to resume deportations, fighters attacked with pistols, grenades and Molotov cocktails, forcing an initial withdrawal. SS-Gruppenführer Jürgen Stroop then directed a systematic assault, burning the ghetto block by block. The resistance command bunker at Miła 18 fell on 8 May; Anielewicz and many of his comrades died there. On 16 May Stroop declared the operation over by blowing up the Great Synagogue of Warsaw.

Aftermath

About 13,000 Jews were killed in the ghetto during the uprising, many burned alive. The roughly 50,000 survivors were sent to concentration and extermination camps, where most perished. A handful of fighters, including Marek Edelman, escaped through the sewers and later fought in the Warsaw Uprising of 1944.

Legacy

The uprising became a symbol of Jewish resistance. The Monument to the Ghetto Heroes, unveiled in 1948, stands at the site; in 2013 the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews opened opposite it.
