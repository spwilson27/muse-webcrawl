Calendar of saints

A medieval calendar of saints, showing feast days for each month

The calendar of saints is the traditional Christian method of organizing a liturgical year by associating each day with one or more saints and referring to the day as the feast day or feast of said saint. The word "feast" in this context does not mean "a large meal, typically a celebratory one," but instead "an annual religious celebration, a day dedicated to a particular saint."

The system arose from the early Christian custom of commemorating martyrs on the dates of their deaths, or birth into heaven, and thus anniversaries, dates of death, and other significant events were marked by annual observances. As the number of recognised saints increased, every day of the year came to be associated with at least one saint. Different Christian traditions maintain their own calendars, which differ in which saints are included and in the ranking of feasts.

History

In the early Church, Christians gathered at the tombs of martyrs on the anniversary of their death (their dies natalis, or "birthday" into heaven) to celebrate the Eucharist. By the fourth century, neighbouring dioceses exchanged lists of feasts and martyrologies began to be compiled. The oldest surviving calendars, such as the Roman Depositio Martyrum of 354, list the feast days of martyrs celebrated in the city.

During the Middle Ages, local calendars proliferated, enriched by regional saints, and printed books of hours popularised the calendar of saints for lay devotion. After the Protestant Reformation, the Church of England and Lutheran churches retained reduced calendars of saints, while many Reformed churches abolished them entirely. The Roman Catholic Church undertook major revisions at the Council of Trent and again in 1969, when Pope Paul VI's reform of the General Roman Calendar removed or made optional a number of saints of doubtful historicity or purely local significance.

Ranks of feasts

In the Roman Rite the celebrations of saints are ranked, in descending order of importance:

  • Solemnities — the highest rank, reserved for the greatest mysteries of the faith and a handful of saints such as St. Joseph and St. John the Baptist.
  • Feasts — including most apostles and evangelists.
  • Memorials — either obligatory or optional; most saints' days fall into this category.
  • Optional memorials — celebrations left to local choice.

The Anglican, Lutheran and Eastern Orthodox traditions have their own systems of ranking, with Orthodoxy distinguishing great feasts, polyeleos and lesser commemorations.

Calendars by tradition

  • General Roman Calendar — the calendar of the Roman Catholic Church for universal celebration, supplemented by national and diocesan calendars.
  • Byzantine and Eastern Orthodox calendars — each day commemorates several saints, with fixed cycles beginning 1 September (the Indiction).
  • Church of England — the calendar in the Book of Common Prayer and Common Worship, distinguishing festivals, lesser festivals and commemorations.
  • Lutheran calendars — retained a modest sanctoral cycle centred on biblical saints and a few post-biblical figures.
  • Coptic and Ethiopian calendars — with their own extensive lists of saints and martyrs.

Name days

In many Catholic and Orthodox countries, a person's name day — the feast day of the saint after whom they are named — is celebrated in a manner similar to a birthday. The custom remains strong in Greece, Russia, Poland, Hungary, Spain and Latin America, among other places.
