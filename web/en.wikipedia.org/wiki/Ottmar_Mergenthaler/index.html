Ottmar Mergenthaler (11 May 1854 – 28 October 1899) was a German-American inventor best known for the Linotype machine, the first device that allowed a typesetter to compose lines of metal type mechanically at a keyboard. His invention transformed printing and made the cheap mass-circulation newspaper possible; Thomas Edison is said to have called the Linotype the "eighth wonder of the world".

Early life

Mergenthaler was born in Hachtel, Württemberg, and apprenticed as a watchmaker before emigrating to the United States in 1872. He settled in Baltimore, where he worked in the precision-instrument shop of his cousin August Hahl, building models for patent lawyers — training that gave him the mechanical skill his later work required.

The Linotype

Sponsored by a syndicate of newspaper publishers seeking faster typesetting for court-reporting and newspaper work, Mergenthaler refined his design through the 1880s. The breakthrough machine, demonstrated at the New-York Tribune in 1886, let an operator press keys that assembled brass letter matrices into a line; the machine then cast the line as a single slug of type metal — a "line o' type", hence Linotype. The Mergenthaler Linotype Company was formed to manufacture it, and the machines spread rapidly through newspaper composing rooms worldwide.

Death and legacy

Plagued by illness and by litigation over control of his company, Mergenthaler died of tuberculosis in Baltimore in 1899, aged 45. Linotype machines remained the industry standard for newspaper and magazine composition until phototypesetting and digital typesetting displaced them in the late 20th century.
