Mammary gland

A mammary gland is an exocrine gland in humans and other mammals that produces milk to feed young offspring. Mammals get their name from the Latin word mamma, "breast". The mammary glands are arranged in organs such as the breasts in primates (for example, humans and chimpanzees), the udder in ruminants (for example, cows, goats, sheep, and deer), and the dugs of other animals (for example, dogs and cats).

Structure

The gland is composed of lobes containing clusters of secretory alveoli (acini). Alveoli drain into small ducts that join into lactiferous ducts, which open at the nipple (or teat). Myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli contract under the influence of the hormone oxytocin to eject milk — the let-down reflex. The number and position of mammary glands vary widely among species, generally correlating with litter size.

Development and control

Mammary glands develop from embryonic mammary ridges ("milk lines"). In humans, a single pair develops on the chest. Growth accelerates at puberty under estrogen and progesterone; during pregnancy, prolactin and other hormones complete the gland's secretory differentiation, and milk production (lactation) begins after birth. The glands are modified sweat glands evolutionarily related to apocrine glands, and they are present in rudimentary form in males.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the mammary gland include mastitis (inflammation, often during lactation), fibrocystic changes, and breast cancer, one of the most common cancers in women. In dairy farming, mammary health (udder health) is a major economic concern.
