History of calculus

DisciplineMathematics
InventorsIsaac Newton, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
PrecursorsArchimedes, Cavalieri, Fermat, Barrow

The history of calculus concerns the development of differential and integral calculus — the mathematics of change and accumulation — culminating in its independent invention by Newton and Leibniz in the late 17th century and a bitter priority dispute.

Precursors

Archimedes used the method of exhaustion to find areas and volumes; medieval Islamic and Indian mathematicians (notably the Kerala school) developed series for trigonometric functions. In the 17th century, Cavalieri's indivisibles, Fermat's tangents and adequality, and Barrow's fundamental theorem prepared the ground.

Newton and Leibniz

Newton developed his "method of fluxions" in 1665–66, applying it to dynamics and gravitation in the Principia (1687). Leibniz published first (1684) with the notation — dy/dx and the integral sign — still used today. Their supporters' priority quarrel split British and Continental mathematics for a century.

Rigour and beyond

The Bernoullis and Euler expanded calculus's reach. In the 19th century Cauchy, Riemann and Weierstrass gave it rigorous foundations in limits and real analysis; the 20th century added non-standard analysis and distribution theory.
