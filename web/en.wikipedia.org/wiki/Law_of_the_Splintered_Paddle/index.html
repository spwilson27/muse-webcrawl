Law of the Splintered Paddle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Law of the Splintered Paddle (Hawaiian: Kānāwai Māmalahoe) was a law promulgated by Kamehameha I, ruler of the Hawaiian Islands, in about 1797. It decreed that every man, woman, and child had the right to "lie by the roadside without fear of harm", protecting non-combatants and travellers from arbitrary violence. It is regarded as one of Hawaiʻi's early human-rights laws and remains part of the law of the State of Hawaii today.

Origin

According to tradition, during the Battle of Mokuʻōhai's aftermath or a raid on Puna, Kamehameha pursued two fishermen who had been fishing in waters he considered his. His leg became caught in a reef crevice, and one of the fishermen, fearing for his life, struck Kamehameha on the head with a paddle, splintering it. The fishermen escaped. Years later, when the fisherman was brought before him expecting death, Kamehameha instead pardoned him and admitted fault, declaring the law so that the weak would be safe from the strong.

Legacy

The law is written into Article 9, Section 10 of the Constitution of Hawaii: "The law of the splintered paddle, mamalahoe kanawai, [declared] by Kamehameha I ... shall be a unique and living symbol of the State's concern for public safety." The symbol of the splintered paddle is used by the Honolulu Police Department, and Māmalahoe is the motto associated with the Kamehameha Schools and a U.S. Army unit, the 100th Infantry Battalion.
