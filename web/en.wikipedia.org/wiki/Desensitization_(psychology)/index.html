In psychology, desensitization is a process that diminishes emotional responsiveness to a negative, aversive, or positive stimulus after repeated exposure to it. It also occurs when an emotional response is repeatedly evoked in situations in which the action tendency associated with the emotion proves irrelevant or unnecessary.

Therapeutic application

Joseph Wolpe developed systematic desensitization in 1958 as a treatment for phobias and anxiety disorders. The patient constructs a graded hierarchy of feared situations and, while in a state of relaxation (originally taught via progressive muscle relaxation), is exposed to progressively more anxiety-provoking items, first in imagination and later in reality. The pairing of the feared stimulus with an incompatible relaxed response is called reciprocal inhibition. Closely related modern methods include exposure therapy and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR).

Media desensitization

The concept is also studied in media psychology: repeated exposure to media violence can blunt emotional reactions to real violence and reduce physiological arousal, a finding central to debates about violent films and video games. Desensitization can likewise arise in professions routinely exposed to suffering, such as medicine and emergency services.
