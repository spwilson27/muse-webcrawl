Prothrombin

Also known asCoagulation factor II
GeneF2 (chromosome 11)
Active formThrombin (factor IIa)

Prothrombin (coagulation factor II) is a protein present in blood plasma that is converted into the enzyme thrombin by a reaction involving factor X (prothrombinase complex) during the clotting of blood. Thrombin then converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble strands of fibrin, and activates other clotting factors.

Structure and activation

Prothrombin is a vitamin K-dependent glycoprotein synthesised in the liver. It contains a Gla domain whose glutamate residues are carboxylated, allowing the protein to bind calcium ions and phospholipid membranes at injury sites. The prothrombinase complex (factor Xa, factor Va, calcium and phospholipid) cleaves prothrombin at two sites to release active thrombin.

Clinical significance

The prothrombin time (PT) test, reported as the INR, measures the extrinsic clotting pathway and is used to monitor warfarin therapy — warfarin works by reducing the liver's production of functional prothrombin and other vitamin K-dependent factors. A common polymorphism, prothrombin G20210A, raises plasma prothrombin levels and increases the risk of venous thrombosis; it is the second most common inherited thrombophilia after factor V Leiden. Rare congenital deficiency causes bleeding. Direct thrombin inhibitors such as dabigatran block the active product rather than the precursor.
