Nanopore sequencing

Nanopore sequencing is a third-generation approach to DNA and RNA sequencing in which a single strand of nucleic acid is passed through a nanoscale pore in a membrane; the sequence is read by measuring changes in an electrical current as successive bases occupy the pore. It requires no amplification or fluorescent labelling and yields reads in real time.

Principle

A voltage across an insulating membrane drives ions through a protein pore (such as alpha-hemolysin or MspA) or a solid-state pore. A motor protein ratchets the strand through one base at a time. Because each short sequence of bases (a k-mer) blocks the current characteristically, software basecalling converts the current trace into a sequence. The method produces very long reads — routinely tens of kilobases and, in record cases, megabases — simplifying genome assembly and the detection of structural variants; it can also sequence RNA directly and detect base modifications such as methylation from the current signal.

Commercialisation

The technology was pioneered scientifically in the 1990s, notably by David Deamer, George Church and Daniel Branton, and commercialised by Oxford Nanopore Technologies, whose pocket-sized MinION sequencer launched in 2015. Larger devices (GridION, PromethION) offer high throughput, and the portability of the smallest instruments has enabled field genomics, from Ebola and Zika outbreak surveillance to sequencing aboard the International Space Station.
