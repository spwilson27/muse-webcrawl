Ectoderm

Gives rise toEpidermis, nervous system, neural crest derivatives, tooth enamel, lens of the eye
SystemDevelopmental biology / embryology

The ectoderm is one of the three primary germ layers formed during early embryonic development, the others being the mesoderm and the endoderm. It is the outermost layer, and it emerges and originates from the outer layer of germ cells during gastrulation. The word ectoderm comes from the Greek ektos meaning "outside" and derma meaning "skin."

Development

In vertebrate embryos, the ectoderm differentiates into two broad domains: the surface (external) ectoderm and the neuroectoderm. During the third week of human development, a portion of the dorsal ectoderm thickens to form the neural plate. Its edges rise as neural folds and fuse in the midline to form the neural tube — the process of neurulation — while cells at the crest of the folds detach as the neural crest, a migratory population sometimes called the "fourth germ layer."

Derivatives

Surface ectoderm

  • Epidermis of the skin and its appendages (hair, nails, sweat and sebaceous glands)
  • Epithelium of the mouth, nasal cavities and sense organs
  • Lens and cornea of the eye
  • Enamel of the teeth (ameloblasts)
  • Anterior pituitary gland
  • Mammary glands

Neuroectoderm

  • The entire central nervous system: brain and spinal cord
  • Peripheral nervous system components from the neural tube and neural crest
  • Melanocytes (pigment cells)
  • Cartilage and bone of the face and skull (from cranial neural crest)
  • Adrenal medulla and sympathetic ganglia
  • Schwann cells and the enteric nervous system

Clinical significance

Defects in closure of the neural tube produce conditions such as spina bifida and anencephaly. A group of genetic disorders called ectodermal dysplasias affects the development of two or more ectodermal structures — hair, teeth, nails and sweat glands — and includes hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia.
