Battle of Madrid
Date8 November 1936 – 23 March 1937
LocationMadrid, Spain
ResultRepublican victory; Nationalist siege of Madrid continues until 1939

The Battle of Madrid was a battle fought from November 1936 to March 1937 during the Spanish Civil War. Nationalist forces under General Francisco Franco attempted to seize the Spanish capital, but Republican forces, including the newly arrived International Brigades and Soviet-supplied materiel, held the city. The battle became one of the most famous episodes of the war, and Madrid remained under siege until its fall in March 1939.

Background

After the military uprising of July 1936, Nationalist columns advanced rapidly through western Spain toward Madrid. By early November 1936, Nationalist troops under General José Enrique Varela had reached the suburbs of the capital. The Republican government of Francisco Largo Caballero fled to Valencia on 6 November, leaving General José Miaja in charge of a Junta de Defensa (Defense Council) to organize the city's resistance. Many observers expected Madrid to fall within days.

The battle

The Nationalist assault began on 8 November 1936, with the main thrust through the Casa de Campo park toward the Manzanares River and the University City. Republican defenses, reinforced by the first International Brigades — the XI and XII Brigades, composed largely of foreign volunteers — held the line. The slogan ¡No pasarán! ("They shall not pass!"), popularized by Dolores Ibárruri, became the rallying cry of the defenders.

Fierce fighting raged in the University City district, where Nationalist troops gained a foothold but could not break through. In March 1937, Nationalist forces attempted a renewed offensive northeast of Madrid at Guadalajara, where Italian expeditionary forces were defeated by Republican troops. Franco thereafter abandoned the direct assault on Madrid and turned to a strategy of attrition and offensives elsewhere in Spain.

Air raids and the siege

Madrid suffered intensive aerial bombardment by the German Condor Legion and Italian aircraft, making it one of the first major European capitals subjected to sustained bombing of civilian districts. The city endured artillery fire and deprivation throughout the remainder of the war. Madrid finally surrendered on 28 March 1939, days before the war's end.

Legacy

The successful defense of Madrid became a symbol of anti-fascist resistance worldwide. The battle featured prominently in the literature and journalism of the war, including works by Ernest Hemingway and George Orwell, and drew international volunteers to the Republican cause.
