Sight-reading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sight-reading, also called a prima vista (Italian for "at first sight"), is the practice of reading and performing a piece of music that the performer has not seen or prepared before. It is a core skill for professional musicians — essential in studio recording, orchestral work, accompaniment and auditions — and is tested in most graded music examinations.

The skill

Effective sight-reading combines several abilities: rapid recognition of rhythmic and melodic patterns, reading ahead of the point of performance (the eye–hand span), internal hearing of the printed page ("audiation"), knowledge of style and harmony that allows prediction of likely continuations, and maintaining tempo and continuity, prioritising rhythm and structure over perfect accuracy of individual notes.

Training

Sight-reading improves chiefly through regular practice with large amounts of unfamiliar material at or below one's technical level, playing without stopping, and studying scores silently. Research in music psychology shows that skilled sight-readers rely heavily on pattern recognition and working memory rather than note-by-note decoding.

Related terms

For singers, the equivalent skill is called sight-singing, often trained through solfège systems. The term is also used more generally for reading aloud an unseen text.
