Mugwort is a common name for several species in the genus Artemisia, most often Artemisia vulgaris, the common mugwort. It is a tall herbaceous perennial plant growing 1–2 m, with dark green, pinnate leaves whose undersides are covered in white woolly hairs, and small, inconspicuous reddish-brown flower heads. Mugwort has a long history of culinary, medicinal, and ritual use across Europe and Asia.

Common mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris), a widespread perennial of roadsides and waste ground.

Uses

In Europe, mugwort was used to flavour beer before hops became standard, and has been used as a culinary herb for fatty meats such as goose. In East Asia, related species are eaten as greens and used to flavour rice cakes — Japanese yomogi and Korean ssuk. In traditional Chinese medicine, mugwort leaves are aged and processed into moxa, the material burned in moxibustion therapy. Mugwort pollen is a significant cause of hay fever in parts of Europe and Asia.

Folklore

Mugwort was one of the nine sacred herbs of Anglo-Saxon tradition, named in the Old English Nine Herbs Charm. In folklore it was hung over doorways, worn as a garland at Midsummer, and carried by travellers as protection against fatigue, wild animals, and evil spirits; it was also traditionally associated with vivid dreams when placed under a pillow.

Ecology

Common mugwort spreads aggressively by rhizomes and is regarded as an invasive weed in parts of North America. It tolerates poor, dry soils and colonises roadsides, railway embankments, and disturbed ground.
