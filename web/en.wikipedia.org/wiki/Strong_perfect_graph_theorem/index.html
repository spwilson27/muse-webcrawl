Strong perfect graph theorem

An odd hole (left) and odd antihole (right) — the only obstructions to perfection

In graph theory, the strong perfect graph theorem is a forbidden graph characterization of the perfect graphs conjectured by Claude Berge in 1961 and proved by Maria Chudnovsky, Neil Robertson, Paul Seymour, and Robin Thomas, announced in 2002 and published in the Annals of Mathematics in 2006.

Statement

A perfect graph is one in which the chromatic number of every induced subgraph equals the size of its largest clique. The theorem states that a graph is perfect if and only if neither it nor its complement contains an induced cycle of odd length at least five — an "odd hole" or "odd antihole". Such graphs are called Berge graphs, so the theorem says every Berge graph is perfect.

Proof and consequences

The proof is long and structural, running to some 150 pages, and proceeds by showing that Berge graphs fall into a few basic classes or admit certain decompositions. Together with the earlier (weak) perfect graph theorem of Lovász (1972), which states that the complement of a perfect graph is perfect, the strong theorem completely characterises perfect graphs by forbidden induced subgraphs. It also leads, with work of Chudnovsky and coauthors, to a polynomial-time algorithm for testing whether a graph is perfect. The authors received the 2009 Fulkerson Prize for the work.
