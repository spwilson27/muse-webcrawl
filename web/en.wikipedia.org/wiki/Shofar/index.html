A shofar (/ʃoʊˈfɑːr/; Hebrew: שׁוֹפָר) is an ancient musical horn typically made of a ram's horn, used for Jewish religious purposes. The shofar is mentioned frequently in the Hebrew Bible and is sounded in synagogue during the month of Elul, on Rosh Hashanah (when hearing its blasts is a biblical commandment) and at the close of Yom Kippur. In ancient Israel it served also for announcing the new moon and festivals, proclaiming the jubilee year, and as a signal in war and assembly.

Construction

A kosher shofar is made from the horn of a kosher animal — most commonly a ram, recalling the Binding of Isaac, in which a ram was substituted for Isaac — and must not come from a cow or calf, because of the episode of the golden calf. Yemenite tradition uses the long spiral horn of the kudu. The horn is heated, straightened or shaped, hollowed, and given a mouthpiece opening; natural curvature is retained in most Ashkenazi shofarot. The instrument has no valves or finger holes; pitch depends on the player's lips and the horn's length, typically producing notes around the harmonic series.

The blasts

Tradition distinguishes three blasts:

  • Tekiah — one long, unbroken sound.
  • Shevarim — three short, wailing sounds.
  • Teruah — nine (or more) very short, staccato blasts.

On Rosh Hashanah, 100 blasts are customarily sounded in the course of the service in prescribed combinations, the concluding tekiah gedolah being one great prolonged blast. Commentators from Saadia Gaon to Maimonides gave the shofar multiple meanings: a call to repentance and awakening, a reminder of the giving of the Torah at Sinai, of the binding of Isaac, and a coronation of God as king.

Historical uses

In the Bible, shofarot heralded the theophany at Sinai, accompanied the Ark, announced the jubilee in Leviticus 25, and sounded in battle — famously in the story of Gideon. In modern Israel the shofar is sounded at events such as the inauguration of a president; a famous photograph records Rabbi Shlomo Goren blowing the shofar at the Western Wall in June 1967.
