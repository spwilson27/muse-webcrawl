Ubuntu (philosophy)

[Image: A circle of people of several generations seated together under a large tree in a South African village, sharing a meal]

Ubuntu (Zulu pronunciation: [ùɓúntʼù]) is a Nguni Bantu term and philosophy emphasising the interconnectedness of all people — often glossed "humanity towards others", or by the maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, "a person is a person through other people". It describes a communitarian worldview in which identity, personhood and well-being are constituted by relationships with others, and it underpins virtues such as compassion, reciprocity, hospitality and consensus-seeking.

Meaning and usage

Related concepts exist across sub-Saharan Africa under cognate names (such as botho in Sotho–Tswana and unhu in Shona). Ubuntu has been invoked as a philosophy of restorative justice, notably by Archbishop Desmond Tutu in South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission after apartheid, where it framed public confession and forgiveness. It has also influenced theology, management theory, diplomacy ("Ubuntu diplomacy" in South African foreign policy) and computing ethics.

Criticism and interpretation

Philosophers debate whether Ubuntu names a single coherent doctrine or a family of related values, and some critics warn against romanticising it or treating it as uniquely African. Others note its practical embedding in customary law, extended-family obligations and communal land tenure. The term also lends its name to the Ubuntu Linux operating system, whose founder Mark Shuttleworth cited the philosophy of shared humanity.
