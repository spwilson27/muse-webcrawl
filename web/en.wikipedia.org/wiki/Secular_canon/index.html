Secular canon

A secular canon was a member of a chapter of canons in the Roman Catholic Church who did not live under a monastic rule, in contrast to canons regular, who followed the Rule of Saint Augustine or another religious rule and lived a common life. Secular canons served cathedral and collegiate churches, singing the daily offices and administering the chapter's property, while retaining personal property and typically living in their own houses near the church.

The canons' houses around a cathedral close were the homes of secular canons.

History

Chapters of clergy serving great churches existed from the early Middle Ages. From the eleventh and twelfth centuries, a distinction sharpened between canons who adopted a regulated common life (canons regular, such as the Augustinian and Premonstratensian canons) and those who continued to live "in the world" (in saeculo), whence "secular" canons. Proposals such as the Rule of Chrodegang, which had required a semi-common life, were largely abandoned by secular chapters by the central Middle Ages.

Organization

A secular chapter was a self-governing corporation headed by officers such as the dean, precentor, chancellor, and treasurer. Its members, or prebendaries, each held a prebend — an endowment, often a parish or estate, whose income supported them. Much of the daily singing of the offices in cathedral chapters was in practice delegated to vicars choral, minor clergy paid to deputize for the canons. Secular canons were often drawn from local noble or burgher families, and canonries served as provisions for younger sons and as rewards for royal or episcopal servants.

Decline

Many secular chapters were dissolved at the Reformation in Protestant territories and during the secularizations of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in Catholic ones. Cathedral chapters of secular canons nevertheless survive in the Catholic Church, now chiefly honorary and liturgical bodies associated with cathedrals.
