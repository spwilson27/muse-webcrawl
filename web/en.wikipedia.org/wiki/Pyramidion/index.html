Pyramidion

Pyramidion
Egyptian namebenbenet (from benben)
FunctionCapstone of a pyramid or tip of an obelisk
MaterialsGranite, quartzite, limestone; sometimes gilded or sheathed in electrum/gold
Notable examplesPyramidion of Amenemhat III (Dahshur); pyramidion of the Red Pyramid; capstone of the Washington Monument (aluminium, modern)

A pyramidion (plural pyramidia) is the uppermost piece or capstone of an Egyptian pyramid, and likewise the pointed tip of an obelisk. In ancient Egyptian it was called the benbenet, after the sacred benben stone of Heliopolis — the primordial mound on which the creator god first stood and with which the phoenix (bennu bird) was associated. Pyramidia were typically made of harder or more prestigious stone than the pyramid body, such as granite or quartzite, and were probably sheathed in gold or electrum so that they caught and reflected the sun's rays, linking the dead king with the sun god Ra.

Surviving examples

Because they crowned the monument, pyramidia were prime targets once a pyramid was ruined, and only a handful survive. The most famous is the black granite pyramidion of Amenemhat III from his pyramid at Dahshur, discovered in 1900 and now in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo: its faces bear inscriptions and a winged sun disc, with texts asking that the king "see" the sun god. A limestone pyramidion associated with the Red Pyramid of Sneferu at Dahshur — possibly the oldest known — was found in fragments nearby and has been reconstructed at the site. Pyramidia of obelisks survive more often; the tips of the Hatshepsut obelisks at Karnak carry relief scenes, and a fallen obelisk pyramidion of granite is preserved at the site.

Modern capstones

The concept persists in modern obelisks: the Washington Monument (completed 1884) is capped by a small aluminium pyramid — aluminium then being a precious metal — inscribed with dedications, and several modern memorial pyramids carry metal capstones.
