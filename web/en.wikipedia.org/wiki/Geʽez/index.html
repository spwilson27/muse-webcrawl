Geʽez (ግዕዝ, Gəʽəz), sometimes spelled Geez and also known as Classical Ethiopic, is an ancient South Semitic language that originated in the region of present-day Eritrea and northern Ethiopia. It was the language of the Aksumite Empire and became the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, a role in which it survives to the present day.

History

The earliest Geʽez inscriptions date from the period of the kingdom of Aksum, in the first centuries CE, written first in a consonantal script derived from Ancient South Arabian and later with full vowel notation. After the translation of the Bible in the 4th–6th centuries, Geʽez developed a rich literature of chronicles, theology, law (such as the Fetha Nagast), and liturgy. It ceased to be a spoken vernacular roughly between the 9th and 13th centuries, displaced by Amharic, Tigrinya, and other languages, but remained the language of learning and worship for centuries, much like Latin in Europe.

Grammar and phonology

Like other Semitic languages, Geʽez builds words from consonantal roots, typically of three consonants, fitted into vowel patterns. It has seven vowels, ejective consonants characteristic of Ethiopian Semitic, a basic word order that is usually verb-final in classical texts, and a rich system of verb derivation. Its script, an abugida, marks vowels by modifying the shapes of consonant characters.

Literature

Geʽez literature includes the Ethiopian Bible — notable for its broad canon, including the Book of Enoch, preserved complete only in Geʽez — royal chronicles, saints' lives (gadlat), hymnody attributed to Saint Yared, and the national epic Kebra Nagast, which recounts the Queen of Sheba's visit to Solomon and the transfer of the Ark of the Covenant to Ethiopia.
