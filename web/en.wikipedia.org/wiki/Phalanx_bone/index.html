Phalanx bone

Part of: Hands and feet (digits)

Number: 56 in humans (14 per hand/foot)

The phalanges (singular: phalanx) are the bones of the digits of the hand and foot in most land vertebrates. In humans, there are fourteen phalanges in each hand and each foot: three in each finger and toe — the proximal, middle (intermediate), and distal phalanges — except for the thumb and big toe (hallux), which have only two, proximal and distal. The name derives from the Greek phalanx, a battle formation of soldiers standing in close ranks, in reference to the rows of small bones.

Structure

Each phalanx is a miniature long bone consisting of a base (proximal end), a shaft (body), and a head (distal end or trochlea). The proximal phalanges articulate with the metacarpals (in the hand) or metatarsals (in the foot) at the knuckle joints; the distal phalanges are small, flattened at their tips into the ungual tuft that supports the nail and fingertip pulp. The middle and distal phalanges of the fifth toe are commonly fused in adults.

Function and clinical significance

The phalanges form the skeleton of the fingers and toes, providing leverage for flexor and extensor tendons and enabling the precision and power grips of the hand. Fractures of the phalanges are among the most common fractures of the upper limb, typically from crush or sports injuries. Congenital variations include polydactyly (extra digits), syndactyly (fused digits), and brachydactyly (shortened digits). In many animals the distal phalanx is encased in a hoof or supports a claw.

Development

Phalanges ossify from primary centres in the shaft prenatally and secondary centres at their bases after birth; the number and proportions of phalanges are diagnostic characteristics distinguishing primate species, and the 2-3-3-3-3 formula describes the human digit pattern.
