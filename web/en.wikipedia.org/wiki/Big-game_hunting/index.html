Big-game hunting

Big-game hunting is the hunting of large game animals for recreation, trophies, meat, or commercially valuable animal by-products such as horns, antlers, tusks, and hides. The term is especially associated with the hunting of Africa's so-called "Big Five"—lion, leopard, rhinoceros, elephant, and Cape buffalo—and with big-game animals elsewhere, including moose, elk, bears, and tigers historically.

History

Royal and aristocratic hunts of large animals are ancient, but the modern sporting tradition developed in the nineteenth century during colonial expansion in Africa and Asia. Figures such as Frederick Selous and, later, Theodore Roosevelt—whose 1909–10 African expedition collected thousands of specimens for the Smithsonian—popularized the safari. Ernest Hemingway's writings further romanticized the pursuit.

Methods and regulation

Big-game hunting typically uses high-powered rifles, though archery seasons exist for species such as deer, elk, and bear in North America. Hunting is regulated through licenses, tags, quotas, and seasons. Trophy hunting programs in parts of Africa are defended by supporters as funding conservation and anti-poaching efforts and providing income to local communities; critics argue that the revenue rarely reaches communities and that killing prime animals harms populations.

Controversy

The killing of Cecil the lion in Zimbabwe in 2015 by an American trophy hunter provoked international outrage and renewed debate. Hunting of endangered species and the import of trophies are restricted by laws such as the U.S. Endangered Species Act and by the CITES convention; legislation such as the Big Cat Public Safety Act addresses the related private trade in big cats.
