Iambic pentameter
Foot: iamb (da-DUM)
Feet per line: five
Syllables: typically ten
Used by: Shakespeare, Milton, Chaucer

Iambic pentameter is a type of metric line used in traditional English poetry and verse drama. It describes a line of five metrical feet ('penta'), each an iamb — an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable, da-DUM — giving a pattern close to natural English speech: 'Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?'

History

Geoffrey Chaucer introduced iambic pentameter into English in the fourteenth century, adapting French and Italian models. Christopher Marlowe made unrhymed iambic pentameter — blank verse — the dominant line of Elizabethan drama, which William Shakespeare developed with extraordinary flexibility, and John Milton used for Paradise Lost. The rhymed heroic couplet of Dryden and Pope is also iambic pentameter.

Variation

Poets vary the strict pattern with trochaic inversions ('Now is the winter of our discontent'), feminine endings (an extra unstressed syllable), spondees, pyrrhics and caesuras, so the line stays expressive rather than mechanical. See Blank verse, Sonnet and Metre (poetry).
