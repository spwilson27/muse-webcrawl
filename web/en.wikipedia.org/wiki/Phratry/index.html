A phratry (from the Greek phratria, "brotherhood", from phratēr, "brother") is a kinship group consisting of two or more clans that are believed to be related by descent, or that are joined for ceremonial, political or other purposes. In classic kinship terminology, phratries sit between the clan and the tribe: several lineages make a clan, several clans make a phratry, and phratries together make a tribe. When a society is divided into exactly two such groupings, anthropologists usually call them moieties instead.

Ancient Greece

The term comes from ancient Greek society, where the phratry was a recognised subdivision of the citizen body. In classical Athens the phratries — organisations of families claiming common ancestry and common cults, such as the Demotionidai — played a formal civic role: they admitted boys to membership (notably at the festival of the Apaturia), thereby witnessing their legitimacy and citizenship, and kept registers used in legal disputes over inheritance and citizen status. Membership in a phratry was an important proof of Athenian citizenship.

Anthropological usage

Lewis Henry Morgan and later kinship theorists adopted the Greek term for comparable groupings worldwide. Phratries have been described among many Native American peoples — for example the phratries of the Hopi and other Pueblo societies, and the clan groupings of the Iroquois — as well as in Aboriginal Australian societies. Phratries often regulate marriage (typically requiring exogamy, marriage outside one's own phratry), organise ritual reciprocity and ceremonies for each other's members, and arrange funerals or compensation payments. Because membership is by putative descent but the groups also act as ceremonial and political associations, phratries illustrate how kinship in many societies blends genealogy with social organisation.
