Biopolymer

Cellulose, the most abundant biopolymer on Earth.

Biopolymers are natural polymers produced by the cells of living organisms. Like other polymers, biopolymers consist of monomeric units that are covalently bonded in chains to form larger molecules. There are three main classes of biopolymers, classified according to the monomers used: polynucleotides (such as DNA and RNA), polypeptides (proteins), and polysaccharides (such as cellulose and starch).

Classes

Polynucleotides are long chains of thirteen or more nucleotide monomers; DNA can contain millions of nucleotides. Polypeptides are chains of amino acids forming proteins, from short peptides to giant molecules such as titin. Polysaccharides are carbohydrate polymers: cellulose and chitin are structural materials, starch and glycogen store energy, and others such as alginate and pectin act as gels. Some biopolymers, like natural rubber and suberin, do not fit neatly into these classes.

Biopolymers versus synthetic polymers

Unlike most synthetic polymers, biopolymers generally have a well-defined, sequence-specific structure (primary structure) because they are synthesised by template-directed enzymatic processes. They are typically biodegradable and renewable, features that motivate their use in food, medicine and materials science.

Applications

Biopolymers are used as food additives and packaging (gelatin, starch, alginate), in medicine (collagen scaffolds, chitosan wound dressings, silk sutures), and as industrial materials (cellulose fibres, natural rubber, bacterial polyesters such as PHAs for bioplastics).
