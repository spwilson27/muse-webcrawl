Precast concrete

Precast concrete is a construction product manufactured by casting concrete in a reusable mould or "form" in a controlled factory environment, curing it, and then transporting the finished elements to the construction site for assembly. It contrasts with cast-in-place (site-cast) concrete, which is poured into formwork at its final position. Typical products include wall panels, beams, columns, floor slabs (including hollow-core planks), piles, pipes, paving, tunnel segments, and bridge girders; elements are commonly prestressed to improve structural performance.

History

The ancient Romans used moulded concrete components, but modern precasting dates from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, with pioneers such as François Hennebique and, in Liverpool in 1905, one of the first large precast panel buildings. Precast systems proliferated after the Second World War for rapid housing and infrastructure, and prestressing — developed by Eugène Freyssinet — greatly extended the spans possible.

Advantages and limitations

Factory production gives consistent quality, controlled curing (often with steam), durable architectural finishes, and rapid on-site erection independent of weather; moulds are reused many times, suiting repetitive designs. Disadvantages include transport and cranage limits on element size and weight, the need for careful design of joints and connections to transfer loads and ensure stability, and less flexibility for late design changes. Connection detailing and progressive-collapse resistance are critical, a lesson reinforced by the partial collapse of the Ronan Point tower in London in 1968.

Applications

Precast concrete is used in stadiums, car parks, bridges, tunnels (such as segmental linings bored tunnel by tunnel), water and sewer infrastructure, and architectural cladding. Precast/prestressed double tees and hollow-core slabs are standard for long-span floors, and tilt-up construction is a related site-cast variant.
