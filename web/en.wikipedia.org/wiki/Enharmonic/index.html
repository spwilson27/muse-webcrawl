Enharmonic

DomainMusic theory
ExampleF♯ and G♭ in equal temperament
Related conceptsEnharmonic modulation, diesis

In music, two notes are enharmonic if they are spelled differently but sound the same pitch in a given tuning system. The most familiar examples arise in twelve-tone equal temperament, where, for instance, F♯ and G♭ are played with the same key of a piano and are therefore enharmonically equivalent. Enharmonic spelling is chosen according to harmonic context: a note is written as a sharp, flat, or natural depending on the key and the chord to which it belongs.

In different tuning systems

Enharmonic equivalence is a property of the temperament, not of the notes themselves. In meantone temperament, F♯ and G♭ are genuinely different pitches, separated by an interval called the diesis, and keyboards were sometimes built with split keys to provide both. In Pythagorean tuning, the difference between enharmonic spellings corresponds to the Pythagorean comma. Only in systems such as equal temperament, where such commas are tempered out, do the notes coincide exactly.

Enharmonic modulation

Composers exploit enharmonic equivalence to modulate between distant keys: a chord is reinterpreted under a different spelling and function. The classic device is the diminished seventh chord, whose symmetrical structure lets any of its notes be treated as a leading note, and the German augmented sixth chord, which can be respelled as a dominant seventh. Such pivots are common in the music of Schubert, Chopin, and Wagner.

Ancient Greek usage

The word derives from the enharmonic genus of ancient Greek music, one of the three genera of tetrachord tuning alongside the diatonic and chromatic, characterized by steps of roughly a quarter tone. The modern sense of the term developed much later, with the rise of chromatic keyboard music.
