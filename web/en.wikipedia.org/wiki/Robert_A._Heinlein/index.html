Robert Anson Heinlein (July 7, 1907 – May 8, 1988) was an American science fiction author, aeronautical engineer, and naval officer. Sometimes called the "dean of science fiction writers", he was among the first to emphasize scientific accuracy in his fiction and became a leading figure in hard science fiction. With Isaac Asimov and Arthur C. Clarke, he was regarded as one of the "Big Three" of English-language science fiction.

Heinlein's work explored social and political themes—individual liberty, self-reliance, the obligations of citizenship, and the relationship between physical and spiritual love—through a body of more than thirty novels and many short stories. Four of his novels won the Hugo Award for Best Novel: Double Star (1956), Starship Troopers (1959), Stranger in a Strange Land (1961), and The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress (1966). He was the first Science Fiction Writers of America Grand Master (1975).

Life

Heinlein grew up in Kansas City, Missouri, and graduated from the United States Naval Academy in 1929. His naval career was cut short by tuberculosis in 1934. After work in politics and engineering, he turned to writing, publishing his first story, "Life-Line", in Astounding Science-Fiction in 1939. During the Second World War he worked as an aeronautical engineer at the Naval Aircraft Factory in Philadelphia. After the war he wrote a series of juvenile novels for Scribner's before moving to adult fiction in the 1950s and 1960s. He spent his later years in California, dying in Carmel in 1988.

Themes and influence

Heinlein popularized ideas such as the competence ideal of the self-reliant protagonist, and coined terms including grok and waldo. His "Future History" stories were an early example of a coherent shared timeline in science fiction. His political views shifted over his career from socialist sympathies in the 1930s to strong libertarian individualism; his work remains both influential and controversial, and it shaped generations of science fiction writers.
