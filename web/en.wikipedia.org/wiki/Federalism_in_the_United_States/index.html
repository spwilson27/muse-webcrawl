Federalism in the United States

Basis: U.S. Constitution (1789)
Levels: Federal, state, local
Key clauses: Supremacy, Commerce, Tenth Amendment

Federalism in the United States is the constitutional division of power between the federal government and the fifty state governments, a system evolving since the Constitution replaced the Articles of Confederation in 1789.

Constitutional framework

The Constitution grants enumerated powers to Congress (Article I), reserves undelegated powers to the states or the people (Tenth Amendment), and makes federal law supreme (Supremacy Clause). Landmark cases — McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) — read federal power broadly.

Historical phases

Scholars describe dual federalism ("layer cake", to the 1930s), cooperative federalism under the New Deal ("marble cake"), and later new federalism devolving functions to states. The Civil War settled that states cannot secede; the Fourteenth Amendment nationalised many rights protections against states.

Modern practice

Fiscal federalism through grants-in-aid, preemption disputes, and state policy experimentation — on cannabis, abortion after Dobbs, and environmental rules — keep the federal balance contested. See Tenth Amendment.
