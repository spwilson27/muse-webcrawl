Copula (linguistics)

In linguistics, a copula (plural: copulas or copulae; abbreviated COP) is a word or phrase that links the subject of a sentence to a subject complement, such as the word is in the sentence "The sky is blue" or the phrase was not being in "It was not being co-operative." The word copula derives from the Latin noun for a "link" or "tie" that connects two different things.

A copula is most commonly a verb; in English, the main copular verb is be, and its forms (am, is, are, was, were, etc.). Other verbs can be used as copulas too, such as seem, become, prove, or look. Copular sentences discuss what the subject is or is like rather than what it does.

Grammatical function

The primary use of a copula is to link a grammatical subject to a predicate that describes or identifies it. The predicate can be a noun phrase ("She is a teacher"), an adjective phrase ("He is happy"), a prepositional phrase ("They are in the garden"), or an adverb phrase. Because the copula itself carries little semantic content, it mainly serves to bear tense, mood, aspect, and agreement features: "I am tired", "She was tired", "They will be tired".

Semanticians and logicians distinguish several kinds of copular sentences: predicational ("The book is heavy"), specificational ("The winner is John"), identificational ("That man is John"), and identity statements ("Cicero is Tully"). The distinction, going back to work by Higgins (1979) and others, matters because the sentences behave differently under embedding, questioning, and negation.

Copula in different languages

Not all languages use a copular verb in the same way. In the present tense, many languages — including Russian, Arabic, Hebrew, Hungarian (in some persons), and Bengali — typically omit the copula entirely: Russian Ivan — vrač ("Ivan [is] a doctor") uses a dash in writing where a verb would stand. Irish and Scottish Gaelic distinguish two copulas: the Irish copula is for classification and identity, and the substantive verb bí (tá) for states and locations. Spanish and Portuguese famously split "to be" between ser (from Latin esse) and estar (from stare), roughly for inherent and temporary properties respectively. In Japanese, the copula da (plain) or desu (polite) follows the predicate, and its analysis — as verb, particle, or auxiliary — has been much debated.

Zero copula

The zero copula is the absence of a copula where one is expected in the standard variety of a language. African American Vernacular English, for example, allows deletion of the copula in sentences like "She Ø happy" precisely where Standard English allows contraction ("She's happy"), a pattern studied by William Labov and others as evidence of systematic rule-governed grammar.
