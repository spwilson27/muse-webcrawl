The Targa Florio was an open road endurance automobile race held in the mountains of Sicily, Italy, near the island's capital, Palermo. Founded by the wealthy Sicilian businessman and motoring enthusiast Vincenzo Florio in 1906, it was one of the oldest motor races in the world and, for much of the twentieth century, one of the most prestigious, rivalling the Mille Miglia and the 24 Hours of Le Mans.

History

Florio, whose family had made a fortune in wine, shipping and tuna fishing, created the race to give Sicily an event of international standing and offered a solid gold plate (the targa) to the winner. The first race, in 1906, was won by Alessandro Cagno in an Itala over a course of three laps totalling about 446 kilometres (277 miles).

The race was run over public mountain roads in the Madonie range. Several circuit layouts were used over the years: the Grande Circuito (about 148 km per lap), the Medio Circuito and, from 1932, the Piccolo Circuito of 72 km (44.7 miles), which remained in use until the end. The short circuit alone contained some 800 corners per lap, hairpins climbing from sea level at Cerda to over 600 metres, and passed through villages such as Collesano, Castelbuono and Campofelice di Roccella, where spectators lined the roads with no protection.

World championship years

From 1955 to 1973 the Targa Florio was a round of the World Sportscar Championship. It was famous for favouring agility over outright power: small, nimble cars from Porsche, Alfa Romeo, Ferrari and Abarth excelled. Stirling Moss and Peter Collins won in 1955 in a Mercedes-Benz 300 SLR. Porsche became the most successful marque, with eleven victories. Nino Vaccarella, a Palermo schoolteacher, became a local hero with wins in 1965, 1971 and 1975. The fastest lap of the piccolo circuito was set by Leo Kinnunen in a Porsche 908/3 in 1970 at an average of about 128.6 km/h (79.9 mph).

Decline and end

After the fatal accidents that struck world championship racing in the early 1970s, the open-road format became untenable. The race lost its championship status after 1973 and continued as a national event until 1977, when a crash killed two spectators and the event was stopped for good; Italian law subsequently banned such races on public roads. Since 1978 the name has lived on as the Targa Florio Rally, a closed-stage rally, and in historic regularity events such as the Targa Florio Classica.

Legacy

The race gave its name to Porsche's "Targa" body style, introduced in 1966 in honour of the company's Sicilian successes, a name later used by other manufacturers. It remains celebrated as perhaps the most demanding road race ever held.
