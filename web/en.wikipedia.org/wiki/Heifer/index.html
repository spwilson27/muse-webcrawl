Heifer

A heifer is a young female bovine (cattle) that has not borne a calf. The term is applied from the time the animal is weaned until she calves for the first time; a female that has given birth once is typically called a first-calf heifer informally, and after further calves simply a cow. In modern dairy and beef production, heifers are managed as the replacement stock of the herd, and their feeding, breeding age and calving management are major determinants of lifetime productivity and profitability.

Terminology

Related terms in cattle husbandry include calf (a young bovine of either sex), yearling (an animal between one and two years of age), steer (a castrated male raised for beef), bull (an intact male), and springer (a heifer close to calving). The word "heifer" derives from Old English heahfore, of uncertain origin.

Management

Heifers are usually bred to calve at around 22–24 months of age in dairy herds, at roughly 55–65% of their mature body weight at breeding. Growth rates are monitored so that animals are large enough at calving to avoid dystocia (difficult birth) yet not over-conditioned, which can impair fertility and milk production. In beef herds, replacement heifers are selected for temperament, conformation and the maternal performance of their dams, and are often bred to calving-ease bulls for their first pregnancy.

In culture

Heifers figure in religious and mythological contexts: the red heifer of the Hebrew Bible, whose ashes were used in purification rites; the heifer sacrificed in ancient Greek ritual; and the Egyptian goddess Hathor, depicted with bovine features. The term also gives its name to the charity Heifer International, which provides livestock to farming families.
