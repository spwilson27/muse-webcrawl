The Putumayo River (Spanish: Río Putumayo; Portuguese: Rio Içá) is a river in South America and one of the tributaries of the Amazon River. Rising in the Andes of southwestern Colombia, it flows generally southeastward for roughly 1,610 kilometres (1,000 miles), forming for much of its course the borders between Colombia and Ecuador and between Colombia and Peru, before entering Brazil, where it is known as the Içá, and joining the Amazon.

Course

The river originates in the Colombian Massif region of the Andes. It descends through the department of Putumayo — to which it gives its name — passing the towns of Puerto Asís and Puerto Leguízamo, then continues along the frontier through dense Amazon rainforest. In Brazil the Içá flows through the state of Amazonas before emptying into the Amazon (Solimões) main stem. The river is navigable by riverboats for much of its lower and middle course and is a vital transport artery for the isolated communities along its banks.

History

The Putumayo region became infamous in the early twentieth century during the Amazon rubber boom. The rubber company operations centred on La Chorrera in the Putumayo district were exposed — notably through the investigations of the Irish diplomat Roger Casement in 1910–1911 — for the enslavement, torture and mass killing of indigenous peoples, including the Huitoto (Witoto), Bora and Andoque, who were forced to collect wild rubber. The Putumayo genocide, as it became known, killed tens of thousands and devastated the region's indigenous population. A border conflict between Colombia and Peru over the Leticia corridor in 1932–1933 also centred on this frontier zone.

Environment

The basin is covered largely by lowland Amazon rainforest of exceptional biodiversity. Its waters carry relatively low sediment loads compared with Andean whitewater rivers further south, and the floodplain supports rich fisheries that sustain local communities. In recent decades the Colombian stretch of the river has been affected by coca cultivation, armed conflict and illicit mining, as well as by oil extraction in the upper basin.

Tributaries

Major tributaries include the Guamués, San Miguel, Güepí, Caucayá, Cara Paraná and Igara Paraná rivers. The San Miguel marks part of the Colombia–Ecuador border before joining the Putumayo.
