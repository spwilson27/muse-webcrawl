Castrato

A castrato (Italian; plural: castrati) is a type of classical male singing voice that was produced by castration of the singer before puberty, or one who, because of an endocrinological condition, never reaches sexual maturity. The practice was most prevalent in Italy from the 16th to the 19th century, where castrati sang in church choirs — women being forbidden to sing in church — and later became the great stars of Baroque opera.

Voice and training

Castration before puberty prevented the boy's larynx from undergoing the changes of puberty, so the vocal cords remained short and the voice retained a soprano or contralto range, while the adult rib cage and lung capacity gave the voice unusual power, breath control, and flexibility. Castrati underwent years of rigorous training in Italian conservatories, developing legendary virtuosity in ornamentation and coloratura. Their voices were often described as combining the sweetness of a boy's voice with the strength of a man's.

History

Castrated singers existed in the Byzantine Empire and elsewhere long before the Italian practice flourished. In Italy, castrati entered the Sistine Chapel choir in the late 16th century and rose to dominate opera seria in the 17th and 18th centuries. Composers such as Handel wrote leading roles for castrati, and singers like Senesino, Caffarelli, and above all Carlo Broschi, known as Farinelli (1705–1782), achieved fame comparable to modern superstars, commanding enormous fees and inspiring frenzied devotion.

Decline

Changing musical tastes, the rise of the tenor hero, and growing moral opposition ended the era of the operatic castrato in the early 19th century. The castrato Alessandro Moreschi (1858–1922), a soloist in the Sistine Chapel, was the last of the tradition; recordings he made in 1902 and 1904 are the only surviving documents of the castrato voice. The practice had effectively been banned in a unified Italy in 1870, and Pope Pius X formally ended the use of castrati in church music in 1903.
