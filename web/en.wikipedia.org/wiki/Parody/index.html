Parody

A parody is a creative work, or a passage within one, that imitates the style, mannerisms, or content of another work, author, or genre, usually with deliberate exaggeration for comic or satirical effect. A successful parody depends on the audience recognising the original being imitated; without that recognition, much of the humour is lost. Parody differs from a mere imitation in that it transforms its source, commenting on it even while reproducing its surface features.

Etymology and origins

The word derives from the Greek parōidía (παρῳδία), a "burlesque song" or poem — literally a "song sung beside" another. In his Poetics, Aristotle credits Hegemon of Thasos with inventing the form, composing burlesque versions of epic verse. Parodic writing flourished in ancient Rome as well, and the mock-heroic tradition continued through the Middle Ages and the Renaissance.

Forms and functions

Parody may target an individual work, the oeuvre of a single creator, or an entire genre. Miguel de Cervantes's Don Quixote (1605) parodied the chivalric romances popular in his day, while Jane Austen's Northanger Abbey gently mocked Gothic novels. In music, "Weird Al" Yankovic built a career on song parodies that reproduce a hit's melody with altered, comic lyrics. In film, works such as Young Frankenstein, Airplane!, Austin Powers, and the Scary Movie series parody horror, disaster, and spy genres respectively.

Literary theorists distinguish parody from related forms: a pastiche imitates in a celebratory or neutral spirit; a spoof is a broad, light-hearted genre imitation; satire uses ridicule to attack vice or folly and may or may not imitate a specific target. Linda Hutcheon famously described parody as "repetition with critical distance," emphasising that it need not be contemptuous of its source.

Legal status

Because parody necessarily copies elements of its target, it often raises copyright questions. In the United States, the Supreme Court held in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994) that a commercial rap parody of Roy Orbison's "Oh, Pretty Woman" could qualify as fair use, giving parody significant protection as transformative commentary. Satire that merely borrows a work to attack something else receives weaker protection. European and other jurisdictions protect parody through exceptions of varying breadth.
