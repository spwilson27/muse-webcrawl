Sapsucker

A sapsucker at its sap wells in a tree trunk.

The sapsuckers are a genus, Sphyrapicus, of North American woodpeckers. As their name suggests, they feed primarily on tree sap, which they obtain by drilling neat rows of shallow holes, called sap wells, into the bark of living trees. They also eat insects, especially those attracted to the sap, and occasionally fruit and buds.

Species

There are four recognised species:

  • Yellow-bellied sapsucker (Sphyrapicus varius) — breeds in Canada and the eastern United States.
  • Red-naped sapsucker (Sphyrapicus nuchalis) — breeds in the Rocky Mountain region.
  • Red-breasted sapsucker (Sphyrapicus ruber) — breeds along the Pacific coast.
  • Williamson's sapsucker (Sphyrapicus thyroideus) — breeds in western North America and shows striking sexual dimorphism.

Feeding and ecology

Sapsuckers maintain their wells by revisiting them regularly and drilling fresh holes as the sap flow changes. The wells are an important food resource for other animals, including hummingbirds, which time their spring migration to coincide with the sapsuckers' activity and drink from the wells, as do bats, porcupines and various insects. Because they attack living trees, sapsuckers can damage orchards and ornamental trees, and heavy feeding can sometimes girdle and kill a tree.

Unlike many woodpeckers, sapsuckers have brush-like tongues adapted for lapping sap rather than long barbed tongues for extracting insects from wood.
