Burning bush is a common name for Euonymus alatus, the winged spindle or winged euonymus, a species of deciduous shrub in the family Celastraceae native to northeastern Asia. The name refers to its spectacular autumn foliage, which turns a vivid fluorescent red — appearing to "burn" — before the leaves fall. The species epithet alatus means "winged", referring to the corky ridges or wings that run along its stems. Introduced to North America around 1860, it became one of the most popular ornamental shrubs of the twentieth century, though it is now regarded as invasive in many eastern forests.

Description

The shrub typically grows 1–3 metres tall in cultivation (larger in the wild), with opposite, finely toothed, dark green leaves 2–7 cm long. Inconspicuous yellow-green flowers appear in spring, followed by small orange-red fruits that split to show red-orange seeds, eaten and spread by birds. Forms vary in their corky winging; the dwarf cultivar 'Compactus' is the most widely planted.

Cultivation and invasiveness

Burning bush tolerates a wide range of soils, shade, and drought once established, and was planted en masse in gardens, hedges, and highway plantings. Its bird-dispersed seeds invade woodlands, where it forms dense thickets that shade out native understory plants; it is listed as invasive in many US states, and several states restrict or ban its sale. Gardeners are encouraged to substitute native shrubs with red autumn colour, such as chokeberry or fragrant sumac; sterile cultivars have been developed in response.

Other plants called burning bush

The name is also applied to other plants, including the gas plant Dictamnus albus, whose volatile oils can ignite on hot days, the summer cypress Bassia scoparia, and species of Combretum. The biblical burning bush encountered by Moses is treated in a separate article.
