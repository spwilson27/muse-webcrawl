Ferdinand Magellan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Victoria, the only ship of Magellan's fleet to complete the circumnavigation]

Ferdinand Magellan (c. 1480 – 27 April 1521) was a Portuguese explorer who organised and led the Spanish expedition that achieved the first circumnavigation of the Earth (1519–1522). Sailing for Charles V to find a westward route to the Spice Islands, his five ships discovered the strait at South America's tip that bears his name, crossed the Pacific — which he named — and reached the Philippines, where Magellan was killed at the Battle of Mactan in April 1521. Juan Sebastián Elcano brought the last ship, Victoria, home to Spain in September 1522 with 18 of the original 270 men, proving the globe's scale and sphericity in a single voyage.

The strait and the Pacific

Magellan, fallen from Portuguese favour after service in India and Morocco, offered Spain his plan backed by the (erroneous) claim that the Moluccas lay in Spain's hemisphere. After suppressing a mutiny at Puerto San Julián in Patagonia (1520) and losing the Santiago, he threaded the 373-mile strait in 38 days and entered, on 28 November 1520, an ocean so calm he called it Pacífico. The crossing took 99 days of starvation — crews ate rats, sawdust and leather — before Guam (March 1521) and the Philippines.

Mactan and the completion of the voyage

Seeking allies and converts, Magellan intervened in a local conflict on Cebu's behalf and was killed by Lapulapu's warriors on Mactan (27 April 1521). The survivors burned one ship, lost another, and under Elcano the Victoria crossed the Indian Ocean and rounded the Cape, reaching Sanlúcar on 6 September 1522 loaded with cloves. Pigafetta's narrative preserved the voyage's record. Magellan's legacy spans the strait, the Magellanic Clouds and Penguins, the International Date Line's discovery-by-confusion (the crew's log was a day behind), and the Philippines' colonisation and Catholicism that followed.
