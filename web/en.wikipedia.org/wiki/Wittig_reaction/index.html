Wittig reaction

The Wittig reaction converts a carbonyl compound into an alkene

The Wittig reaction or Wittig olefination is a chemical reaction of an aldehyde or ketone with a triphenyl phosphonium ylide (often called a Wittig reagent) to give an alkene and triphenylphosphine oxide. It was discovered in 1954 by Georg Wittig, for which he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1979, shared with Herbert C. Brown for his work on organoboron compounds. The Wittig reaction is most commonly used to convert aldehydes and ketones to alkenes, and is especially valuable because it places the double bond at a precisely defined position — the site of the original carbonyl group — unlike elimination reactions, which can give mixtures of positional isomers.

Reaction mechanism

The key intermediate in the reaction is the phosphonium ylide, prepared by treating a phosphonium salt with a strong base such as n-butyllithium or sodium amide. The ylide carbon, bearing both a negative charge and an adjacent positively charged phosphorus, attacks the carbonyl carbon of the aldehyde or ketone. The resulting betaine and/or oxaphosphetane intermediate then collapses, with formation of the very strong phosphorus–oxygen double bond providing the thermodynamic driving force, ejecting triphenylphosphine oxide and forming the alkene.

Mechanistic studies, including low-temperature NMR observation of oxaphosphetanes, support a concerted asynchronous [2+2] cycloaddition pathway for most cases rather than a discrete betaine intermediate. The stereochemical outcome depends on the ylide: unstabilized ylides generally give mainly Z (cis) alkenes, whereas stabilized ylides (bearing an electron-withdrawing group) give mainly E (trans) alkenes.

Variations

The Schlosser modification allows the production of E-alkenes from unstabilized ylides. The Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons reaction uses phosphonate carbanions instead of phosphonium ylides; it typically gives E-alkenes and produces water-soluble phosphate by-products that are easier to remove than triphenylphosphine oxide. Related olefinations include the Peterson olefination, the Julia olefination, and the Tebbe and Petasis methylenations.

Applications

The Wittig reaction has been used in the total synthesis of numerous natural products, including vitamin A and vitamin D derivatives, prostaglandins, and complex molecules such as taxol and leukotrienes. Its variant, the intramolecular Wittig reaction, can form ring systems. Industrially, a Wittig step was used in the synthesis of vitamin A. A notable feature is its tolerance of a wide range of functional groups — esters, ethers, nitriles, and even unprotected hydroxyl groups in some circumstances.

Limitations

Sterically hindered ketones react slowly or not at all. The stoichiometric production of triphenylphosphine oxide — a high molecular weight by-product — is a drawback in atom-economy and purification terms, and efforts continue to make the reaction catalytic in phosphorus.
