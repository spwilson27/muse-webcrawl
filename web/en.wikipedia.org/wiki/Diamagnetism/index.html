Diamagnetism is the property of materials that are repelled by a magnetic field; an applied magnetic field creates an induced magnetic field in them in the opposite direction, causing a repulsive force. In contrast, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic materials are attracted by a magnetic field. Diamagnetism is a quantum mechanical effect that occurs in all materials; when it is the only contribution to the magnetism, the material is called diamagnetic.

History

In 1778, S. J. Brugmans first observed that bismuth and antimony were repelled by magnetic fields. Michael Faraday coined the term diamagnetism in 1845, realising that all materials respond to an applied magnetic field in some way. Pierre Curie later found that diamagnetic susceptibility is generally independent of temperature.

Mechanism

Diamagnetism arises from the realignment of electron orbits under an external field: the field alters the orbital motion of electrons, producing small currents whose magnetic moment opposes the applied field, in accordance with Lenz's law. Unlike paramagnetism, it does not depend on permanent atomic magnetic moments or on temperature.

Materials and applications

Most organic compounds, water, and many metals such as copper, mercury, gold and bismuth are diamagnetic; bismuth has one of the strongest diamagnetic responses among ordinary materials. Superconductors exhibit perfect diamagnetism (the Meissner effect), expelling magnetic fields entirely. Strong diamagnetic forces allow levitation of water-based objects, famously demonstrated with a levitating frog by Andre Geim and co-workers in 1997.
