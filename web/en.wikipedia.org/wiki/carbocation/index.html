Carbocation

A carbocation is an ion with a positively charged carbon atom. Among the simplest examples are the methanium ion CH5+, the ethanium ion C2H7+, the acetonium ion CH3CO+, and the benzenium ion C6H7+, which are found in superacids. Until the early 1970s, all carbocations were called carbonium ions; in modern terminology, carbonium ions have five or more bonds and are nonclassical structures, whereas classical carbenium ions have exactly three bonds.

Structure and stability

A classical carbocation has an sp2-hybridized carbon with an empty p orbital and a trigonal planar geometry. Stability increases with substitution: tertiary carbocations are more stable than secondary, which are more stable than primary, because alkyl groups donate electron density by hyperconjugation and induction. Resonance-stabilized carbocations — allylic and benzylic cations — are especially stable, while vinyl and aryl cations are highly unstable. Rearrangements, in which a hydride or alkyl group shifts to an adjacent carbocation centre (Wagner–Meerwein rearrangements), are characteristic of carbocation chemistry and convert less stable cations into more stable ones.

History

Carbocations were proposed as reactive intermediates in the early 20th century, and their mechanisms were developed by Hughes, Ingold, and Whitmore in the 1930s. The long-running "nonclassical ion" controversy concerned the structure of the 2-norbornyl cation. George Olah's work with superacids such as magic acid (a mixture of fluorosulfonic acid and antimony pentafluoride) allowed carbocations to be prepared as stable, long-lived species and studied directly by NMR spectroscopy; Olah received the 1994 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

Role in reactions

Carbocations are the key intermediates of SN1 substitution and E1 elimination reactions, and they arise in electrophilic aromatic substitution (as arenium ions), Friedel–Crafts reactions, alkene polymerization, petroleum cracking and isomerization, and the biosynthesis of terpenes, where enzyme-controlled carbocation cascades build complex polycyclic skeletons.
