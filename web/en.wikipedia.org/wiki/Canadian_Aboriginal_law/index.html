The Supreme Court of Canada, whose decisions shape Aboriginal law

Canadian Aboriginal law is the body of law in Canada concerning the rights of the original peoples of the land — the First Nations, Inuit, and Métis — and their relationship with the Crown. It encompasses Aboriginal title, Aboriginal rights, treaty rights, the duty to consult, and the statutory regime of the Indian Act.

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 "recognizes and affirms" the existing Aboriginal and treaty rights of the Aboriginal peoples of Canada. Landmark Supreme Court of Canada decisions have defined the content of those rights and the obligations of governments.

Historical foundations

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 reserved lands not ceded by treaty for Indigenous nations and established that only the Crown could acquire their land, and then only by agreement. Treaties — including the pre-Confederation treaties, the Numbered Treaties (1871–1921), and modern comprehensive land claims agreements — form a central pillar of the legal framework. The Indian Act of 1876 created the reserve and band system and the legal category of "status Indian."

Aboriginal title and rights

In Calder v British Columbia (1973), the Supreme Court recognised that Aboriginal title exists in Canadian law. R v Sparrow (1990) set out the test for infringements of section 35 rights. Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997) defined Aboriginal title as a right to the land itself and gave weight to oral histories as evidence. In Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia (2014), the Court issued the first declaration of Aboriginal title in Canada.

Duty to consult

Haida Nation v British Columbia (2004) established the Crown's duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate Indigenous peoples when contemplating conduct that may adversely affect claimed or established rights — a duty grounded in the honour of the Crown.
