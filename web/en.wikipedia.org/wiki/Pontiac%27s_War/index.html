Pontiac's War (also called Pontiac's Rebellion or Pontiac's Conspiracy) was a conflict launched in 1763 by a loose confederation of Native American peoples in the Great Lakes and Ohio Country against the British military occupation of the region following France's defeat in the French and Indian War. It is named for Pontiac, an Ottawa war chief who led the attack on Fort Detroit.

Causes

Native nations had allied with France and resented British commander Jeffery Amherst's policies: he ended the customary gift-giving that cemented alliances, restricted trade in ammunition, and permitted settlement pressure on Native lands. A religious revival preached by the Lenape prophet Neolin — urging rejection of European goods and ways — helped unite disparate peoples.

Course of the war

In May 1763 Pontiac's forces besieged Fort Detroit, while allied warriors captured eight smaller British forts, including Michilimackinac, taken by a stratagem during a game of lacrosse. Frontier settlements in Pennsylvania and Virginia were raided, driving thousands of settlers east. British counteroffensives — Henry Bouquet's victory at Bushy Run (1763) and expeditions into the Ohio Country (1764) — together with disease, French refusal to help, and Amherst's notorious correspondence suggesting the distribution of smallpox-infected blankets at Fort Pitt, brought the fighting to a close. Pontiac made formal peace in 1766.

Legacy

The war prompted Britain's Royal Proclamation of 1763, reserving lands west of the Appalachians for Native peoples — a measure that angered American colonists and fed the grievances leading to the American Revolution. Although the uprising did not expel the British, it demonstrated the power of intertribal resistance and reshaped imperial policy.
