Altruism (biology)

Sterile worker insects are the classic case of biological altruism
DefinitionBehaviour that lowers the actor's fitness while raising another's
Key explanationsKin selection, reciprocal altruism, group selection
Classic examplesSocial insects, alarm calls, vampire bat food sharing

In biology, altruism refers to behaviour by an individual that increases the fitness of another individual while decreasing its own fitness. The definition is framed in terms of reproductive consequences, not intentions or emotions, so it can be applied to any organism. Altruism posed an apparent problem for the theory of evolution by natural selection: how can genes for costly helping spread if helpers leave fewer descendants?

Theoretical explanations

The principal solution is kin selection, formalised by W. D. Hamilton in 1964. A gene promoting altruism can spread when the benefit to relatives, weighted by their relatedness (r), exceeds the cost to the actor — Hamilton's rule, rb > c. This explains the extreme altruism of social insects, in which sterile workers raise their sisters, and alarm calls that warn relatives of predators. Reciprocal altruism, proposed by Robert Trivers, explains helping among unrelated individuals when the favour is likely to be returned, as in vampire bats sharing blood meals with roostmates that failed to feed. Group- and multi-level selection remain more controversial explanations.

Examples

Besides eusocial insects, biological altruism is seen in cooperative breeding birds and mammals, sentinel behaviour in meerkats, food sharing in primates and bats, and even in microorganisms, where cells may sacrifice themselves to release resources or toxins benefiting clone-mates. Human altruism adds cultural and psychological mechanisms — empathy, reputation, and moral norms — beyond those operating by genes alone.
