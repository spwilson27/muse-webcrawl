Clonal selection

Clonal selection theory is a scientific theory in immunology that explains the functions of cells of the immune system (lymphocytes) in response to specific antigens invading the body. The theory was proposed by Australian immunologist Frank Macfarlane Burnet in 1957, building on an earlier idea by Niels Jerne, and it became the foundation of modern cellular immunology.

Postulates

Burnet's theory rests on several postulates. Each lymphocyte bears a single type of receptor with a unique specificity. For a cell to be activated, its receptor must bind its corresponding antigen with sufficient affinity. The activated cell proliferates to form a clone of cells bearing identical receptors. Finally, lymphocytes bearing receptors specific for self molecules are deleted at an early stage of development, producing tolerance to self.

Mechanism

When an antigen enters the body, it selects the rare B or T cell whose receptor happens to fit it. That cell divides repeatedly—a process known as clonal expansion—producing large numbers of identical descendants. Some become short-lived effector cells (plasma cells secreting antibodies, in the case of B cells), while others persist as long-lived memory cells that allow a faster, stronger response on re-exposure to the same antigen. This is the cellular basis of immunological memory exploited by vaccination.

Clonal selection in other fields

The principle has been extended beyond immunology: in viticulture, clonal selection refers to the evaluation and propagation of superior vine clones; in computing, clonal selection algorithms are a class of artificial immune systems used in optimization and pattern recognition.

Historical reception

Initially speculative, the theory was confirmed as techniques for studying single lymphocytes and monoclonal antibodies (developed by Köhler and Milstein in 1975) became available. Burnet shared the 1960 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work on acquired immunological tolerance.
