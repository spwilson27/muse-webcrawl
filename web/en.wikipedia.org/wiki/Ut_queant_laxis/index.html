Ut queant laxis


The hymn in square notation

Ut queant laxis is a Latin hymn in honour of John the Baptist, traditionally attributed to the eighth-century Lombard historian Paul the Deacon. It is famous far beyond liturgy: in the eleventh century the music theorist Guido d'Arezzo took the opening syllables of its phrases to name the notes of the scale, giving rise to solfège.

The Guidonian syllables

Each successive phrase of the hymn's melody begins one scale degree higher: Ut queant laxis, Resonare fibris, Mira gestorum, Famuli tuorum, Solve polluti, Labii reatum — "So that your servants may, with loosened voices, sing of your wondrous deeds, dissolve the guilt from their stained lips, O Saint John." The initial syllables Ut–Re–Mi–Fa–Sol–La became the names of the hexachord degrees. Later, si (from Sancte Iohannes) was added for the seventh degree, and in the seventeenth century the awkward ut was largely replaced by the open-sounding do — producing the modern do-re-mi system.

Liturgical use

The hymn is sung at Vespers of the feast of the Nativity of John the Baptist (24 June). Its melody, unusually, starts each of the first six phrases on the next degree of the scale, which is what made it a ready-made teaching aid for singers learning to sight-read chant.
