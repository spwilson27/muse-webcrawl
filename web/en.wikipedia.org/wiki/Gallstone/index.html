Gallstone

A gallstone is a stone formed within the gallbladder out of precipitated bile components. The medical term for the presence of gallstones is cholelithiasis. Most people with gallstones (about 80%) are asymptomatic; when a stone blocks the cystic duct or common bile duct it can cause severe pain (biliary colic) and complications such as cholecystitis, cholangitis and pancreatitis.

Types and formation

Gallstones are classified by composition. Cholesterol stones, the most common type in Western countries, form when bile contains too much cholesterol relative to the bile salts and lecithin that keep it dissolved, combined with gallbladder stasis. Pigment stones are composed mainly of bilirubin polymers and calcium salts; black pigment stones are associated with haemolytic conditions and cirrhosis, while brown pigment stones are associated with bacterial infection of the bile ducts. Mixed stones are also common.

Signs and symptoms

The classic symptom is episodic pain in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium, often after a fatty meal, lasting from minutes to a few hours. Persistent blockage of the cystic duct produces acute cholecystitis with fever and localised tenderness (Murphy's sign); stones in the common bile duct can cause obstructive jaundice, dark urine and pale stools, and may trigger acute pancreatitis or ascending cholangitis — the combination of pain, fever and jaundice is known as Charcot's triad.

Risk factors

Risk increases with age, female sex, pregnancy and oestrogen exposure, obesity, rapid weight loss, a family history, haemolytic disorders and certain medications. The traditional mnemonic summarised risk as the "five Fs": female, fat, fertile, forty and fair, though gallstones occur in all populations.

Diagnosis and treatment

Abdominal ultrasound is the standard first-line investigation. Asymptomatic stones usually need no treatment. Symptomatic disease is most often treated definitively by laparoscopic cholecystectomy (removal of the gallbladder). Stones in the common bile duct may be removed endoscopically by ERCP. Oral ursodeoxycholic acid can dissolve small cholesterol stones in selected patients but works slowly and stones often recur.
