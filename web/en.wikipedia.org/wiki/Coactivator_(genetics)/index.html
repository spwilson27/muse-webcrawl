Coactivator (genetics)

A coactivator is a type of transcriptional coregulator that binds to an activator (a transcription factor) to increase the rate of transcription of a gene or set of genes. The activator contains a DNA binding domain that binds either to a DNA promoter site or a specific DNA regulatory sequence called an enhancer. Binding of the activator-coactivator complex increases the speed of transcription by recruiting general transcription machinery to the promoter, therefore increasing the expression of the genes.

Mechanism

The use of a coactivator allows specific control of gene expression depending on the cell type, developmental stage, or environmental signals. Coactivators typically possess intrinsic enzymatic activity, most notably histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity, which acetylates lysine residues on histone proteins. This loosens the chromatin structure around a gene, making its promoter more accessible to RNA polymerase II and the general transcription factors.

Some coactivators also function as scaffolding proteins. The large multisubunit Mediator complex, for example, physically bridges activators bound at enhancers with the preinitiation complex assembled at the promoter.

Classes of coactivators

Well-studied coactivators include the histone acetyltransferases CREB-binding protein (CBP) and its paralogue p300, the steroid receptor coactivator (SRC/p160) family that serves nuclear receptors, and PPARγ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism. Many coactivators contain bromodomains or other modules that read post-translational modifications on histones.

Clinical significance

Mutations in coactivator genes can cause developmental disorders; loss of one copy of the CREBBP gene, encoding CBP, causes Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome. Because coactivators integrate signals from many transcription factors, their dysregulation is also implicated in cancer and metabolic disease, making them targets of ongoing drug-discovery research.
