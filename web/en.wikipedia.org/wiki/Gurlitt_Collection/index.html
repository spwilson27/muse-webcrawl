Gurlitt Collection

The Gurlitt Collection was a hoard of some 1,500 works of art inherited by the German art collector Cornelius Gurlitt (1932–2014), son of the Nazi-era art dealer Hildebrand Gurlitt. The collection's existence became public in November 2013 after German authorities, investigating a tax case, seized the works from Cornelius Gurlitt's Munich apartment in 2012. It became a major international case in the field of Nazi-looted art and restitution.

Background

Hildebrand Gurlitt, though of partly Jewish ancestry himself, was one of only four dealers authorised by the Nazi regime to sell "degenerate art" confiscated from German museums, and he also bought works in occupied France, acquiring many pieces at depressed prices from Jewish owners under persecution. After the war he claimed his collection had been destroyed in the bombing of Dresden.

Discovery

Cornelius Gurlitt came to official attention in 2010 when Swiss customs found him carrying a large amount of cash on a train from Zurich. In February 2012, prosecutors searched his Schwabing apartment and removed about 1,280 works, including pieces by Picasso, Matisse, Chagall, Beckmann, Nolde, Kirchner, Dix, and Liebermann; a further group of works was later found at his house in Salzburg. The story broke in the magazine Focus in November 2013, causing a worldwide sensation.

Provenance research and legacy

A German task force ("Schwabing Art Trove") investigated the provenance of the works; only a small number were confirmed as Nazi-looted and restituted to heirs, while hundreds remained of unclear status. Gurlitt, who died in May 2014, bequeathed the collection to the Kunstmuseum Bern in Switzerland, which accepted the bequest under conditions excluding works suspected of being looted. Selections were exhibited in Bern and Bonn in 2017–18 under the title "Degenerate Art" — Confiscated and Sold.
