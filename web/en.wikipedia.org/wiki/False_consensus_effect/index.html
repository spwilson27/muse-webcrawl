False consensus effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, the false consensus effect, also known as consensus bias, is an attributional type of cognitive bias whereby people tend to overestimate the extent to which their opinions, beliefs, preferences, values, and habits are normal and typical of those of others. In other words, people tend to assume that their own opinions and choices are much more common and appropriate than they actually are.

The effect derives its name from the tendency of a group or individual to incorrectly assume that everyone else in the group shares their viewpoint. It is related to the broader tendency toward social projection.

Origins

The false consensus effect was first named and studied systematically by social psychologists Lee Ross, David Greene, and Pamela House in a series of experiments published in 1977. In one classic study, participants who agreed to wear a sign advertising a local restaurant estimated that a larger share of their peers would also agree than did participants who refused.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. People selectively associate with others who share their views (selective exposure), making their own position seem more prevalent. Motivationally, assuming consensus flatters one's self-image by making one's opinions seem normal and correct. Cognitively, one's own opinion is the most salient and available example, and people anchor estimates of others on themselves and adjust insufficiently.

Implications

The effect contributes to overconfidence in debates, polarization, and pluralistic ignorance. In politics, partisans on each side tend to overestimate the popularity of their own position. In marketing and negotiation, it can distort estimates of customer preferences and of the other side's likely behaviour.
