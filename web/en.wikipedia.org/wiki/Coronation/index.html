Coronation

A coronation is the act of placement or bestowal of a crown upon a monarch's head. The term also generally refers not only to the physical crowning but to the ceremony wherein the act of crowning occurs, along with the presentation of other items of regalia, marking the formal investiture of a monarch with regal power.

Coronations are often — though not universally — religious rites, in which the monarch is anointed with holy oil and blessed, signifying divine sanction for their rule. In modern constitutional monarchies the legal authority of the sovereign usually derives from accession law rather than the ceremony itself, and most European monarchies have replaced or simplified coronation rituals: Norway, the Netherlands and others now use inauguration or blessing ceremonies, while the United Kingdom retains the medieval rite.

History

Crowning rituals have ancient roots — Egyptian pharaohs, Persian kings and Roman emperors all used forms of crowning or diadem-bestowal. In Christian Europe, anointing on the biblical model (as Samuel anointed David) became central: Charlemagne was crowned by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800. The British coronation service at Westminster Abbey, recognisably similar since the coronation of Edgar in 973 and codified in the Liber Regalis (c. 1382), includes recognition, oath, anointing, investiture with regalia (orb, sceptres, ring and spurs), crowning with St Edward's Crown, enthronement and homage.

Coronations today

Elaborate coronations continue in a few countries, including Thailand and some Pacific kingdoms, while Japan's enthronement ceremonies serve a similar function. The most recent British coronation was that of Charles III and Camilla on 6 May 2023.
