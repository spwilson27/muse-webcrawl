Realism (international relations)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Realism, a school of thought in international relations theory, holds that the international system is anarchic — lacking any authority above the state — and that states, as the principal actors, pursue power and security in their own interest. Realists trace their lineage to Thucydides, Machiavelli and Hobbes; the modern school crystallised with E. H. Carr's critique of interwar "utopianism" (1939) and Hans Morgenthau's Politics Among Nations (1948), which grounded politics in human nature's will to power (classical realism).

Variants

Kenneth Waltz's neorealism (structural realism, 1979) relocated the cause to the system's structure: anarchy plus the distribution of capabilities compels self-help and balancing regardless of leaders' preferences. Offensive realists (Mearsheimer) argue states maximise power; defensive realists hold they seek enough security, warning that over-expansion provokes balancing. Neoclassical realism reintroduces domestic variables. Realism's policy corollaries — scepticism of crusading interventions, attention to great-power spheres and escalation risks, insistence on defining interests narrowly — make it a permanent contender in debates from the Cold War to Ukraine and Taiwan, praised for prudence and criticised for amorality, neglecting economics, institutions and ideas, and under-predicting cooperation and change (such as the Cold War's peaceful end).
