United States Naval Aviator

A United States Naval Aviator is a commissioned officer in the United States Navy or United States Marine Corps who has qualified as a pilot of naval aircraft. Naval aviators fly a wide variety of fixed-wing and rotary-wing aircraft from aircraft carriers, amphibious assault ships, and shore bases, performing missions that include air combat, strike warfare, anti-submarine warfare, surveillance, electronic warfare, and logistics support.

History

Naval aviation began in 1910–1911, when Eugene Ely made the first takeoff from and landing on a warship, and the Navy designated its first pilots. Naval aviation expanded enormously during the Second World War, when carrier-based aircraft proved decisive in the Pacific Theater. Since then, naval aviators have flown in every major American conflict, from Korea and Vietnam to the Gulf War and later operations.

Training

Student naval aviators complete primary flight training, then are selected for a pipeline (strike, maritime, rotary, or tilt-rotor) and proceed through intermediate and advanced training. Training culminates in carrier qualification and the award of the distinctive gold Naval Aviator insignia, known as "wings of gold," typically after roughly two years of training. Newly winged aviators then report to a Fleet Replacement Squadron to learn their specific fleet aircraft.

Insignia

The Naval Aviator badge consists of a shield flanked by outstretched wings with an anchor at the center. A related badge, the Naval Flight Officer insignia, is worn by non-pilot aircrew officers.
