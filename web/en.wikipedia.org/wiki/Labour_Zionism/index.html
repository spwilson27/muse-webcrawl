Labour Zionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Labor Zionism (or socialist Zionism) is the left-wing tradition of the Zionist movement, holding that a Jewish state could be built not by appealing to governments or wealthy patrons but through the labour of Jewish settlers working the land in Palestine themselves. It became the dominant strain of Zionism in the Yishuv (the Jewish community in Mandatory Palestine) and in Israel, where the Labor Party and its predecessors governed from independence in 1948 until 1977.

Ideas

The ideology combined Jewish national renewal with socialist principles. Thinkers such as Nachman Syrkin and Ber Borochov argued that Jews in the diaspora were kept in an "inverted pyramid" of occupations and needed to become farmers and workers — a process A. D. Gordon elevated into a near-religious "religion of labour". Key values included the "conquest of labour" (kibbush ha'avoda), self-reliance, and the building of a model egalitarian society.

Institutions

  • Kibbutzim and moshavim – collective and cooperative agricultural settlements.
  • Histadrut (founded 1920) – the trade union federation that also ran businesses, health services and cultural institutions, effectively a state within a state.
  • Haganah – the pre-state defence organisation, closely tied to the labour movement.
  • Mapai (founded 1930) – the dominant labour party led by David Ben-Gurion, later merged into the Israeli Labor Party.

Decline

Labour dominance ended with the Likud victory of 1977. Economic liberalisation, demographic change and criticism of the kibbutz model eroded the movement through the late twentieth century, though its institutions — above all the kibbutzim and the Labor Party — remain part of Israeli life.
