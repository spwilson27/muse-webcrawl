Biological pump

Biological pump
[Image: marine snow falling through the water column]
Type: Oceanographic / biogeochemical process
Transports: Carbon, nutrients
Estimated flux: ~5–12 Gt of carbon per year exported from surface waters

The biological pump (or ocean carbon biological pump) is the ocean's biologically driven sequestration of carbon from the atmosphere and land runoff to the deep ocean interior and seafloor sediments. It is the part of the oceanic carbon cycle responsible for the cycling of organic matter formed mainly by phytoplankton during photosynthesis, and its subsequent export downward as dead cells, faecal pellets, and aggregates known as marine snow.

Mechanism

In the sunlit surface ocean (the euphotic zone), phytoplankton fix carbon dioxide into organic matter. Some of this material escapes grazing and sinks. As particles descend, microbes remineralise most of them back into dissolved inorganic carbon, which can remain stored in the deep ocean for centuries to millennia. A small fraction reaches the seafloor and is buried in sediments, removing carbon from the active cycle for geological timescales. Active transport by vertically migrating zooplankton and fish also contributes to the export flux.

Importance

Without the biological pump, atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations would be substantially higher than today — model estimates suggest by roughly 200 parts per million or more. The pump thus exerts a first-order control on Earth's climate. Its efficiency varies with nutrient supply (especially iron), temperature, and ocean circulation, and researchers study how climate change, ocean acidification, and deoxygenation may strengthen or weaken it.

Related concepts

The biological pump works alongside the solubility pump, the physical transport of dissolved CO₂ into cold deep waters. Proposals for ocean iron fertilisation have sought to artificially enhance the biological pump, though their effectiveness and ecological safety remain controversial.
