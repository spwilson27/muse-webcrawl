Turbomachinery

Image placeholder: turbomachinery

Turbomachinery, in mechanical engineering, describes machines that transfer energy between a rotor and a fluid, including both turbines and compressors. While a turbine transfers energy from a fluid to a rotor, a compressor transfers energy from a rotor to a fluid. The two types are described together because they share fundamental principles and often operate in the same installation, such as a gas turbine engine.

Classification

Turbomachines may be classified by the direction of flow through the rotor. In axial-flow machines the fluid moves parallel to the axis of rotation, as in most jet engine compressors and turbines. In radial-flow (centrifugal) machines the fluid enters or leaves radially, as in centrifugal compressors and turbocharger turbines. Mixed-flow machines combine both directions. Machines are also grouped by function: compressors, pumps and fans add energy to the fluid, while turbines extract energy from it.

Principles of operation

The operation of a turbomachine is governed by the Euler turbomachine equation, which relates the specific work transferred to the change in the fluid's angular momentum across the rotor. Blade rows — rotating rotors alternating with stationary stators — turn and diffuse or accelerate the flow; the geometry of the velocity triangles at blade inlet and outlet determines the pressure rise or drop and the work exchanged. Designs are commonly characterised by quantities such as flow coefficient, stage loading, degree of reaction and specific speed.

Applications

Turbomachinery underpins modern power generation and transport. Applications include gas turbines and steam turbines in power stations, aircraft jet engines, turbochargers and superchargers for engines, hydraulic turbines in hydroelectric plants, pumps, compressors in refrigeration and process industries, and wind turbines. Efficiencies of large modern machines can exceed 90 per cent, and continuing advances in blade aerodynamics, cooling and materials have pushed gas turbine inlet temperatures well beyond the melting point of the blade alloys themselves.

History

Water wheels and windmills are ancient turbomachines, and Hero of Alexandria described a reaction steam turbine (the aeolipile) in the first century AD. Practical development came in the nineteenth century with the water turbines of Fourneyron, Francis and Pelton, followed by Charles Parsons's steam turbine of 1884 and the gas turbine work of the early twentieth century, culminating in Frank Whittle's and Hans von Ohain's jet engines of the 1930s and 1940s.
