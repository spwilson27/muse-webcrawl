Garifuna (Karifuna) is an Arawakan language spoken by the Garifuna people along the Caribbean coasts of Honduras, Guatemala, Belize and Nicaragua, and in diaspora communities in the United States. It descends principally from the Island Carib language of the Lesser Antilles, itself an Arawakan language with a notable Cariban (Kalinago) lexical influence and later contributions from French, English and Spanish.

History

The Garifuna emerged on the island of Saint Vincent from the union of West African survivors of wrecked slave ships with the indigenous Island Carib population. After defeat by the British in 1796, the Garifuna were exiled first to Baliceaux and then, in 1797, to the island of Roatán off Honduras, from where they spread along the Central American Caribbean coast. Their language travelled with them and remains a central marker of Garifuna identity. In 2001, UNESCO proclaimed the language, dance and music of the Garifuna a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

Features

Garifuna is unusual for its system of gender-based vocabulary: historically, men and women used partly different words for a number of everyday concepts, a distinction that traces back to the Carib men's and Arawak women's languages of Saint Vincent. Most of these differences have faded, but traces survive, for example in some kinship terms and in the verbs and numerals used in traditional speech. The language is agglutinative, with a subject–verb–object order and extensive use of prefixes and suffixes.

Status

Garifuna is taught in some schools in Belize, Honduras and Guatemala, and bilingual intercultural education programmes exist, but the language is considered vulnerable as younger speakers shift toward Spanish and English. Cultural celebrations such as Garifuna Settlement Day (19 November in Belize) promote the language alongside punta music and traditional food.
