Sternum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The sternum (commonly called the breastbone) is a long, flat bone located in the central part of the chest. It connects to the ribs via costal cartilage and forms the front of the rib cage, protecting the heart, lungs, and major blood vessels. Along with the ribs and thoracic vertebrae, it forms the thoracic cage of the axial skeleton.

Structure

The sternum consists of three parts. The manubrium is the broad upper portion, articulating with the clavicles and the first and part of the second costal cartilages; its upper border bears the jugular (suprasternal) notch. The body is the flat, elongated middle section, formed from fused segments (sternebrae), articulating with the costal cartilages of ribs 2–7. The junction of manubrium and body, the sternal angle (of Louis), is an important palpable landmark at the level of the second rib. The xiphoid process is the small, cartilaginous lower tip, which ossifies gradually with age and provides attachment for abdominal muscles.

Development

The sternum develops from paired cartilaginous bars that fuse in the midline. Failure of complete fusion can produce a sternal foramen — a hole in the body of the sternum present in a minority of people — or, in severe cases, a cleft sternum. The arrangement of ossification centres is also used in forensic anthropology as a rough indicator of age.

Clinical significance

The sternum is a common site for harvesting bone marrow for biopsy and transplantation. In cardiac surgery, a median sternotomy — splitting the sternum vertically — provides access to the heart and mediastinum. Sternal fractures can occur in blunt chest trauma and may signal underlying cardiac injury. During cardiopulmonary resuscitation, compressions are delivered to the lower half of the sternal body. Congenital deformities include pectus excavatum (sunken chest) and pectus carinatum (pigeon chest), which involve abnormal growth of the sternum and costal cartilages.
