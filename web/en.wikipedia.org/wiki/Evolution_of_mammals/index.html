Evolution of mammals

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The evolution of mammals has passed through many stages since the first appearance of their synapsid ancestors in the Pennsylvanian sub-period of the late Carboniferous period. By the mid-Triassic there were many synapsid species that looked like mammals. The lineage leading to today's mammals split in the Jurassic; synapsids from this period include Dryolestes, more closely related to extant placentals and marsupials than to monotremes, and Ambondro, more closely related to monotremes. Later on, the eutherian and metatherian lineages separated; the metatherians are the animals more closely related to the marsupials, while the eutherians are those more closely related to placentals.

From synapsids to mammals

Mammals belong to the Synapsida, one of the two great amniote lineages. Early synapsids — popularly but incorrectly called "mammal-like reptiles" — included the sail-backed pelycosaurs such as Dimetrodon, followed by the more mammal-like therapsids and, among them, the cynodonts, which by the Late Triassic had evolved many mammalian features: a differentiated dentition, an enlarged brain case, hair (inferred from later descendants) and probably lactation. The first true mammals (Mammaliaformes such as Morganucodon) appeared around 225 million years ago.

Mesozoic mammals

Throughout the "Age of Reptiles", most mammals were small, nocturnal insectivores — a pattern sometimes called the nocturnal bottleneck, which shaped keen senses of smell, hearing and touch. Nevertheless, Mesozoic mammals were more diverse than once thought, including gliders (Volaticotherium), swimmers (Castorocauda), burrowers and even the badger-sized Repenomamus, known to have eaten baby dinosaurs.

Cenozoic radiation

The Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs, and in the ensuing Cenozoic era mammals underwent an explosive adaptive radiation into the vacated ecological niches, producing the modern orders — primates, rodents, bats, carnivorans, even-toed and odd-toed ungulates, and the wholly aquatic cetaceans. Defining mammalian traits include hair, three middle-ear bones, a neocortex, and mammary glands that nourish the young with milk.
