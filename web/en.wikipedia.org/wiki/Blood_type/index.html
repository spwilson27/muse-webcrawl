Blood type

A blood type (blood group) is a classification of blood by the presence or absence of inherited antigens on red blood cells. The best known systems are ABO and Rh (Rhesus); 40+ group systems are recognised in total. Blood type decides transfusion compatibility — the wrong type can kill.

ABO and Rh

ABO antigens are sugars added to red-cell surfaces: type A has A antigens, B has B, AB both, O neither. Plasma carries the opposite antibodies (anti-A in type B people, and so on), the result of exposure to similar antigens in food and microbes. The Rh system hinges mainly on the D antigen: Rh-positive people have it. Type O-negative is the ‘universal donor’ for red cells; AB-positive the ‘universal recipient’.

Transfusion and pregnancy

Before transfusion, blood is typed and cross-matched. In pregnancy, an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive baby can become sensitised; later pregnancies risk haemolytic disease of the newborn, now prevented with anti-D immunoglobulin. Group frequencies vary by population — B is commoner in Asia, O in Indigenous American populations.
