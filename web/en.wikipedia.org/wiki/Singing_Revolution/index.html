Singing Revolution

The Singing Revolution is a phrase coined by the Estonian artist and activist Heinz Valk in 1988 to describe the mass demonstrations and non-violent resistance by which Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania — the Baltic states, annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940 — regained their independence in 1988–1991. The name reflects the centrality of song: for a century and a half, mass choral song festivals had been the principal vessel of Baltic national identity under foreign rule, and in the late 1980s hundreds of thousands gathered at song festival grounds to sing the banned national anthems and folk songs, ring ancient hill forts, form human chains and confront Soviet power with a discipline of non-violence that gave the movement its other emblematic moment — the Baltic Way of 23 August 1989, when some two million people joined hands across 600 km (370 mi) of the three republics. The revolutions culminated, after bloodshed in Vilnius and Riga in January 1991, in international recognition of restored Baltic independence in September 1991.

Background

The Baltic states, independent between the wars, were assigned to the Soviet sphere by the secret protocols of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact (1939), occupied in 1940, and annexed after stage-managed elections — an annexation the United States and most Western democracies never recognised in law. Deportations in 1941 and 1949, armed "Forest Brothers" resistance into the 1950s, and decades of Russification and immigration followed; by the 1980s ethnic Latvians were barely half the population of their own republic. The national song festivals — Estonia's Laulupidu dated from 1869 — survived under Soviet management as the one permitted mass expression of nationhood. Gorbachev's glasnost opened the space in which that expression could become politics.

The movement

The revolution began over the environment and memory together: Estonia's 1987 "Phosphorite War" against new mines and the Hirvepark demonstration in Tallinn on the anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact opened the public sphere. In 1988 Popular Fronts formed in all three republics; forbidden flags (the blue-black-white, the carmine-red-white-carmine, the yellow-green-red) reappeared; and the singing began in earnest — at the Tartu pop festival and Tallinn's song festival grounds in the summer of 1988, crowds of 100,000 and more sang through the nights ("Eestlane olen ja eestlaseks jään" — "I am Estonian and I shall remain Estonian"), prompting Valk's phrase "the Singing Revolution". In November 1988 Estonia's Supreme Soviet declared its laws supreme over Soviet ones — the first such sovereignty declaration in the USSR. Lithuania's Sąjūdis movement and the Latvian Popular Front moved in parallel; on 23 August 1989, the pact's fiftieth anniversary, the Baltic Way linked the three capitals in the longest human chain then recorded — the image that announced the movement to the world.

Independence

On 11 March 1990 Lithuania became the first Soviet republic to declare its independence restored, drawing a Soviet economic blockade; Estonia and Latvia followed with transition declarations that spring. In January 1991, as Moscow cracked down, Soviet troops stormed the Vilnius television tower, killing fourteen unarmed defenders, and OMON units killed seven in Riga — attacks that galvanised rather than broke resistance (civilians built barricades around the parliaments and sang on them), and brought international condemnation. Referenda in all three republics endorsed independence by overwhelming margins. The failure of the August 1991 coup in Moscow finished the matter: the Baltic states proclaimed full independence and were recognised in quick succession by country after country, by the Soviet State Council on 6 September 1991, and admitted to the United Nations on 17 September 1991 — their legal continuity with the pre-1940 republics, central to their claim throughout, accepted by the world.

Legacy

The Singing Revolution became a canonical case of successful non-violent resistance and a touchstone of Baltic identity: the song festivals (inscribed by UNESCO as a Masterpiece of the Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003) remain the republics' central national ritual, and the term has passed into general use for revolutions carried by mass cultural expression. The story is told in the 2006 documentary film The Singing Revolution.
