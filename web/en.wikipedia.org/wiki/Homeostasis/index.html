Homeostasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homeostasis is the tendency of a living system to maintain internal stability — a steady state of temperature, pH, water, salts and nutrients — despite external change. The concept descends from Claude Bernard's "milieu intérieur" (1865) and was named by Walter Bradford Cannon in 1926.

Mechanisms

Homeostasis works mainly through negative feedback: a sensor detects deviation from a set point, and effectors act to reverse it. Examples include thermoregulation (sweating and shivering around 37 °C), blood-glucose control by insulin and glucagon, blood-pressure regulation by baroreceptors (relevant to hypertension), and osmotic control of body water by the kidneys. Breathing rate adjusts blood CO₂ and pH minute by minute.

Failure of homeostasis is disease or death: fever is a raised set point; diabetes is failed glucose homeostasis; heat stroke is failed thermoregulation. The principle extends by analogy to ecosystems and to engineering control systems, and it underlies much of physiology and medicine.
