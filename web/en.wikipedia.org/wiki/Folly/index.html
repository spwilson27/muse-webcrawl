Folly

A folly is a building constructed primarily for decoration, but suggesting through its appearance some other purpose, or of such extravagant appearance that it transcends the range of other garden buildings generally associated with follies or landscape gardens.

Eighteenth-century English landscape gardening and French landscape gardening often featured mock Roman temples, symbolising classical virtues. Other 18th-century garden follies represented Chinese temples, Egyptian pyramids, ruined medieval castles or abbeys, or Tatar tents, to represent different continents or historical eras. Sometimes they represented rustic villages, mills, and cottages to symbolise rural virtues. Many follies, particularly those built since the 18th century, also have practical uses, such as providing shelter or acting as a hunting lodge.

Characteristics

The concept of the folly is somewhat ambiguous, but these characteristics are generally common: they have no purpose other than as an ornament; they are often eccentric in design or construction; and they typically draw the eye as part of a designed landscape. Follies are often named after the individual who commissioned or designed them. The word "folly" derives from the French folie, meaning foolishness or madness — a reflection of the perceived extravagance of spending money on structures of no practical use.

Famous examples

Notable follies include the Broadway Tower in Worcestershire, England; the sham castle at Hagley Hall; the Dunmore Pineapple in Scotland, shaped like the fruit; the Petit Trianon and the Hameau de la Reine at Versailles; and the many eye-catchers built on aristocratic estates across Britain, France, and Germany during the 18th and 19th centuries.
