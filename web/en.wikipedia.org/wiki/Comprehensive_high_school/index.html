Comprehensive high school

Comprehensive high schools are the most popular form of public high school in the United States, as compared to the comprehensive schools in other countries. They are general, large-scale schools that admit students of all abilities and provide a wide curriculum, including college-preparatory academic courses, general education, and vocational and technical subjects, within a single institution and usually a single local catchment.

History

The comprehensive high school model took shape in the first half of the 20th century as American secondary education expanded from a small, largely college-preparatory system into mass education. The 1918 Cardinal Principles of Secondary Education report recommended that high schools serve all youth with a differentiated curriculum. Harvard president James Bryant Conant championed the model in The American High School Today (1959), arguing that a single comprehensive school serving a whole community, with ability grouping within subjects and a broad programme of electives, best combined democratic equality with academic excellence. Conant recommended high schools have graduating classes of at least one hundred students to support this breadth of offerings.

Characteristics

Comprehensive high schools typically:

  • Enroll students without selective admissions tests, based on residence.
  • Offer multiple tracks or pathways — advanced/honours and Advanced Placement courses, standard college-preparatory courses, and career and technical education.
  • Provide a wide range of extracurricular activities, athletics, and support services under one roof.
  • Are large compared with earlier or alternative models, often serving many hundreds or several thousand students.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics have argued that comprehensive high schools can become impersonal "shopping malls" in which rigorous academic demands are diluted, a critique made in the 1980s reform literature. Alternatives and supplements within American public education include magnet schools, charter schools, small schools and small learning communities, early college high schools, and exam schools with selective admissions, though the comprehensive high school remains the dominant institutional form of American secondary education.
