Ars moriendi

Ars moriendi
MeaningLatin: "The Art of Dying"
Dateearly 15th century (c. 1415 and c. 1450 versions)
GenreDevotional manual; blockbook

The Ars moriendi ("The Art of Dying") are two related Latin texts, dating from about 1415 and 1450, that offer advice on the protocols and procedures of a good death in late medieval Catholic spirituality — how to die well, according to the Christian precepts of the time. They circulated widely: the shorter version, with eleven woodcut illustrations, became one of the first and most popular block books of the 15th century, printed in numerous editions across northern Europe.

Context

The texts arose in the aftermath of the Black Death, when sudden death without a priest was common and laypeople might have to guide the dying themselves. The long version (c. 1415), possibly connected with the Council of Constance circle, builds on earlier works by Jean Gerson. The short version (c. 1450, probably Netherlands) condenses the material into a picture-book format for use at the deathbed.

Contents

The works describe the deathbed struggle (agon) in which the dying person (the moriens) faces five temptations — loss of faith, despair, impatience, vainglory (spiritual pride) and avarice (attachment to family and goods) — each presented by demons and countered by angels offering the corresponding inspiration. The final woodcut shows the triumphant death: the soul, depicted as a small naked figure, received by angels while demons scatter in disarray. The texts also prescribe questions to ask the dying, prayers, and the conduct expected of attendants.

Influence

The Ars moriendi shaped European attitudes to death for two centuries, was translated into most western European vernaculars — William Caxton printed an English version in 1490 — and influenced later works such as Erasmus's De praeparatione ad mortem (1534) and the Anglican tradition of Jeremy Taylor's Holy Dying (1651). Its imagery is a major source for the late-medieval memento mori and Dance of Death traditions.
