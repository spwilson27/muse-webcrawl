History of Las Vegas

The history of Las Vegas, Nevada, spans from its days as a desert watering stop on trails west to its founding as a railroad town in 1905 and its transformation, after the legalisation of gambling in 1931, into the world's most famous centre of casino entertainment.

Early history

The Las Vegas Valley was home to Southern Paiute people, and its artesian springs made it a stop on the Old Spanish Trail. Rafael Rivera is credited as the first non-Native visitor, in 1829; he named the area las vegas ("the meadows"). Mormon missionaries built a fort in 1855 but abandoned it within two years. The valley became part of the United States with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo in 1848.

Founding and growth

In 1905 the San Pedro, Los Angeles and Salt Lake Railroad auctioned off lots at the site, and Las Vegas was incorporated as a city in 1911. Construction of the nearby Hoover Dam (1931–1936) brought thousands of workers and federal spending during the Great Depression. Nevada's legalisation of gambling in 1931, and its liberal divorce laws, laid the foundations of the resort economy.

The casino era

The first resort on the future Las Vegas Strip, El Rancho Vegas, opened in 1941; Bugsy Siegel's Flamingo followed in 1946. Organised-crime investment financed much of the Strip's early growth, while headliners such as Frank Sinatra and the Rat Pack made Las Vegas the "Entertainment Capital of the World". Howard Hughes's purchases in the late 1960s began the shift to corporate ownership, cemented when Nevada allowed corporate casino licensing in 1969. The nearby Nevada Test Site made atomic-bomb viewing a tourist attraction in the 1950s.

Megaresorts and modern Las Vegas

Steve Wynn's Mirage (1989) inaugurated the megaresort era, followed by themed giants such as Bellagio, The Venetian and Caesars properties, while downtown Fremont Street was reinvented with the Fremont Street Experience. After setbacks in the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, the metropolitan area grew to more than two million residents and added major-league sports, including the NFL's Raiders and the NHL's Golden Knights. Concerns over the water supply from Lake Mead remain the region's central long-term challenge.
