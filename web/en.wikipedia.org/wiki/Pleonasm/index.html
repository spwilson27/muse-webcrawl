Pleonasm

Pleonasm (from Ancient Greek pleonasmós, "excess") is the use of more words or word-parts than is necessary for clear expression: examples are "black darkness", "burning fire", and "free gift". Such redundancy may be used for emphasis, stylistic effect, or clarity, but pleonastic expressions are also commonly regarded as errors or as marks of careless style. The opposite of pleonasm is the use of fewer words than necessary, as in ellipsis.

Usage

Many pleonasms are idiomatic and firmly established in the language: "safe and sound", "null and void", "each and every", "tuna fish" (in American usage), and "PIN number" — a well-known example of an acronym followed by a word already contained in it. Others arise from grammatical forms whose meaning overlaps, such as "I saw it with my own eyes" or "repeat again". In many cases the redundant word adds emphasis, rhythm, or an archaic or formal tone, and removing it changes the register though not the literal meaning.

Pleonasm and tautology

Pleonasm is closely related to tautology, in which the same idea is stated twice in different words. Style guides often advise writers to remove pleonastic words where they contribute nothing, but linguists note that redundancy in natural language also serves practical purposes: it can guard against misunderstanding in noisy communication, mark emphasis, and satisfy conventions of politeness or formulaic speech (such as in legal doublets like "cease and desist").

Examples

  • "advance planning" — planning is necessarily in advance
  • "past history" — history is necessarily past
  • "join together" — joining necessarily brings things together
  • "unexpected surprise" — a surprise is by definition unexpected
  • "true fact" — a fact is by definition true
