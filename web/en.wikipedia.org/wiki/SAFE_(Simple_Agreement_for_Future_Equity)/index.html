A Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE) is a financing contract used by startup companies to raise seed capital. Introduced by the accelerator Y Combinator in 2013, a SAFE gives an investor the right to receive equity in the company at a future priced funding round (or liquidity event), in exchange for capital provided immediately. Unlike a convertible note, a SAFE is not a debt instrument: it carries no interest rate and no maturity date.

Mechanics

When a priced equity round later occurs, the SAFE converts into shares, typically at a price determined by one or both of two investor-favourable terms: a valuation cap, which sets a maximum company valuation for the conversion price, and a discount rate on the price paid by the new investors. Some SAFEs include a "most favoured nation" clause entitling the holder to adopt better terms offered to later SAFE investors. In a sale or dissolution of the company before conversion, the investor generally may recover their purchase amount or convert into shares at the cap.

Post-money SAFE

In 2018 Y Combinator revised the instrument into the "post-money" SAFE, in which the valuation cap is calculated on a post-money basis — that is, including all converting SAFEs themselves. This makes the percentage ownership each SAFE will represent determinable at signing, a transparency benefit for founders, though it also means founders bear the dilution from all SAFEs in the round.

Criticism

SAFEs are praised for being short, standardised, and cheap to execute compared with negotiated seed rounds. Critics note that investors hold no shares, voting rights, or creditor protections until conversion, that stacked SAFEs can obscure a startup's capitalisation table, and that the absence of a maturity date removes a deadline that might otherwise force a reckoning for struggling companies.
