The Book of Lamentations (Hebrew: אֵיכָה, Eikhah, from its opening word meaning "How") is a collection of poetic laments over the destruction of Jerusalem and the First Temple by the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar II in 586 BCE. In the Hebrew Bible it belongs to the Ketuvim ("Writings") and is one of the Five Megillot (scrolls); in Christian Bibles it is placed after the Book of Jeremiah, in keeping with the long-standing tradition that the prophet Jeremiah composed it.

The book consists of five poems. The first four are acrostics: each verse begins with a successive letter of the 22-letter Hebrew alphabet, with chapter 3 tripling the pattern (66 verses). The poems personify Jerusalem as a widowed, bereaved woman ("Daughter Zion"), describe the horrors of siege and famine, confess the people's sins, and yet voice a stubborn hope grounded in God's steadfast love: "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning" (3:22–23).

Authorship and date

Ancient tradition, reflected in the Septuagint's preface and in later Jewish and Christian sources, attributes Lamentations to Jeremiah, who witnessed Jerusalem's fall. The vivid, eyewitness quality of the descriptions supports a date close to the events, in the late 6th century BCE. Modern scholars debate whether the poems share a single author; differences in style and perspective suggest they may have been composed by different poets within the exilic community and later gathered into one scroll.

Contents

  • Chapter 1 — Jerusalem sits solitary, "like a widow"; her suffering is ascribed to her transgressions, and she pleads for God to see her affliction.
  • Chapter 2 — God's wrath is the agent of destruction: he has "bent his bow like an enemy" and poured out fury on Daughter Zion.
  • Chapter 3 — An individual sufferer ("I am the man who has seen affliction") moves from despair to the book's great confession of hope in God's mercies.
  • Chapter 4 — A contrast between Zion's former glory and her present degradation, including harrowing scenes of famine during the siege.
  • Chapter 5 — A communal prayer (not an acrostic) describing the hardships of the survivors and ending with the plea, "Restore us to yourself, O LORD" (5:21).

Themes

Lamentations grapples with theodicy: the tension between God's justice (Jerusalem's sins deserved punishment) and God's compassion. It gives liturgical form to grief rather than explaining it away, and its insistence on addressing God even in accusation has made it a model for communal lament in times of catastrophe.

Liturgical use

In Judaism, Lamentations is read aloud on Tisha B'Av, the fast day commemorating the destruction of both Temples, traditionally chanted in a mournful cantillation. In Christian tradition, passages are read during Holy Week, notably in the Tenebrae services of the Triduum, where the verses were also applied to the suffering of Christ; Renaissance composers such as Tallis, Byrd, and Palestrina set portions of the text.
