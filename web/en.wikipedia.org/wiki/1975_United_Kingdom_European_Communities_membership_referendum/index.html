The 1975 United Kingdom European Communities membership referendum, held on June 5, 1975, was the first nationwide referendum in British history. Voters were asked whether the United Kingdom should remain in the European Community (the "Common Market"), which it had joined on January 1, 1973 under Edward Heath's Conservative government. The result was a decisive Yes: 67.2 percent voted to stay in, and every county and region of the UK returned a Yes majority except Shetland and the Western Isles.

Background

Harold Wilson's Labour Party won the October 1974 general election promising to renegotiate the terms of British membership and then put them to the people. The renegotiation, concluded at the Dublin European Council in March 1975, produced modest changes on budget contributions, Commonwealth trade, and regional policy. Wilson then called the referendum, suspending collective cabinet responsibility so that ministers could campaign on either side—an unprecedented "agreement to differ."

Campaign

The Yes campaign, "Britain in Europe," was backed by most of the press, big business, and senior figures of both main parties, including Wilson, Heath, and Jeremy Thorpe. The No side, "National Referendum Campaign," united the Labour left (Tony Benn, Michael Foot), the Conservative right (Enoch Powell), and Ulster Unionists. Yes outspent No by a wide margin.

Result and legacy

Turnout was 64 percent. The two-to-one verdict settled Britain's membership for a generation and was invoked in later European debates until the 2016 referendum produced the opposite outcome. The 1975 vote established the precedent for UK-wide referendums on constitutional questions.
