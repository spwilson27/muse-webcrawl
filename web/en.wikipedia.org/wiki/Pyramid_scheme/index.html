Pyramid scheme

A pyramid scheme is a business model that recruits members via a promise of payments or services for enrolling others into the scheme, rather than supplying investments or the sale of products to the public. As recruitment multiplies, recruiting becomes quickly impossible, and most members are unable to profit; pyramid schemes are unsustainable and often illegal.

How it works

In the classic "eight-ball" or aeroplane gifting model, participants pay to join at a lower level and are promised a large payout once they reach the top, funded by later entrants. Because the number of new recruits required grows geometrically — 2, 4, 8, 16… — the pool of potential recruits in any population is exhausted after relatively few levels, leaving the great majority of participants with losses. Mathematically, such schemes cannot be sustained regardless of the goodwill of participants.

Related schemes and law

Pyramid schemes differ from Ponzi schemes, in which a central operator fabricates investment returns, and from legitimate multi-level marketing, which sells actual products to consumers — though the boundary is contested and regulators, including the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, test whether rewards depend primarily on recruitment rather than retail sales. Pyramid schemes are prohibited in many countries. Notorious examples include the collapse of MMM in Russia and the pyramid that devastated Albania's economy in 1996–97, contributing to civil unrest.
