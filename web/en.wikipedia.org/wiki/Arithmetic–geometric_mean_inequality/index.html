Arithmetic–geometric mean inequality

Visual proof of the AM–GM inequality for two numbers

FieldMathematics (inequalities)
Also known asAM–GM inequality

In mathematics, the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, or more briefly the AM–GM inequality, states that the arithmetic mean of a list of non-negative real numbers is greater than or equal to the geometric mean of the same list; and further, that the two means are equal if and only if every number in the list is the same (in which case they are both equal to that number).

The simplest non-trivial case — i.e., with more than one variable — for two non-negative numbers x and y, is the statement that (x + y)/2 ≥ √(xy), with equality precisely when x = y. This case can be seen from the fact that the square of a real number is always non-negative, applied to (√x − √y)2 ≥ 0.

Generalizations

The AM–GM inequality generalizes to weighted means, in which the numbers are combined with weights, and to the inequality between the arithmetic mean and other power means. It can also be generalized to integral form via Jensen's inequality applied to the logarithm function. Related results include the inequality between the geometric and harmonic means, forming the chain HM ≤ GM ≤ AM.

Proofs

Many proofs exist. Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave a proof by "forward-backward induction": proving the result for 2, then for all powers of 2 by repeated doubling, then filling in the remaining cases by induction downward. Other proofs proceed by induction, by rearrangement, or through the exponential function inequality.

Applications

The inequality is a workhorse of olympiad mathematics and analysis, used to establish bounds, optimize quantities under constraints, and prove convergence results. In economics and information theory, related mean inequalities underpin results on growth rates and entropy.
