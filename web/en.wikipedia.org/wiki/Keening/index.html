Keening

Keening (Irish: caoineadh) is a traditional form of vocal lament for the dead, practised historically in Ireland and the Scottish Highlands and paralleled in funeral customs around the world, from Mediterranean moirologia to Finnish-Karelian laments. The keen combines improvised verse praising the deceased, recounting their genealogy and deeds, and expressing grief and reproach, delivered in a wailing melodic style often over the corpse at the wake and during the funeral procession.

The practice

Keening was led by a bean chaointe ("keening woman"), sometimes a professional paid in kind, who might begin with a low murmur over the body, rising to passionate extempore verse interspersed with the communal cry — the ochón refrain. The lament typically addressed the dead directly, asked why they had left, praised their generosity, and sometimes cursed those blamed for the death. The famous 18th-century Caoineadh Airt Uí Laoghaire (Lament for Art O'Leary), composed by Eibhlín Dubh Ní Chonaill for her murdered husband, is considered a masterpiece of Irish oral literature.

Decline

The Catholic Church long disapproved of keening as pagan and disorderly, and synods repeatedly tried to suppress it. The practice faded through the 19th and 20th centuries with anglicisation, emigration and changing funeral customs; the last traditional keeners died in the mid-20th century, though recorded examples survive in sound archives and the tradition is studied and occasionally revived by singers today.
