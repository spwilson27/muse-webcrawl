National Schism

Period: 1915–1917 (effects to 1935)
Parties: Eleftherios Venizelos vs King Constantine I
Issue: Greek entry into the First World War

The National Schism (Greek: Εθνικός Διχασμός, Ethnikos Dichasmos) was the deep division of Greek society and politics between 1915 and 1917, centred on the rivalry between Prime Minister Eleftherios Venizelos and King Constantine I over whether Greece should enter the First World War on the side of the Entente.

Background

Venizelos, the Cretan statesman who had become prime minister in 1910, favoured joining Britain and France, expecting Greece to gain territory in Asia Minor and Thrace. Constantine, married to the Kaiser's sister Sophia and educated in Germany, insisted on armed neutrality, which in practice favoured the Central Powers.

The crisis

The dispute came to a head in 1915. After Venizelos twice resigned under royal pressure, the Entente landed troops at Salonika (Thessaloniki) in October 1915 at Venizelos's invitation. In August 1916 Venizelist officers launched a revolt in Thessaloniki and formed a rival Provisional Government of National Defence, recognised by the Entente. Greece effectively had two governments: the royalist government in Athens and the Venizelist government in Thessaloniki controlling Macedonia, Crete and the Aegean islands.

Fighting broke out in Athens in November–December 1916 (the Noemvriana) between Entente forces and royalist troops and reservist bands, who also persecuted Venizelists. In June 1917, under an Entente ultimatum, Constantine left Greece (without formally abdicating), his second son Alexander became king, and Venizelos returned as prime minister of a reunified Greece, which formally entered the war on the Entente side.

Consequences

The Schism poisoned Greek politics for decades. It fuelled the cycle of coups, the Asia Minor Catastrophe of 1922, the trial and execution of the Six, republican–monarchist conflict, and arguably divisions that resurfaced in the Greek Civil War of the 1940s.
