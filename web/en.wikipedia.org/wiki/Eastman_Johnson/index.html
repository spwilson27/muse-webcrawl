Eastman Johnson

Jonathan Eastman Johnson (July 29, 1824 – April 5, 1906) was an American painter and co-founder of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City. He achieved fame for his genre paintings — detailed scenes of everyday American life — and later for portraits of prominent figures including Abraham Lincoln, Nathaniel Hawthorne and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. He has been called the "American Rembrandt" for his mastery of chiaroscuro learned during his years in the Netherlands.

Early life and training

Johnson was born in Lovell, Maine, in 1824 and grew up in Maine and Washington, D.C., where his father was a government official. He began his career drawing crayon portraits, then studied lithography in Boston. In 1849 he travelled to Düsseldorf, Germany, a leading centre of academic painting, and then to The Hague, where he studied Dutch masters for several years before returning to the United States in 1855.

Career

Johnson first won wide attention with Negro Life at the South (1859), depicting enslaved people in Washington, D.C. His depictions of rural New England life, such as The Old Stagecoach (1871) and his maple-sugaring and cranberry-harvest scenes, made him one of the most popular American genre painters. In later years he turned increasingly to portraiture. He was elected to the National Academy of Design and helped found the Metropolitan Museum of Art in 1870.

Legacy

Johnson died in New York in 1906. His work is held by major American museums, and he is regarded as a central figure in 19th-century American art for his sympathetic, closely observed images of American life.
