Giant's Causeway

LocationCounty Antrim, Northern Ireland
GeologyInterlocking basalt columns
Agec. 60 million years (Paleocene volcanism)
DesignationUNESCO World Heritage Site (1986)

The Giant's Causeway (Irish: Clochán an Aifir or Clochán na bhFomhórach) is an area of about 40,000 interlocking basalt columns on the north coast of County Antrim in Northern Ireland, the result of an ancient volcanic fissure eruption. It was declared a World Heritage Site by UNESCO in 1986 and is Northern Ireland's most popular tourist attraction.

Geology

During the Paleocene, intense volcanic activity associated with the opening of the North Atlantic forced molten basalt through chalk beds. As the thick lava flow cooled and contracted, it fractured into regular — mostly hexagonal — pillars, much as drying mud cracks. Similar formations occur at Fingal's Cave on Staffa in Scotland, on the same lava flow.

Legend

In Irish mythology the columns are the remains of a causeway built by the giant Fionn mac Cumhaill (Finn MacCool) to cross to Scotland and fight his rival Benandonner. In one version Fionn fled home on seeing Benandonner's size, and his wife disguised him as a baby; Benandonner, reasoning that the father of so huge an infant must be a giant among giants, fled back to Scotland, tearing up the causeway behind him.

Tourism

Owned largely by the National Trust, the site includes named formations such as the Organ, the Chimney Stacks and the Giant's Boot, and a visitor centre opened in 2012.
