Coup d'état

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A coup d'état (French for "stroke of state"), usually shortened to coup, is the illegal and overt seizure of a state by a small group — typically elements of the military, security services or political elite — displacing the incumbent government outside constitutional procedures. Coups differ from revolutions (mass movements transforming the social order) and from foreign invasions, though they may be externally supported and may trigger civil war.

History and patterns

Coups are as old as states (praetorian interventions in Rome) but became a mass phenomenon after 1945, especially in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and Southeast Asia, where praetorian militaries claimed guardianship roles. Cold War superpowers sponsored or blessed many. Global coup frequency declined after the Cold War under regional anti-coup norms (the African Union suspends coup regimes) and democratic conditionality, before a resurgence in the early 2020s in the Sahel and elsewhere (Mali, Guinea, Burkina Faso, Niger, Gabon, Sudan, Myanmar). Research (Powell and Thyne's dataset) links success to coordination signals that the old regime cannot resist, control of the capital and communications, and speed; failed coups often precede purges and repression.

Varieties and law

Variants include the self-coup (autogolpe), where an incumbent dissolves other institutions (Fujimori's Peru, 1992); palace coups within ruling parties; and "constitutional" or "soft" coups alleged where legal procedures are weaponised — a contested extension of the concept. International law treats coup governments through recognition practice rather than prohibition, though regional treaties increasingly mandate non-recognition and sanctions.
