Breakdown voltage

[Image placeholder: electrical breakdown arc]

The breakdown voltage of an electrical insulator is the minimum voltage that causes a portion of the insulator to experience electrical breakdown and become electrically conductive. For diodes, the breakdown voltage is the minimum reverse voltage that causes the diode to conduct appreciably in reverse. Some devices, such as TRIACs, also have a forward breakdown voltage.

Electrical breakdown

Materials classified as insulators are made of atoms with tightly bound electrons. When a sufficiently strong electric field is applied, electrons can be stripped from atoms or accelerated enough to ionize other atoms by collision, producing an avalanche of charge carriers. The material then changes abruptly from an insulator to a conductor, often accompanied by an arc or spark. This process is called electrical breakdown.

Gases

Under standard conditions at atmospheric pressure, air requires about 3 million volts per metre to break down, according to Paschen's law, which relates breakdown voltage to the product of gas pressure and electrode distance. The minimum breakdown voltage for air is about 327 volts, reached at a pressure-distance product of roughly 0.567 Torr·cm.

Solids and liquids

In solid insulators, breakdown can permanently damage the material, melting or puncturing a channel through it. Insulating liquids such as transformer oil are used both for insulation and cooling, and may recover their insulating properties after a discharge if not contaminated. Breakdown strength is usually expressed in kilovolts per millimetre.

Semiconductor devices

In diodes, reverse breakdown occurs by two mechanisms: Zener breakdown, dominant at low voltages (below about 5–6 V), and avalanche breakdown, dominant at higher voltages. Zener diodes are deliberately operated in breakdown to provide a stable reference voltage. Exceeding the rated breakdown voltage of an ordinary diode or transistor can destroy the device through excessive current and heating.
