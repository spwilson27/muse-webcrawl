The Caroline Islands are a widely scattered archipelago of small islands in the western Pacific Ocean, north of New Guinea, extending roughly 3,500 kilometres from Palau in the west to Kosrae in the east. Politically they are divided between the Federated States of Micronesia (the central and eastern islands) and the Republic of Palau (the western group). Major island groups include Palau, Yap, Chuuk (Truk) Lagoon, Pohnpei, and Kosrae.

History

The islands were settled by Austronesian-speaking peoples more than two thousand years ago; the remarkable stone city of Nan Madal on Pohnpei was built by the Saudeleur dynasty from about the 12th century. Spanish explorers reached the islands in the 16th century, and Spain formally claimed them, naming them for King Charles II (Latin: Carolus). Effective Spanish control was slight, however.

In 1899, after the Spanish–American War, Spain sold the Carolines to Germany. Japan seized them in 1914 and administered them under a League of Nations mandate. During the Second World War the great Japanese naval base at Truk Lagoon was devastated by the American Operation Hailstone (February 1944). After the war the islands became part of the U.S.-administered Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands until the Federated States of Micronesia and Palau became independent in free association with the United States (1986 and 1994 respectively).

Geography and culture

The archipelago mixes high volcanic islands with low coral atolls. The indigenous Caroline Islanders are master open-ocean navigators, traditionally voyaging by outrigger canoe using star paths and swell patterns without instruments. Languages belong to the Micronesian branch of the Oceanic family.
