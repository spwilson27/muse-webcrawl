A pony is a type of small horse (Equus ferus caballus), typically characterised as having a specific conformation and temperament. Depending on the context, a pony may be a horse that is under an approximate or exact height at the withers, or a small horse with a particular build: thicker mane, tail and overall coat, proportionally shorter legs, wider barrel, heavier bone, thicker neck and shorter head.

Definition

For many purposes, an adult horse is called a pony if it stands 14.2 hands (58 inches, 147 cm) or less at the withers. The International Federation for Equestrian Sports (FEI) defines the cutoff at 148 cm without shoes and permits up to 150 cm in competition. However, some breeds, such as the Icelandic horse and the Fjord horse, are always called horses regardless of size, while certain miniature breeds are classified separately.

History

Ponies developed as a landrace adapted to harsh, marginal environments where fodder was scarce. Many pony breeds originated in northern and western Europe, in regions such as the British Isles, Scandinavia and Brittany. Historically, ponies were used for riding, driving, pack work and pulling carts in mines and on farms.

Uses

Today ponies are widely used as children's mounts, in driving and showing, and in therapeutic riding programmes. Many pony breeds, including the Welsh pony, Connemara, Shetland and Exmoor, are popular worldwide.

Characteristics

Compared with horses, ponies are often hardier, more economical to keep, and longer-lived; many live beyond 30 years. They can also have a reputation for stubbornness and intelligence.
