Ghost imaging

Schematic of a ghost imaging experiment with correlated photon pairs

Ghost imaging (also called two-photon imaging or correlated-photon imaging) is an optical imaging technique in which an image of an object is reconstructed from light that has never directly interacted with a spatially resolving detector viewing the object. Instead, photons from an entangled or classically correlated source are split into two arms: one beam passes through the object and is collected by a single-pixel "bucket" detector with no spatial resolution, while the other beam travels to a spatially resolving detector (often a camera) that never sees the object. Correlating the two measurement records reconstructs the image.

History

The first ghost imaging experiments were performed in the mid-1990s using entangled photon pairs produced by spontaneous parametric down-conversion, notably by the group of Yanhua Shih at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. The phenomenon was initially interpreted as a purely quantum effect arising from photon entanglement, but it was later shown that ghost imaging can also be achieved with classically correlated (thermal or pseudo-thermal) light, sparking debate about which aspects of the technique are genuinely quantum.

Computational ghost imaging

In computational ghost imaging, the reference arm is replaced by a computer: a spatial light modulator projects a sequence of known patterns onto the object, and only the bucket detector signal is measured. The image is computed from the correlation between the known patterns and the measured intensities. With a single-pixel detector this is closely related to the single-pixel camera and to compressive sensing, which allows the image to be reconstructed from fewer measurements than the number of pixels.

Applications

Ghost imaging is of interest for imaging at wavelengths where good array detectors are unavailable or expensive, for imaging through scattering media, for low-light-level imaging of light-sensitive samples, and for remote sensing. Variants include ghost diffraction, ghost imaging with X-rays, and temporal ghost imaging of fast signals.
