Binocular vision

Binocular vision is vision in which an animal has two eyes positioned so that their visual fields overlap, allowing the brain to combine the two slightly different retinal images into a single three-dimensional percept. Because the eyes are separated horizontally (by about 6–7 cm in humans), each sees the world from a slightly different viewpoint; the resulting difference, called binocular disparity, is the basis of stereopsis, the vivid perception of depth.

Advantages

Binocular vision provides several benefits: stereoscopic depth judgement useful for grasping, catching, and navigating; a wider total visual field than one eye alone (in humans about 200 degrees horizontally, with roughly 120 degrees of overlap); binocular summation, improving contrast sensitivity and acuity; and redundancy — partial compensation if one eye is damaged. Forward-facing eyes and binocular overlap are characteristic of predators and of primates, whose arboreal life and hand–eye coordination demand precise depth perception, whereas prey animals tend to have laterally placed eyes maximising panoramic surveillance.

Mechanism and disorders

For single binocular vision, the eyes must be aligned so images fall on corresponding retinal points, within a tolerance region called Panum's fusional area. Specialised neurons in the visual cortex respond to specific disparities. Misalignment of the eyes (strabismus) can cause double vision (diplopia) or, in children, suppression of one eye's image leading to amblyopia ("lazy eye"). Other binocular functions include vergence eye movements and the integration of disparity with monocular depth cues such as perspective and motion parallax. Binocular rivalry — alternating perception when the two eyes receive incompatible images — is a major tool in the neuroscience of consciousness.
