Compactata

The Compactata (Czech: Kompaktáta) were an agreement concluded in 1433 between the Council of Basel and the moderate Hussites (Utraquists) of Bohemia, and formally proclaimed at Jihlava in 1436. The agreement granted the Bohemians a set of concessions — most notably communion in both kinds (sub utraque specie) — in return for their acknowledgement of the Council and of Sigismund as King of Bohemia. The name derives from the Latin compacta, "agreements".

Background

The Hussite Wars (1419–1434) had resisted repeated crusades launched against Bohemia after the execution of Jan Hus at the Council of Constance in 1415. The Four Articles of Prague (1420) demanded free preaching, communion in both kinds for the laity, the reduction of clerical wealth and political power, and public punishment of mortal sins. By the early 1430s both sides sought a negotiated settlement: the Council of Basel, opened in 1431, invited Hussite representatives to negotiate, an unprecedented step for a movement condemned as heretical.

Negotiations

A Bohemian delegation, including the priest John of Rokycany and the English Hussite Peter Payne, debated with conciliar legates led by Cardinal Giuliano Cesarini in Basel in early 1433. Lengthy theological disputation gradually narrowed the differences. On 30 November 1433 the two parties agreed a text — the Compactata of Basel — granting communion in both kinds to those of age who desired it, provided the practice was explained as not necessary to salvation by divine command in the form the radical Hussites claimed.

The agreement was solemnly proclaimed at the Diet of Jihlava on 5 July 1436, where Sigismund was recognised as king. The radical Taborites, who rejected the compromise, had already been defeated at the Battle of Lipany in 1434 by a coalition of Utraquists and Catholics.

Contents

The Compactata permitted:

  • Communion in both kinds for lay people who had reached the age of discretion;
  • Free preaching of the word of God by approved preachers;
  • Clergy were not to exercise secular dominion over property (interpreted restrictively);
  • Public sins were to be punished by those in authority — again in a qualified form.

Each article was accepted only in a modified, carefully hedged form, quite different from the original Four Articles of Prague.

Aftermath

Pope Eugenius IV and his successors never fully accepted the Compactata, and Pope Pius II formally annulled them in 1462, declaring the concessions revoked. Nevertheless the agreement remained the legal basis of the Utraquist church in Bohemia for generations, and communion in both kinds remained a defining feature of Bohemian religious life until the re-Catholicisation after the Battle of White Mountain in 1620. Historians regard the Compactata as a landmark: the first occasion on which the Latin Church formally negotiated a settlement with a movement it had condemned as heretical.
