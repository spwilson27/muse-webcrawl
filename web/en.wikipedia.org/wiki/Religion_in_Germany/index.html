Religion in Germany

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Cologne Cathedral

Christianity is the largest religion in Germany. It was introduced to the area of modern Germany by 300 AD, while parts of that area belonged to the Roman Empire, and later by Frankish rule in the north. In the early 16th century, Germany was the starting point of the Protestant Reformation.

Before World War II, about two-thirds of the German population was Protestant and one-third was Roman Catholic. Today, the two main Christian churches — the Roman Catholic Church and the Evangelical Church in Germany (EKD) — each account for roughly a quarter of the population, while a large and growing share of Germans, approaching half, are religiously unaffiliated.

Demographics

According to recent estimates, around 45–50% of the population identify as Christian, divided roughly evenly between Catholics and Protestants. Muslims form the second-largest religious group at roughly 5–6% of the population, most of Turkish origin. Smaller communities include Orthodox Christians, other Christians, Buddhists, Hindus, Jews (about 0.2%), and members of other religions. The proportion of people with no religion is particularly high in the eastern states, a legacy of state atheism in the former East Germany.

History

The Germanic peoples were converted to Christianity from the 4th century onward. In 1517, Martin Luther published his Ninety-five Theses in Wittenberg, sparking the Reformation; the Peace of Augsburg (1555) and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) established the principle that rulers could determine the religion of their territories. The 19th century saw the Kulturkampf between the Prussian state and the Catholic Church. Under Nazi rule, the churches' relationship with the regime ranged from collaboration to resistance, including the Confessing Church movement. After 1949, the Federal Republic developed a cooperative model of church–state relations, while the German Democratic Republic promoted atheism.

Legal status

The Basic Law guarantees freedom of religion. Major religious bodies may obtain the status of a corporation under public law, which entitles them to have the state collect a church tax from their members. Germany has no state church.
