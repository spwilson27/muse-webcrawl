Polyethylene glycol

Chemical structure of polyethylene glycol.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG; /ˌpɒliˈɛθəlˌiːn ˈɡlaɪˌkɒl/) is a polyether compound derived from petroleum with many applications, from industrial manufacturing to medicine. PEG is also known as polyethylene oxide (PEO) or polyoxyethylene (POE), depending on its molecular weight. The structure of PEG is commonly expressed as H−(O−CH2−CH2)n−OH.

Grades

PEGs are prepared by polymerisation of ethylene oxide and are commercially available over a wide range of molecular weights, designated by a number (e.g. PEG 400, PEG 3350, PEG 8000). Lower-molecular-weight PEGs are viscous liquids; higher ones are waxy solids or flakes.

Medical uses

PEG is used as an excipient in many pharmaceutical products, as a solvent, and in bowel preparations for surgery or colonoscopy. Macrogol (PEG 3350) is a widely used osmotic laxative for the treatment of constipation. Covalent attachment of PEG to protein drugs — PEGylation — slows their clearance from the body; PEG is also used in the lipid nanoparticle formulations of mRNA vaccines.

Other applications

  • Surfactants, lubricants and humectants in cosmetics and toothpaste.
  • Wood preservation: PEG bulks waterlogged archaeological wood, notably in the conservation of the warship Vasa.
  • A stationary phase and calibration standard in chromatography; a common mass spectrometry calibrant.

Safety

PEGs are generally regarded as biologically inert and safe, though rare hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, have been documented.
