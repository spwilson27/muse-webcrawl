Japanese dragons (ryū or tatsu) are legendary creatures in Japanese mythology and folklore, most often depicted as large, serpentine, wingless beings associated with water — the sea, rivers, rain, and storms. Unlike the malevolent dragons of Western tradition, Japanese dragons are generally benevolent deities, though they can be dangerous when angered.

Origins and appearance

Japanese dragon lore blended the Chinese dragon (long) with indigenous water-serpent myths and Buddhist traditions from India, where nāga kings guarded scriptures and treasures. Japanese dragons are conventionally drawn with three claws (Chinese imperial dragons have five), a camel-like head, horns, whiskers, and a mane, sometimes carrying a wish-granting jewel.

Myths

Central tales include Ryūjin, the Dragon King ruling an undersea palace (Ryūgū-jō), who lent his tide-jewels to legendary heroes and received the fisherman Urashima Tarō as a guest; the eight-headed, eight-tailed serpent Yamata no Orochi, slain by the storm god Susanoo, from whose tail came the sword Kusanagi; and Watatsumi, the sea god of the earliest chronicles. Dragon kings governing rain were enshrined at Shinto shrines and Buddhist temples where prayers for rain were offered.

In culture

Dragons remain prominent in Japanese art (from Hokusai's prints to temple carvings), festivals, tattoos, and modern popular culture, from anime to the Pokémon character Gyarados.
