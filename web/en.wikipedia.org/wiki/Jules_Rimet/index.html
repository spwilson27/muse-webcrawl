Jules Rimet (14 October 1873 – 16 October 1956) was a French football administrator who served as the third president of FIFA from 1921 to 1954 — still the longest tenure in the organization's history — and who founded the FIFA World Cup. The original World Cup trophy was named the Jules Rimet Trophy in his honour.

Career

Rimet, a Paris lawyer and devout Catholic inspired by the social teachings of his church, co-founded the Red Star football club in 1897 and helped organize French football, becoming the first president of the French Football Federation in 1919. Elected FIFA president in 1921, he worked through the 1920s to create a global championship for national teams, overcoming the opposition of the Olympic movement, which controlled international football at the time.

The World Cup

Rimet's efforts bore fruit at the 1928 FIFA Congress in Amsterdam, which approved a world championship; the first tournament took place in Uruguay in 1930, with Rimet personally carrying the trophy — sculpted by Abel Lafleur — to Montevideo aboard the ship Conte Verde. He presented the cup to the winning Uruguayan captain. He remained president through the tournaments of 1934, 1938 and, after the Second World War, 1950 and 1954, seeing the World Cup established as the world's largest single-sport event.

Legacy

The Jules Rimet Trophy was awarded permanently to Brazil after its third victory in 1970; it was stolen in Rio de Janeiro in 1983 and never recovered. Rimet is buried at Bagneux near Paris; a stadium and numerous streets in France bear his name.
