Angiography

Angiography or arteriography is a medical imaging technique used to visualize the inside, or lumen, of blood vessels and organs of the body, with particular interest in the arteries, veins, and the heart chambers. This is traditionally done by injecting a radio-opaque contrast agent into the blood vessel and imaging using X-ray–based techniques such as fluoroscopy.

History

The Portuguese physician and neurologist Egas Moniz developed cerebral angiography in 1927, injecting contrast into the carotid artery to visualize brain tumors. In 1929 Werner Forssmann performed the first cardiac catheterization on himself, and in 1953 Sven-Ivar Seldinger introduced the Seldinger technique of percutaneous vessel access, which made selective angiography much safer. Mason Sones accidentally pioneered selective coronary angiography in 1958, and Melvin Judkins and Andreas Grüntzig later advanced coronary catheterization and angioplasty.

Techniques

In conventional catheter angiography, a thin catheter is advanced through an artery (usually the femoral or radial artery) to the vessel of interest, contrast is injected, and rapid sequences of X-ray images (digital subtraction angiography, DSA) record the vessel filling. Alternatives that reduce or avoid catheterization include computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), which image vessels using intravenous contrast with CT or MRI scanners.

Types and uses

Coronary angiography is used to diagnose coronary artery disease and guide angioplasty and stenting. Cerebral angiography evaluates aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations and stroke. Pulmonary angiography historically diagnosed pulmonary embolism, and fluorescein angiography images the retinal circulation in eye disease. Angiography also maps blood supply before surgery and locates internal bleeding.

Risks

Risks include bleeding or hematoma at the access site, allergic reaction or kidney injury from iodinated contrast, arterial dissection, embolism, and radiation exposure. These risks are weighed against the diagnostic benefit, and less invasive CTA and MRA are preferred when they can answer the clinical question.
