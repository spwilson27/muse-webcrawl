Letchworth Garden City, commonly known as Letchworth, is a town in the North Hertfordshire district of Hertfordshire, England, about 35 miles north of London. It is the world's first garden city, founded in 1903 to put into practice the ideas of Ebenezer Howard, whose book To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform (1898, reissued as Garden Cities of To-morrow) proposed self-contained communities combining the advantages of town and country, surrounded by a permanent agricultural green belt.

History

Before 1903, Letchworth was a small agricultural parish with a population of fewer than 100, recorded in the Domesday Book. In 1903 the Garden City Pioneer Company purchased about 3,800 acres of farmland around the villages of Letchworth, Willian, and Norton for roughly £40 per acre. The architects Barry Parker and Raymond Unwin produced the town plan, with Charles Bennett and Wilson Bidwell designing many early buildings. A design competition and strict building codes produced a town of low-density housing, generous gardens, tree-lined avenues, and a zoned industrial area. The foundation stone of the first house was laid in 1904. Industries such as printing, corset-making (the Spirella company), and later electronics and the Kryn & Lahy (UK) metal works anchored the economy.

The town's unusual land ownership — the whole estate held by a single company, with ground rents funding community services — led to decades of controversy, a hostile takeover battle in the 1960s, and finally the creation of the Letchworth Garden City Heritage Foundation, a charitable community benefit society that has managed the estate since 1995.

Features

Letchworth pioneered many features later copied worldwide: a green belt, zoning of industry, cul-de-sacs, and a cottage style that defined the English suburb. Its centre is laid out around Broadway, a broad processional avenue with formal gardens; the town claims the United Kingdom's first roundabout (the Sollershott Circus, 1909) and one of its oldest cinemas, the Broadway Cinema (1936), and some of the earliest purpose-built factory buildings in Britain. The town inspired Howard's second garden city at Welwyn (1920) and the garden-suburb movement internationally. Temperance sentiment among its founders meant Letchworth had no public house until a referendum allowed one in 1958; the town also has an enduring association with idealistic and "crank" communities, lampooned affectionately by George Orwell in The Road to Wigan Pier.

Transport and today

Letchworth Garden City railway station, on the line between London King's Cross and Cambridge, opened in 1905; the A1(M) motorway passes nearby. The town today has a population of about 33,000 and functions partly as a commuter town while retaining significant local employment and its distinctive garden-city landscape.
