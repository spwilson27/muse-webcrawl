Lamins, also known as nuclear lamins, are fibrous proteins in the type V intermediate filament family that form the nuclear lamina, a meshwork lining the inner surface of the nuclear envelope in most animal cells. They provide structural support to the nucleus and are involved in chromatin organization, regulation of gene expression, DNA repair, and the disassembly and reassembly of the nuclear envelope during cell division.

Types

In humans, three genes encode lamins. The LMNA gene on chromosome 1 produces the A-type lamins, lamin A and lamin C, by alternative splicing; lamin A is synthesized as a precursor, prelamin A, which is processed by farnesylation and cleavage by the protease ZMPSTE24. The B-type lamins, lamin B1 (from LMNB1) and lamin B2 (from LMNB2), are expressed in most or all cells throughout development, whereas A-type lamins appear mainly after differentiation.

Structure and function

Like other intermediate filament proteins, lamins have a central alpha-helical rod domain flanked by globular head and tail domains, and they assemble into filaments. The lamina interacts with chromatin, particularly heterochromatin at the nuclear periphery (lamina-associated domains), and with inner nuclear membrane proteins such as emerin. During mitosis, phosphorylation by kinases such as CDK1 depolymerizes lamins, allowing the nuclear envelope to break down.

Clinical significance

Mutations in LMNA cause a group of diseases called laminopathies, which include Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, dilated cardiomyopathy, familial partial lipodystrophy, and Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, a premature ageing disorder caused by accumulation of a permanently farnesylated mutant prelamin A known as progerin. Mutations in lamin B genes are rarer and are associated with conditions such as adult-onset leukodystrophy caused by LMNB1 duplication.
