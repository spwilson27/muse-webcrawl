BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) is an algorithm and program for comparing primary biological sequence information, such as the amino-acid sequences of proteins or the nucleotides of DNA and/or RNA sequences. Introduced in 1990 by Stephen Altschul and colleagues at the U.S. National Institutes of Health, BLAST lets a researcher search a query sequence against a library or database of sequences and identify matches above a threshold of similarity — the standard first step for inferring a gene's function, identifying species, or finding evolutionary relatives of a sequence.

How it works

Rather than aligning the query against every database entry in full (as in the Smith–Waterman algorithm), BLAST uses a heuristic: it breaks the query into short words, finds database entries containing matching words (seeds), extends those hits in both directions as local alignments scored with substitution matrices such as BLOSUM62 or PAM, and reports alignments whose scores are statistically significant, typically expressed as an expect value (E-value). Variants include BLASTP (protein–protein), BLASTN (nucleotide), BLASTX (translated nucleotide vs protein), TBLASTN and TBLASTX, plus the position-specific PSI-BLAST and the much faster MMseqs2-style successors used for huge datasets.

Impact

The 1990 BLAST paper became one of the most highly cited publications in science (over 100,000 citations), and the NCBI BLAST web service is a foundational tool of genomics and modern biology.
