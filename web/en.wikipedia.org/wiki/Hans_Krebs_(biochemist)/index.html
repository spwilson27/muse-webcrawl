Hans Krebs (biochemist)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sir Hans Adolf Krebs (1900–1981) was a German-British biochemist. This article, under the disambiguated title Hans Krebs (biochemist), covers the scientist famed for the citric acid cycle, to distinguish him from other people of the same name, including the German general Hans Krebs (1898–1945), the last Chief of Staff of the German Army in the Second World War.

The Krebs cycle

The citric acid cycle, also called the tricarboxylic acid cycle or Krebs cycle, is the central metabolic pathway by which aerobic organisms oxidise carbohydrates, fats and amino acids to carbon dioxide, generating the reduced coenzymes that drive ATP synthesis in the electron transport chain. Krebs established its cyclic sequence of intermediates—citrate, isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinate, fumarate, malate and oxaloacetate—at Sheffield in 1937.

Other work

Krebs had earlier, in 1932, described the urea cycle with Kurt Henseleit, the pathway by which mammals convert ammonia into urea. With Hans Kornberg he later identified the glyoxylate cycle in microorganisms and plants. He shared the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Fritz Lipmann.
