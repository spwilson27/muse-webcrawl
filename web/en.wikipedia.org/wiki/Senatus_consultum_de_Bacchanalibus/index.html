Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus

The Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus ("Decree of the Senate concerning the Bacchanalia") is a decree of the Roman Senate issued in 186 BC that restricted the cult of Bacchus throughout Italy. The text survives on a bronze tablet found at Tiriolo in Calabria in 1640, one of the earliest substantial documents in Latin, and the affair is narrated by the historian Livy in Book 39 of his history.

The decree

According to Livy, the consul Spurius Postumius Albinus investigated the Bacchanalia—nocturnal, secretive rites said to involve crime and conspiracy—and reported to the Senate, which responded with a sweeping suppression. The decree did not abolish the cult outright but brought it under state control: no shrine could be maintained, no priest appointed, no common fund held, and no gathering of more than five worshippers (no more than two men and three women) could meet without permission of the Senate and the urban praetor. Violations were punishable by death.

Significance

The episode is a key source for Roman religion, senatorial authority over Italy, and early Latin epigraphy; the tablet's archaic Latin preserves forms such as Bacanalibus and shows the Senate asserting jurisdiction over allied Italian communities.
