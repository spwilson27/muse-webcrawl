History of the Jews

The history of the Jews and Judaism spans more than three thousand years, from the ancient Israelites of the Levant through the Babylonian captivity, the Second Temple period, the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, and nearly two millennia of diaspora across the Middle East, Europe, and later the Americas. Jewish communities produced enduring religious, legal (halakhic), philosophical and literary traditions while facing recurrent persecution, expulsions and, in the twentieth century, the Holocaust. The Zionist movement led to the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, today home — together with the United States — to most of the world's roughly 15–16 million Jews.

The Western Wall, a retaining wall of the Second Temple platform, is Judaism's holiest prayer site.

Ancient Israel and Judah

According to the Hebrew Bible, the Israelites emerged in Canaan under figures such as Abraham, Moses and David; archaeology first attests "Israel" on the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC). The kingdoms of Israel and Judah fell to Assyria (722 BC) and Babylon (586 BC) respectively; the Babylonian exile was followed by a Persian-sponsored return and the rebuilding of the Temple. Under the Hasmoneans (from 140 BC) Judah regained independence before Roman conquest in 63 BC.

Roman era and the diaspora

The Jewish–Roman wars (AD 66–73, 115–117, 132–135) ended Jewish sovereignty in Judea; the Temple's destruction in AD 70 transformed Judaism toward synagogue worship and rabbinic law, codified in the Mishnah (c. 200) and the Talmuds. Large diaspora communities already existed from Alexandria to Babylonia, and Jews spread throughout the Roman and Persian worlds and, after the Arab conquests, the Islamic world.

Medieval period

In Muslim lands, Jews generally held dhimmi status and at times flourished — the "Golden Age" of Jewish culture in Islamic Spain produced figures such as Maimonides. In Christian Europe, Jews faced restrictions, blood libels (from Norwich, 1144), expulsions — from England (1290), France (1306, 1394) and Spain (1492) — and massacres during the Crusades and Black Death. Ashkenazi Jewry shifted eastward into Poland-Lithuania; Sephardic exiles settled across the Ottoman Empire and North Africa.

Emancipation and modernity

The Enlightenment and the Haskalah (Jewish enlightenment) preceded legal emancipation, granted in revolutionary France (1791) and across most of Europe by the later nineteenth century. Yet antisemitism persisted and racialised: the Dreyfus Affair in France, pogroms in the Russian Empire, and mass emigration — over two million Jews left Eastern Europe for the United States between 1881 and 1914. Zionism, organised by Theodor Herzl from 1897, sought a Jewish national home in Palestine.

The Holocaust and after

Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered approximately six million Jews in the Holocaust (1941–1945), destroying most of European Jewry. In 1948 the State of Israel was proclaimed; it absorbed Holocaust survivors and, in following decades, most Jewish communities of the Middle East and North Africa, and later large numbers from the former Soviet Union and Ethiopia. Jewish life continues in a large global diaspora, centred in Israel and North America.
