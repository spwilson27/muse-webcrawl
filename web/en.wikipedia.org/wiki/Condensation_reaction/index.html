Condensation reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Esterification: a carboxylic acid and an alcohol condense, releasing water

A condensation reaction is a type of chemical reaction in which two molecules combine to form a single molecule, usually with the loss of a small molecule such as water. If water is lost, the reaction is also known as a dehydration synthesis. These reactions may be considered the opposite of hydrolysis reactions.

Examples

Many condensation reactions follow a nucleophilic acyl substitution or an aldol-like mechanism. Classic examples include:

— Esterification: a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol to form an ester plus water.
— Amide formation: a carboxylic acid and an amine form an amide; repeated in amino acids, this yields the peptide bonds of proteins.
— Aldol condensation: an enol or enolate reacts with a carbonyl compound to form a β-hydroxy carbonyl, often followed by dehydration to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl.
— Claisen condensation: two esters form a β-keto ester.
— Acetal formation: an aldehyde or ketone reacts with alcohols with loss of water.

In biochemistry and polymers

Condensation reactions build the macromolecules of life: amino acids polymerize into proteins, nucleotides into nucleic acids, and monosaccharides into polysaccharides, each step releasing water. In industrial chemistry, step-growth polymerization (polyesters such as PET, polyamides such as nylon) proceeds by repeated condensation, and removing the small-molecule byproduct drives the reaction to high molecular weights.
