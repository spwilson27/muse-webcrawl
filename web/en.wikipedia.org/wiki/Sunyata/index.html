Śūnyatā

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Śūnyatā (Sanskrit: शून्यता; Pali: suññatā), often translated as emptiness, voidness or openness, is a central concept in Buddhism holding that all phenomena are empty of inherent, independent existence (svabhāva). Because everything arises in dependence on causes and conditions — the doctrine of dependent origination — nothing possesses a fixed, self-standing essence. Śūnyatā does not mean that things do not exist at all, but that they exist only relationally and conventionally.

Development

In early Buddhist texts, "emptiness" is used mainly in meditation contexts and in the analysis of experience as empty of a self (anattā). The concept was expanded dramatically in the Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras, such as the Heart Sutra and Diamond Sutra, which apply emptiness to all dharmas without exception. The philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 2nd century CE) systematised the doctrine in his Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, arguing that emptiness and dependent origination are the same teaching viewed from different angles, and famously that emptiness itself is empty — it is not a metaphysical substance or absolute.

Interpretation

Within Mahāyāna schools, śūnyatā is closely linked with compassion: realising that self and others lack separate, fixed natures loosens attachment and aversion. Later traditions developed the idea further — Yogācāra spoke of the emptiness of the duality of subject and object, and some Tibetan and East Asian schools connect emptiness with Buddha-nature. Misunderstanding śūnyatā as nihilism is a perennial concern in Buddhist teaching; the classical image is that emptiness wrongly grasped is like a snake grasped by the wrong end.
