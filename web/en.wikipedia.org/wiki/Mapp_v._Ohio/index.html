Mapp v. Ohio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961), is a landmark decision of the US Supreme Court in which the Court held that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches and seizures is inadmissible in state courts — extending the federal exclusionary rule of Weeks v. United States (1914) to the states by incorporation through the Fourteenth Amendment, overruling Wolf v. Colorado (1949) in the process.

Cleveland police, seeking a bombing suspect and gambling paraphernalia, forced entry into Dollree Mapp's home in 1957 without producing a valid warrant, found obscene materials, and prosecuted her for possessing them. Justice Clark's majority reasoned that exclusion is “an essential part of both the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments” — the only effective deterrent to police illegality and a demand of judicial integrity. Mapp completed the Warren Court's criminal-procedure revolution (with Gideon and Miranda) in applying the Bill of Rights against the states; later Courts carved exceptions (good faith, Leon, 1984; attenuation; knock-and-announce, Hudson, 2006) while reaffirming the rule's core. Dollree Mapp's own conviction was reversed; the underlying obscenity charge evaporated.
