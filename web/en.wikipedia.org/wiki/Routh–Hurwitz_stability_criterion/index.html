Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion

In control system theory, the Routh–Hurwitz stability criterion is a mathematical test that determines whether all roots of the characteristic polynomial of a linear system have negative real parts — that is, whether the system is stable — without actually solving for the roots.

The criterion

The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are arranged in a Routh array (tabular scheme introduced by Edward John Routh in 1877, building on work by Adolf Hurwitz in 1895). The number of roots in the right half of the complex plane equals the number of sign changes in the first column of the array. If there are no sign changes, the system is stable. Special cases — a zero appearing in the first column, or an entire row of zeros — are handled by epsilon substitution or by using the derivative of the auxiliary polynomial, respectively.

Applications

The criterion is a staple of classical control engineering for checking closed-loop stability and for finding ranges of controller gains (such as the ultimate gain in PID tuning) that keep a feedback system stable.
