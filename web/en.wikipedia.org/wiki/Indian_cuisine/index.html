Indian cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An Indian thali with curries, dal, rice and breads]

Indian cuisine consists of a variety of regional and traditional cuisines native to the Indian subcontinent. Given the diversity of soil, climate, culture, ethnic groups and occupations, these cuisines vary substantially and use locally available spices, herbs, vegetables and fruits. Indian food is also heavily influenced by religion — Hindu, Muslim, Jain, Sikh and Christian traditions — and by centuries of trade and rule, from the Mughals to the Portuguese and British.

India is famous for its Curry dishes, tandoor cooking, biryanis, dosas, and an unmatched vegetarian repertoire built on lentils, beans and dairy. Spice blends such as garam masala, and techniques such as tempering (tadka), define its flavours.

History

Wheat, rice and lentils were cultivated in the Indus Valley five thousand years ago. Ayurvedic tradition classified foods and flavours; Buddhism and Jainism strengthened vegetarian practice. Muslim rule brought kebabs, biryani and rich Mughlai sauces; the Portuguese introduced chillies, potatoes and tomatoes from the Americas, without which modern Indian food is unimaginable. Tea became a national drink under British plantation culture.

Regional cuisines

The north cooks wheat breads (roti, naan, paratha), tandoori meats and creamy tomato-based curries; the south favours rice, dosa and idli, coconut and tamarind; Bengal is famed for fish and sweets such as rasgulla; Goa blends Indian and Portuguese flavours in vindaloo; the west includes Gujarati vegetarian thalis and Mumbai street food.

Meals

A thali assembles many small dishes — dal, vegetables, rice, bread, pickle, yoghurt and a sweet — on one platter. Meals are often eaten with the right hand or with bread as a scoop; chai (spiced milk tea) is drunk throughout the day.
