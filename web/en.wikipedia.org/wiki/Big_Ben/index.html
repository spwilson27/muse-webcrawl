Big Ben is the nickname for the Great Bell of the Great Clock of Westminster, at the north end of the Palace of Westminster in London, England. The name originally referred to the bell itself but is commonly used for the clock and the whole clock tower. The tower, designed by Augustus Pugin in the Perpendicular Gothic style, was completed in 1859 and renamed the Elizabeth Tower in 2012 to mark the Diamond Jubilee of Elizabeth II; it was previously known simply as the Clock Tower.

History

The present tower was built as part of Charles Barry's design for the new Palace of Westminster after the old palace was destroyed by fire in 1834. It was Pugin's last design before his death in 1852. The Great Bell was cast by the Whitechapel Bell Foundry on 10 April 1858; an earlier bell had cracked during testing. The current bell itself developed a crack two months after first striking in 1859, and since then it has been struck with a lighter hammer and rotated so the hammer strikes undamaged metal, giving Big Ben its characteristic imperfect tone.

The clock

The Great Clock, designed by Edmund Beckett Denison and built by Edward John Dent and Frederick Dent, is renowned for its accuracy. Its mechanism, completed in 1854, uses Denison's double three-legged gravity escapement. The four clock dials are 6.9 metres (22.5 ft) in diameter, set in iron frames decorated with Pugin's gilding. The clock is regulated by stacking pennies on the pendulum to adjust its rate. The tower stands 96 metres tall with 334 steps to the belfry.

Cultural significance

Big Ben and the Elizabeth Tower are among the most prominent symbols of the United Kingdom and of London. The sound of the bell has been broadcast by the BBC since 1924 and marks the hour in the famous Westminster Quarters chimes. The tower is a Grade I listed building and part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A major conservation project between 2017 and 2022 restored the tower, returning the dial frames to their original Prussian blue colour scheme.
