Off-the-grid or off-grid is a characteristic of buildings and a lifestyle designed in an independent manner without reliance on one or more public utilities. The term "off-the-grid" traditionally refers to not being connected to the electrical grid, but can also include other utilities like water, gas, and sewer systems, and can scale from residential homes to small communities. Off-the-grid living allows for buildings and people to be self-sufficient, which is advantageous in isolated locations where normal utilities cannot reach and is also attractive to those who want to reduce environmental impact and cost of living.

Generally, an off-grid building must be able to supply energy and potable water for itself, as well as manage food, waste and wastewater.

Energy

Energy for electrical power and heating can be derived from burning hydrocarbons (such as in diesel generators or propane heaters), or generated on-site with renewable energy sources such as solar power, wind power, or micro hydroelectric power. Additional forms include geothermal energy. In northern locations where sunlight is scarce in winter, a combination of sources is often required. Batteries — historically lead-acid, increasingly lithium-ion — store surplus energy for use at night or during calm, cloudy periods. Most off-grid homes are designed to use far less energy than a typical grid-connected house, relying on efficient appliances, LED lighting and good insulation.

Water and waste

Potable water is commonly supplied by a drilled well or by rainwater harvesting from roofs into cisterns, with filtration and disinfection for safety. Wastewater is typically handled by septic systems, composting toilets or constructed wetlands. Many jurisdictions regulate these systems closely, so fully off-grid homes may face permitting hurdles even where no utility connection exists.

Motivations

People choose off-grid living for many reasons: remoteness, lower long-term costs, environmental concern, a desire for independence and resilience, or distrust of institutions. Popular culture has amplified the idea through television programmes about homesteaders in Alaska and elsewhere, and through the tiny-house movement. Critics note that the lifestyle can involve significant upfront costs and labour, and that strict self-sufficiency in food, medicine and manufactured goods is rarely achieved in practice.
