The Basílica i Temple Expiatori de la Sagrada Família ("Basilica and Expiatory Church of the Holy Family") is a large unfinished Roman Catholic minor basilica in the Eixample district of Barcelona, Catalonia, Spain. Designed by the Catalan architect Antoni Gaudí (1852–1926), it is his most famous work and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Construction began in 1882 under architect Francisco de Paula del Villar, who planned a conventional neo-Gothic church. After Villar resigned in 1883, Gaudí took over and radically transformed the design, combining Gothic and curvilinear Art Nouveau (Modernisme) forms inspired by nature. Gaudí devoted the last years of his life entirely to the project; at the time of his death in 1926, less than a quarter of the church was complete.

Work continued intermittently through the twentieth century, interrupted by the Spanish Civil War, during which anarchists destroyed many of Gaudí's original plans and models. Post-war architects reconstructed the design from surviving fragments and photographs. In November 2010, Pope Benedict XVI consecrated the church and proclaimed it a minor basilica. The central tower of Jesus Christ, planned to reach 172.5 metres and make the Sagrada Família the tallest church building in the world, has been a focus of recent construction, with major structural completion long projected for the mid-2020s and decorative work continuing beyond.

Design

The basilica has three grand facades: the Nativity facade (east), richly decorated and largely built under Gaudí; the stark, angular Passion facade (west), with sculptures by Josep Maria Subirachs; and the Glory facade (south), still under construction. Eighteen spires are planned, representing the Twelve Apostles, the Virgin Mary, the four Evangelists and, tallest of all, Jesus Christ. The interior resembles a forest of branching columns that support hyperboloid vaults, flooding the space with coloured light from stained-glass windows designed by Joan Vila-Grau.

Visiting

The Sagrada Família is one of Spain's most visited monuments, drawing several million visitors a year. Admission fees from visitors fund the ongoing construction, which is managed by a private foundation.
