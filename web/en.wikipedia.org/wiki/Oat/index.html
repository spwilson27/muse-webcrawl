The oat (Avena sativa), sometimes called the common oat, is a species of cereal grain grown for its seed, which is known by the same name (usually in the plural, unlike other cereals and pseudocereals). Oats are used for human consumption as oatmeal, including as steel-cut oats or rolled oats. Oats are a nutrient-rich food associated with lower blood cholesterol when consumed regularly. Oats are also commonly used as feed for horses when extra carbohydrates and the subsequent boost in energy are required. The oat hull can be crushed into oat flour. Oat straw is used as animal bedding and sometimes as livestock feed.

Origin

The wild ancestor of A. sativa and the closely related minor crop A. byzantina is A. sterilis, a naturally hexaploid wild oat. Genetic evidence suggests the ancestral forms of A. sterilis grew in the Fertile Crescent of the Near East. Oats are thought to have emerged as a secondary crop, originally as a weed of the primary cereals wheat and barley, before being domesticated in cooler, wetter areas of Europe. Oats were a staple human food in Scotland and other cool northern climates long before they were widely accepted elsewhere; Samuel Johnson's dictionary famously (and slightingly) defined oats as "a grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people."

Cultivation

Oats are best grown in temperate regions. They have a lower summer heat requirement and greater tolerance of rain than other cereals, such as wheat, rye or barley, so they are particularly important in areas with cool, wet summers, such as Northwest Europe. Oats are an annual plant and can be planted either in autumn (for late summer harvest) or in the spring (for early autumn harvest). The largest producers are Russia, Canada, Spain, Australia, Poland, and Finland.

Uses

Human food

Oats are eaten as porridge, rolled oats, oatcakes, flapjacks, granola and muesli, and are widely used in plant-based milk substitutes ("oat milk"). Oat bran is the outer casing of the oat and is especially rich in the soluble fiber beta-glucan, consumption of which is associated with reduced LDL cholesterol. Oats are naturally low in gluten, though commercial supplies are often contaminated with wheat; certified gluten-free oats are available for people with coeliac disease, although a small minority react to the oat protein avenin itself.

Animal feed

Historically a large share of the oat crop went to feeding horses and other livestock. Oat straw is prized as bedding, and whole oats remain a traditional feed for racehorses and cattle.

Nutrition and health

Oats are rich in carbohydrates, provide good-quality protein for a cereal (about 13% by weight when uncooked), and are a source of manganese, phosphorus, magnesium, iron, zinc, folate, and B vitamins. Oats are the only food source of avenanthramides, a group of antioxidants. Regular consumption of oat beta-glucan has been shown in meta-analyses to modestly reduce total and LDL cholesterol.
