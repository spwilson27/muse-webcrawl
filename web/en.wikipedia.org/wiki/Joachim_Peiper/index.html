Joachim Peiper (30 January 1915 – 14 July 1976) was a German Waffen-SS officer of the Nazi era who served as adjutant to Heinrich Himmler and commanded armoured units of the 1st SS Panzer Division Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler. During the Battle of the Bulge in December 1944, his battle group, Kampfgruppe Peiper, was responsible for the Malmedy massacre of American prisoners of war and for the murder of Belgian civilians along its route, as well as earlier killings of civilians in Italy (Boves, 1943) and on the Eastern Front. Peiper was convicted of war crimes at the Malmedy massacre trial in 1946 and sentenced to death; the sentence was commuted, and he was released in 1956.

After his release Peiper worked for Porsche and Volkswagen and later lived in France. He was murdered in 1976 when his house at Traves was attacked and burned by unknown assailants.

Early life and SS career

Peiper was born in Berlin into a middle-class military family. He joined the Hitler Youth and, in 1933, the SS (SS-Verfügungstruppe), receiving officer training at the SS-Junkerschule Braunschweig. In 1938 he joined Himmler's personal staff, becoming his first adjutant in 1939; he accompanied Himmler on inspection tours, including of concentration camps and the occupied east, and married Sigurd (Sigi) Hinrichsen, a secretary in Himmler's office, in 1939. Historians note that his staff service placed him close to the planning of mass crimes, a proximity he later denied meaningful knowledge of.

Second World War

Peiper saw combat with the Leibstandarte in Poland, the Netherlands, France, the Balkans, and from 1941 on the Eastern Front, where he rose from company to battalion command and acquired a reputation as an aggressive armoured-infantry commander — and, in Soviet and later accounts, responsibility for atrocities including the burning of villages. Temporarily commanding in Italy after the Italian armistice in September 1943, his unit murdered 23 civilians in the village of Boves near Cuneo — an incident investigated by Italian and later German authorities (a 1968 German proceeding against Peiper was dropped for insufficient evidence). Back on the Eastern Front and then in Normandy in 1944, Peiper took command of SS Panzer Regiment 1.

Malmedy and the Ardennes offensive

In the Ardennes offensive (December 1944), Kampfgruppe Peiper — the spearhead of the 1st SS Panzer Division, with about 4,800 men and 117 tanks — advanced through Belgium toward the Meuse. On 17 December 1944, near the Baugnez crossroads south of Malmedy, troops of the Kampfgruppe murdered 84 American prisoners of war of Battery B, 285th Field Artillery Observation Battalion — the Malmedy massacre. Along the route at Stavelot, Trois-Ponts, La Gleize, and elsewhere, the battle group also killed more than 100 Belgian civilians, and other prisoners were murdered at other points. Out of fuel and encircled, Peiper abandoned vehicles at La Gleize and escaped eastward on foot with remaining men on 24–25 December.

Trial and imprisonment

Peiper was tried by a U.S. military court at Dachau in 1946 together with 73 other defendants in the Malmedy massacre trial and sentenced to death by hanging. The proceedings were later criticised for interrogation methods at Schwäbisch Hall (allegations examined by the Simpson Commission and a U.S. Senate subcommittee); death sentences were reviewed and commuted amid Cold War politics. Peiper's sentence was commuted to life and then to a term; he was the last of the Malmedy defendants released, leaving Landsberg Prison in December 1956. He denied ordering the Malmedy killings and gave shifting accounts — variously attributing them to subordinate units and circumstances — which historians have weighed against substantial documentary and witness evidence of his command responsibility and of killings ordered within the Kampfgruppe.

Later life and death

Peiper worked in the West German automobile industry (Porsche, then Volkswagen in managerial training roles) before settling at Traves in eastern France in 1972, working as a translator of military history books. His presence in France became public in 1976; former Resistance members protested, he received death threats, and he sent his family back to Germany while remaining with a rifle and dog. On the night of 13–14 July 1976, his house was attacked with shotguns and firebombed; Peiper was killed and the house destroyed. The crime was never solved and no one was convicted; responsibility was claimed in some accounts by a group calling itself "The Avengers." Peiper remains a controversial figure, celebrated in some far-right and apologetic Waffen-SS literature as a soldier, and treated by mainstream historians as a war criminal implicated in multiple massacres.
