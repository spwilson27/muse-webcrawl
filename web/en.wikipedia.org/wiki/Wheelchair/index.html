Wheelchair

A wheelchair is a mobilized form of chair equipped with wheels, used when walking is difficult or impossible due to illness, injury, problems related to old age, or disability. Wheelchairs come in a wide variety of formats and are propelled manually by the seated individual, pushed by a caregiver, or driven by electric motors.

History

Depictions of wheeled chairs appear as early as ancient China and Greece. In 1595 the Spanish king Philip II was drawn in an elaborate armchair on wheels. The English inventor John Dawson's "Bath chair" of the late 18th century became a common sight at spa towns. Harry Jennings and Herbert Everest, an American paraplegic engineer, built the first lightweight, collapsible steel wheelchair in 1933 and founded Everest & Jennings, which dominated the market for decades. The Canadian activist and inventor George Klein and colleagues developed the first powered wheelchair in the 1950s for injured veterans.

Design and fitting

Modern manual chairs are built from aluminium or titanium, and ultralight rigid or folding frames are individually fitted: seat width and depth, back height, wheel camber, and centre of gravity all affect propulsion efficiency and pressure-ulcer risk. Cushions and postural supports are prescribed alongside the chair. Powered chairs may use alternative controls — head arrays, sip-and-puff switches, chin joysticks — for users with limited hand function.

Society and access

The wheelchair is a universal symbol of accessibility (the International Symbol of Access, designed in 1968). Accessibility legislation such as the Americans with Disabilities Act requires step-free access in public buildings, and wheelchair users remain central to the disability rights movement. Globally, the WHO estimates tens of millions of people need a wheelchair but lack access to an appropriate one.
