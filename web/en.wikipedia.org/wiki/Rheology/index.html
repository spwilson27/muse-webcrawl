Rheology

Rheology
FieldPhysics, materials science, continuum mechanics
StudiesDeformation and flow of matter
Key quantitiesViscosity, shear stress, shear rate, storage and loss moduli
Term coined1920s, by Eugene C. Bingham

Rheology is the study of the flow of matter, primarily in a fluid state but also of "soft solids" and solids under conditions in which they respond with plastic flow rather than deforming elastically to an applied force. It is a branch of physics concerned with materials whose mechanical behaviour lies between the idealised extremes of elasticity (Hookean solids) and viscosity (Newtonian fluids). The term was coined by Eugene C. Bingham after a suggestion by a colleague, Markus Reiner, from the Greek ῥέω (rhéō, "flow") and -λογία (-logia, "study of"); the society motto is a phrase attributed to Heraclitus, πάντα ῥεῖ ("everything flows").

Scope

Rheology describes complex viscoelastic materials—polymers, suspensions, emulsions, foodstuffs, biological fluids, pastes, and granular matter—whose properties depend on the rate, duration and history of deformation. Practical applications include plastics processing, paints and coatings, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, food texture, drilling muds, concrete, and the flow of blood (hemorheology).

Key concepts

  • Shear thinning and thickening — viscosity that decreases (paints, ketchup) or increases (cornstarch suspensions) with shear rate.
  • Thixotropy — time-dependent decrease of viscosity under shear, with recovery at rest.
  • Yield stress — a minimum stress below which a material (e.g. toothpaste, mayonnaise, some gels) behaves as a solid.
  • Viscoelasticity — combined viscous and elastic response, measured by storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G″, and by phenomena such as the Weissenberg effect (rod-climbing) and die swell.
  • Deborah number — the ratio of a material's relaxation time to the timescale of observation, indicating solid-like versus liquid-like behaviour.

Measurement

Rheological properties are measured with rheometers and viscometers. Rotational rheometers impose controlled shear between plates, cones or concentric cylinders; capillary and extensional rheometers probe other flow types. Small-amplitude oscillatory shear is the standard technique for characterising viscoelastic structure without destroying it.

History

The Society of Rheology was founded in the United States in 1929, and its Journal of Rheology remains a leading publication. British rheologists later produced a celebrated volume of whimsical research, the "Cat" (pitch-drop style) experiments and photographs collected under the auspices of the British Society of Rheology.
