Damages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, damages are an award, typically of money, paid to a claimant as compensation for loss or injury — the remedy at the heart of most civil litigation. Compensatory damages aim to put the injured party in the position they would have occupied had the wrong not occurred (in tort) or had the contract been performed (in contract), and divide into general damages (non-quantifiable losses such as pain and suffering) and special damages (provable financial losses).

Types and limits

Beyond compensation lie: nominal damages (vindication without substantial loss), aggravated damages (injured feelings from high-handed wrongs), exemplary/punitive damages (punishment and deterrence — routine in the US, restricted in England since Rookes v Barnard), liquidated damages (genuine pre-estimates fixed by contract), and statutory damages (notably in copyright). Recovery is limited by remoteness/foreseeability (Hadley v Baxendale in contract), the duty to mitigate, contributory fault, and, in some jurisdictions, legislative caps. Structured settlements, periodical payments and damages-based agreements shape modern practice, as do debates over quantifying non-economic loss and funding mass claims.
