Pit-house

A pit-house (or pit house) is a type of dwelling partly dug into the ground and roofed over, used by many cultures around the world, especially in cold climates. The excavated floor and earth insulation protect inhabitants from temperature extremes, while a roof of timber, sod, or thatch completes the structure. Entry was often through a hole in the roof by ladder, which also served as a smoke hole.

Distribution and examples

Pit-houses are known prehistorically in Europe, Asia, and the Americas. They include the Grubenhäuser of early medieval northern Europe, the semi-subterranean winter houses of the Ancestral Puebloans and Hohokam of the North American Southwest (precursors of the kiva), the pit dwellings of Jōmon-period Japan (tateana jūkyo), and traditional dwellings of Siberian and sub-Arctic peoples.

Construction

Typically, a pit up to about a metre or more deep was lined or left earthen, posts supported a roof frame, and the roof was covered with branches, grass, sod, or soil. A central hearth and storage pits in the floor were common. In the American Southwest, pit-house villages preceded the above-ground pueblos, and the form survives ceremonially in the kiva.
