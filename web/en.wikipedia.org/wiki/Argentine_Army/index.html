Argentine Army

Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers, the Argentine Army's ceremonial cavalry.

The Argentine Army (Spanish: Ejército Argentino) is the land warfare branch of the Armed Forces of the Argentine Republic. Officially founded on 29 May 1810, days after the May Revolution, it is the senior and largest of Argentina's armed services, with roughly 50,000 personnel. Under Argentine law it is one of the three branches answerable to the President as commander-in-chief through the Ministry of Defense.

History

The Army's traditions reach back to the militia forces that repelled the British invasions of the River Plate (1806–1807) and to the Army of the Andes with which José de San Martín crossed the Andes in 1817 to liberate Chile and Peru. It fought in the nineteenth-century civil wars, the War of the Triple Alliance against Paraguay, and frontier campaigns, and was professionalised under German influence around 1900. In the twentieth century the Army repeatedly intervened in politics, leading coups in 1930, 1943, 1955, 1962, 1966 and 1976; the 1976–1983 dictatorship's "Dirty War" gravely damaged its standing.

Modern army

Conscription was abolished in 1995 after the death of a conscript, Ómar Carrasco, and the Army became an all-volunteer force. Its forces include armoured brigades equipped with the TAM medium tank, mechanised and mountain brigades, the Regiment of Mounted Grenadiers "General San Martín" (the presidential guard), special forces and army aviation. Argentine Army units have served extensively in United Nations peacekeeping missions, notably in Cyprus, Haiti and the former Yugoslavia.
