Spiritual care

Spiritual care is the provision of support addressing the spiritual, existential and religious needs of individuals, most commonly in healthcare settings such as hospitals, hospices and care homes. It is typically delivered by chaplains and trained spiritual care practitioners, and is offered to people of all faiths and none.

Scope

Spiritual care concerns questions of meaning, purpose, hope, connection and transcendence that frequently arise during illness, bereavement and dying. Practitioners listen to patients and families, help them draw on their own beliefs and values, and may facilitate religious practices such as prayer or ritual when requested. Unlike pastoral care within a single tradition, modern spiritual care in public institutions is deliberately inclusive and non-proselytising.

In healthcare

Research in palliative care has linked attention to spiritual needs with improved quality of life and patient satisfaction. Spiritual care is recognised as a component of holistic care alongside medical, psychological and social support, and forms part of the World Health Organization's definition of palliative care. Healthcare chaplains commonly complete training in clinical pastoral education and may be board certified in some countries.

Providers

While chaplains are the specialist providers, nurses, physicians and other staff are also expected to recognise spiritual distress and refer patients appropriately. Volunteers trained and supervised by chaplaincy departments may also contribute to spiritual care programmes.
