Marquess of Queensberry Rules

The Marquess of Queensberry Rules, also known as the Queensberry Rules, are a code of generally accepted rules in the sport of boxing. They were drafted in London in 1865 and published in 1867. The rules were named after John Douglas, 9th Marquess of Queensberry, who publicly endorsed them, although they were written by John Graham Chambers of the Amateur Athletic Club.

Boxing under the Queensberry Rules

The rules

The twelve rules specified, among other things, that matches should take place in a 24-foot-square ring; that no wrestling or hugging was allowed; that rounds should last three minutes with one minute of rest between them; that a fighter knocked down had ten seconds to rise unaided; and that gloves of a fair size should be worn. The introduction of gloves, timed rounds, and the ten-count fundamentally transformed boxing from bare-knuckle prizefighting into the modern sport.

Impact

The first championship fight conducted under the Queensberry Rules is generally considered to be the 1892 heavyweight bout between John L. Sullivan and James J. Corbett, which Corbett won. Within a few decades the rules had displaced the older London Prize Rules almost everywhere, and their core features — gloves, rounds, and scoring — remain the foundation of modern boxing regulations.
