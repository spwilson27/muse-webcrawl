Mushroom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A market basket of mixed mushrooms: porcini, shiitake, oyster and button]

Edible mushrooms are the fleshy fruiting bodies of fungi, eaten across nearly every food culture — gathered wild, as with porcini, chanterelles and morels, or cultivated, as with the white button mushroom and its older selves, cremini and portobello (all one species, Agaricus bisporus). Fungi are not plants: they feed by absorbing decayed matter or by trading with tree roots in mycorrhizal partnership, which is why the great wild species resist farming and command foragers' prices. China produces the large majority of the world's cultivated mushrooms.

History and cultivation

People have eaten mushrooms since prehistory — Ötzi the Iceman carried fungal pieces, perhaps medicinal. Rome prized Amanita caesarea (and feared its poisonous cousins in an age of convenient imperial deaths). Commercial cultivation began in France, where melon-growers noticed mushrooms in their compost and Parisian growers moved production into abandoned quarries — hence champignon de Paris. Spawn science in the 20th century industrialised the button mushroom on pasteurised compost. Asia's traditions are older and broader: shiitake on oak logs in China and Japan for a millennium, straw mushrooms and wood-ears in everyday cooking, and fermentation-adjacent fungi like koji that blur the category entirely.

Risk and reward

Mushrooms are umami made visible — rich in glutamates, B vitamins, and (when exposed to sunlight) vitamin D — but the forager's rule rules: some of the world's deadliest poisons are mushrooms, and the death cap resembles edible species on several continents. Cultivation solved safety for the species it can grow; wild species remain a knowledgeable pleasure, celebrated in autumn markets from Catalonia to Yunnan, where wild-mushroom hotpot season is a civic event.
