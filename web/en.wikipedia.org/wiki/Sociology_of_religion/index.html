Sociology of religion

The sociology of religion is the study of the beliefs, practices and organisational forms of religion using the tools and methods of sociology. It examines religion as a social phenomenon — its role in social cohesion and conflict, its relationship to economic and political life, and its change over time — without adjudicating the truth of religious claims. The discipline was founded by the classics of sociology: Émile Durkheim analysed religion as society worshipping itself and a source of collective solidarity; Max Weber studied religion's role in economic change and rationalisation; and Karl Marx treated religion as ideology expressing and masking material conditions ("the opium of the people").

Classical theories

Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), based on Australian Aboriginal totemism, defined religion as a unified system of beliefs and practices relative to sacred things uniting adherents into a moral community, and argued that ritual generates "collective effervescence" binding society together. Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905) traced how Calvinist doctrines of predestination and calling encouraged the disciplined accumulation he saw as characteristic of modern capitalism; his comparative studies of China, India and ancient Judaism developed a typology of religious ethics and authority (charismatic, traditional, rational-legal).

Major themes

Secularisation — the thesis that modernisation erodes religion's social significance — dominated postwar debate; its critics point to religious vitality in the United States and the global South, leading to revised accounts (José Casanova, Peter Berger's later work) and to "religious economy" models (Stark and Finke) that treat churches as firms competing for adherents. Other central topics include the church–sect typology of Weber and Ernst Troeltsch, denominations and new religious movements, civil religion (Bellah), religion and social stratification, gender and religion, and the resurgence of public religion and fundamentalism.
