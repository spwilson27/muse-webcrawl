Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BC – AD 65), usually known as Seneca the Younger or simply Seneca, was a Roman Stoic philosopher, statesman and dramatist. The son of the rhetorician Seneca the Elder, he was born at Cordoba in Hispania and educated in Rome. He served as quaestor and senator, was exiled to Corsica by Claudius in AD 41 on a charge of adultery, and was recalled in 49 to become tutor — then chief adviser — to the young emperor Nero.

Adviser to Nero

With the praetorian prefect Burrus, Seneca effectively guided the empire during Nero's first years, the celebrated quinquennium Neronis, drafting the emperor's speeches and accumulating great wealth. His influence waned as Nero matured; after Burrus's death in 62 Seneca withdrew from court. Implicated — probably unjustly — in the Pisonian conspiracy of 65, he was ordered by Nero to take his own life, which he did in the manner of Socrates and Cato, an end described by the historian Tacitus.

Philosophical works

Seneca's surviving writings are the most extensive body of Latin Stoicism: twelve Moral Essays (dialogues and treatises) on anger, providence, the shortness of life, consolation and clemency — the last addressed to Nero — and the 124 Letters to Lucilius, a late masterpiece of practical ethics on time, death, friendship and self-examination. His Naturales Quaestiones surveys meteorology and natural phenomena. Critics ancient and modern have noted the tension between his preaching of poverty and his fortune, a discrepancy Seneca himself acknowledged.

Tragedies

Ten tragedies are transmitted under Seneca's name, among them Medea, Phaedra, Thyestes and the Orestes-cycle play Agamemnon. Violent, rhetorical and haunted by ghosts and revenge, they profoundly influenced Renaissance drama — including Elizabethan revenge tragedy and Shakespeare — far more than the Greek originals, many of which were then unknown in the West. His prose satire, the Apocolocyntosis, mocks the deification of Claudius.

Legacy

Seneca was a favourite author of the early Church (which even forged a correspondence with St Paul), of Montaigne and of the Renaissance humanists. His maxim-rich style made him one of the most quoted of Roman writers; the phrase "we learn more from living with men than from books" — and the modern self-help Stoicism movement — continue his influence.
