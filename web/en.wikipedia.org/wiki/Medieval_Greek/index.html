Medieval Greek, also known as Byzantine Greek, is the stage of the Greek language spoken and written in the Byzantine Empire during the Middle Ages, conventionally dated from the Arab conquests and the transformation of the empire in the 7th century to the Fall of Constantinople in 1453. It developed from the Hellenistic Koine and in turn gave rise to Modern Greek.

Characteristics

Spoken Medieval Greek continued the phonological changes begun under the Koine: the ancient system of vowel length and pitch accent disappeared, several vowels and diphthongs merged (iotacism), and voiced fricatives developed from the old voiced stops. In morphology, many irregular and synthetic forms were progressively replaced by more analytic constructions; the infinitive was gradually lost in favour of finite subordinate clauses — a hallmark of the later Balkan Sprachbund — and the dative case retreated, surviving mainly in fixed expressions.

Diglossia

Written Byzantine Greek was characterised by diglossia: official, theological and high literary writing imitated classical Attic Greek to varying degrees, while a lower-register language closer to speech was used in chronicles, saints' lives, romances and legal documents. Vernacular literature flowered from the 12th century, notably in the epic of Digenes Akritas, the Ptochoprodromic begging poems, and later the romances and Cretan literature. The tension between learned and demotic registers persisted into modern Greece as the "language question", resolved only in 1976 in favour of Demotic.

Loanwords and influence

Medieval Greek absorbed vocabulary from Latin (administrative and military terms, many inherited from the early empire), from Slavic and Turkish during the later centuries, and from Italian and French in the crusader states; conversely it lent many words to Slavic languages, Romanian, Turkish and the liturgical vocabulary of Orthodox Christianity.

Study

The standard reference works include the Lexikon zur byzantinischen Gräzität and Kriaras' dictionary of medieval vernacular literature. Byzantine texts survive in vast numbers of manuscripts, copied in the distinctive minuscule book hands developed from the 9th century onward.
