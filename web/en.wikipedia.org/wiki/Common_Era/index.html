Common Era

The Common Era (CE) is one of the year notations used for the Gregorian calendar (and its predecessor, the Julian calendar), the world's most widely used calendar era. Before the Common Era (BCE) is the era before CE. BCE and CE are alternatives to the Dionysian AD and BC system respectively: a year in the Common Era numbering runs identically to the Anno Domini system — for example, 2026 CE corresponds to AD 2026, and 44 BCE corresponds to 44 BC.

History

The Anno Domini system was devised by the monk Dionysius Exiguus in 525 to date Easter. The English expression "common era" appears by the early 18th century, and variants like "Vulgar Era" were used by writers including Johannes Kepler, who used annus aerae vulgaris. Jewish scholars adopted CE/BCE in the 19th century to avoid the explicitly Christian Anno Domini ("year of our Lord"). Through the later 20th century, CE/BCE spread in academic publishing, museums, and interfaith contexts as a religiously neutral alternative.

Usage and debate

Style guides in archaeology, history, and religious studies commonly prescribe BCE/CE; several national curricula and broadcasters use it as standard. Supporters argue the notation keeps the now-international calendar count while avoiding a confessional label. Critics respond that it merely renames a system whose epoch still marks the traditional (and, historians note, slightly miscalculated) date of Jesus's birth, and some Christians view abandoning AD as unnecessary. There is no year zero in either system: 1 BCE is followed directly by 1 CE.
