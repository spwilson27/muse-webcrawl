Phlebotomy

Phlebotomy
Definition: Incision of a vein to draw blood
Common site: Median cubital vein, antecubital fossa
Purposes: Laboratory testing, blood donation, therapeutic venesection

Phlebotomy (from Greek phleps, "vein", and tomia, "cutting") is the process of making an incision in a vein with a needle to draw blood. In modern medicine the procedure — also called venipuncture or venesection — is performed to obtain blood samples for laboratory analysis, to collect donated blood, or as a treatment in specific conditions.

Modern practice

A phlebotomist typically draws blood from the median cubital vein in the bend of the elbow, using a tourniquet, evacuated tubes, and aseptic technique. Capillary sampling from a fingertip or heel is used for small volumes, especially in infants. Phlebotomy is one of the most common invasive medical procedures, and proper technique is essential for accurate test results; errors in collection (for example haemolysed samples or a wrong order of draw) can distort laboratory values.

Therapeutic phlebotomy

Scheduled removal of blood remains a treatment for a few specific disorders: hereditary haemochromatosis (to reduce iron overload), polycythaemia vera (to reduce red cell mass), and porphyria cutanea tarda. This therapeutic use is distinct from the indiscriminate bloodletting of earlier medicine.

History

The modern procedure descends historically from bloodletting, practised for over two thousand years on the humoral theory that illness resulted from imbalanced bodily fluids. Performed with lancets, fleams and leeches by physicians and barber-surgeons, bloodletting was applied to fevers, inflammation and many other conditions; its harms were increasingly recognised in the 19th century, and Pierre Louis's numerical method showed it worsened outcomes in pneumonia. By the early 20th century routine bloodletting had been abandoned, leaving the diagnostic and narrowly therapeutic roles of modern phlebotomy.
