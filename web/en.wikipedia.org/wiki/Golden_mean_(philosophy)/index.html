Golden mean (philosophy)

The golden mean or golden middle way is the desirable middle ground between two extremes, one of excess and the other of deficiency. The idea appears in several ancient traditions and is most closely associated with Aristotle's doctrine of the mean in the Nicomachean Ethics.

Aristotle's doctrine of the mean

Aristotle held that moral virtue is a habit of choosing the mean between two vices — one of excess and one of deficiency — relative to the individual and the situation. Courage, for example, is the mean between cowardice (deficiency) and recklessness (excess); generosity lies between stinginess and prodigality. The mean is not a simple arithmetic midpoint but is determined by practical wisdom (phronesis), as a prudent person would judge it.

Other traditions

The concept parallels the Confucian Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong), the Buddhist Middle Way avoiding extremes of indulgence and asceticism, and the Delphic maxim "nothing in excess." The Roman poet Horace praised the aurea mediocritas — the golden mediocrity or middle way — in his Odes.

Criticism and use

Critics note that some things (such as murder or envy, for Aristotle) admit no mean at all, and that the doctrine risks becoming a fallacy of moderation when one extreme is simply wrong. Nevertheless the golden mean remains an influential ideal of balanced, temperate character.
