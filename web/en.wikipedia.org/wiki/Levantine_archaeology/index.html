Levantine archaeology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Levantine archaeology is the archaeological study of the Levant, the eastern Mediterranean region encompassing modern Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Lebanon, Syria, and adjacent areas. Once dominated by "biblical archaeology", whose aim was to illuminate the text of the Bible, the field has since broadened into the study of all cultures of the region from the Palaeolithic period through the Ottoman era.

History of the discipline

Systematic exploration began in the nineteenth century with surveys such as Edward Robinson's travels and the work of the Palestine Exploration Fund, founded in 1865. Early excavators including William F. Albright framed the field around biblical history, while Kathleen Kenyon's stratigraphic excavations at Jericho and Jerusalem in the 1950s and 1960s introduced rigorous methods developed in British prehistoric archaeology. From the 1970s the discipline absorbed the concerns of processual and anthropological archaeology, and scholars increasingly preferred the neutral term "Levantine archaeology" to the theologically charged "biblical archaeology".

Periods

  • Prehistoric periods: The Levant is a key corridor for early human dispersals out of Africa, with sites such as Ubeidiya and Qesem Cave. The Natufian culture of the Epipalaeolithic saw some of the earliest sedentary communities, followed by the Neolithic Revolution in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, when farming and herding first developed.
  • Bronze and Iron Ages: Urban Canaanite city-states, Egyptian imperial involvement, the collapse at the end of the Late Bronze Age, and the emergence of Israel, Judah, and their neighbours, including the Philistines, Phoenicians, and Arameans.
  • Classical and later periods: Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, early Islamic, Crusader, Mamluk, and Ottoman remains are all studied within the discipline.

Methods and debates

Excavation of multi-layered mounds (tells) relies on stratigraphy and ceramic typology for dating, increasingly supplemented by radiocarbon dating and Bayesian modelling. Major debates have included the dating and historicity of the United Monarchy (the "high versus low chronology" controversy), the nature of the Israelite settlement, and the ethics of excavation in contested territory.
