Musical development

This article is about the unfolding of musical abilities over the lifespan. For the development section of a sonata, see Development (music).

Musical development refers to the processes by which humans acquire musical capacities — perceiving pitch, rhythm and timbre, singing, moving to music, and eventually performing, improvising and composing — from infancy through childhood and beyond. It is studied within music psychology, developmental psychology and music education, and depends on both maturation and enculturation into the musical practices of one's environment.

Early development

Research shows that even newborns respond preferentially to music and to infant-directed singing; foetuses in the last trimester respond to sound, and infants can remember melodies heard before birth. Young infants prefer consonant intervals, detect changes in melody and rhythm, and by about six months show sensitivity to the scales and metres of their own culture's music, a perceptual narrowing similar to that found in language acquisition. Babies spontaneously move rhythmically to music, though true synchronisation to a beat develops later in childhood.

Childhood

Children's singing typically progresses from chant-like monotone play through expanding pitch range and contour control to accurate singing of whole songs by about age five. Theorists have proposed stage models: the Swiss psychologist's and the "spiral" model of Swanwick and Tillman describe children moving through mastery of materials, imitation, imaginative play and increasingly structural awareness. Formal tuition, aptitude and home musical environment all influence the pace of development; methods such as Suzuki, Kodály and Dalcroze eurhythmics structure instruction around developmental principles.

Adolescence and adulthood

Adolescence brings intensified use of music for identity, mood regulation and peer bonding, and musical taste becomes a marker of personality and group membership. Adults without formal training retain sophisticated implicit knowledge of tonality and musical expectation, while expertise research emphasises the role of long-term deliberate practice in elite performance.
