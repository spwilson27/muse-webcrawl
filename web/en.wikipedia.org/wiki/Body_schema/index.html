Body schema

A body schema is a model of the body that includes the position of every limb in space, maintained and updated by proprioceptive, vestibular, tactile, and visual signals. The concept is used in neuroscience, psychology, and philosophy to describe the largely unconscious system that allows an organism to control and coordinate movement, reach for objects, and navigate space. The body schema is distinguished from the body image, which refers to conscious perceptions, beliefs, and attitudes about one's own body.

The body schema tracks limb position and posture for movement control

History of the concept

The concept originates with the neurologists Henry Head and Gordon Holmes, who in 1911 proposed a "postural schema" — a plastic internal model against which movements and changes of posture are measured. The term was later adopted and extended by philosophers such as Maurice Merleau-Ponty, who used it (as schéma corporel) in his phenomenology of embodied perception, and by cognitive scientists studying motor control, tool use, and disorders of body awareness.

Features

The body schema is dynamic and plastic. It operates mostly below consciousness, is organized for action rather than for perception, and can incorporate tools: experiments with macaques by Atsushi Iriki and colleagues showed that after using a rake to retrieve food, the monkeys' visual receptive fields expanded to include the tool, as if it had become part of the hand's schema. In humans, the schema updates during growth, adapts to prostheses, and can be temporarily altered by illusions such as the rubber hand illusion, in which synchronous stroking of a hidden real hand and a visible fake hand induces a feeling of ownership over the fake hand.

Disorders

Damage to parietal cortex can disturb the body schema, producing conditions such as hemispatial neglect (ignoring one side of the body and space), autotopagnosia (inability to point to body parts on command), and somatoparaphrenia (denial of ownership of a limb, sometimes with attribution of it to another person). Phantom limbs after amputation are interpreted as persistence of the body schema for a limb that no longer exists. Disturbances of the body schema are also discussed in relation to eating disorders and body dysmorphic disorder, although these are more often linked to body image.

Body schema versus body image

Shaun Gallagher's influential distinction holds that body image comprises intentional states — perceptions, mental representations, beliefs, and attitudes about the body — whereas body schema comprises the pre-reflective system of sensory-motor capacities that control posture and movement without conscious monitoring. The two systems normally cooperate but can dissociate, as in the classic case of deafferentation patient Ian Waterman, who lost proprioception and must consciously guide his movements using vision, effectively substituting body image for a damaged body schema.
