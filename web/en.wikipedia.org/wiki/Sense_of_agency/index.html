Sense of agency

The sense of agency is the subjective awareness of initiating, executing, and controlling one's own volitional actions in the world — the experience that "I" am the one causing an action or its effects. It is a central topic in cognitive neuroscience, psychology, and philosophy of action.

Mechanisms

A leading account, the comparator model, holds that when a motor command is issued, the brain generates an efference copy used to predict the sensory consequences of the movement; when actual feedback matches the prediction, the action is experienced as self-caused, and its sensory effects are attenuated. Retrospective cues also matter: people are more likely to feel agency when an outcome follows their action quickly and consistently (the principles of priority, consistency, and exclusivity in Daniel Wegner's theory of apparent mental causation).

Experimental measures

Researchers measure the sense of agency through phenomena such as intentional binding, the perceived temporal compression between a voluntary action and its effect, and through explicit judgements of control in tasks involving distorted or delayed feedback. The rubber hand illusion and related paradigms probe the related sense of body ownership.

Clinical relevance

Disturbances of agency are prominent in schizophrenia, where delusions of control and passivity phenomena involve experiencing one's own actions as caused by outside forces; altered agency is also studied in functional movement disorders and depersonalization.
