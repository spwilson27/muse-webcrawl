Rhizome (philosophy)

Rhizome is a philosophical concept developed by Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari in their works Capitalism and Schizophrenia (1972–1980), in particular the introduction to A Thousand Plateaus (1980), titled "Introduction: Rhizome". Borrowed from botany — where a rhizome is a horizontal underground stem that sends out roots and shoots from its nodes — the concept describes a model of knowledge and social organisation that is non-hierarchical, with multiple entry and exit points.

Arborescence versus rhizome

Deleuze and Guattari contrast the rhizome with the arborescent (tree-like) model that they see as dominating Western thought: rooted in a single origin, branching by binary divisions, and organised hierarchically, as in Porphyry's tree or Chomsky's syntactic trees. A rhizome, by contrast, "connects any point to any other point", is composed of heterogeneous elements, and can be broken at any point and restart along new lines — a property they call rupture. Their well-known summary is that a rhizome is a map, not a tracing: it is open, connectable, detachable and reversible.

Principles

The introduction lists principles of connection, heterogeneity, multiplicity, asignifying rupture, cartography and decalcomania. The concept has been taken up in post-structuralist philosophy, cultural studies, education theory and discussions of the internet, whose decentralised structure is often (if loosely) compared to a rhizome.

Criticism

Critics, notably in science studies, have questioned whether the metaphor does real analytical work or merely redescribes networks in biological language. Umberto Eco had earlier proposed the rhizome as a model for the labyrinthine structure of knowledge in The Open Work and later writings.
