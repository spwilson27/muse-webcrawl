The Advanced Passenger Train (APT) was a tilting high-speed train developed by British Rail during the 1970s and early 1980s. It was designed to run at up to 155 mph (250 km/h) on Britain's existing curving routes — especially the West Coast Main Line — without the cost of building new straight track, by leaning into curves to reduce passenger discomfort.

Development

Work began at the British Rail Research Division in Derby in the late 1960s. The experimental APT-E, powered by gas turbines, ran in 1972 and set a British rail speed record of 152.3 mph in 1975 (later raised to about 162 mph). The prototype electric APT-P (Class 370) followed, introducing articulated coaches, hydrokinetic brakes and an active tilt system controlled by sensors on the leading bogie.

Service and cancellation

APT-P entered limited passenger service between London Euston and Glasgow in December 1981. The launch was plagued by bad weather, technical failures and a hostile press; passengers also complained that the tilt system caused motion sickness ("tilt nausea"). The trains were withdrawn after only a few days, reintroduced quietly in 1982 and used on relief services until 1986, when the project was cancelled and most vehicles scrapped. One APT-P set is preserved at Crewe Heritage Centre.

Legacy

Although the APT itself failed, its tilt technology was sold to Fiat Ferroviaria and fed into the successful Pendolino family; decades later, Pendolino trains (Class 390) built with that lineage were introduced on the same West Coast route. The parallel, non-tilting InterCity 125 became Britain's enduring high-speed success of the era.
