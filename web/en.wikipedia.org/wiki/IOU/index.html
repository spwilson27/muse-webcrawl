IOU

An IOU (abbreviated from the phrase "I owe you") is usually an informal document acknowledging debt. An IOU differs from a promissory note in that an IOU is not a negotiable instrument and does not specify repayment terms such as the time of repayment. IOUs usually specify the debtor, the amount owed, and sometimes the creditor. IOUs may be signed or carry distinguishing marks or designs to ensure authenticity. In some business contexts, an IOU may be treated as a formal record for accounts receivable.

A simple handwritten IOU acknowledging a small personal debt.

Characteristics

Unlike a promissory note, an IOU merely acknowledges that a debt exists and does not contain a promise to pay. For that reason, IOUs are generally considered weaker in legal enforceability, although courts in many jurisdictions will admit a signed IOU as evidence of a debt or even treat it as implying an obligation to repay. A complete IOU typically states the date, the amount, the names of the parties, and the signature of the borrower.

History

The written abbreviation "IOU" is attested in English from the early 17th century (as I.O.U., a phonetic rendering of "I owe you"). Informal debt acknowledgements of this kind date back much further, to tally sticks and clay tablets used to record obligations in ancient commerce.

California IOUs

In July 2009, during a budget impasse, the U.S. state of California issued registered warrants — widely called "IOUs" — to pay many of its obligations. More than 450,000 warrants with a face value of about $2.6 billion were issued before the programme ended that September; major banks initially accepted them for deposit and then stopped, and the warrants traded at a discount on secondary markets.
