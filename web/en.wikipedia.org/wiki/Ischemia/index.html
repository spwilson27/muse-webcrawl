Ischemia

Ischemia (also spelled ischaemia) is a restriction in blood supply to tissues, causing a shortage of oxygen and glucose needed for cellular metabolism. It is generally caused by problems with blood vessels — atherosclerotic narrowing, blood clots (thrombosis or embolism), external compression, or vasospasm — and results in damage or dysfunction of the affected tissue. Unlike hypoxia, which can arise despite adequate perfusion, ischemia also impairs the removal of metabolic waste products.

Forms

Cardiac ischemia of the heart muscle produces angina pectoris and, if prolonged, myocardial infarction (heart attack). Cerebral ischemia underlies ischemic stroke, the most common type of stroke. Mesenteric ischemia affects the intestines and can progress rapidly to bowel infarction. Limb ischemia may be chronic, as in peripheral artery disease causing claudication, or acute, typically from an embolus, presenting with the classic "six Ps": pain, pallor, pulselessness, perishing cold, paraesthesia and paralysis.

Pathophysiology

Without oxygen, cells shift to anaerobic metabolism, generating lactate and acidosis; ATP depletion disables ion pumps, causing cellular swelling, calcium influx and activation of destructive enzymes. Prolonged ischemia leads to necrosis (infarction). Restoring blood flow, while essential, can itself cause reperfusion injury through oxidative stress and inflammation.

Treatment

Management aims to restore perfusion as quickly as possible: thrombolytic drugs, anticoagulation, angioplasty and stenting, embolectomy, or bypass surgery, together with treatment of the underlying cause and risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes and smoking.
