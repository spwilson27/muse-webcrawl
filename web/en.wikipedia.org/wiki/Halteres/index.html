Halteres (singular haltere) are small, knobbed structures found in two groups of insects. In the true flies (order Diptera) they are modified hindwings, reduced to stalked, club-shaped organs located behind the single pair of functional wings. In male Strepsiptera (twisted-wing parasites) the halteres are instead modified forewings, an example of convergent evolution.

Function

Halteres oscillate in antiphase with the wings during flight. When the fly's body rotates, Coriolis forces deflect the tips of the beating halteres, and arrays of campaniform sensilla at their bases detect the resulting strain, sending timing-coded signals to the neck and wing steering muscles. In effect, the halteres act as vibrating gyroscopes, supplying the rapid feedback that gives flies their extraordinary aerial agility. Experiments in which halteres are removed show that flies cannot maintain stable flight.

Development and evolution

In Drosophila, haltere development is controlled by the homeotic gene Ultrabithorax, which suppresses wing development in the third thoracic segment; mutations convert halteres into a second pair of wings — the famous four-winged fly. Some flies, such as crane flies, have especially conspicuous halteres, while in calyptrates a flap called the calypter may cover them.
