The poblano (Capsicum annuum) is a mild chili pepper originating in the state of Puebla, Mexico. Named after the demonym for Puebla's inhabitants, it is one of the most popular peppers in Mexican cuisine. When dried, the poblano is called an ancho (or, in some preparations, is confused with the pasilla, a different pepper).

Description

The poblano plant grows to about 60 cm (2 ft) tall and produces large, heart-shaped fruits typically 7.5–15 cm long and about 5–7.5 cm wide, with thick, dark green walls that turn deep red or brown when fully ripe. Poblanos are mild, usually measuring 1,000 to 2,000 Scoville heat units, though individual peppers vary and occasional pods are notably hotter. Their size and sturdy flesh make them ideal for stuffing.

Culinary uses

The signature dish is chiles en nogada, a seasonal specialty of Puebla in which roasted poblanos are stuffed with picadillo (a sweet-savoury meat and fruit filling), covered with a walnut cream sauce, and garnished with pomegranate seeds and parsley—displaying the green, white, and red of the Mexican flag. Poblanos are also the pepper of chile relleno, in which the roasted, peeled pepper is stuffed with cheese or meat, battered, and fried. Other preparations include rajas con crema (strips of roasted poblano in cream) and use in moles, salsas, soups, and tamales. Dried poblanos (anchos) are a staple dried chile, rehydrated for sauces such as mole poblano and adobos.

Cultivation and naming

Poblanos are grown commercially in Mexico and the southwestern United States. In the United States they are sometimes mislabelled "pasilla," particularly in California—an error arising from the use of "pasilla" for the dried chilaca in Mexico. "Poblano" can also refer, in Spanish, to a person from Puebla.
