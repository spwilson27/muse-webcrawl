The Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) is flight control software implemented on the Boeing 737 MAX. It was designed to push the aircraft's nose down automatically in certain manual-flight, high angle-of-attack conditions, so that the MAX would handle like the previous 737 generation and could be certified under the same type rating, minimising pilot retraining.

Design and background

The 737 MAX's larger CFM LEAP-1B engines, mounted further forward and higher than on earlier models, altered the aircraft's pitch behaviour at high angles of attack. MCAS compensated by commanding nose-down stabiliser trim when sensors indicated a high angle of attack with flaps up and autopilot off. In the original implementation it could activate repeatedly and relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor; it was not described in the flight crew operations manual, and many pilots were unaware of it before the first crash.

Accidents and grounding

Erroneous angle-of-attack data caused MCAS to force the nose down on Lion Air Flight 610 in October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 in March 2019; both aircraft crashed, killing 346 people in total. The worldwide 737 MAX fleet was grounded from March 2019. Investigations criticised Boeing's design and certification process and the FAA's oversight. In the redesigned system, MCAS compares both angle-of-attack sensors, activates only once per event, and has limited authority so pilots can override it. The FAA recertified the type in November 2020, and Boeing agreed to a $2.5 billion settlement with the U.S. Department of Justice in 2021.
