Báb

The Báb (born Siyyid ʿAlí Muḥammad Shírází, 20 October 1819 – 9 July 1850) was an Iranian religious leader who founded Bábism and is a central figure of the Baháʼí Faith. In 1844 he declared himself the Báb ("Gate"), claiming to be the channel to the Hidden Imam of Twelver Shi'a Islam and, in later writings, an independent prophet and bearer of a new revelation. His followers, the Bábís, spread rapidly in Persia, provoking a harsh response from the Qajar state and Shi'a clergy.

Life

Born in Shiraz to a merchant family claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad, ʿAlí Muḥammad worked in the family trade before turning to religious study, influenced by the millenarian Shaykhi school. On 22 May 1844 he declared his mission to Mullá Husayn, the first of his disciples known as the Letters of the Living. His principal writings include the Persian Bayán and the Qayyúmu'l-Asmáʼ, which abrogated Islamic law and announced the imminent coming of a greater figure, "He whom God shall make manifest."

Persecution and execution

After clashes between Bábís and authorities, the Báb was imprisoned in the fortresses of Maku and Chihriq in Azerbaijan. Following a trial in Tabriz, he was executed by firing squad on 9 July 1850 at the age of thirty. Thousands of his followers were killed in the ensuing persecutions. His remains were eventually transferred to Haifa, where the Shrine of the Báb on Mount Carmel now stands.

Legacy

Baháʼís regard the Báb as a Manifestation of God and the forerunner of Baháʼu'lláh, founder of the Baháʼí Faith, who was himself a Bábí. A minority of his followers, the Azalis, followed Subh-i-Azal instead. The Báb's teachings introduced a new calendar, progressive social principles, and the concept of successive divine revelations.
