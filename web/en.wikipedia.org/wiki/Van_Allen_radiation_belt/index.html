Van Allen radiation belt

A Van Allen radiation belt is a zone of energetic charged particles, mostly from the solar wind, captured and held by a planet's magnetosphere. Earth's two main belts were discovered in 1958 by James Van Allen using data from the Explorer 1 satellite — the first major discovery of the space age.

Structure

The inner belt extends roughly 1,000–12,000 km above Earth, containing high-energy protons; the outer belt, about 13,000–60,000 km, consists mainly of electrons, separated by a "slot region". Particles spiral along magnetic field lines, bouncing between mirror points near the poles and drifting around the planet. The South Atlantic Anomaly, where the inner belt dips lowest, troubles satellites and the International Space Station. NASA's Van Allen Probes (2012–2019) found a transient third belt and showed the belts swell and shrink with solar storms.

Implications

The belts are a radiation hazard for spacecraft electronics and crewed missions — Apollo astronauts crossed them quickly, receiving modest doses — and their dynamics are central to space weather. Other planets, notably Jupiter, have far stronger belts. Compare the Sun-linked puzzle of the solar neutrino problem.
