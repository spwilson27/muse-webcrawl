Envelope detector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An envelope detector (sometimes called a peak detector) is an electronic circuit that takes a relatively high-frequency signal as input and provides an output that is the envelope of the original signal — that is, a smooth curve tracing the extremes of the waveform. The circuit consists, in its simplest form, of a diode and a capacitor: the diode rectifies the signal and the capacitor charges to the peak of each cycle and discharges slowly through a load resistor between cycles.

A simple envelope detector: the output (red) follows the envelope of the modulated carrier.

Operation

When the input signal rises above the voltage stored on the capacitor, the diode conducts and the capacitor charges rapidly to the input's peak value. When the input falls below the capacitor voltage, the diode becomes reverse-biased and the capacitor discharges slowly through the load resistance R. If the time constant RC is chosen correctly — long compared with the carrier period but short compared with the period of the modulating signal — the output voltage tracks the peaks of the input, reproducing the envelope.

Envelope detectors are the standard means of demodulating amplitude-modulated (AM) signals: the audio information in an AM radio broadcast is carried in the envelope of the carrier wave, so recovering the envelope recovers the audio. Historically, the earliest radio receivers — crystal radios — used a crystal diode (such as a galena "cat's whisker") as an envelope detector, requiring no power supply.

Design considerations

If the RC time constant is too small, the output ripples excessively at the carrier frequency and modulation depth is lost. If it is too large, the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough to follow a rapidly falling envelope, causing diagonal clipping — a form of distortion in which the output decays along an exponential curve rather than tracing the signal's peaks, most severe at high modulation indices and high modulating frequencies. Practical designs choose RC as a compromise, roughly:

RC ≈ 1 / (2π fm) · √(1 − m²) / m

where fm is the highest modulating frequency and m is the modulation index.

Applications

  • Demodulation in AM radio receivers
  • Automatic gain control (AGC) circuits, where the envelope provides a measure of signal strength
  • Music production, where envelope followers extract amplitude contours from audio signals to control filters and effects
  • Radar and communications equipment for signal detection
