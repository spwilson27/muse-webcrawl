Balassa–Samuelson effect

The Balassa–Samuelson effect, proposed independently by Béla Balassa and Paul Samuelson in 1964, explains differences in real exchange rates and price levels between rich and poor countries. It holds that faster productivity growth in the tradable-goods sector of richer countries raises wages economy-wide, making non-tradable services relatively more expensive there.

Proposed1964
AuthorsBéla Balassa; Paul Samuelson
FieldInternational economics
RelatedPenn effect, purchasing power parity

Mechanism

Because tradable goods prices are set on world markets, higher tradable-sector productivity in a rich country supports higher wages. Labour mobility pushes wages up in the non-tradable sector too, even without productivity gains, so haircuts, housing services, and restaurant meals cost more where incomes are higher. Consequently price-level indices converted at market exchange rates show rich countries as systematically more expensive — the related Penn effect seen in purchasing power parity comparisons.

Implications

The effect predicts that fast-growing economies experience real exchange-rate appreciation, whether through nominal appreciation or higher inflation, complicating life in fixed-rate regimes such as the eurozone, where catching-up members like the Baltic states saw higher inflation. Empirical support is broad but debated in magnitude. See also real exchange rate.
