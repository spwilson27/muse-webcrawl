The Salton Sea is a shallow, landlocked, highly saline body of water in Southern California's Imperial and Coachella valleys, sitting on the San Andreas Fault at the northern end of the Imperial Valley. It is California's largest lake by surface area. The sea was created accidentally in 1905, when an irrigation canal from the Colorado River breached and the river flowed unchecked into the Salton Sink for nearly two years, flooding the basin that had historically held the much larger prehistoric Lake Cahuilla.

History and ecology

In the 1950s and 1960s the sea became a popular resort destination—the "Salton Riviera"—with marinas, yacht clubs and motels at communities such as Salton City and Bombay Beach. Rising salinity and agricultural runoff, including fertiliser-driven algal blooms and massive fish die-offs, drove decline from the 1970s. The sea became a critical habitat on the Pacific Flyway, supporting hundreds of bird species and vast numbers of migratory waterfowl, even as its fishery collapsed to hardy tilapia.

Shrinkage and restoration

Since water transfers under the 2003 Quantification Settlement Agreement reduced agricultural runoff—the sea's main inflow—it has shrunk rapidly, exposing toxic dust-laden playa that worsens air quality in some of California's poorest communities, while salinity has climbed past 60 grams per litre, far saltier than the ocean. California has funded mitigation and restoration projects, including habitat ponds and dust suppression, and the area's brines have attracted interest for lithium extraction, dubbed "Lithium Valley".
