Photometry (astronomy)

Photometry, in astronomy, is the technique of measuring the flux or intensity of light radiated by astronomical objects. This radiation is measured through a telescope using a photometer, an instrument made using a device that is sensitive to light, often now a CCD or CMOS detector. Photometry is used to determine the brightness of stars, asteroids, galaxies, and transients such as supernovae, and to detect exoplanets by the tiny dimming they cause when transiting their host stars.

Methods

Measurements are usually made through standard coloured filters defining a photometric system — most famously the UBV system (ultraviolet, blue, visual) introduced by Johnson and Morgan, and extensions such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey's ugriz bands. Combining measurements through several filters yields colours and colour indices, which indicate temperature and other physical properties. Aperture photometry sums the light within a circular aperture around a star; PSF photometry fits the point spread function, which is better in crowded fields. Differential photometry measures a target relative to nearby comparison stars, cancelling atmospheric variations and enabling the millimagnitude precision needed for exoplanet transit surveys and variable-star light curves.

Calibration

Raw instrumental magnitudes are corrected for atmospheric extinction and transformed onto a standard system using observations of standard stars. Historically, photometry was performed by eye and by photographic plates; photomultiplier tubes enabled precise photoelectric photometry in the mid-twentieth century, before electronic area detectors became standard. Space missions such as Kepler and TESS have carried out photometric surveys of hundreds of thousands of stars from above the atmosphere.
