The Quasi-Zenith Satellite System (QZSS), also known as Michibiki, is a Japanese satellite-based augmentation system and regional navigation satellite system. Its satellites occupy highly inclined, elliptical quasi-zenith orbits so that at least one satellite is near the zenith over Japan at all times, improving the availability of positioning signals in urban canyons and mountainous terrain where buildings and terrain block signals from the Global Positioning System (GPS).

The asymmetric figure-eight ground track of a quasi-zenith orbit keeps a satellite high over Japan for most of its orbital period.

History

The Japanese government authorized the system in 2002 as a joint public-private project. The first satellite, Michibiki (QZS-1), was launched in September 2010. Three further satellites were launched in 2017, and QZSS began official services on 1 November 2018 with a four-satellite constellation. A replacement for the first satellite, QZS-1R, was launched in October 2021, and Japan plans to expand the constellation to seven satellites for standalone positioning independent of GPS, with a longer-term goal of eleven satellites.

Services

QZSS broadcasts GPS-complementary signals and augmentation services, including the Centimetre Level Augmentation Service (CLAS), which delivers nationwide high-accuracy positioning in Japan, and a satellite report system for disaster and crisis management relaying emergency messages to areas where terrestrial networks are damaged.
