Self-defence in international law

Self-defence (international law)
Key sourceUN Charter, Article 51
Customary criteriaNecessity and proportionality (Caroline test)
RelatedCollective self-defence; Security Council authorization; pre-emptive self-defence debate

Self-defence in international law is the principal exception, alongside authorization by the United Nations Security Council, to the general prohibition on the use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state (Article 2(4) of the UN Charter). Article 51 of the Charter provides: "Nothing in the present Charter shall impair the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs against a Member of the United Nations, until the Security Council has taken measures necessary to maintain international peace and security." Measures taken in self-defence must be immediately reported to the Security Council.

Requirements

Under customary international law, force in self-defence must be necessary and proportionate — criteria traced to the 1837 Caroline affair and Daniel Webster's formulation that necessity must be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation." The International Court of Justice has confirmed these requirements in cases such as Nicaragua v. United States (1986), Oil Platforms (2003), and its advisory opinion on nuclear weapons (1996). In Nicaragua, the Court also held that only an "armed attack" of sufficient scale and effects triggers Article 51 — mere frontier incidents or assistance to rebels may not — and that collective self-defence requires the victim state's request.

Contested questions

Several questions remain disputed. Anticipatory or pre-emptive self-defence — action against an imminent attack before it occurs — is defended by some states under the "inherent right" language and the Caroline standard, but rejected by others who read Article 51 restrictively ("if an armed attack occurs"); preventive strikes against remote threats are widely considered unlawful. Self-defence against non-state actors, such as terrorist groups operating from another state's territory, expanded in practice after the September 11 attacks and Security Council Resolutions 1368 and 1373, with debates over attribution and the "unwilling or unable" doctrine. Other debates concern protection of nationals abroad, humanitarian intervention, and whether massive cyber operations can constitute armed attacks.
