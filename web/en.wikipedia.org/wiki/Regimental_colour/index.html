Regimental colour

Type: Military flag / standard
Used by: Infantry regiments, esp. British and Commonwealth armies
Companion: Sovereign's (King's/Queen's) Colour

The regimental colour is one of the pair of ceremonial flags — called colours — carried by infantry regiments of the British Army and other Commonwealth armies. Its companion is the Sovereign's colour (the King's Colour or Queen's Colour). Together the colours embody the honour, spirit and traditions of the regiment.

Description

The regimental colour is a flag in the regiment's facing colour (the colour of its historic uniform facings), bearing the regimental badge, title and battle honours. The Sovereign's colour is based on the Union Flag and likewise carries battle honours. Colours are made of silk, are richly embroidered, and are carried on pikes with gilt finials.

Role in battle and ceremony

Until the late nineteenth century, colours were carried into battle as rallying points for the regiment, defended by picked soldiers; the last British colours carried in action were at Laing's Nek in 1881. Today they are used on ceremonial occasions such as Trooping the Colour and are treated with great reverence: consecrated when presented, they are never destroyed when retired but "laid up" in a church or public building.

Battle honours

Regiments emblazon selected battle honours on their colours as a public record of distinguished service. The number permitted on each colour is limited by regulation, so regiments choose the honours of greatest significance.
