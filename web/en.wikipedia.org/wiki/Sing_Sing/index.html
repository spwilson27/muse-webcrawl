Sing Sing Correctional Facility, formally the Sing Sing Correctional Facility, is a maximum-security prison operated by the New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision in the village of Ossining, New York, on the east bank of the Hudson River about 30 miles (48 km) north of New York City. Opened in 1826, it is one of the oldest and most famous prisons in the United States; its name derives from the Native American Sint Sink people who inhabited the area, and it gave rise to the slang phrase "sent up the river" for imprisonment.

History

The prison was built under Elam Lynds, warden of Auburn Prison, who marched 100 convicts from Auburn to the site in 1825; the prisoners quarried marble on site and constructed the prison themselves, beginning an early regime of strict silence and hard labour known as the Auburn system. Sing Sing became known for harsh discipline, but also for reform under warden Lewis Lawes (1920–1941), who improved conditions, education and recreation. Its electric chair, nicknamed "Old Sparky", was used for 614 executions between 1891 and 1963, including those of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg in 1953.

Notable inmates and escapes

Famous inmates have included bank robber Willie Sutton, who escaped in 1932; serial killer Albert Fish, executed there in 1936; and organised-crime figure Charles "Lucky" Luciano. The prison's fame in popular culture — in gangster films of the 1930s and the phrase "the Big House" — made it a byword for American imprisonment.

The facility today

Modern Sing Sing houses roughly 1,500–1,700 men in several cell blocks on a 55-acre site. The original 1825 cell block, among the oldest surviving prison structures in the United States, has been the subject of museum proposals. Educational and vocational programmes, including college courses run with nearby institutions, operate at the facility.
