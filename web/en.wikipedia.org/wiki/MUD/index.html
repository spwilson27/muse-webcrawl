A MUD (originally multi-user dungeon, later also multi-user dimension or multi-user domain) is a multiplayer real-time virtual world, usually text-based or storyboarded. MUDs combine elements of role-playing games, hack and slash, player versus player, interactive fiction, and online chat. Players can read or view descriptions of rooms, objects, other players, and non-player characters, perform actions, and interact with each other and the world using text commands that resemble a natural language, such as north, take sword, or say hello.

History

The first MUD, retroactively called MUD1, was created in 1978 by Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle at the University of Essex, inspired by text adventures such as Zork and by Dungeons & Dragons. When the university connected its network to ARPANET in 1980, MUD1 became arguably the first internet multiplayer game. Derivatives spread widely in the late 1980s and 1990s: TinyMUD (1989) emphasised social interaction and world-building over combat; LPMud (1989) provided a programmable object-oriented engine; DikuMUD (1991) became the basis of many combat-oriented games; and MUSHes, MOOs and MUCKs diversified the family. Bartle's later taxonomy of player types (achievers, explorers, socialisers, killers) originated in MUD culture.

Gameplay and culture

Traditional gameplay loops involve exploring a text-described world, fighting monsters, collecting treasure and experience points, and solving quests, alone or in groups. Persistent characters and economies prefigured those of graphical MMORPGs. MUD communities developed rich conventions: wizards (administrators and builders), ranked players, in-game weddings and funerals, and extensive player-created areas and code. Some MUDs operated for decades; Achaea, Dreams of Divine Lands (1997) was notable for a working in-game economy and politics, and The Eternal City and others continue today.

Legacy

MUDs are the direct ancestors of modern MMORPGs such as EverQuest and World of Warcraft, whose designers frequently cite MUD experience; the term "mob" for a computer-controlled creature comes from MUD's "mobile object". They also influenced virtual worlds, online community design, and academic internet studies, being among the first persistent social spaces on networks.
