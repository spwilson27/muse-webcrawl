Sedimentary basin

Placeholder image: layered sedimentary strata accumulated in a subsiding basin.

A sedimentary basin is a region of the Earth's crust that has subsided over long periods of geological time, allowing sediment to accumulate to thicknesses of kilometres. Sedimentary basins are the principal sites for the formation and preservation of sedimentary rocks and are of great economic importance because they host most of the world's oil, natural gas, and coal deposits, as well as groundwater aquifers.

Formation

Basins form by several mechanisms of lithospheric subsidence, including stretching of the crust during rifting, cooling and thermal contraction after rifting, flexure of the lithosphere under the load of mountain ranges (producing foreland basins), and dynamic effects of mantle flow. As the basin subsides, rivers, seas, and lakes deposit sediment that buries earlier layers; continued burial compacts the sediment and converts it to rock, and may mature organic matter into petroleum.

Types

  • Rift basins formed by crustal extension, e.g. the North Sea.
  • Passive margin basins on subsiding continental edges, e.g. the US Gulf Coast.
  • Foreland basins beside mountain belts, e.g. the Persian Gulf region.
  • Intracratonic basins in continental interiors, e.g. the Williston and Michigan basins.
  • Forearc and back-arc basins associated with subduction zones.
