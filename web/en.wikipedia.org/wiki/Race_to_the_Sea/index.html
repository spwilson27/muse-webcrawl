The Race to the Sea (French: Course à la mer; German: Wettlauf zum Meer) is the name given to the sequence of battles and manoeuvres on the Western Front of the First World War between mid-September and mid-October 1914, after the German retreat from the Marne and the Allied counter-advance to the Aisne. Rather than a deliberate dash for the coast, it was a series of reciprocal attempts by the French, British, and German armies to outflank each other's northern flank, each failure extending the front further north-west until it reached the North Sea coast of Belgium.

Course of events

After the First Battle of the Aisne (13–28 September) settled into trench fighting, each side probed northward for an open flank. Fighting spread through Picardy, Artois, and Flanders: battles at Albert, Arras, and along the Lys, as the British Expeditionary Force redeployed from the Aisne to Flanders to shorten its supply lines from the Channel ports. The Belgian army, having fallen back from Antwerp (which fell on 10 October), held a final strip of national territory behind the river Yser, opening the sluices at Nieuwpoort to flood the coastal plain during the Battle of the Yser (16–31 October). To the south, the desperate First Battle of Ypres (19 October – 22 November) closed the last gap.

Consequences

When the "race" ended, a continuous line of trenches ran some 475 miles from the Belgian coast to the Swiss frontier, and the war of movement in the West was over. The resulting stalemate defined the Western Front for the next four years.
