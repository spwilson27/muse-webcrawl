Extradition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Extradition, in international law, is an act by which one state delivers to another an individual accused or convicted of committing a crime in the other state. It is normally governed by bilateral treaties (or multilateral conventions) and domestic statutes, and by principles including double criminality (the act must be criminal in both states), specialty (the person may be tried only for the offences for which surrender was granted) and bars on extradition for political offences, for offences punishable by death without assurances, or where trial or punishment would violate human rights.

Practice

Requests are made diplomatically and decided through judicial and executive phases; high-profile contests — publishers, hackers, financiers and dissidents among them — test the system's balances, as in the long-running Assange proceedings (ending in a 2024 plea agreement) and litigation over prison conditions and forum bars. Within the EU, the European Arrest Warrant (2004) replaced extradition between member states with streamlined judicial surrender, now qualified by rule-of-law and detention-condition case law. Non-extradition of nationals by some civil-law states, and the absence of treaties (a recurring refuge issue), keep the field politically charged, while "extraordinary rendition" — transfer outside legal process — is widely condemned as unlawful.
