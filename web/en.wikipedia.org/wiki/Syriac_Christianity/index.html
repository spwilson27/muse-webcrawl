Syriac Christianity

Syriac Christianity (Syriac: mšiḥāyūṯā suryāyṯā) is the tradition of Eastern Christianity that developed in the Syriac-speaking world of the Near East — centred on Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa) and Antioch — and uses Classical Syriac, a literary dialect of Aramaic, as its liturgical and theological language. It is distinguished by its rich literature, including the Peshitta Bible, the poetry of Ephrem the Syrian, and an extensive tradition of scholarship and translation that preserved and transmitted Greek learning. Through missionary activity, Syriac Christianity spread along the Silk Road to Persia, India (the Saint Thomas Christians) and Tang-dynasty China.

Branches

Following the Christological controversies of the 5th century, Syriac Christianity divided broadly into two traditions. The East Syriac tradition of the Church of the East (historically, though controversially, labelled "Nestorian") developed in the Persian Empire, centred on Seleucia-Ctesiphon and the School of Nisibis; its modern heirs include the Assyrian Church of the East, the Ancient Church of the East and the Chaldean Catholic Church. The West Syriac tradition, associated with the miaphysite (Oriental Orthodox) Syriac Orthodox Church, was reorganised by Jacob Baradaeus in the 6th century and centred on Antioch and monasteries such as Mor Gabriel in Tur Abdin; its heirs include the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Syriac Catholic Church and, in India, the Malankara churches. Maronite tradition also has Syriac roots.

History

Christianity reached Edessa very early — tradition attributes its founding to the apostle Thomas and his disciples Addai and Mari. Bardaisan, Tatian (author of the Diatessaron gospel harmony) and Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306–373) shaped early Syriac theology and hymnography. Syriac scholars later became key translators of Greek philosophy and medicine into Arabic in Abbasid Baghdad. The Church of the East's missions planted churches across Central Asia; the Xi'an Stele (781) records Christianity's arrival in China in 635. Mongol-era upheavals, Timur's massacres, and, in the 20th century, the Assyrian genocide (Sayfo) of 1915 and the Simele massacre of 1933 devastated Syriac communities, driving a large diaspora to Europe, the Americas and Australia, where Syriac churches and the Aramaic language are maintained.

Liturgy and language

East and West Syriac rites have distinct anaphoras (eucharistic prayers), including the ancient Anaphora of Addai and Mari, notable for lacking an institution narrative in its oldest form. Classical Syriac remains the liturgical language, written in three scripts (Estrangela, Serto and the East Syriac script), while vernacular Neo-Aramaic languages such as Turoyo and Suret are spoken by many adherents.
