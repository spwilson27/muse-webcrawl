Executive Order 10340

Executive Order 10340
Signed by:Harry S. Truman
Date:April 8, 1952
Subject:Seizure of the steel mills
Result:Invalidated in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer

Executive Order 10340, formally titled Directing the Secretary of Commerce to Take Possession of and Operate the Plants and Facilities of Certain Steel Companies, was signed by United States President Harry S. Truman on April 8, 1952. Facing a nationwide strike by the United Steelworkers of America during the Korean War, and unwilling to use the injunction machinery of the Taft–Hartley Act, Truman ordered Secretary of Commerce Charles W. Sawyer to seize and operate the nation's steel mills, arguing that a stoppage in steel production would endanger the war effort and national defence.

Background

The dispute arose from a stalemate over wages: the Wage Stabilization Board recommended an increase that the steel companies would accept only if the government authorised compensating price rises, which the administration refused. As the strike deadline arrived, Truman addressed the nation by radio and television and announced the seizure, asserting broad inherent executive power under the Constitution and his role as Commander in Chief. The steel companies immediately sued, and federal troops and managers took over the mills pending the courts' decision.

Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer

On June 2, 1952, the Supreme Court held 6–3 that the seizure was unconstitutional: the President had neither a statutory grant of authority nor a constitutional power to seize private property in these circumstances, since Congress had considered and rejected seizure authority when passing Taft–Hartley. Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion confined presidential power to its constitutional and statutory sources. Justice Robert H. Jackson's concurrence — proposing the famous three-part framework analysing presidential action according to whether Congress has authorised, been silent on, or forbidden it — has become the most influential statement of the law of presidential power and is routinely invoked in separation-of-powers cases. Truman complied at once; the mills returned to their owners, the steelworkers struck for over seven weeks, and a settlement close to the original board recommendation ended the dispute.

Compare Executive Order 6102 (1933), Franklin D. Roosevelt's order restricting private gold ownership.
