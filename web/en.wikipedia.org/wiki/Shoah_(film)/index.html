Shoah (film)

Shoah is a 1985 French documentary film about the Holocaust, directed by Claude Lanzmann. At over nine hours long, the film presents Lanzmann's interviews with survivors, witnesses and perpetrators during visits to German Holocaust sites across Poland.

Content

The film divides into two eras. It includes testimony from survivors including Simon Srebnik, a survivor of Chełmno, and Mordechaï Podchlebnik; Jewish prisoners who worked in gas chambers; Richard Glazar and Rudolf Vrba; and bystanders and perpetrators such as Franz Suchomel, an SS officer at Treblinka, and historian Raul Hilberg. Lanzmann deliberately used no archival footage and no musical score, relying on present-day landscapes and faces.

Shoah – the Hebrew word for "catastrophe" – was chosen over "Holocaust" by Lanzmann. Filming took place over 11 years, in Poland, Israel, the United States and elsewhere, and editing the 350 hours of footage took five years.

Reception and legacy

Premiered in Paris in 1985, Shoah was widely acclaimed as a masterpiece of documentary cinema, though its length limited theatrical exhibition; it was broadcast on television in many countries. Critic Roger Ebert called it "one of the noblest films ever made." It won numerous awards including the Caligari Prize at Berlin and a BAFTA. Lanzmann later released related films from unused material, including Sobibor, October 14, 1943, 4 p.m. (2001) and The Last of the Unjust (2013).
