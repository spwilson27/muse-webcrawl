Pump-jet

A pump-jet, also called a hydrojet or water jet, in its strict naval sense is a marine propulsion system in which a ducted rotor (impeller) and stator accelerate water through a nozzle to produce thrust. The term is especially applied to the shrouded propulsors used on submarines and torpedoes, where a rotor and stator are enclosed in an annular duct. More loosely, "pump-jet" is used interchangeably with waterjet propulsion of the kind fitted to fast ferries and personal watercraft.

Design

A submarine pump-jet consists of a rotating impeller with multiple blades followed by fixed stator vanes, all enclosed in a duct. The stator removes the swirl imparted by the rotor, converting rotational energy into axial thrust, and the duct shapes the inflow. Because the blades operate inside the duct with carefully controlled tip clearances, cavitation inception is delayed, and the propulsor can develop high thrust at low rotational speed.

Advantages and disadvantages

The principal advantage is quietness: by suppressing tip vortices and delaying cavitation, a pump-jet greatly reduces the acoustic signature of a submarine compared with an open skewed propeller. Pump-jets also give good maneuverability and protect the rotor from damage. Their disadvantages are greater weight, cost, and mechanical complexity, and lower efficiency than an open propeller at low speeds in some applications.

Applications

Pump-jets are fitted to many modern nuclear submarines, including the British Trafalgar and Astute classes, the French Barracuda class, and the United States Seawolf and Virginia classes, as well as heavyweight torpedoes such as the Spearfish. Related waterjet units propel high-speed ferries, patrol boats, and jet skis, where the intake draws water from beneath the hull and expels it through a steerable nozzle.
