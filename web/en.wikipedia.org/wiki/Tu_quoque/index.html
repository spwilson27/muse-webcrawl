Tu quoque

Tu quoque (Latin for "you also", pronounced [tuː ˈkwoːkwe]) is a type of informal fallacy — a form of the ad hominem argument — that attempts to discredit an opponent's position by charging them with hypocrisy: pointing out that the opponent has acted inconsistently with, or is also guilty of, the very thing under discussion. Instead of addressing the merits of an argument, it answers criticism with criticism, as in "How can you tell me not to smoke when you smoked for years yourself?"

Structure

The fallacy has the general form:

  1. Person A claims that X is true (or that Y is wrong).
  2. Person B points out that A's own actions (or past claims) are inconsistent with the claim.
  3. Therefore, B concludes, A's claim is false or can be dismissed.

The conclusion does not follow: a claim can be true even if the person making it is a hypocrite. A doctor who smokes gives no less sound advice in saying smoking is harmful. For this reason tu quoque is classed with the relevance fallacies.

Related notions

The everyday expression of the fallacy is the retort "look who's talking" or the fallacy of "two wrongs make a right". In political discourse a related rhetorical strategy is "whataboutism", in which a state or speaker answers criticism by pointing to comparable faults of the critic — a technique associated particularly with Soviet responses to Western criticism during the Cold War ("And you are lynching Negroes"). A legitimate, narrower use of consistency arguments exists in ethics and law — for instance, testing a universal moral rule by applying it to the person who proposes it — but this challenges the rule's application rather than pretending to refute a factual claim.
