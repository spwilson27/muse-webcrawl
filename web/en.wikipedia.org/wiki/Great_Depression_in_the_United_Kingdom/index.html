Great Depression in the United Kingdom

Unemployed workers in Britain during the Depression.

The Great Depression in the United Kingdom was a period of severe economic downturn during the 1930s. Although the depression in Britain was somewhat less severe in output terms than in the United States or Germany — helped by Britain's relatively early departure from the gold standard in 1931 — it produced prolonged mass unemployment that devastated the industrial regions of the north of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, while the south-east and Midlands recovered sooner on the strength of new industries and a housing boom.

Background and course

Britain's staple export industries — coal, cotton textiles, shipbuilding and heavy engineering — had struggled throughout the 1920s after the return to the gold standard at the pre-war parity in 1925 left the pound overvalued. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 collapsed world trade and drove unemployment from around one million to a peak of nearly three million in 1932, roughly 22 per cent of the insured workforce. The crisis of 1931 forced the abandonment of gold, a cut in unemployment benefit and public salaries, and the formation of a National Government under Ramsay MacDonald. Devaluation, cheap money at 2 per cent Bank Rate, and protective tariffs after the Import Duties Act 1932 underpinned a gradual recovery.

Social impact

In the worst-hit towns, such as Jarrow in County Durham — where the closure of Palmer's shipyard pushed unemployment towards 70 per cent — long-term joblessness brought malnutrition, poor housing and lasting community trauma. The Jarrow March of 1936, in which 200 men walked to London to present a petition, became the emblematic protest of the decade, alongside the hunger marches organised by the National Unemployed Workers' Movement. The hated household means test for unemployment assistance provoked widespread resentment. By contrast, falling prices raised real incomes for those in work, and suburbs spread around London and Birmingham as car, electrical and aircraft industries grew — a regional divide that left a deep mark on British politics and culture.
