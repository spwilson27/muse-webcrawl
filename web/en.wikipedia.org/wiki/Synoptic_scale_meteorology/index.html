Synoptic scale meteorology

Synoptic scale meteorology is the study of weather systems on the synoptic scale — horizontal length scales of the order of 1,000 kilometres (about 620 miles) or more, corresponding to the scale of typical mid-latitude depressions. It derives its name from the practice of analyzing simultaneous ("synoptic") observations plotted on weather maps, the foundation of weather forecasting since the telegraph made near-real-time data collection possible in the 19th century.

Systems studied

  • Extratropical cyclones and the anticyclones associated with them, including their frontal structure (cold fronts, warm fronts, occluded fronts).
  • High- and low-pressure systems and the surface weather analysis of isobars.
  • Upper-air patterns: troughs, ridges, and the polar-front jet stream that steers surface systems.
  • Tropical cyclones, which are at the large end of the synoptic scale.

Methods

Synoptic meteorologists analyze surface and upper-air charts, satellite and radar imagery, and numerical weather prediction output. Classical theory — the Norwegian cyclone model developed by the Bergen School around 1920, and later baroclinic instability theory — explains the life cycle of mid-latitude storms that dominate day-to-day weather over the continents. Synoptic-scale features typically evolve over timescales of days, between the mesoscale (tens to hundreds of kilometres — thunderstorms, squall lines) and the planetary scale (Rossby waves, the general circulation).
