Musical motif

In music, a motif (or motive) is a short musical phrase, a salient recurring figure, or a succession of notes that has some special importance in, or is characteristic of, a composition. A motif is generally the smallest structural unit possessing thematic identity — "the smallest identifiable melodic or rhythmic idea" — and is used as building material for larger themes and entire movements.

The famous four-note motif of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony.

Characteristics

A motif is defined by its intervallic and rhythmic profile rather than by exact pitches: it can be repeated transposed, inverted, augmented, diminished, or fragmented while remaining recognisable. Composers develop motifs through sequence, variation, and combination — a technique central to the Classical style, whose thematic economy analysts from Heinrich Schenker to Arnold Schoenberg (who called it "developing variation") have emphasised.

Famous examples

The most famous motif in Western music is the opening of Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 (1808): three short notes and a long one (short–short–short–long), which recurs throughout the symphony and unifies its four movements. In opera, Richard Wagner developed the leitmotif — a motif associated with a person, place, object, or idea, transformed as the drama unfolds — to a high art in the Ring cycle. The B-A-C-H motif (B♭–A–C–B natural in German notation) has been used by many composers as a tribute to J. S. Bach.

Motif vs. theme

Whereas a theme is a more complete musical idea, often a full melody, a motif is a fragment — a "germ cell" from which themes are built. A phrase may be composed of several motifs, and motifs may be combined contrapuntally or stated simultaneously in different voices.
