The Theogony (Greek: Theogonia, 'the genealogy or birth of the gods') is a poem by Hesiod (c. 8th–7th century BC) describing the origins and genealogies of the Greek gods, composed c. 730–700 BC. It is written in the epic dialect of Homeric Greek and contains 1,022 lines.

Summary

The poem begins with an invocation to the Muses of Mount Helicon. After a cosmogony beginning with Chaos, Gaia (Earth), Tartarus and Eros, it traces successive generations: the Titans, led by Cronus, and then the Olympians led by Zeus, who defeats the Titans in the Titanomachy and the monster Typhon.

Successions

Three successive rulers of the cosmos—Uranus (Sky), Cronus, and Zeus—each overthrow their predecessor. The poem catalogues Zeus's marriages and offspring, the distribution of honours among the gods, and the origins of Prometheus, Pandora, and the races of mankind, themes Hesiod expands in Works and Days.

Influence

The Theogony is one of the most important early sources for Greek mythology, systematising scattered traditions into a coherent genealogy that shaped later literature from Homer to the tragedians and beyond. Parallels with Near Eastern succession myths, such as the Hittite Kumarbi cycle, are widely noted by scholars.
