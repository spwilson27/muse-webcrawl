Thought-terminating cliché

A thought-terminating cliché (also known as a semantic stop-sign, a thought-stopper, or a cliché thinking term) is a form of loaded language, commonly a catchphrase or proverb, that is intended to end an argument or debate by discouraging further critical thought. The term was popularised by psychiatrist Robert Jay Lifton in his 1961 book Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism, where he described the "loaded language" of totalitarian environments.

Thought-terminating cliché
Coined byRobert Jay Lifton (1961)
FieldRhetoric, psychology, logical fallacies

Description

Lifton wrote that the language of totalist environments is characterised by the thought-terminating cliché: "The most far-reaching and complex of human problems are compressed into brief, highly reductive, definitive-sounding phrases, easily memorized and easily expressed." Such phrases function as "semantic stop-signs" that end analysis by appearing to answer a question while in fact dismissing it.

Examples

Common examples include "it is what it is", "everything happens for a reason", "boys will be boys", "that's just your opinion", "agree to disagree", and slogans such as "blood and soil" or "the Party is always right" in ideological contexts. In propaganda and cult settings, stock phrases may be used to deflect criticism of a leader or doctrine, for example by labelling doubts as the product of a member's own spiritual failings.

Analysis

Writers on rhetoric and critical thinking treat thought-terminating clichés as related to loaded language and to informal fallacies such as thought-stopping appeals to ridicule or dismissive hand-waving. Because the phrases are brief, familiar and socially acceptable, they can close down discussion without the listener noticing that no argument has been answered. Lifton considered the phenomenon a mechanism of "milieu control" in totalist groups, but the term is now applied widely to political debate, corporate jargon, and everyday conversation.
