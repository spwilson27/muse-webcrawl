Tesseract

A three-dimensional projection of a rotating tesseract (placeholder)

In geometry, the tesseract is the four-dimensional analogue of the cube: just as a cube is formed by extruding a square into a third dimension, a tesseract is formed by extruding a cube into a fourth. It is one of the six convex regular 4-polytopes, and is also called an 8-cell, octachoron, or four-dimensional hypercube.

Geometry

The tesseract is bounded by eight cubical cells. It can be unfolded into eight cubes in three-dimensional space, just as a cube can be unfolded into six squares in two dimensions; such unfoldings are called nets, and the tesseract has 261 distinct nets. The standard tesseract in Euclidean 4-space is the convex hull of the points (±1, ±1, ±1, ±1).

Projections

Because humans cannot perceive four spatial dimensions directly, tesseracts are usually studied through projections and cross-sections. A common projection shows a smaller cube nested inside a larger one, with corresponding vertices joined — the three-dimensional "shadow" of the four-dimensional object. Rotating the tesseract in four dimensions produces the familiar tumbling motion in such animations. Parallel projections give a rhombic dodecahedron envelope, while cell-first and vertex-first projections give other characteristic solids.

History and culture

The word "tesseract" was coined by Charles Howard Hinton in 1888 in his book A New Era of Thought, from the Greek téssereis aktînes ("four rays"), referring to the four edges through each vertex. The shape appears widely in fiction, from Madeleine L'Engle's A Wrinkle in Time to the Marvel Cinematic Universe, and the net of a tesseract forms the cross in Salvador Dalí's painting Crucifixion (Corpus Hypercubus).
