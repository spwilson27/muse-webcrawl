Sidekick

A sidekick is a close companion or subordinate of a protagonist in a story, who appears as a supporting character subordinate in importance to the main character. Sidekicks provide companionship, comic relief, expositional dialogue, and an audience surrogate, and they allow the hero to voice thoughts aloud rather than in soliloquy. The term originally referred to a type of pocket in a pair of trousers, and later to a close friend or "pal", in late 19th- and early 20th-century slang.

History and examples

The sidekick is one of the oldest character functions in literature: Enkidu accompanies Gilgamesh, Patroclus fights beside Achilles, and Sancho Panza serves as squire and foil to Don Quixote. In detective fiction, Dr. Watson narrates the adventures of Sherlock Holmes, asking the questions readers would ask; in superhero comics the device flourished from 1940 onward — Robin the Boy Wonder (Batman), Bucky Barnes (Captain America), and Jimmy Olsen (Superman's pal) — aimed partly at giving young readers a character their own age to identify with. The Lone Ranger had Tonto; radio and film westerns gave comic sidekicks a stock role; and modern examples include Samwise Gamgee in The Lord of the Rings, Ron Weasley and Hermione Granger in Harry Potter, and companions in the long-running series Doctor Who.

Functions and criticism

Sidekicks may be less capable than the hero, providing contrast that heightens the hero's competence, or may supply specialized skills the hero lacks. Villains have sidekicks too — henchmen and lieutenants who serve as secondary antagonists. Critics have discussed the colonial and racial overtones of some classic pairings, in which the sidekick is a subordinate of a different race, class, or species, and modern writers frequently subvert the trope by making the sidekick the narrator, the smarter partner, or the eventual hero of a spin-off. The sidekick is closely related to, but distinct from, the confidant, the foil, and the comic relief character.
