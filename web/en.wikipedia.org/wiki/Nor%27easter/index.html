Nor'easter

Nor'easter
Areas affectedEast Coast of the United States, Atlantic Canada
SeasonTypically September to April, peaking in winter
EffectsHeavy rain or snow, hurricane-force gusts, coastal flooding, beach erosion

A nor'easter (also northeaster) is a strong area of low pressure that develops along the East Coast of North America and typically brings heavy precipitation, gale-force winds and rough seas to the northeastern United States and Atlantic Canada. The name derives from the strong northeasterly winds that blow in from the ocean ahead of the storm.

Nor'easters most often form when cold Arctic air sweeping southward meets warm air over the Gulf Stream offshore. The sharp temperature contrast fuels rapid intensification, and many of the most destructive storms in American history — blizzards, floods and coastal disasters — have been nor'easters.

Formation

Two classic storm tracks are recognized. In the "Miller Type-A" pattern, a low forms over the Gulf of Mexico or off the Southeast coast and rides northeastward just offshore. In the "Miller Type-B" pattern, a low over the Ohio Valley weakens while a new coastal low develops near the Delmarva Peninsula and becomes dominant. When the storm deepens rapidly — a process called bombogenesis — impacts can rival those of a hurricane.

Impacts

Depending on the season and the temperature profile, a nor'easter can produce flooding rains, heavy wet snow, or a crippling blizzard, together with prolonged onshore winds that pile water against the coast, causing storm surge, dune erosion and damage to shore communities. Notable examples include the Great Blizzard of 1888, the Ash Wednesday Storm of 1962, the Storm of the Century in 1993, and the January 2015 and 2016 blizzards.
