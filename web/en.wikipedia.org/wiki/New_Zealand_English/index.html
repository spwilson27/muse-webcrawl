New Zealand English (NZE) is the variety of English spoken and written by most New Zealanders. Its accent is distinctive within the English-speaking world, characterised by raised short front vowels (so that 'fish and chips' is jocularly rendered as 'fush and chups'), and its vocabulary includes many borrowings from te reo Māori.

Phonology

NZE is generally non-rhotic, except for a rhotic enclave in Southland and parts of Otago (the 'Southland burr'). Its most famous feature is the raising of the short front vowels: the TRAP vowel approaches DRESS, DRESS approaches KIT, and KIT is centralised, producing the stereotyped 'fush and chups' effect noted since the early 20th century.

Other features include the NEAR–SQUARE merger (so 'beer' and 'bear' sound alike for many speakers), a dark 'l', and intonation with rising terminals, especially in informal speech.

Vocabulary

NZE includes words from te reo Māori such as whānau (family), kai (food), haka, kiwi and bach (holiday home — also called a crib in the south), alongside terms like dairy (corner shop), jandals (flip-flops), togs (swimsuit), tramping (hiking) and 'sweet as'. Use of Māori words in everyday and official English has grown markedly.

History

English arrived with colonisation from the 1840s, and a distinct New Zealand accent was recognisable by the early 20th century, developing from the mix of British, Irish, Scottish and Australian inputs. Linguists such as those at the University of Canterbury's Origins of New Zealand English (ONZE) project have traced its formation using recordings of early settlers. NZE is close to Australian English but differs in vowel quality and some vocabulary.
