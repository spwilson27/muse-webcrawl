John Jordan "Buck" O'Neil Jr. (November 13, 1911 – October 6, 2006) was an American first baseman and manager in the Negro American League, mostly with the Kansas City Monarchs. After his playing days, he worked as a scout and became the first African American coach in Major League Baseball. In his later years he became a popular and renowned speaker and interview subject, helping to renew widespread interest in the Negro leagues, and played a major role in establishing the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum in Kansas City, Missouri. He was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame in 2022 as an executive.

Early life

O'Neil was born in Carrabelle, Florida, and grew up in Sarasota, where he was denied the opportunity to attend high school because of segregation. He worked in the celery fields and as a shoeshine boy before earning an opportunity to attend Edward Waters College in Jacksonville through a football scholarship. His nickname "Buck" came from the co-owner of the Miami Giants, Buck O'Neal, whom he briefly played for in semi-pro ball.

Playing career

O'Neil signed with the Memphis Red Sox for the 1937 season and was traded to the Kansas City Monarchs in 1938. A smooth-fielding first baseman and a steady line-drive hitter, he helped the Monarchs win several pennants and the 1942 Negro World Series. His career was interrupted by service in the United States Navy during World War II. In 1948 he became player-manager of the Monarchs and managed the club through its final glory years, tutoring future major leaguers such as Ernie Banks and Elston Howard.

Scouting and coaching

After the Monarchs declined, O'Neil became a scout for the Chicago Cubs, signing future Hall of Famers Lou Brock and Billy Williams. In 1962 the Cubs named him a coach, making him the first Black coach in the American or National leagues. He later scouted for the Kansas City Royals and was named Midwest scout of the year in 1988.

Later life and legacy

O'Neil gained national fame through Ken Burns's 1994 documentary Baseball, in which his storytelling charmed millions. He co-founded the Negro Leagues Baseball Museum and worked tirelessly to secure pensions and recognition for former Negro league players. In 2006 he fell one vote short of election to the Hall of Fame in a special election for Negro league figures; he responded graciously and delivered a beloved speech at the induction ceremony for the seventeen who were elected. He died later that year. In December 2021 a special committee elected him to the Hall of Fame, and he was inducted posthumously in July 2022.
