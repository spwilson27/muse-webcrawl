Amputation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amputation is the removal of a limb by trauma, medical illness, or surgery. As a surgical measure, it is used to control pain or a disease process in the affected limb, such as malignancy or gangrene. In some cases, it is carried out on individuals as a preventive surgery for such problems.

Common causes: diabetes and vascular disease, trauma, cancer, infection
Types: above-knee, below-knee, toe, foot, transradial, transhumeral, disarticulation

Causes

The leading cause of amputation in developed countries is poor circulation, most often from diabetes and peripheral arterial disease, accounting for the majority of lower-limb amputations. Other causes include severe trauma, cancerous tumours of bone or muscle, serious infection such as gas gangrene, frostbite, and congenital limb deficiency. Traumatic amputations occur in road accidents, industrial injuries, and warfare.

Procedure and recovery

The surgeon removes the diseased tissue while preserving as much healthy limb length, viable skin, and joint function as possible, fashioning the residual limb (stump) to bear a prosthesis. Rehabilitation involves wound healing, physiotherapy, and prosthetic fitting. Many amputees experience phantom limb sensations or phantom pain in the missing limb, treated with medication, mirror therapy, and graded motor imagery.

History

Amputation is one of the oldest surgical procedures; evidence of it dates to Neolithic times, and prosthetic devices such as the wooden Cairo toe are known from ancient Egypt. Mortality fell dramatically with the introduction of tourniquets, anaesthesia, and antisepsis in the 19th century.
