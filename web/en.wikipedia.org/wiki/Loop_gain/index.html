Loop gain

A feedback loop; its loop gain is the product of the forward gain and the feedback factor

In electronics and control system theory, loop gain is the sum of the gain, expressed as a ratio or in decibels, around a feedback loop. Feedback loops are widely used in electronics in amplifiers and oscillators, and more generally in electronic and biological control systems to control systems.

Definition

For a system with forward-path gain A and feedback factor β, the loop gain is Aβ (with sign conventions varying by author): the gain a signal experiences in travelling once around the closed loop. The closed-loop gain of a negative-feedback amplifier is A / (1 + Aβ); when the loop gain is large, the closed-loop gain approaches 1/β, set almost entirely by the feedback network and therefore stable and predictable.

Stability

Loop gain as a function of frequency determines stability. By the Barkhausen criterion, a loop will oscillate at a frequency where the loop gain has magnitude one and a total phase shift of 360° (for positive feedback). Nyquist and Bode analysis of the loop gain's magnitude and phase gives gain and phase margins — measures of how close a negative-feedback system is to instability — and designers add compensation to keep those margins adequate. The concept is equally applied outside electronics, for example to the chemical control of breathing, where a high loop gain in respiratory control is associated with unstable, periodic breathing patterns.
