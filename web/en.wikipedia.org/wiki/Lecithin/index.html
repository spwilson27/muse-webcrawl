Lecithin (from the Greek lekithos, λεκιθος, "egg yolk") is a generic term to designate any group of yellow-brownish fatty substances occurring in animal and plant tissues which are amphiphilic — they attract both water and fatty substances (and so are both hydrophilic and lipophilic) — and are used for smoothing food textures, emulsifying, homogenizing liquid mixtures, and repelling sticking materials.

Lecithins are mixtures of glycerophospholipids including phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol, and phosphatidic acid. Lecithin was first isolated in 1845 by the French chemist and pharmacist Théodore Gobley, who in 1847 characterised it from egg yolk and named it after the Greek word for egg yolk. Gobley later identified lecithin in a variety of biological materials including venous blood, bile, brain tissue and fish eggs.

Production

Commercial lecithin is obtained mainly as a by-product of vegetable oil refining. Soy lecithin is extracted from crude soybean oil during the degumming process, in which water is added to precipitate the phospholipids. Egg lecithin is extracted from egg yolk; sunflower and rapeseed lecithins are increasingly used as alternatives for consumers avoiding soy or egg. The crude product can be dried to a powder, de-oiled, or enzymatically modified to adjust its emulsifying properties.

Properties and uses

Because lecithin molecules have a water-loving (hydrophilic) phosphate head and fat-loving (hydrophobic) fatty-acid tails, lecithin is an effective emulsifier and lubricant. It is widely used in the food industry (additive E322) in chocolate — where it reduces viscosity and cocoa butter requirements — margarine, baked goods, instant powders and as a release agent in cooking sprays. In pharmaceuticals it is used in liposomal drug delivery and as a dispersing agent. Industrial uses include paints, cosmetics and animal feed.

Biology and nutrition

Phosphatidylcholine is a major component of cell membranes and of the lipid bilayer, and a source of choline, an essential nutrient used in the synthesis of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. Lecithin is sold as a dietary supplement, although most people obtain sufficient choline from a normal diet. Research into claimed benefits for cholesterol and cognitive decline has produced mixed results. Lecithin is generally recognised as safe; because most commercial lecithin derives from soy or egg, it is relevant to people with those allergies, though residual protein levels are very low.
