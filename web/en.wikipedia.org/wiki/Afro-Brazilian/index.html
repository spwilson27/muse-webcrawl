Afro-Brazilian

Afro-Brazilians (Portuguese: afro-brasileiros) are Brazilians who have predominantly sub-Saharan African ancestry. The term most often refers to Brazilians classified in the national census as preto (Black) or pardo (brown/mixed), who together make up a majority of Brazil's population—over half of roughly 216 million people. Brazil received more enslaved Africans during the transatlantic slave trade than any other country in the Americas, an estimated 4.9 million people, and it was the last country in the Western world to abolish slavery, in 1888.

PopulationOver 100 million (pretos and pardos combined, 2022 census)
RegionsThroughout Brazil; largest numbers in Bahia, Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, Minas Gerais

History

Portuguese colonists began importing enslaved Africans to Brazil in the sixteenth century to work in sugar plantations, later in gold mines and coffee estates. Enslaved people resisted through escape, forming quilombos—maroon settlements such as Palmares, led by Zumbi, which survived most of the seventeenth century. Slavery was abolished by the Golden Law (Lei Áurea) of 13 May 1888, signed by Princess Isabel, without provisions for the integration of the freed population.

In the twentieth century, Afro-Brazilian movements fought racial discrimination. Although Brazil promoted an image of "racial democracy," scholars and activists have documented persistent inequalities in income, education, and exposure to violence. Affirmative action policies introduced in the 2000s, including university quotas (formalised by a 2012 law), sought to address these disparities.

Culture

African heritage deeply shaped Brazilian culture. Samba and the Rio Carnival, capoeira (a martial art disguised as dance), and the cuisine of Bahia—including acarajé and moqueca—all have Afro-Brazilian roots. Religious traditions such as Candomblé and Umbanda preserve Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu spiritual practices. November 20, the anniversary of Zumbi's death, is observed as Black Consciousness Day (Dia da Consciência Negra), a public holiday in many states and, since 2023, nationally.

Demographics

In the 2022 census, about 10% of Brazilians identified as preto and about 45% as pardo. Afro-Brazilians form the majority in states such as Bahia, where Salvador is often called the largest Black city outside Africa. Afro-Brazilians remain underrepresented in politics, media, and corporate leadership relative to their share of the population.
