An anterior cruciate ligament injury occurs when the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) of the knee is stretched, partially torn or completely ruptured. The ACL, one of the four main knee ligaments, connects the femur to the tibia and resists forward translation and rotation of the tibia. ACL tears are among the most common serious sports injuries, with higher rates in female athletes in sports such as football (soccer), basketball and skiing.

Mechanism and presentation

Most tears are non-contact injuries: sudden deceleration, landing from a jump, or a pivoting/cutting manoeuvre with the foot planted. Athletes often report a "pop", rapid joint swelling (haemarthrosis) within hours, pain, and a feeling that the knee gives way. Associated injuries to the menisci and collateral ligaments are common.

Diagnosis

Physical examination — the Lachman test, anterior drawer test and pivot-shift test — is often diagnostic. MRI confirms the tear and shows associated damage. The injury is also graded by completeness (sprain, partial tear, complete tear).

Treatment and prognosis

Initial management follows RICE principles (rest, ice, compression, elevation). Some patients, particularly less active "copers", manage without surgery through structured rehabilitation, but persistent instability and the wish to return to pivoting sports usually lead to arthroscopic ACL reconstruction, using autograft (hamstring, patellar tendon or quadriceps tendon) or allograft tissue. Rehabilitation takes many months; return to high-level sport typically comes at around nine months or later, with elevated risks of re-injury and of later knee osteoarthritis.

Prevention

Neuromuscular training programmes (such as FIFA 11+ and PEP) that teach landing mechanics and strengthen the hamstrings and core substantially reduce ACL injury rates, particularly in adolescent female athletes.
