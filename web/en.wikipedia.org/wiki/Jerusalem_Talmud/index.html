Jerusalem Talmud

The Jerusalem Talmud (Hebrew: תַּלְמוּד יְרוּשַׁלְמִי, romanized: Talmud Yerushalmi), also known as the Palestinian Talmud, is a collection of rabbinic notes on the second-century Jewish oral tradition known as the Mishnah. Naming it the "Jerusalem Talmud" is something of a misnomer, because it was compiled in the Galilee rather than in Jerusalem; scholars often prefer "Palestinian Talmud". It was compiled by Jewish scholars in the Roman province of Syria Palaestina, principally in the academies of Tiberias, Sepphoris and Caesarea, and was brought to a close in about 400 CE.

Content

The Jerusalem Talmud comprises the Mishnah tractates of four of the six orders (Zeraim, Moed, Nashim and Nezikin, plus tractate Niddah from Tohorot). Its Gemara is written in Jewish Palestinian Aramaic, a western Aramaic dialect, mixed with Hebrew. Compared with the Babylonian Talmud it is shorter, less fully edited, and more focused on the agricultural laws applicable in the Land of Israel.

Transmission

The text survives essentially in a single complete manuscript, the Leiden Manuscript, copied in 1289 by Jehiel ben Jekuthiel; the standard printed edition derives from the Venice printing of 1523–24. Because of political unrest in Roman Palestine, the work ends abruptly around the early fifth century, and it long stood in the shadow of the more authoritative Babylonian Talmud, though it remains a primary source for the Judaism of late-antique Palestine and for Jewish law concerning the Land of Israel.
