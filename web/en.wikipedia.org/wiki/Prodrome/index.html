Prodrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In medicine, a prodrome is an early sign or symptom (or set of signs and symptoms) that often indicates the onset of a disease before more diagnostically specific signs and symptoms develop. It is derived from the Greek word prodromos, meaning "running before". Prodromes may be non-specific symptoms or, in a few cases, may clearly indicate a particular disease.

Examples

  • Infectious diseases: fever, malaise, headache, and loss of appetite precede the characteristic rash in measles and chickenpox; a "prodromal" tingling or burning sensation may precede the blisters of herpes labialis or shingles.
  • Migraine: the prodrome consists of symptoms such as fatigue, food cravings, neck stiffness, and mood changes occurring hours to days before the attack, distinct from the aura phase.
  • Schizophrenia and psychosis: the prodromal phase describes the period of gradually emerging attenuated symptoms — social withdrawal, declining function, unusual thoughts — before the first full psychotic episode; this concept underlies early-intervention services for people at "ultra-high risk" of psychosis.
  • Parkinson's disease: a long prodromal phase may include loss of smell, constipation, depression, and REM sleep behaviour disorder years before motor symptoms appear.

Significance

Recognising a prodrome can allow earlier diagnosis, treatment, or preventive measures — for instance, starting antivirals at the prodromal stage of shingles, or monitoring people in a psychosis prodrome for conversion to frank illness.
