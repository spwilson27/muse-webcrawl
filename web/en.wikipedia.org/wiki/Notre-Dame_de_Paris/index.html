Notre-Dame de Paris ("Our Lady of Paris") is a medieval Catholic cathedral on the Île de la Cité in Paris, France, and one of the finest and most famous examples of French Gothic architecture. Dedicated to the Virgin Mary, it has been the seat of the Archbishop of Paris and a symbol of the French capital for more than eight centuries.

Construction

The cornerstone was laid in 1163 by Bishop Maurice de Sully in the presence of Pope Alexander III, and the choir and high altar were completed by 1182. The western façade with its twin towers and the great rose windows were added in the 13th century. Innovatively, the cathedral's thin walls are supported by an external system of flying buttresses, allowing walls to be pierced by large stained-glass windows. The building was completed in its essentials by about 1345.

History

The cathedral witnessed major events of French history: Philip the Fair convened the first Estates-General there in 1302; Henry VI of England was crowned King of France there in 1431; and Napoleon Bonaparte crowned himself Emperor there in 1804, as depicted in Jacques-Louis David's famous painting. During the French Revolution it was desecrated and rededicated as a Temple of Reason. Victor Hugo's 1831 novel The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (Notre-Dame de Paris) helped inspire the great 19th-century restoration led by the architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc, who added the celebrated spire.

2019 fire and restoration

On 15 April 2019 a fire broke out beneath the roof during restoration works, destroying the spire, most of the timber roof, and part of the vaulting, though the stone vaults largely held and the main structure, the two towers, and the great organ survived. An international fundraising campaign raised more than €800 million, and a meticulous restoration rebuilt the spire in oak as Viollet-le-Duc had designed it. The cathedral reopened to the public on 8 December 2024.

Architecture and art

Among its treasures are three rose windows dating from the 13th century, the 1730 organ, the great bells including the 13-tonne bourdon Emmanuel, and the treasury holding relics such as the Crown of Thorns. The façade's Gallery of Kings — 28 statues of kings of Judah, decapitated during the Revolution — was restored in the 19th century.
