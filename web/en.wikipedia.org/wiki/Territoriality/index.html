Territoriality

Territoriality is the behavior by which an animal lays claim to and defends an area against others of its species, and sometimes other species as well. The defended area is called a territory. Territorial behavior is found in many groups of animals, including birds, mammals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, insects, and other invertebrates, and may be displayed by individuals, pairs, or groups. Territories typically contain resources such as food, water, shelter, nest sites, or mates that the holder seeks to monopolize.

Functions

By defending a territory, an animal gains priority access to the resources it contains, which can improve feeding success, mating opportunities, and offspring survival. Territorial defense has costs — time, energy, and risk of injury — so territories tend to be defended only when the benefits of exclusive resource access outweigh the costs, an idea formalized as "economic defendability."

Advertisement and defense

Territories are commonly advertised by conspicuous signals such as song in birds, scent marking in mammals, and visual displays in fish and lizards. Such signals often allow rivals to assess one another and avoid costly fights; the "dear enemy" effect describes how established neighbors, whose boundaries are settled, are treated less aggressively than strangers. Actual physical combat occurs mainly when signals fail, for example during territory establishment.

In humans

The term is also applied metaphorically to human behavior and social organization, including personal space, property, and the geopolitical notion of territorial integrity, though the extent to which human territoriality is instinctive remains debated.
