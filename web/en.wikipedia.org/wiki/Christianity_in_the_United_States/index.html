Christianity in the United States

Christianity is the largest religion in the United States, professed by a majority of the population, though its share has declined in recent decades. Surveys in the early 2020s found roughly six in ten American adults identifying as Christian, down from about eight in ten in the early 1990s, with the religiously unaffiliated the fastest-growing group.

Denominations

Protestants form the largest bloc, divided among evangelical, mainline, and historically Black traditions; Baptists are the largest single Protestant family. Roman Catholics are the largest single denomination, strong in the Northeast, Southwest, and urban areas, in part through immigration. Smaller communities include Orthodox Christians, Latter-day Saints (centred in Utah), Jehovah's Witnesses, and many others.

History

Christianity arrived with Spanish and French colonisation and English settlements such as Virginia and Puritan New England. The First and Second Great Awakenings shaped American evangelicalism; the nineteenth century saw Catholic immigration, the birth of new movements, and African American churches formed under slavery and segregation. The First Amendment's disestablishment coexisted with a broadly Protestant public culture for much of the nation's history.

Contemporary trends

Mainline Protestant membership has fallen sharply since the 1960s; evangelical churches grew, then levelled off; and "nones" — atheists, agnostics, and the unaffiliated — reached roughly three in ten adults in recent Pew surveys. Christianity remains influential in politics, civil religion, and public life.
