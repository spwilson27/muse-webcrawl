Hail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hail is a form of solid precipitation consisting of balls or irregular lumps of ice, each of which is called a hailstone. Unlike ice pellets, which are smaller and translucent, and graupel, which is soft and opaque, hailstones are generally at least 5 millimetres (0.2 in) in diameter and are produced chiefly inside cumulonimbus clouds in strong thunderstorms with intense updrafts.

Formation

Hail forms when supercooled water droplets freeze onto condensation nuclei — dust, or a small ice pellet — and the growing stone is carried by powerful updrafts through regions of the cloud rich in supercooled water, accumulating layers of ice. Clear and opaque layers record the changing conditions of successive trips through the cloud. When the updraft can no longer support the stone's weight, or it is thrown into a downdraft, it falls to the ground, sometimes at speeds exceeding 100 km/h. Regions with frequent strong convection — the Great Plains of North America ("Hail Alley"), parts of Argentina, central Europe, northern India and Bangladesh, and the Highveld of South Africa — experience the most hail.

Size and damage

Hail size is commonly compared to familiar objects: pea (6 mm), dime, quarter, golf ball (43 mm), tennis ball, baseball and softball (about 114 mm). The largest verified hailstone in the United States fell at Vivian, South Dakota, on 23 July 2010, measuring 20 cm (8 in) in diameter and weighing 0.88 kg. Hailstorms cause billions of dollars of damage annually to crops, roofs, vehicles and aircraft, though deaths are rare; notable fatal events include the 1888 Moradabad storm in India and a 2000 storm in China. Severe-thunderstorm warnings in the United States are issued when hail reaches 1 inch (25 mm) in diameter.

Detection and suppression

Meteorologists detect hail with weather radar — high reflectivity combined with signatures such as a three-body scatter spike indicates large hail — and measure its kinetic energy with hail pads. Cloud seeding with silver iodide has long been attempted, especially in parts of Europe and North America, to encourage many small stones rather than few large ones, but its effectiveness remains scientifically contested; anti-hail nets and hail cannons are also used to protect orchards, vineyards and car dealerships.
