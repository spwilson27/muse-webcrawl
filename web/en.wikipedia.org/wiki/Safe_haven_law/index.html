Safe haven law

Safe haven laws (also known in some states as Baby Moses laws, in reference to the biblical infant Moses) are statutes in the United States that decriminalise the leaving of unharmed newborn infants with statutorily designated private persons or at designated places, so that the child becomes a ward of the state. The laws are intended to prevent infants from being abandoned in unsafe conditions, where they risk injury or death.

Safe haven signs mark locations — often hospitals and fire stations — where a newborn may be surrendered legally and anonymously.

History

Texas enacted the first safe haven law in the United States in 1999, following a series of widely reported cases in which newborns had been abandoned in dangerous places. The idea spread rapidly: by 2008 all fifty states, as well as Puerto Rico, had adopted some form of safe haven legislation.

Provisions

While the details vary by state, safe haven laws typically specify a maximum age for the surrendered infant — ranging from a few days to one year, with many states setting the limit at 30 days. Designated safe havens commonly include hospitals, fire stations, police stations and emergency medical services. A parent who surrenders an unharmed infant at such a site, within the statutory period, is generally protected from prosecution for abandonment and may remain anonymous. Some states also allow the parent a period in which to reclaim the child before parental rights are terminated and the child is placed for adoption.

More recently, several states have authorised the use of temperature-controlled "baby boxes" at fire stations and hospitals, allowing an infant to be surrendered anonymously with an automatic alarm alerting staff.

Criticism and debate

Supporters argue the laws save infants' lives by giving desperate parents a safe, legal alternative. Critics contend that the laws may not reach the mothers most at risk, that they can undermine fathers' rights, and that evidence for their effect on rates of unsafe abandonment and neonaticide remains mixed. Analogous arrangements exist in several other countries, including Germany's Babyklappe (baby hatches) and Japan's "stork's cradle".
