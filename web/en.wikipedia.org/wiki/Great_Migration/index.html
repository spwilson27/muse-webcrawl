Great Migration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Migration was the mass movement of approximately six million African Americans out of the rural Southern United States to the urban Northeast, Midwest, and West between 1910 and 1970. It was one of the largest internal migrations in American history and profoundly reshaped the country's demography, culture, and politics. Historians often divide it into a First Great Migration (1910–1940), driven by wartime industrial labour demand, and a Second Great Migration (1940–1970), which was larger and directed more heavily to the West Coast.

Causes

Migrants were pushed by Jim Crow segregation, disfranchisement, racial violence including lynching, and the collapse of agricultural livelihoods under the boll weevil, floods, and mechanisation of cotton farming. They were pulled by industrial jobs in northern steel mills, meatpacking plants, automobile factories, and wartime defence industries; by labour agents and Black newspapers such as the Chicago Defender, which actively encouraged migration; and by chain migration along rail lines — Mississippians to Chicago, Texans and Louisianans to California, Virginians and Carolinians to New York and Philadelphia.

Effects

Migration transformed northern and western cities, creating major Black urban communities in Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, New York (Harlem), Los Angeles, and Oakland, and powering cultural flowerings such as the Harlem Renaissance and the urban blues and jazz traditions. Migrants faced housing segregation, restrictive covenants, redlining, and periodic race riots, but gained higher wages, better schools, and the franchise, converting Black votes into a pivotal constituency in national politics. The movement also remade the South, draining labour and eventually pressing southern institutions to change. From the 1970s, a reverse "New Great Migration" of Black Americans to the metropolitan South began.
