Gettysburg campaign

The Gettysburg campaign was a military invasion of Pennsylvania by the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia under General Robert E. Lee in the summer of 1863, during the American Civil War. It culminated in the Battle of Gettysburg (1–3 July 1863), the bloodiest battle of the war and, together with the fall of Vicksburg, one of its decisive turning points. The campaign ended with Lee's retreat across the Potomac River into Virginia in mid-July.

Background and aims

After his victory at Chancellorsville in May 1863, Lee persuaded the Confederate government to let him carry the war north. His aims included relieving war-torn Virginia during the growing season, gathering supplies and horses in Pennsylvania's rich farmland, drawing the Union Army of the Potomac away from Virginia, encouraging Northern peace sentiment, and possibly winning a victory on Northern soil that might secure European recognition. Lee reorganised his army into three corps under Longstreet, Ewell and A. P. Hill.

Course of the campaign

Beginning on 3 June, the Confederates slipped away from the Rappahannock and marched up the Shenandoah Valley, clearing it of Union forces at the Second Battle of Winchester. Ewell's corps crossed the Potomac and advanced through Maryland into Pennsylvania, reaching the outskirts of Harrisburg, while J. E. B. Stuart's cavalry made a wide, controversial ride around the Union army that left Lee blind to his opponent's movements. The Union command passed from Joseph Hooker to George G. Meade on 28 June. The armies collided at Gettysburg on 1 July; after three days of fighting — including the fighting at Little Round Top, the Peach Orchard and Pickett's Charge — Lee was defeated and began his retreat on 4 July, the same day Vicksburg surrendered.

Retreat and aftermath

Lee's wagon trains of wounded stretched for miles; his army, cornered against a flooded Potomac at Williamsport, held off Union pursuit and crossed into Virginia on the night of 13–14 July after fighting rear-guard actions at Falling Waters and through the Blue Ridge passes (the Battle of Manassas Gap). Meade was criticised, including by President Lincoln, for not destroying Lee's army. The campaign cost the Confederates roughly a third of Lee's men — losses the South could not replace — and ended serious Confederate offensive operations in the east; Lee never again invaded the North.
