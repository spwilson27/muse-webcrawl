Burden of proof (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The burden of proof is the duty of a party in a trial to produce evidence that will support a claim or prove a disputed fact. Two burdens interlock: the burden of persuasion (the risk of losing if the fact-finder remains unconvinced, measured against the applicable standard of proof) and the burden of production (going forward with enough evidence to put an issue properly before the court).

Standards and allocation

Standards ascend: preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not) in most civil cases; clear and convincing evidence for grave civil matters (fraud, termination of parental rights, civil commitment); and beyond a reasonable doubt for criminal convictions, where the presumption of innocence places the burden on the prosecution for every element of the offence (In re Winship). Allocation mostly follows the pleadings (the claimant proves their claim; the defendant proves affirmative defences such as insanity or self-defence, with constitutional limits — states may not shift the burden on elements, Mullaney v. Wilbur, though they may assign defences, Patterson v. New York). Presumptions are burden-allocating devices (some irrebuttable, and then effectively rules of substance). The rhetoric of burdens dominates closing argument — it is how the law distributes the risk of error between parties and, criminally, between citizen and state.
