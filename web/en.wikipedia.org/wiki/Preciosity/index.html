Préciosité

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Préciosité (French pronunciation: [pʁesjɔzite]) was a French literary and social style of the 17th century, centred on the salons of aristocratic and upper-bourgeois women known as the précieuses. It prized wit, refinement of language and manners, elaborate metaphors, and a studied delicacy in matters of love and gallantry, reacting against the perceived coarseness of the court of Henri IV.

Origins and character

The movement grew out of the salon of the Marquise de Rambouillet, whose Hôtel de Rambouillet gathered writers and aristocrats from the 1620s. Later salons, notably that of Madeleine de Scudéry, fostered the long heroic romances of the period — Scudéry's own Clélie (1654–1660) famously included the Carte de Tendre, an allegorical map of the stages of love. The précieuses cultivated an elevated vocabulary, discarding words judged vulgar and coining periphrases and neologisms, some of which survived in standard French.

Criticism and legacy

The style was satirized by Molière in Les Précieuses ridicules (1659) and Les Femmes savantes (1672), which mocked its affectations while also registering anxiety about learned women. Modern scholarship has revalued préciosité as an important vehicle of women's literary culture and as a stage in the refinement of the French language; the Académie française project of codifying usage overlapped with salon practice.
