Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990
Enacted1 December 1990; effective 1 June 1991
Codified17 U.S.C. § 106A
Rights grantedAttribution and integrity for works of visual art

The Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 (VARA) is a United States federal law that grants certain moral rights to artists of works of visual art — the first federal statute in the US to do so. Codified at 17 U.S.C. § 106A, VARA implements, in part, the moral-rights obligations of the Berne Convention.

Rights conferred

VARA gives the author of a work of visual art — narrowly defined as a painting, drawing, print, sculpture or exhibition photograph existing in a single copy or limited edition of 200 or fewer — the rights of attribution (to claim authorship, and to disclaim authorship of a mutilated work) and integrity (to prevent intentional distortion, mutilation or modification prejudicial to the artist's honour or reputation, and to prevent destruction of a work of "recognized stature"). The rights last for the life of the author, cannot be transferred, but may be waived in a signed writing. Works made for hire are excluded.

Notable cases

In Cohen v. G&M Realty (the "5Pointz" case), a federal court awarded $6.75 million in statutory damages after a developer whitewashed celebrated aerosol murals at the 5Pointz complex in Queens, New York — a judgment affirmed on appeal in 2020. Other litigation has tested VARA's limits for site-specific sculpture and art incorporated into buildings, issues on which VARA interacts with property and design rights such as the design patent.
