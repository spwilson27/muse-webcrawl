Intersection theory

Intersection theory is a main branch of algebraic geometry which deals with geometric objects where classical notions of intersection — such as counting the points where two curves on a surface meet — are extended by algebraic tools that keep track of multiplicities and degenerate configurations. The theory provides rigorous foundations for enumerative geometry, the study of counting geometric figures satisfying given conditions.

Overview

Classical geometry already contained intersection counts: Bézout's theorem states that two plane projective curves of degrees m and n meet in exactly mn points when counted with multiplicity over an algebraically closed field. Intersection theory generalises this by assigning to a smooth variety an algebraic structure — the Chow ring of cycles modulo rational equivalence — equipped with an intersection product that behaves predictably even when cycles do not meet transversally.

History

Enumerative questions were pursued by Schubert in the nineteenth century; his "calculus" was famously listed by Hilbert as his fifteenth problem, calling for a rigorous foundation. Modern foundations were built by Weil, Chevalley, Chow, and later by Fulton and MacPherson, whose work on intersection homology and refined intersection products placed the subject at the centre of algebraic geometry.

Applications

Intersection theory underlies computations in Gromov–Witten theory and mirror symmetry, where intersection numbers on moduli spaces of curves count rational curves on Calabi–Yau manifolds — counts dramatically confirmed in the 1990s by comparisons with predictions from string theory.
