Lockheed Have Blue was the code name for a proof-of-concept stealth aircraft demonstrator built by Lockheed's Skunk Works for the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) and the United States Air Force. First flown in secret in 1977, the two Have Blue prototypes demonstrated that a carefully shaped aircraft could have an extraordinarily small radar cross-section, and they paved the way for the F-117 Nighthawk, the world's first operational stealth attack aircraft.

Design

Have Blue's radical shape grew out of the Skunk Works' analysis of the Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev's work on the diffraction of electromagnetic waves. Because the computing power of the mid-1970s could only calculate radar reflections from flat surfaces, the designers covered the aircraft in flat, sharply angled facets arranged so that radar energy would be deflected away from its source rather than reflected back. The result—a slab-sided, dart-like machine with a sharply swept wing, inward-canted twin tails, and engines buried behind radar-blocking intake grills—was nicknamed the "Hopeless Diamond" and was inherently unstable, requiring a quadruple-redundant fly-by-wire system adapted from the F-16. It was built quickly and cheaply from existing components, including General Electric J85 engines, and carried no radar, weapons, or operational equipment.

Testing and legacy

The first prototype flew on 1 December 1977 from Groom Lake, Nevada, with test pilot Bill Park at the controls. Testing confirmed the tiny radar signature, reportedly comparable to a small metal ball bearing. Both prototypes were eventually lost in crashes—the first in 1980 after a landing-gear failure, the second in 1985 following an engine fire—though both pilots ejected safely. The wreckage was buried at the test site, and the programme remained classified for years. Have Blue's success convinced the Air Force to fund the Senior Trend programme, which produced the F-117 and revolutionised air warfare by proving that stealth could be built into a flyable combat aircraft.
