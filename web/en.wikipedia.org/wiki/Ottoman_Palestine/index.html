Ottoman Palestine refers to the four centuries during which the region of Palestine was ruled by the Ottoman Empire, from its conquest by Sultan Selim I in 1516–1517 until British forces under General Allenby captured it in 1917–1918 during the First World War. For most of this period the area was administered as districts of the provinces of Damascus and later Beirut, with Jerusalem and its surroundings eventually organised as a separate mutasarrifate answerable directly to Constantinople.

Conquest and early rule

Selim I defeated the Mamluk Sultanate at Marj Dabiq (1516) and Gaza, taking Palestine almost without a fight. His son Suleiman the Magnificent rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem (1535–1542) that still surround the Old City today and restored the Dome of the Rock's tilework. Palestine's economy rested on agriculture — grain, olive oil, soap from Nablus, and later Jaffa oranges — and on the taxes and endowments (waqf) tied to Jerusalem's holy places.

Local powers and reform

Central control often weakened in practice: local strongmen such as the Bedouin-supported Fakhr al-Din II in the 17th century and Zahir al-Umar in 18th-century Galilee ruled semi-independently. Muhammad Ali's Egypt occupied Palestine from 1831 to 1840 before the Ottomans retook it. The Tanzimat reforms that followed brought new land codes (1858), telegraph and railway links — including the Jaffa–Jerusalem railway (1892) and the Hejaz Railway (1908) — foreign consulates, missionary schools, and growing European involvement. The late 19th century also saw the first waves of modern Jewish immigration (the First and Second Aliyahs) and the beginnings of organised Zionist settlement, alongside rising Arab national consciousness.

End of Ottoman rule

During the First World War Palestine was a frontline theatre: the Ottomans repelled British attacks on Gaza twice in 1917, but Allenby's forces took Beersheba, Gaza and Jerusalem that year, and won the decisive Battle of Megiddo in September 1918. The territory then passed to British administration, formalised as the Mandate for Palestine in 1920–1923.
