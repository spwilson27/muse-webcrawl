Slutsky equation

The Slutsky equation (or Slutsky identity) in economics, named after Eugen Slutsky, relates changes in Marshallian (uncompensated) demand to changes in Hicksian (compensated) demand. It decomposes the effect of a price change on the quantity demanded of a good into a substitution effect and an income effect.

The equation

For a good i whose price changes, the Slutsky equation states:

∂xi/∂pj = ∂hi/∂pj − xj · ∂xi/∂m

where xi is Marshallian demand, hi is Hicksian demand, pj is the price of good j, and m is income. The first term on the right is the substitution effect — the change in compensated demand as the consumer adjusts along a fixed utility level. The second term is the income effect: a price rise reduces real purchasing power, and the size of this effect depends on how much of good j the consumer buys (xj) and on how demand for good i responds to income.

Interpretation

The substitution effect of an own-price increase is always non-positive: when a good becomes more expensive, consumers substitute away from it. The income effect can go either way. For a normal good, a price increase lowers real income and therefore lowers demand, reinforcing the substitution effect. For an inferior good, the income effect works in the opposite direction, and if it is large enough it can in principle outweigh the substitution effect, producing a Giffen good whose demand rises when its price rises.

History and significance

Slutsky derived the relationship in his 1915 paper "Sulla teoria del bilancio del consumatore" ("On the theory of the consumer's budget"), published in the Italian journal Giornale degli Economisti. The paper went largely unnoticed until it was taken up in the 1930s by economists including John Hicks and R. G. D. Allen, after which it became a cornerstone of modern consumer theory. The Slutsky matrix of compensated price effects is symmetric and negative semidefinite, properties that underpin integrability theory and tests of rational consumer behaviour.
