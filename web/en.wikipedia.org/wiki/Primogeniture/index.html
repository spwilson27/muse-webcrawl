Primogeniture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Primogeniture is the right, by law or custom, of the firstborn legitimate child to inherit the parent's entire or main estate in preference to shared inheritance among all or some children, any illegitimate child or any collateral relative. In most contexts, it means the inheritance of the firstborn son (it may also mean by the firstborn daughter). It can be contrasted to ultimogeniture, the right of the youngest child.

History

Primogeniture was widespread in the monarchies of Europe and spread to colonies and former colonies. In feudal society it served to keep large estates and titles intact rather than fragmenting them among many heirs, and it aligned succession to a throne or noble dignity with a single, predictable rule. In England, the common-law rule of male primogeniture governed the descent of land from the Norman Conquest until the reforms of 1925–26. Younger sons of the aristocracy, excluded from the main inheritance, traditionally pursued careers in the church, the military, the law, or commerce.

Variations

Absolute primogeniture (or cognatic primogeniture) gives the firstborn child precedence regardless of sex. It was adopted by Sweden in 1980, followed by several other European monarchies, including the Netherlands, Norway, Belgium, Denmark (2009) and the United Kingdom (through the Succession to the Crown Act 2013, applying to those born after 28 October 2011).

Male-preference primogeniture, long the norm in most monarchies, allows a female to inherit only if she has no living brothers and no deceased brother left surviving legitimate descendants. Agnatic primogeniture, exemplified by the Salic law of the Franks, excludes females entirely from succession.

Modern usage

Most modern legal systems no longer apply primogeniture to private estates, where intestacy rules typically divide property among all children. It remains significant chiefly in the succession rules of reigning and formerly reigning royal houses, and in some hereditary peerages and titles.
