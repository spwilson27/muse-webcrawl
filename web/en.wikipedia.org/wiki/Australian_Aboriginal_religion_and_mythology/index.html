Australian Aboriginal religion and mythology comprises the sacred traditions of Aboriginal Australians, among the oldest continuous religious traditions in the world. Aboriginal spirituality is not a single uniform system: each language group has its own traditions, though common themes — above all the Dreaming and a profound connection between people, ancestors and Country (land) — recur across the continent.

The Dreaming

The Dreaming (in Arrernte, Tjukurrpa) refers both to the era in which ancestral beings travelled the land, creating its features, animals, plants and laws, and to the ongoing spiritual reality those beings established. As an ancestor moved, it left a trail of sites and songs — "songlines" — that later custodians maintain through ceremony, weaving together geography, morality, kinship and identity.

Beings and stories

Widely known figures include the Rainbow Serpent, associated with water, creation and fertility across many regions; Baiame, a sky father of south-eastern groups; and the Wandjina of the Kimberley, depicted in distinctive rock art. Mythology also encompasses trickster and culture heroes, and narratives explaining natural phenomena, many of which preserve remarkably ancient environmental knowledge.

Practice and transmission

Knowledge is transmitted orally and through ritual, song, dance, sand drawing, bark and rock painting, and carved objects, with access to some knowledge restricted by age, gender and initiation. Ceremonies maintain the land and the law laid down by the ancestors. Since colonisation, Aboriginal people have defended these traditions, which underpin native title law's recognition of continuing connection to Country.
