John Tyler (29 March 1790 – 18 January 1862) was the tenth president of the United States, serving from 1841 to 1845, after briefly holding office as the tenth vice president in 1841. He was elected vice president on the 1840 Whig ticket with President William Henry Harrison, and became president after Harrison's death thirty-one days into his term — the first president to succeed to office upon the death of a predecessor.

Early life and career

Tyler was born into a prominent Virginia family at Greenway Plantation in Charles City County. His father, John Tyler Sr., was a judge and governor of Virginia. The younger Tyler graduated from the College of William & Mary in 1807, studied law, and was admitted to the bar at nineteen. He served in the Virginia House of Delegates, the U.S. House of Representatives, as governor of Virginia, and in the U.S. Senate. Originally a Democratic-Republican and then a Democrat, he broke with President Andrew Jackson over the nullification crisis and the removal of deposits from the Bank of the United States, aligning with the new Whig Party.

Accession and the Tyler precedent

When Harrison died of pneumonia on 4 April 1841, Tyler took the presidential oath of office, insisted he was president in full — not merely acting president — and refused to accept mail addressed to the "Vice President" or "Acting President". This "Tyler precedent" for full succession was followed by later vice presidents and was ultimately codified in the Twenty-fifth Amendment in 1967.

Presidency

Tyler's strict constructionism and states'-rights views soon put him at odds with the Whig congressional programme of Henry Clay. He twice vetoed bills to create a national bank; his entire cabinet except Secretary of State Daniel Webster resigned, and the Whigs expelled him from the party — earning him the nickname "His Accidency". Despite the isolation of his administration, Tyler signed the Webster–Ashburton Treaty with Britain (1842), settling the Maine–New Brunswick boundary, and the Preemption Act of 1841. In foreign affairs his administration negotiated the Treaty of Wanghia with China.

Annexation of Texas

Tyler made the annexation of Texas the crowning project of his presidency. A first treaty of annexation was rejected by the Senate in 1844, but after the election of the expansionist James K. Polk, Tyler secured annexation by joint resolution of Congress, which he signed on 1 March 1845, three days before leaving office. Texas entered the Union later that year.

Later life

Tyler retired to Sherwood Forest Plantation in Virginia, which he had purchased in 1842. He fathered fifteen children across two marriages — more than any other president. As sectional crisis deepened, he presided over the failed Washington Peace Conference of 1861, then supported Virginia's secession and was elected to the Confederate House of Representatives. He died in Richmond on 18 January 1862, before he could take his seat, and his death was the only one of a U.S. president not officially recognised in Washington because of his Confederate allegiance.
