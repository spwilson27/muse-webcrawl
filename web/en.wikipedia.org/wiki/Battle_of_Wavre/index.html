The Battle of Wavre was fought on 18–19 June 1815, during the Waterloo campaign of the Napoleonic Wars, at the same time as the decisive Battle of Waterloo a few miles to the west. A French force under Marshal Emmanuel de Grouchy engaged the Prussian rearguard under General Johann von Thielmann at the town of Wavre on the River Dyle.

Background

After the Prussian defeat at Ligny on 16 June, Napoleon detached Grouchy with about 33,000 men to pursue the Prussians and prevent them joining Wellington's army. Blücher's army, however, retreated north on Wavre rather than east away from Wellington, keeping open the possibility of marching to Wellington's aid. On 18 June the bulk of the Prussian army marched west toward Waterloo, leaving Thielmann's III Corps to hold Wavre against Grouchy.

The battle

Grouchy attacked the Prussian positions along the Dyle in the afternoon of 18 June. Fighting centred on the bridges at Wavre and the villages of Bierges and Limale. Vandamme's corps eventually forced a crossing at Limale late in the day, and on the morning of 19 June the French drove Thielmann back; but by then the guns at Waterloo had fallen silent, and news arrived of Napoleon's defeat. Grouchy conducted an orderly retreat into France.

Assessment

Although the French won the ground, the battle was a Prussian strategic success: Thielmann's outnumbered corps held Grouchy's wing away from Waterloo while Blücher's main body intervened decisively against Napoleon. Grouchy's conduct — his literal obedience to orders to pursue the Prussians and his refusal to march toward the sound of the guns — has been debated ever since.
