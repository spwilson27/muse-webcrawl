Egyptian mongoose

Egyptian mongoose

Scientific name: Herpestes ichneumon

Family: Herpestidae

Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

Range: Africa, Iberian Peninsula, Middle East

The Egyptian mongoose (Herpestes ichneumon), also known as the ichneumon, is a mongoose species native to the Iberian Peninsula, coastal regions of the Mediterranean, Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. It is the species of mongoose familiar to the ancient Egyptians, who revered it and depicted it in tomb paintings; Herodotus reported that it was embalmed and buried in sacred places.

Description

The Egyptian mongoose has a long, low body with short legs, a pointed muzzle and a long, bushy tail that tapers to a black tuft. Its coarse fur is grizzled grey to reddish brown. Adults measure roughly 48–60 cm in head-and-body length with a tail of 33–54 cm, and weigh about 1.7–4 kg. Its anal glands secrete a strongly scented substance used in marking.

Distribution and habitat

In Africa it occurs across most of the continent outside the Sahara's driest core and the Congo forest, favouring savanna, scrub and wetlands. In Europe it is found in Spain and Portugal, where it may be native or introduced in antiquity, and has been expanding northwards. It is generally found near water and dense cover.

Behaviour and diet

Unlike most mongooses it is chiefly diurnal. It lives singly, in pairs or in small family groups. It feeds on rodents, birds and their eggs, reptiles — including venomous snakes, to whose venom it has some resistance — amphibians, fish, insects and fruit. It is a capable swimmer and often hunts along riverbanks.
