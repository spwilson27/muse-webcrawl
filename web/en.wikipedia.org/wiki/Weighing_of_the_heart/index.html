Weighing of the heart

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The weighing of the heart was a central ceremony in ancient Egyptian religion, described most fully in the Book of the Dead. After death, the deceased was led into the Hall of Two Truths, where their heart was weighed on a great balance against the feather of Ma'at, the goddess representing truth, justice and cosmic order.

The ceremony

The heart — called the ib — was believed by the Egyptians to be the seat of thought, emotion, memory and the soul. It was the only organ left inside the body during mummification. At the judgement, the jackal-headed god Anubis adjusted the balance while the ibis-headed Thoth recorded the result, and the ceremony was presided over by Osiris, god of the afterlife, seated with Isis and Nephthys behind him.

Before the weighing, the deceased recited the Negative Confession, a list of forty-two sins they declared they had not committed, addressed to forty-two assessor gods. A heart scarab, often inscribed with a spell from the Book of the Dead, was placed over the heart to prevent it from betraying its owner during the judgement.

Outcome

If the heart balanced exactly with the feather, the deceased was declared maa-kheru ("true of voice", justified) and admitted to the Field of Reeds, a paradisiacal mirror of earthly Egypt. If the heart was heavy with wrongdoing, it was devoured by Ammit, a composite monster part crocodile, part lion and part hippopotamus, and the soul was condemned to a second and final death — ceasing to exist entirely.
