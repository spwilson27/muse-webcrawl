Children's Day (Japan)

Children's Day (こどもの日, Kodomo no hi) is a public holiday in Japan which takes place annually on May 5 and is the final national holiday of Golden Week. It is a day set aside to respect children's personalities and to celebrate their happiness. Designated a national holiday by the Japanese government in 1948, it descends from the traditional festival of Tango no sekku, one of the five annual seasonal festivals, which historically celebrated boys; the counterpart festival for girls, Hinamatsuri, is held on March 3.

Traditions

Families with sons traditionally raise koinobori, carp-shaped windsocks, on poles outside their homes — a black carp for the father, a red or pink carp for the mother, and additional colourful carp for each child. The carp symbolises strength and perseverance, since carp swim upstream and, in Chinese legend, become dragons upon reaching the Dragon Gate. Inside the home, families may display a samurai helmet (kabuto) or warrior dolls (gogatsu ningyō) as symbols of strength and good health.

Food

Special foods include kashiwamochi, rice cakes filled with sweet red bean paste and wrapped in oak leaves — oak symbolising prosperity because its old leaves do not fall until new ones grow — and chimaki, sweet rice dumplings wrapped in bamboo or iris leaves, more common in western Japan. In some regions, people take baths with iris leaves (shōbuyu) for good health.

History

Tango no sekku originated in ancient China and was observed at the imperial court in Japan from the Nara period. The date — the fifth day of the fifth month — was associated with driving away evil spirits and, in the samurai era, with celebrating boys' growth into warriors. After World War II the day was renamed Children's Day and dedicated to all children, while remaining rich in its historical boys' festival imagery.
