Molecular spectroscopy

Molecular spectroscopy is the study of the absorption of light and other electromagnetic radiation by materials consisting of molecules and of the subsequent emission of radiation from them, used to probe the structure, energy levels and dynamics of molecules. The measurement of the wavelength and intensity of absorbed or emitted radiation reveals information about molecular energy levels, bond strengths, molecular geometry and the interactions between molecules.

Molecular energy levels: electronic, vibrational and rotational transitions.

Types of molecular spectra

Molecular energy levels are divided into electronic, vibrational and rotational components, and each gives rise to characteristic spectra in different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum:

  • Electronic spectroscopy — transitions between electronic states, usually in the ultraviolet and visible regions; includes UV–Vis absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy.
  • Vibrational spectroscopy — transitions between vibrational levels, observed in the infrared (infrared spectroscopy) or through scattering (Raman spectroscopy).
  • Rotational spectroscopy — transitions between rotational levels of gas-phase molecules, observed in the microwave and far-infrared regions. It yields precise bond lengths from moments of inertia.
  • Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy — transitions between nuclear spin states in a magnetic field, in the radiofrequency region, giving detailed structural information.

Principles

A molecule absorbs radiation when the photon energy hν matches the energy difference between two quantized molecular states, ΔE = hν. Because rotational energy spacings are much smaller than vibrational spacings, which are in turn much smaller than electronic spacings, bands arising from electronic transitions contain fine structure from simultaneous vibrational and rotational changes. The Born–Oppenheimer approximation allows these motions to be treated separately to a good approximation.

Selection rules determine which transitions are allowed. For example, infrared absorption requires a change in the molecular dipole moment during the vibration, whereas Raman scattering requires a change in polarizability. Symmetrical molecules such as N2 and O2 are infrared-inactive but Raman-active.

Applications

Molecular spectroscopy underpins analytical chemistry and many other fields:

  • Identification and quantification of substances in chemistry, forensics and environmental monitoring
  • Determination of molecular structure and conformations
  • Astronomy: identification of molecules in interstellar clouds and planetary atmospheres from their characteristic spectra
  • Medical diagnostics, including breath analysis and tissue imaging
  • Studies of reaction kinetics and short-lived intermediates by time-resolved techniques
