Unanimity

Unanimity is agreement by every member of a group. A decision is unanimous when all participants vote or consent in favour, with no dissenting votes. Unanimity is required by some legal and institutional rules—most famously for criminal verdicts of juries in many jurisdictions—and is the standard aimed at by consensus decision-making processes. When unanimity is a formal requirement, a single dissenter can block action, effectively holding a veto.

Jury verdicts in criminal trials are a leading example of decisions that often require unanimity.

Uses

Unanimous verdicts have traditionally been required of juries in England and Wales (though majority verdicts were introduced in 1967) and in United States federal criminal trials; the U.S. Supreme Court held in Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) that the Sixth Amendment requires unanimity for convictions in state criminal trials as well. Unanimity rules also appear in international bodies, in constitutional amendment procedures, and historically in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth's liberum veto, where any single deputy could dissolve the Sejm—an extreme case frequently blamed for the Commonwealth's paralysis.

Assessment

Defenders argue unanimity protects minorities, forces full deliberation, and legitimises outcomes; critics note that it can empower holdouts, encourage coercion or compromise verdicts, and cause deadlock. Social-choice theory studies unanimity as the Pareto criterion: if everyone prefers one option to another, the group outcome should rank it higher.
