Liquidated damages

Liquidated damages (also referred to as liquidated and ascertained damages) are damages whose amount the parties designate during the formation of a contract for the injured party to collect as compensation upon a specific breach. When damages are not predetermined, the amount awarded is generally measured by actual loss and is known as unliquidated damages.

[Image: A large construction project, where delay damages clauses are common]

Purpose

Liquidated damages clauses are common where actual loss would be difficult to quantify in advance, such as delay in construction contracts, where a fixed sum per day or week of delay is stipulated. They provide certainty to both parties and avoid the cost of proving actual loss.

Enforceability and the penalty rule

Under the common law, a liquidated damages clause is enforceable only if it is not a penalty. Traditionally, the stipulated sum had to be a genuine pre-estimate of loss; a sum extravagant in comparison with the greatest conceivable loss would be struck down as a penalty. In the United Kingdom, the Supreme Court reformulated the test in Cavendish Square Holding BV v Makdessi (2015), asking instead whether the clause imposes a detriment out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in enforcing the contract. Many civil law systems, by contrast, permit penalty clauses, sometimes subject to judicial moderation.
