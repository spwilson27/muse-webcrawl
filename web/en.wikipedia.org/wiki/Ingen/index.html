Ingen

Ingen (隠元隆琦, Ingen Ryūki, 7 December 1592 – 19 May 1673) was a Chinese Buddhist monk of the Linji school who traveled to Japan in 1654 and founded the Ōbaku school of Zen Buddhism. He is one of the most significant figures in the history of Japanese Buddhism of the Edo period, and his influence extended to Japanese calligraphy, sencha tea culture, and cuisine.

Life

Ingen was born in Fuqing, in Fujian Province, during the Ming dynasty. He became a monk at Mount Huangbo (Japanese: Ōbaku), the mountain from which his later school took its name, and rose to become abbot of Wanfu Temple there. As the Ming dynasty collapsed amid the Manchu conquest, many Chinese monks and refugees sought safety abroad. Invited by Chinese merchants and monks of the Chinese community in Nagasaki, Ingen sailed to Japan in 1654 at the age of 61, accompanied by some twenty disciples.

After several years in Nagasaki, he was granted an audience with the shogun Tokugawa Ietsuna in Edo. In 1661 the shogunate granted him land at Uji, south of Kyoto, where he built Manpuku-ji, constructed in contemporary Chinese Ming style. There he established Ōbaku Zen as a distinct school, introducing Ming-era Chinese monastic practice, chanting in Chinese pronunciation, and a rule that combined Zen with Pure Land elements such as recitation of the name of Amitābha.

Legacy

Ingen's arrival invigorated Japanese Zen, which some contemporaries felt had grown stagnant. In addition to religion, he and his followers influenced Japanese culture broadly: the sencha (steeped leaf tea) tradition traces to the Ōbaku circle; fucha ryōri, a Chinese-style vegetarian cuisine, is still served at Manpuku-ji; and the string bean, called ingen-mame in Japanese, is traditionally said to have been introduced by him. Ingen was also an accomplished calligrapher, counted with his disciples Mokuan Shōtō and Sokuhi Nyoitsu as one of the "Three Brushes of Ōbaku." He died at Manpuku-ji in 1673, having named his successor line through Chinese abbots for several generations.
