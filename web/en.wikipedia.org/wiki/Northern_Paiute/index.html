Northern Paiute

Great Basin desert, Northern Paiute homeland

The Northern Paiute are a Native American people of the Great Basin region of the western United States, traditionally occupying territory in present-day eastern California, south-eastern Oregon, north-western Nevada and south-western Idaho. They call themselves Numa (or Nimi), "the People". Their language, also called Northern Paiute or Paviotso, belongs to the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family.

Traditional life

Northern Paiute bands lived in family groups that moved seasonally through the high desert, harvesting pine nuts, grass seeds, roots and berries, fishing in lakes such as Pyramid Lake and Walker Lake, and hunting pronghorn, rabbits and waterfowl. The Owens Valley Paiute of California developed simple irrigation systems for wild plant plots. Renowned basket weavers, they produced fine coiled and twined baskets for gathering and cooking.

Contact and conflict

Euro-American settlement, mining booms and the introduction of livestock disrupted Paiute subsistence from the 1850s, leading to conflicts including the Pyramid Lake War of 1860 and the Snake War of 1864–1868. The prophet Wovoka, founder of the Ghost Dance movement of 1889–90, was a Northern Paiute from Nevada. Survivors were gathered onto reservations including Pyramid Lake, Walker River, Burns Paiute, Fort McDermitt and several in the Owens Valley.

Today

Federally recognised Northern Paiute tribes today govern reservations and colonies in Nevada, Oregon and California, working on water rights, land claims and the revitalisation of the language, which is endangered but taught in tribal schools.
