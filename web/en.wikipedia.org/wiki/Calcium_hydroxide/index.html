Calcium hydroxide (traditionally called slaked lime) is an inorganic compound with the chemical formula Ca(OH)2. It is a colourless crystal or white powder and is produced when quicklime (calcium oxide) is mixed or slaked with water. It has many names including hydrated lime, caustic lime, builders' lime, cal, and pickling lime. Calcium hydroxide is used in many applications, including food preparation, where it has been identified as E number E526.

Preparation

Calcium hydroxide is produced commercially by treating lime (CaO) with water, an exothermic reaction known as slaking: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. In the laboratory it can be prepared by mixing aqueous solutions of calcium chloride and sodium hydroxide. The naturally occurring mineral form, portlandite, is relatively rare but is found in some volcanic and metamorphic rocks.

Properties and uses

A suspension of fine calcium hydroxide particles in water is called milk of lime; the clear saturated solution is limewater, a medium-strength base that reacts with acids and can attack some metals. On exposure to air it absorbs carbon dioxide and reverts to calcium carbonate, the basis of its use in traditional lime mortar and whitewash. Major uses include flue-gas treatment, water and sewage treatment, the paper industry (in the kraft process), and as a pH regulator and firming agent in foods — notably in the nixtamalization of maize and in pickling. In dentistry it is used in endodontic dressings. Saturated limewater turning milky when carbon dioxide is bubbled through it is a classic chemical test for CO2.
