Market manipulation

Market manipulation is a type of market abuse where there is a deliberate attempt to interfere with the free and fair operation of the market, creating artificial, false or misleading appearances of supply, demand, or price of a security, commodity or currency. Market manipulation is prohibited in most countries, including by the United States Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and the European Union's Market Abuse Regulation.

Types

  • Pump and dump — inflating a security's price through false promotion, then selling at the peak.
  • Wash trading — simultaneously buying and selling the same asset to create artificial volume.
  • Spoofing — placing large orders with no intent to execute, to mislead other traders, then cancelling them.
  • Cornering the market — acquiring enough of an asset's supply to control its price.
  • Bear raid — spreading false rumours while short selling to drive a price down.
  • LIBOR manipulation — the benchmark interest-rate scandal in which banks submitted false rates.

Detection and enforcement

Regulators such as the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission use trade surveillance, whistle-blower reports and statistical analysis to detect manipulation, distinguishing it from legitimate trading strategies such as a genuine market order or a lawful repurchase programme. Enforcement actions can bring fines, disgorgement, trading bans and criminal prosecution.

Examples

Famous historical episodes include the Hunt brothers' attempted corner of the silver market in 1979–80, which ended in the collapse known as Silver Thursday, and the manipulation of energy markets by Enron traders during the California electricity crisis.
