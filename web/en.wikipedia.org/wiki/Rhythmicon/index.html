TypeElectronic rhythm machine
InventorsLéon Theremin (Lev Termen); Henry Cowell

The Rhythmicon—also known as the Rhythmicone or Polyrhythmophone—is an electro-mechanical instrument built in 1931–1932 by the Russian physicist Léon Theremin (Lev Sergeyevich Termen) in collaboration with the American composer Henry Cowell. It is generally considered the world's first electronic drum machine, predating commercial drum machines by several decades.

Background

Cowell had developed a theory of rhythmic relationships set out in his book New Musical Resources (written 1919, published 1930), in which he proposed that rhythm and pitch are governed by the same mathematical relationships: just as the notes of the harmonic series stand in simple frequency ratios, rhythmic patterns could be built from simultaneous tempi in ratios such as 3:4:5. Such complex polyrhythms were beyond the reliable ability of human performers to execute precisely, so Cowell sought a machine that could play them. He approached Theremin, then living in New York and famous for the theremin, and commissioned the instrument.

Design and operation

The Rhythmicon works on optical principles. A keyboard of seventeen keys allows the player to select notes; each key corresponds to a periodic rhythm whose speed is proportional to the frequency of the corresponding pitch in the harmonic series. Inside the instrument, discs perforated with patterns of holes rotate in front of light sources and photocells, generating rhythmic pulses. The pitch produced by each key rises with the speed of its rhythm, so that the instrument demonstrates Cowell's idea of a direct correspondence between rhythm and pitch: the fundamental sounds once per beat, the second harmonic twice, and so on up to the sixteenth partial. The machine could thus produce intricate, perfectly periodic polyrhythms that no ensemble could play by hand.

Use and legacy

Cowell composed works for the instrument, including Rhythmicana (1931), a concerto for Rhythmicon and orchestra, though the piece was not performed until decades later. Only three Rhythmicons were built; the original is held by the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, D.C. The instrument's expense and mechanical temperamental nature limited its adoption, and Cowell eventually turned to other means of realising complex rhythms.

Despite its obscurity, the Rhythmicon is recognised as a landmark in the history of electronic music and a direct ancestor of the drum machine and the sequencer. Its underlying concept—that rhythm can be treated as a spectrum parallel to the harmonic series—also influenced later composers, and Cowell's rhythmic theories fed into the work of his student John Cage and others in the American experimental tradition.
