Ketchup

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bottle of tomato ketchup with tomatoes]

Ketchup is a sweet-and-tangy sauce made today principally from tomatoes, vinegar, sugar and salt with spices — the world's best-selling table sauce, emblematic on Hamburgers and fries. The word comes from the Chinese kê-tsiap, a fermented fish sauce; British adaptations in the 18th century used mushrooms, walnuts or oysters before tomato ketchup triumphed in 19th-century America.

H. J. Heinz's version (from 1876) set the standard of a thick, stable, bottled sauce. Ketchup is a concentrated source of Umami and of the tomato antioxidant lycopene, and a cultural shorthand — from diner counters to debates about refrigeration and what ketchup may touch (Chicago hot-dog purists forbid it).

1. Varieties

Varieties

Banana ketchup (Philippines), curry ketchup (Germany, partner to currywurst), and mushroom ketchup (Britain) extend the family; reduced-sugar and organic versions answer modern tastes.
