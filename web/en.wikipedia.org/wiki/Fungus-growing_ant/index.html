Fungus-growing ant

Fungus-growing ants (tribe Attini, in the strict sense the attine ants) are ants that cultivate fungi as their primary food source. The ants feed plant material to underground fungal gardens; the fungus digests the plant matter, and the ants in turn eat the fungus, particularly swollen hyphal tips called gongylidia. The mutualism originated roughly 50–60 million years ago in South America.

Agriculture

Attine colonies maintain their gardens with remarkable care: workers weed out contaminants, apply antibiotic secretions produced by symbiotic bacteria (such as Pseudonocardia), and remove a specialized parasitic fungus, Escovopsis, that attacks the gardens. Different attine lineages practice "lower" agriculture with unspecialized fungal cultivars or "higher" agriculture with highly domesticated cultivars that depend entirely on the ants.

Leafcutter ants

The best-known fungus growers are the leafcutter ants of the genera Atta and Acromyrmex, whose workers cut fresh leaves and flowers and carry them to the nest as substrate. Large Atta colonies may contain millions of workers with strong caste differentiation and are major herbivores in Neotropical ecosystems.

Evolution

Fungus farming in ants evolved once, in the common ancestor of the attine tribe, and is one of the few cases of true agriculture outside humans. The ants, their fungal cultivars, the Escovopsis parasite, and antibiotic-producing bacteria form a four-part symbiosis that has coevolved for tens of millions of years.
