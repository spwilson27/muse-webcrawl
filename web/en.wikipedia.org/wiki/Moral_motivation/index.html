Moral motivation

Moral motivation refers to the influence that moral judgements, beliefs, emotions, and perceptions exert on an agent's decisions and actions — in short, what moves a person to act rightly. The topic stands at the intersection of moral psychology, metaethics, and moral philosophy generally, and is central to debates about whether moral judgements are necessarily motivating.

Internalism and externalism

Motivational internalism holds that there is a necessary connection between judging an action right and being motivated (at least to some degree) to perform it. On this view, a person who sincerely judges that she ought to do something but feels absolutely no inclination to do it is either insincere, irrational, or does not fully grasp the judgement. Internalism is often associated with non-cognitivist theories and with Michael Smith's influential formulation in The Moral Problem (1994).

Motivational externalism denies the necessary connection: moral judgements are ordinary beliefs, which motivate only in combination with independent desires, following the Humean theory of motivation. The figure of the amoralist — who sincerely accepts moral claims but is entirely unmoved by them — is the externalist's key test case. David Brink and others have defended the coherence of the amoralist against internalist arguments.

Sources of moral motivation

Philosophers and psychologists have proposed many sources of moral motivation, including:

  • Reason — on Kant's account, recognition of duty motivates through respect (Achtung) for the moral law, a feeling produced by reason itself.
  • Sympathy and empathy — central to Hume and Adam Smith, and to modern care ethics.
  • Emotions — guilt, shame, indignation and admiration, which can move agents even against their settled beliefs.
  • Identity — acting in accordance with one's conception of oneself as a certain kind of person (Bernard Williams; Christine Korsgaard's notion of practical identity).
  • Socialisation and habit — Aristotle's account of virtue as cultivated disposition, in which the virtuous person takes pleasure in acting well.

Empirical research

Psychologists such as Augusto Blasi have studied moral motivation through the lens of moral identity, finding that people who rank moral traits as central to their self-concept show greater moral consistency. Social intuitionist models, such as Jonathan Haidt's, argue that moral action is often driven by fast, emotionally laden intuitions, with reasoning arriving afterwards as justification. Research on psychopathy — involving agents with intact moral knowledge but blunted affective responses — is frequently cited in the philosophical debate about whether purely cognitive moral judgement can motivate.
