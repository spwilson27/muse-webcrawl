John Knox (c. 1514 – 24 November 1572) was a Scottish minister, theologian, and writer who was a leader of the country's Reformation. He was the founder of the Church of Scotland and is considered one of the founders of Presbyterianism.

Born at Giffordgate, a street in Haddington, East Lothian, Knox is believed to have been educated at the University of St Andrews and to have worked as a notary-priest. Influenced by early church reformers such as George Wishart, he joined the movement to reform the Scottish church. He was caught up in the ecclesiastical and political events that involved the murder of Cardinal David Beaton in 1546 and the intervention of the regent Mary of Guise.

Early life

John Knox was born sometime between 1505 and 1515 at Giffordgate, Haddington, a small town some 30 kilometres east of Edinburgh. His father, William Knox, was a farmer. His mother died when he was a child. Knox was educated at the grammar school of Haddington, then studied theology at the University of St Andrews under John Major, one of the greatest scholars of the time. He was ordained a Catholic priest in 1536, and thereafter worked as a notary and as a tutor to the sons of local lairds.

Conversion and the siege of St Andrews Castle

In the early 1540s Knox came under the influence of the reformed preacher George Wishart, who had fled Scotland in 1538 but returned in 1544. Knox became Wishart's bodyguard and close associate. After Wishart was burned at the stake for heresy in March 1546 on the orders of Cardinal David Beaton, Beaton was himself murdered in May 1546 by a group of Protestant conspirators who seized St Andrews Castle. Knox joined the garrison in 1547 and preached his first sermon in the castle's parish church. When a French fleet forced the castle's surrender in July 1547, Knox was taken prisoner and served nineteen months as a galley slave aboard a French ship.

Exile in England and on the Continent

Released in 1549 through English government intervention, Knox was licensed to work in the Church of England, serving a congregation in Berwick-upon-Tweed and then in Newcastle upon Tyne. He declined a bishopric and, after the accession of the Catholic Mary I in 1553, fled to the Continent. He ministered to English exile congregations in Frankfurt am Main and Geneva, where he met John Calvin and absorbed the Reformed theology that would shape the Scottish church. In Geneva Knox published his most famous tract, The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women (1558), aimed at the Catholic queens Mary I of England and Mary of Guise.

Return to Scotland and the Reformation

Knox returned to Scotland in May 1559, in the midst of a rising by the Protestant Lords of the Congregation against the regent Mary of Guise. His preaching at Perth provoked riots and the destruction of religious houses. In 1560 the Scottish Parliament abolished papal authority, adopted a Reformed Confession of Faith, and forbade the celebration of Mass. Knox and five colleagues drafted the First Book of Discipline, which set out an ambitious programme of parish schools and poor relief. He became minister of St Giles' Cathedral in Edinburgh.

Confrontations with Mary, Queen of Scots

From 1561, when Mary, Queen of Scots returned from France to rule, Knox clashed repeatedly with the Catholic queen over the Mass and her marriage plans. His recorded interviews with Mary — recounted in his History of the Reformation in Scotland — are among the most dramatic exchanges in Scottish history. Knox survived plots and an assassination attempt, and remained a dominant public voice until his death in Edinburgh on 24 November 1572. He was buried in the churchyard of St Giles'.

Legacy

Knox's preaching and writing laid the foundations of the Scottish Reformation and of Presbyterian church government, which spread from Scotland to Ireland, North America, and beyond. His History of the Reformation in Scotland, published posthumously, remains a primary source for the period. His demand for a school in every parish underpinned Scotland's later reputation for universal education.
