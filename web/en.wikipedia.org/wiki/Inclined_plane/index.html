Inclined plane

Forces on a block resting on an inclined plane.

An inclined plane, also known as a ramp, is a flat supporting surface tilted at an angle, with one end higher than the other, used as an aid for raising or lowering a load. The inclined plane is one of the six classical simple machines defined by Renaissance scientists. Inclined planes are used to move heavy loads over vertical obstacles; examples vary from a ramp used to load goods into a truck, to a person walking up a ramp to reach a higher floor.

Mechanical advantage

Moving an object up an inclined plane requires less force than lifting it straight up, at a cost of an increase in the distance moved. The mechanical advantage of an inclined plane, the factor by which the force is reduced, is equal to the ratio of the length of the sloped surface to the height it spans. For a frictionless plane inclined at angle θ to the horizontal, the force needed to hold a load of weight W is W sin θ, so gentler slopes give greater advantage but require longer ramps.

Friction

In practice friction reduces the advantage and can hold a load stationary on the slope: an object will remain at rest if the angle is below the angle of repose, where the component of gravity along the plane is balanced by static friction. Inclined plane problems — resolving weight into components parallel and perpendicular to the surface — are a staple of introductory mechanics teaching.

History

Ramps were used in ancient construction, including for moving stone blocks in Egyptian building projects, though the details remain debated. Simon Stevin derived the law of the inclined plane in 1586 using his famous thought experiment of a wreath of spheres draped over a triangular prism. Galileo used inclined planes to slow the acceleration of falling bodies and study their motion, rolling balls down grooved ramps to measure the law of falling bodies.

Applications

Everyday applications include loading ramps, wheelchair access ramps, roads and railways climbing hills (where grades are limited by adhesion), aircraft escape slides, and the wedge and screw, which can be understood as inclined planes — a screw is a plane wrapped around a cylinder.
