An automatic transmission (sometimes abbreviated AT) is a multi-speed transmission used in motor vehicles that does not require any input from the driver to change forward gears under normal driving conditions. Vehicles equipped with conventional automatics need only accelerator and brake pedals, freeing the driver from operating a clutch and gear lever.

Hydraulic automatics

The classic automatic combines a fluid torque converter with one or more planetary (epicyclic) gearsets. Combinations of clutches and brakes lock different elements of the gearsets to yield the available ratios — modern units offer eight to ten forward speeds. Early examples include the 1940 General Motors Hydra-Matic, the first mass-produced fully automatic transmission. Shift control, once purely hydraulic, is now electronic, adapting shift points to driving style and conditions.

Other types

Continuously variable transmissions (CVTs) vary the drive ratio steplessly, usually via a belt or chain between variable-diameter pulleys. Dual-clutch transmissions (DCTs) use two clutches for odd and even gears to achieve very fast shifts, while automated manual transmissions (AMTs) robotise a conventional manual gearbox. In common usage "automatic" may refer to any transmission that shifts without driver intervention, including these types.

Prevalence

Automatics have long dominated the North American market, while manuals historically prevailed in Europe and much of Asia; improved efficiency and the growth of hybrid and electric vehicles — which usually need no multi-speed gearbox at all — have narrowed the gap worldwide.
