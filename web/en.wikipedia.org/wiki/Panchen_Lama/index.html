Panchen Lama

The Panchen Lama is a tulku (reincarnate lama) lineage in the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, second in spiritual rank only to the Dalai Lama. The Panchen Lama's traditional seat is Tashilhunpo Monastery in Shigatse, Tibet. The title derives from paṇḍita (scholar) and the Tibetan chenpo (great): "Great Scholar." The Panchen Lama is regarded as an emanation of the Buddha Amitābha.

The lineage

The first Panchen Lama, Khedrup Gelek Pelzang (1385–1438), was a disciple of Tsongkhapa, the founder of the Gelug school. The title was applied retroactively to him and two other early incarnations; Lobsang Chökyi Gyaltsen (1570–1662), the fourth Panchen Lama, was the first to hold the title in his lifetime, receiving it from the Fifth Dalai Lama. Traditionally, each of the two lamas plays a role in recognizing the other's reincarnation.

Historical role

The Panchen Lamas wielded considerable political as well as religious authority in Tibet, sometimes in rivalry with the Dalai Lamas in Lhasa. Chinese emperors of the Qing dynasty cultivated relations with the Panchen Lamas; the Sixth Panchen Lama traveled to the court of the Qianlong Emperor in 1780. In the 20th century, the Ninth Panchen Lama spent years in exile in China after a dispute with the Thirteenth Dalai Lama's government over taxation.

The 10th and 11th Panchen Lamas

The 10th Panchen Lama, Choekyi Gyaltsen (1938–1989), initially cooperated with the People's Republic of China after 1959 but in 1962 submitted the 70,000 Character Petition, a detailed critique of Chinese policies in Tibet, for which he was imprisoned for over a decade. Shortly before his sudden death in January 1989, he had been partially rehabilitated and delivered a speech critical of past policies.

After his death, the search for the 11th Panchen Lama produced a lasting dispute. In May 1995 the Dalai Lama recognized six-year-old Gedhun Choekyi Nyima; days later the boy and his family were taken into Chinese custody and have not been seen publicly since — human rights groups have called him "the world's youngest political prisoner." Beijing installed another boy, Gyaincain Norbu (also rendered Gyaltsen Norbu), who serves in official roles in China but is not recognized by most Tibetans in exile.
