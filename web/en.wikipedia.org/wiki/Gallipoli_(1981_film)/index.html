Gallipoli is a 1981 Australian war drama film directed by Peter Weir and starring Mel Gibson and Mark Lee. The film follows two young Australian sprinters who enlist in the Australian Imperial Force during the First World War and are sent to fight in the Gallipoli campaign in Turkey. It is regarded as a classic of the Australian New Wave and a defining film about the ANZAC legend.

Plot

In Western Australia in 1915, Archy Hamilton (Mark Lee), a talented young sprinter from a farming family, dreams of competing at the highest level but feels compelled to enlist. He meets Frank Dunne (Mel Gibson), a cynical, working-class former railway worker and fellow runner who initially wants nothing to do with the war. The two become close friends during a race meeting and eventually travel together to Perth, where both enlist — Frank, having failed to get into the Light Horse, joins the infantry alongside Archy.

After training in Egypt, where they enjoy leave beneath the pyramids, the men are shipped to Gallipoli. Frank, now a runner (messenger) for a battalion headquarters, is separated from Archy, who takes part in the Battle of the Nek on 7 August 1915 — an ill-fated bayonet charge against entrenched Turkish machine guns. Ordered to attack despite the failure of the preceding waves, Archy's unit goes over the top. Frank sprints desperately across the battlefield to deliver a message that might countermand the attack, but arrives moments too late; the film ends on a freeze-frame of Archy being struck by bullets, echoing a photograph of a runner at the finish line.

Production and themes

Weir and screenwriter David Williamson drew on C. E. W. Bean's official history of the ANZACs and on veterans' accounts. The film explores the loss of innocence, mateship, and the tension between British command decisions and Australian soldiers who bore their consequences. Its score by Brian May makes prominent use of Tomaso Albinoni's Adagio in G minor and electronic passages by Jean-Michel Jarre.

Reception

Gallipoli won eight AFI Awards, including Best Film, Best Director, and Best Actor for Gibson, and was a major box-office success in Australia. It remains one of the most acclaimed Australian films and is frequently shown in connection with Anzac Day commemorations.
