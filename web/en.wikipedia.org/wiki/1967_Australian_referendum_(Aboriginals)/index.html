The 1967 Australian referendum, held on 27 May 1967, asked voters to approve two changes to the Constitution of Australia relating to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. It remains the most successful referendum in Australian history, passing with 90.77 per cent of the national vote and majorities in all six states.

Changes proposed

The referendum proposed, first, to delete the words "other than the aboriginal race" from section 51(xxvi), which had excluded Aboriginal people from the Commonwealth Parliament's power to make special laws for people of any race. Second, it proposed to repeal section 127 entirely, which had provided that "aboriginal natives shall not be counted" when reckoning the population of the Commonwealth or a state — a provision that had excluded Aboriginal people from the census counts used for electoral and financial purposes.

Campaign

The proposal had bipartisan support: it was put forward by the Holt Liberal–Country Party government and supported by the Labor opposition. Campaigning was led by Aboriginal and non-Indigenous activists, including Faith Bandler of the Federal Council for the Advancement of Aborigines and Torres Strait Islanders (FCAATSI), and the Aboriginal activist and poet Oodgeroo Noonuccal. Because no federal "No" case was published, the campaign was overwhelmingly positive.

Results and significance

The Yes vote exceeded 90 per cent nationally. A popular misconception holds that the referendum gave Aboriginal people the right to vote or Australian citizenship; in fact, federal voting rights had been extended in 1962, and citizenship dated from 1949. The referendum's real effects were to allow the Commonwealth to legislate for Aboriginal people and to require their inclusion in population counts. It is widely regarded as a landmark in the Australian reconciliation movement and a turning point in Indigenous affairs policy.
