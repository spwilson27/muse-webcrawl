Granger Laws

The Granger Laws were a series of laws passed in western states of the United States after the American Civil War to regulate grain elevator and railroad freight rates to the benefit of farmers, who were being pressured by ever-increasing rail costs. The laws were passed in the states of Minnesota, Iowa, Wisconsin, and Illinois.

The laws were sponsored by a group of farmers known as the National Grange of the Order of Patrons of Husbandry, generally known simply as the Grange, an organization founded in 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley. The Granger Laws effectively made price discrimination by railroads illegal.

Background

After the Civil War, farmers in the Midwest were dependent on railroads and grain-elevator operators to transport and store their crops. A small number of railroads, often effectively monopolies in a given region, could charge rates that farmers regarded as extortionate. The Grange grew rapidly in the 1870s and pressed state legislatures to act.

The laws

Illinois passed a law in 1871 to regulate the railroads, putting into effect what became known as the Granger Laws. The laws fixed maximum freight and passenger rates, prohibited rate discrimination, and created state commissions to oversee railroad practices. Wisconsin's 1874 Potter Law was among the most stringent, setting detailed maximum rate schedules.

Supreme Court reaction

The constitutionality of the Granger Laws was upheld in the landmark case Munn v. Illinois (1877), in which the Supreme Court held that a state could regulate a private business that was "affected with a public interest," such as grain elevators and railroads. Other decisions of 1877, collectively known as the Granger Cases, sustained similar regulation.

In Wabash, St. Louis & Pacific Railway Co. v. Illinois (1886), however, the Court held that states could not regulate interstate commerce, a limitation that helped prompt Congress to pass the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887, creating the Interstate Commerce Commission and transferring much rate regulation to the federal level.

Legacy

The Granger Laws marked an early assertion of the regulatory power of government over large corporations and laid the groundwork for later Progressive Era reforms and federal regulation of railroads and other industries.
