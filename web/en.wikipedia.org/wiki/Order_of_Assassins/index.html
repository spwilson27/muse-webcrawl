The Order of Assassins or simply the Assassins (from Arabic: Ḥashshāshīyīn) were a Nizari Isma'ili order that existed in Persia and Syria from the 11th to the 13th centuries. Founded by Hassan-i Sabbah, the order maintained a network of mountain fortresses — most famously Alamut in Persia and Masyaf in Syria — and became renowned, and feared, for the targeted killings of political and religious figures carried out by its devotees, the fidā'iyyīn (fedayeen).

Weak in conventional military strength compared with the Seljuk Empire and later the Crusader states, the Nizaris used assassination as a deliberate strategy of deterrence and survival. Their victims included the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk (1092) and, in the Levant, figures such as Conrad of Montferrat (1192). The Persian branch was destroyed by the Mongol invasion under Hülegü in 1256, when Alamut surrendered; the Syrian branch was subdued by the Mamluk sultan Baibars by 1273.

Etymology

The name "Assassin" derives from the Arabic hashshāshīn, commonly interpreted as "hashish users", a label applied by the order's enemies. Modern scholars generally regard the hashish stories popularised in the West — including the tale, relayed by Marco Polo, of drugged devotees shown a garden of paradise — as polemical legends rather than fact.

Origins

The Nizaris arose from a succession dispute in the Fatimid Caliphate in 1094, supporting the claim of Nizar against his brother al-Musta'li. Hassan-i Sabbah, a Persian Isma'ili missionary, had seized the fortress of Alamut in 1090 and built a decentralised state of valley fortresses across Persia and later Syria.

Tactics and organisation

Fedayeen were trained devotees prepared to carry out killings, often in public and with a dagger, frequently accepting death in the attempt. The order was hierarchically organised, with ranks from the chief at Alamut down through missionaries (da'is) and the fedayeen themselves.

End of the order

In 1256 Hülegü's Mongol army compelled the surrender of Alamut, and its last lord, Rukn al-Din Khurshah, was killed; the Persian Nizari state ceased to exist. The Syrian fortresses held out longer but were absorbed by the Mamluks by the early 1270s. The Nizari imamate survived in concealment, and the community continues today under the Aga Khans.

Legacy

The order's reputation gave the word "assassin" to many languages as a term for a targeted murderer of prominent figures. The Assassins have been a rich subject for literature and popular culture, including the Assassin's Creed video game series, which loosely adapts their history.
