Brush border

A brush border (striated border or brush border membrane) is the microvilli-covered surface of simple cuboidal and simple columnar epithelium found in different parts of the body. Microvilli are approximately 100 nanometers in diameter and their length varies from approximately 100 to 2,000 nanometers. Because individual microvilli are so small and are tightly packed in the brush border, individual microvilli can only be resolved using electron microscopes; with a light microscope they can usually only be seen collectively as a fuzzy fringe at the surface of the epithelium. This fuzzy appearance gave rise to the term brush border, as early anatomists noted that this structure bore a resemblance to bristles on a paintbrush.

The brush border morphology increases a cell's surface area, a trait which is especially useful in absorptive cells. Absorptive cells that need to absorb lots of nutrients from the lumen, such as the enterocytes of the small intestine and the cells of the proximal convoluted tubule in the kidney, use the microvilli structure to increase their surface area and thus increase the rate of absorption.

Locations

Small intestine

The microvilli that constitute the brush border have enzymes for this final part of digestion anchored into their apical plasma membrane as integral membrane proteins. These enzymes are found near to the transporters that will then allow absorption of the digested materials. In the small intestine, the brush border enzymes include lactase, sucrase-isomaltase, maltase-glucoamylase, aminopeptidases and enterokinase.

Kidney

In the kidney, the brush border is found in the proximal convoluted tubule of the nephron. The microvilli here are somewhat shorter than those in the intestine and the border stains strongly with the periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) technique. The proximal tubule brush border contains enzymes such as alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, and plays a major role in reabsorbing glucose, amino acids, ions and water from the glomerular filtrate.

Placenta

Brush borders are also present on the syncytiotrophoblast surface of the human placenta, where they mediate exchange between maternal and fetal blood.

Clinical significance

Damage to the intestinal brush border occurs in coeliac disease, where villous atrophy and loss of brush-border enzymes such as lactase lead to malabsorption. Congenital deficiencies of brush border enzymes, for example congenital sucrase-isomaltase deficiency and congenital lactase deficiency, cause intolerance of specific sugars. In the kidney, loss of the proximal tubule brush border is an early histological sign of acute tubular injury.
