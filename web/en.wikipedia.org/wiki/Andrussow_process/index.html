Andrussow process

The Andrussow process (also called the Andrussow oxidation) is an industrial process for the production of hydrogen cyanide (HCN) from methane, ammonia and oxygen over a platinum-based catalyst. It is the dominant commercial route to hydrogen cyanide, a key intermediate for adiponitrile (used in nylon), sodium cyanide for gold mining, and methyl methacrylate. The process was developed by the German chemist Leonid Andrussow at IG Farben, who published it in 1927 and patented it in the following years.

Chemistry

In the process, a mixture of methane and ammonia is reacted with oxygen (usually air) at about 1,200 °C over gauzes of a platinum–rhodium alloy (typically 90% Pt / 10% Rh). The overall reaction is:

CH4 + NH3 + 1.5 O2 → HCN + 3 H2O

The reaction is strongly exothermic, and part of the methane combusts to provide the heat required. Contact time over the catalyst is only a few milliseconds, and the product gas — containing roughly 8–10% hydrogen cyanide — is rapidly quenched to prevent decomposition of the HCN.

Process operation and byproducts

Unreacted ammonia is removed by absorption in sulfuric acid (forming ammonium sulfate) or phosphoric acid, and the hydrogen cyanide is absorbed in water or used directly in adjacent plants. Competing side reactions produce carbon monoxide, hydrogen and nitrogen, and some methane passes through unreacted. Catalyst gauzes lose platinum over time through volatilization; catch gauzes of palladium alloy recover part of the metal.

Alternative processes

The other major industrial route is the BMA process (Blausäure aus Methan und Ammoniak), developed by Degussa, in which methane and ammonia react endothermically in externally heated platinum-lined tubes without oxygen, giving a richer HCN stream plus hydrogen. Hydrogen cyanide is also recovered as a byproduct of acrylonitrile manufacture by the Sohio process.
