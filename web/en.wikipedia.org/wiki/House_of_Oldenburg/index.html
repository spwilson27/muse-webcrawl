The House of Oldenburg is an ancient North German dynasty whose members have ruled, among other countries, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Greece, and — through the Holstein-Gottorp branch — Russia. It is one of Europe's most influential royal houses, and the current monarchs of Denmark and Norway and the king of the United Kingdom (through the Glücksburg line of his mother) have Oldenburg ancestry. The family takes its name from Oldenburg in Lower Saxony, where its first known member, Elimar I, was count from 1101.

Rise to the Danish throne

The dynasty's fortunes transformed in 1448, when Count Christian of Oldenburg was elected king of Denmark as Christian I, subsequently becoming king of Norway (1450) and Sweden (1457) under the Kalmar Union. His descendants ruled Denmark-Norway for centuries. The main line became extinct in the male line in 1863 with Frederick VII, and the crown passed to Christian of Schleswig-Holstein-Sonderburg-Glücksburg, who reigned as Christian IX and became known as the "father-in-law of Europe" for the marriages of his children into many royal houses.

Branches

The Glücksburg branch has provided kings of Denmark, Norway (from 1905), and Greece (1863–1924 and 1935–1973). The Holstein-Gottorp branch ruled in Sweden (1751–1818) and, through Peter III, the Russian Empire, where its descendants reigned until 1917. Other branches ruled the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein and the Grand Duchy of Oldenburg within Germany until 1918.
