162173 Ryugu, provisionally designated 1999 JU3, is a near-Earth object and a potentially hazardous asteroid of the Apollo group. It measures approximately 900 metres (3,000 ft) in diameter and is a carbonaceous C-type asteroid with a distinctive "spinning top" shape caused by its rotation. It is named after Ryūgū-jō, the undersea palace of the dragon king in Japanese folklore.

Discovery and naming

Ryugu was discovered on 10 May 1999 by astronomers of the Lincoln Near-Earth Asteroid Research (LINEAR) project at the Lincoln Laboratory Experimental Test Site near Socorro, New Mexico. The name Ryugu was announced in 2015 and references the tale of Urashima Tarō, in which a fisherman is carried to the undersea palace Ryūgū and returns with a mysterious box — a parallel to a sample-return mission.

Hayabusa2 mission

The Japanese space agency JAXA launched the Hayabusa2 spacecraft in December 2014. It arrived at Ryugu on 27 June 2018, deployed the MINERVA-II rovers and the MASCOT lander (which made the first successful landings on an asteroid), and collected subsurface and surface samples using tantalum projectiles. Hayabusa2 departed in November 2019 and its return capsule landed near Woomera, Australia, on 5 December 2020, delivering about 5.4 grams of material to Earth. Analysis of the samples has found water-bearing minerals, organic compounds, and amino acids.

Physical characteristics

Ryugu is a rubble pile with bulk density around 1.19 g/cm³ and very low albedo (about 0.045), making it one of the darkest objects observed in the Solar System. Its equatorial ridge gives it an oblate, top-like shape. Because its orbit brings it close to Earth, it is classified as potentially hazardous, though it poses no known impact threat in the foreseeable future.
