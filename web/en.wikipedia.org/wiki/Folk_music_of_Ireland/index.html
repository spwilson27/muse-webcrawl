Folk music of Ireland

The folk music of Ireland, usually called Irish traditional music (Irish: ceol traidisiúnta), encompasses dance music (reels, jigs, hornpipes, polkas, slides and mazurkas), slow airs, and songs in Irish and English, including sean-nós ("old style") unaccompanied singing. It is played in sessions in pubs and homes and at fleadhanna (festivals) organised by Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann, and it spread worldwide through the Irish diaspora, deeply influencing American country, bluegrass and folk music.

Instruments and style

The core melody instruments are the fiddle, wooden Irish flute, tin whistle, uilleann pipes (Ireland's distinctive bellows-blown bagpipes), button accordion, concertina, tenor banjo, mandolin and harp; accompaniment comes from guitar, bouzouki, piano and the bodhrán frame drum. Melodies are usually played in unison with dense ornamentation — rolls, crans, cuts and triplets on fiddle, flute and pipes. Regional styles are recognised, from the driving music of Donegal and Sligo to the lyrical styles of Clare and East Galway.

History

The tradition has roots in the music of Gaelic Ireland and the harping tradition patronised by the old aristocracy (the blind harper Turlough O'Carolan, 1670–1738, composed hundreds of tunes). It absorbed dances and instruments from Britain and Europe in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Mass emigration carried it to America, where 78 rpm recordings by Michael Coleman, James Morrison and Paddy Killoran in the 1920s reshaped the music at home. The folk revival of the 1960s, Seán Ó Riada's Ceoltóirí Chualann and groups such as The Chieftains, The Dubliners, Planxty and The Bothy Band renewed its popularity; the Fleadh Cheoil (founded 1951) and the 1994 Riverdance phenomenon brought Irish music and dance to global audiences.

Songs

The song tradition includes rebel and emigration ballads ("The Fields of Athenry", "Danny Boy" to the tune of the Londonderry Air), music-hall songs, and the highly ornamented Irish-language sean-nós. Song and dance tunes alike are preserved in landmark collections from Edward Bunting's manuscripts (1792 onward) to Francis O'Neill's Music of Ireland (1903).
