Red phosphorus is an allotrope of phosphorus, a dark red to violet powder that is much more stable and far less toxic than white phosphorus. It is produced by heating white phosphorus to about 250–300 °C in the absence of air, which converts the reactive P4 tetrahedra into a polymeric network. Unlike white phosphorus, red phosphorus does not ignite spontaneously in air at room temperature and does not glow in the dark.

Structure

Red phosphorus is largely amorphous and consists of long chains and networks formed by linked P4 units. More ordered crystalline variants include violet (Hittorf's) phosphorus, which has a complex monoclinic structure of cross-linked tubes, and fibrous red phosphorus. Heating red phosphorus under pressure can produce black phosphorus, the most thermodynamically stable allotrope.

Applications

The best-known use of red phosphorus is in the striking surface of safety matchboxes: the striker contains red phosphorus mixed with powdered glass and binder; when a match head (containing an oxidiser such as potassium chlorate) is drawn across it, friction converts a trace of red phosphorus to white phosphorus, igniting the match. Red phosphorus is also used in flame retardants, smoke bombs, incendiary compositions, and in the semiconductor and fertiliser industries as an intermediate.

Safety and regulation

Red phosphorus is comparatively benign, though it can be contaminated with residual white phosphorus and burns on strong heating to give phosphorus pentoxide fumes. Because it can be converted into white phosphorus and is a precursor in the illicit manufacture of methamphetamine, its sale is regulated in several countries — in the United States it is a DEA List I chemical.
