Héloïse (c. 1100/1101 – 16 May 1164), also known as Héloïse d'Argenteuil, was a French nun, abbess, philosopher and writer, celebrated for her learning and for her love affair and correspondence with the philosopher Peter Abelard. Renowned in her own lifetime as one of the most educated women in Europe, she is remembered for her surviving letters, which are among the most remarkable documents of twelfth-century intellectual and emotional life.

Early life and the affair with Abelard

Héloïse was raised and educated at the convent of Sainte-Marie at Argenteuil, where she gained an unusual reputation for scholarship: she knew Latin, Greek and reportedly Hebrew, and studied philosophy and theology as well as literature. She came to live in Paris with her uncle Fulbert, a canon of Notre-Dame, who engaged the famous teacher Peter Abelard to tutor her, around 1115–1116.

Teacher and pupil became lovers. Héloïse became pregnant and was sent to Abelard's family in Brittany, where she gave birth to a son, named Astrolabe. To placate Fulbert, the pair married in secret — but Héloïse famously resisted marriage, arguing (in Abelard's later account) that it would damage his career and that she preferred to be his mistress rather than his wife, valuing love given freely over the bond of marriage. When the marriage was publicised against their wishes, Fulbert's kinsmen took revenge: they broke into Abelard's room at night and castrated him. Héloïse, at Abelard's insistence, took the veil at Argenteuil.

Abbess of the Paraclete

When the nuns of Argenteuil were expelled in 1129, Abelard gave Héloïse and her community his oratory of the Paraclete, near Nogent-sur-Seine, which she built into a flourishing abbey. As its abbess she was widely respected: she governed the house for over thirty years, corresponded with bishops and abbots, and received commendations from figures such as Bernard of Clairvaux and Pope Eugenius III. She also composed the Problemata Heloissae, a set of 42 questions on difficulties in Scripture addressed to Abelard, and is credited with hymns and other writings for her community.

The Letters

The correspondence between Héloïse and Abelard survives in a famous collection. It begins with Abelard's Historia Calamitatum ("The Story of My Calamities"), a letter of consolation to a friend that fell into Héloïse's hands and prompted her to write to him. Her first letters are passionate and unrepentant: she declares that she sought nothing in him but himself, and that she would rather be called his whore than be empress as another man's wife. The later letters turn to the governance of her abbey and the request for a rule for nuns, but scholars continue to debate the authenticity, composition and meaning of the exchange, which some have proposed was edited or even authored as a unified literary work.

Whatever their precise genesis, Héloïse's letters — whether she is treated as their historical author or as the compelling voice constructed in the texts — present one of the earliest and most powerful statements of a woman's intellectual and emotional independence in medieval literature.

Death and legacy

Héloïse died on 16 May 1164 at the Paraclete and was buried beside Abelard, who had died in 1142 and whose body she had received into the abbey. Their shared tomb became a place of pilgrimage; in the nineteenth century their remains were transferred to Père Lachaise Cemetery in Paris, where their Gothic tomb remains one of the cemetery's most visited monuments, attracting lovers who leave letters there. The story of Abelard and Héloïse has inspired countless works, from the poetry of Alexander Pope ("Eloisa to Abelard", 1717) to modern novels, plays and films.
