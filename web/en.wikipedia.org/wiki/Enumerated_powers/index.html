Enumerated powers

The enumerated powers (also called expressed powers, explicit powers or delegated powers) of the United States Congress are the powers granted to the federal government that are listed in the United States Constitution. Most are found in Article I, Section 8. The Constitution embodies the principle of federalism: the federal government has only the powers granted to it, while the states retain the rest, as confirmed by the Tenth Amendment.

The list

Article I, Section 8 grants Congress power to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts and excises; borrow money; regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Native American tribes (the Commerce Clause); establish rules of naturalization and bankruptcy; coin money and punish counterfeiting; establish post offices; promote science and the arts by granting copyrights and patents; constitute lower federal courts; punish piracies; declare war; raise and support armies and a navy; and make rules for the armed forces.

Necessary and Proper Clause

The final clause of Section 8 — the power "to make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers" — was expansively read in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), sustaining implied powers such as chartering a national bank.

Interpretation and limits

How far enumerated powers extend, especially commerce power, has been contested from the New Deal cases through United States v. Lopez (1995) and NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), where the Court again treated enumeration as a real limit on federal authority.
