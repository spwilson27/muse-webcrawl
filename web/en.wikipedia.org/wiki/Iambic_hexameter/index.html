Iambic hexameter

Iambic hexameter is a metrical line of verse consisting of six iambic feet — twelve syllables in its basic form, each foot being an iamb (unstressed followed by stressed, or in classical quantitative metre, short followed by long). In ancient Greek verse the equivalent line, the iambic trimeter, counted the six iambs in three pairs (metra) and was the standard metre of dialogue in tragedy and comedy; in modern terms the same line is described as iambic hexameter.

Scansion of a six-foot iambic line: da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM.

In classical poetry

The iambic trimeter/hexameter was considered the metre closest to natural speech and was used by Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, and Aristophanes for dialogue. Roman poets, notably Seneca in his tragedies and the writers of Roman comedy following Greek models, adopted the iambic senarius. Horace used iambic metres extensively in his Epodes.

In later literature

In English, pure iambic hexameter (the alexandrine's iambic cousin) appears as the closing line of the Spenserian stanza, which ends each nine-line stanza with a six-foot line after eight pentameters. In French poetry the twelve-syllable alexandrine — syllabic rather than strictly iambic — became the canonical line of classical tragedy. In Russian literature, iambic hexameter was used by poets of the 18th century for odes and tragedies before iambic tetrameter became dominant.
