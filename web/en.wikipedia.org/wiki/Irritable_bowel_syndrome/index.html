Irritable bowel syndrome

Irritable bowel syndrome
Specialty: Gastroenterology
Symptoms: Diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain
Usual onset: Before 45 years of age
Causes: Unknown
Frequency: ~10–15% (developed world)

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by a group of symptoms that commonly include abdominal pain, abdominal bloating, and changes in the consistency of bowel movements. These may occur over a long time, sometimes for years. IBS can negatively affect quality of life and may lead to missed school or work, or reduced productivity at work.

Disorders such as anxiety, major depression, and chronic fatigue syndrome are common among people with IBS.

Signs and symptoms

The primary symptoms of IBS are diarrhea, constipation, or a mixture of both, typically accompanied by abdominal pain and bloating. Pain often improves after a bowel movement. Subtypes are classified by predominant stool pattern: IBS-D (diarrhea-predominant), IBS-C (constipation-predominant), IBS-M (mixed), and IBS-U (unsubtyped).

Causes

The cause of IBS is not known, but multiple factors have been proposed — including alterations in gut motility, visceral hypersensitivity, brain–gut interactions, changes in the gut microbiome, low-grade inflammation, and psychological stress. A bout of infectious gastroenteritis can trigger a post-infectious form of IBS.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on symptoms using the Rome IV criteria, after excluding conditions with similar presentations such as celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal cancer. There is no specific laboratory test for IBS.

Management

Management focuses on symptom relief: dietary changes (including low-FODMAP diets), soluble fiber, antispasmodics, laxatives or antidiarrheals, certain medications acting on the gut, and psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy in some cases. Education and reassurance are important.

Epidemiology

IBS affects an estimated 10–15% of the population in the developed world, with onset most often before age 45. It is more common in women than men. IBS does not increase the risk of serious gastrointestinal disease or shorten life expectancy.
