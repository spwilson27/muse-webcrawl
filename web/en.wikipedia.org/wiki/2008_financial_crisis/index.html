2008 financial crisis

The 2008 financial crisis, also known as the global financial crisis (GFC), was a severe worldwide economic crisis that began in the United States housing market and became the worst financial collapse since the Great Depression. Triggered by the bursting of the US housing bubble and widespread losses on subprime mortgage-backed securities, it caused the failure or rescue of major financial institutions, a global credit freeze, deep recessions in most advanced economies, and large-scale government interventions including bank bailouts and unconventional monetary policy.

Causes

Explanations emphasise mutually reinforcing factors: a long housing bubble fed by low interest rates, lax lending standards and subprime and adjustable-rate mortgages; securitisation that packaged risky loans into mortgage-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations stamped with investment-grade ratings; high leverage in investment banks and the shadow banking system; complex derivatives such as credit default swaps (notably those written by AIG); failures of credit rating agencies and regulators; and assumptions that house prices could not fall nationwide. When prices turned in 2006–07, defaults rose and the opaque securities became impossible to value, destroying trust between financial institutions.

Timeline of the crisis

Distress surfaced in 2007 with the collapse of subprime lenders, the freezing of BNP Paribas funds in August 2007, and the run on the UK bank Northern Rock. In March 2008 Bear Stearns was rescued through a forced sale to JPMorgan Chase. The crisis peaked in September 2008: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac entered conservatorship, Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on 15 September — the largest in US history — Merrill Lynch was sold to Bank of America, and the insurer AIG received an emergency Federal Reserve facility to avert collapse. Money market funds "broke the buck", interbank lending seized up, and stock markets worldwide plunged; the ensuing Great Recession spread through trade and banking channels, later morphing into the European sovereign debt crisis.

Responses

Governments and central banks responded with deposit guarantees, bank recapitalisations, the US Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP, authorised at $700 billion), dramatic interest-rate cuts, quantitative easing, and coordinated action at the G20 London summit of April 2009. Iceland's banking system collapsed entirely, and several European states required international rescue programmes.

Consequences and reform

Millions lost homes and jobs; US unemployment reached 10 per cent and output losses were estimated in the trillions of dollars. Reforms included the US Dodd–Frank Act, the international Basel III capital framework, stress testing, resolution regimes for failing banks, and the creation of the Financial Stability Board to coordinate global regulation. The crisis reshaped politics — fuelling movements from Occupy Wall Street to the Tea Party — and remains debated among economists over how far it was foreseeable and preventable.
