Biblical studies

Biblical studies is the academic application of a set of diverse disciplines to the study of the Bible (the Old Testament and New Testament). For its theory and methods, the field draws on disciplines ranging from archaeology and ancient history to literary criticism, linguistics, philology, and social science. Biblical studies is practiced in universities and seminaries worldwide, by scholars of varied religious backgrounds and of none.

Sub-disciplines

  • Textual criticism — the reconstruction of the most accurate text of the biblical books from the surviving manuscripts, including the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint, and the Dead Sea Scrolls.
  • Biblical archaeology — the excavation and interpretation of the material culture of the ancient Near East and the biblical world.
  • Historical criticism — including source criticism (identifying the documents behind the text, such as the sources of the Pentateuch), form criticism, and redaction criticism.
  • Literary criticism — narrative, rhetorical, and canonical approaches to the Bible as literature.
  • Exegesis and hermeneutics — the interpretation of texts and the theory of interpretation, including historical-grammatical method and newer reader-oriented approaches.

History

While interpretation of scripture is as old as the texts themselves, modern biblical studies emerged with Renaissance humanism, the Reformation, and especially the Enlightenment, when scholars such as Baruch Spinoza and Richard Simon began treating the Bible as a historically produced collection of documents. In the nineteenth century, scholars such as Julius Wellhausen developed the documentary hypothesis for the Pentateuch, and textual critics like Tischendorf — discoverer of Codex Sinaiticus — transformed knowledge of the New Testament text. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1946–47 revolutionized study of the Hebrew Bible and Second Temple Judaism.

Institutions and journals

The Society of Biblical Literature (founded 1880) is the principal professional society in North America and publishes the Journal of Biblical Literature. Other major venues include the Harvard Theological Review, the Journal for the Study of the Old Testament, and New Testament Studies. Biblical studies is often taught within theology faculties in Europe and religious studies departments elsewhere.
