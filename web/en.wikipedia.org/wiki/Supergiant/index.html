Supergiant

Supergiants are among the most massive and most luminous stars. They occupy the top region of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram with absolute visual magnitudes between about −3 and −8. The temperature and spectral type classes of supergiants include the whole range from about class O to M, and their radii are typically from 30 to over 1,000 times the radius of the Sun. Supergiants are short-lived: massive stars evolve off the main sequence into the supergiant phase before ending as supernovae.

Red supergiants like Betelgeuse are among the largest known stars by volume.

Properties

Supergiants are classified by luminosity class Ia (bright supergiants), Iab, and Ib (less luminous supergiants). Blue supergiants such as Rigel and Deneb are hot and luminous, while red supergiants such as Betelgeuse and Antares are cool, enormous, and often variable, losing mass through strong stellar winds. Their spectra show narrow absorption lines because of their low surface gravity, a key diagnostic used to distinguish them from dwarfs of the same spectral type.

Evolution

Stars initially more massive than about eight solar masses leave the main sequence after exhausting core hydrogen and pass through supergiant phases as they burn heavier elements in shells and cores. Depending on mass loss and composition, a supergiant may explode as a Type II or stripped-envelope supernova, leaving a neutron star or black hole. Blue loops and transitions between blue and red supergiant states occur in intermediate-mass models.
