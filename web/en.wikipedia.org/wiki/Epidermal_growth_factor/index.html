Epidermal growth factor

Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is a protein that stimulates cell growth and differentiation by binding to its receptor, EGFR. Human EGF is a 6-kDa protein with 53 amino acid residues and three intramolecular disulfide bonds.

History

EGF was discovered by Stanley Cohen through experiments in the 1950s and 1960s on factors in mouse submaxillary glands that accelerated eyelid opening and tooth eruption in newborn mice. Cohen's work, together with Rita Levi-Montalcini's discovery of nerve growth factor, was recognised with the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Function

EGF is found in many body fluids, including saliva, milk, urine, and plasma. Binding of EGF to the extracellular domain of EGFR triggers receptor dimerisation and activation of its intracellular tyrosine kinase, initiating signalling cascades such as the MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt, and JAK/STAT pathways. These drive proliferation, survival, migration, and differentiation of epithelial and other cell types, and EGF plays important roles in wound healing and tissue maintenance.

Medical relevance

Overexpression or activating mutation of EGFR is common in cancers of the lung, colon, head and neck, and pancreas; drugs that block the pathway — monoclonal antibodies such as cetuximab and small-molecule kinase inhibitors such as gefitinib and erlotinib — are established cancer therapies. Recombinant EGF has also been used to promote healing of diabetic foot ulcers and burns in some countries, and EGF appears as an ingredient in some cosmetic products, though such uses remain controversial.
