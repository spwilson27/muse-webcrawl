Pachomius (/pəˈkoʊmiəs/; Greek: Παχώμιος Pakhṓmios; Coptic: Ⲡⲁϧⲱⲙ; c. 292 – 9 May 348), also known as Saint Pachomius the Great, was an Egyptian saint generally regarded as the founder of Christian cenobitic monasticism. His name derives from the Coptic pakhom, meaning "the eagle" or "falcon".

Life

Pachomius was born around 292 in the Thebaid of Upper Egypt to pagan parents. According to his hagiography, at the age of about twenty he was forcibly conscripted into the Roman army during a campaign against the Persians. Like many soldiers of the time he was kept in harsh conditions; he was deeply moved when local Christians in Thebes visited the imprisoned recruits and brought them food and comfort, asking nothing in return. After his release, Pachomius resolved to learn more about the Christian faith, was baptised around 314, and withdrew to live as a hermit under the guidance of the anchorite Palaemon.

According to tradition, while living in solitude near the deserted village of Tabennisi on the east bank of the Nile, Pachomius heard a voice instructing him to build a dwelling there to which many monks would come. This marked a turning point: instead of the solitary life, Pachomius organised a community in which monks lived together under a common rule, sharing meals, work, and prayer while wearing a common habit. His brother John joined him, and the community grew rapidly; the monks built their own houses, church, and refectory.

The Pachomian federation

The monastery at Tabennisi was the first of what became a federation of monasteries — eventually nine monasteries for men and two for women, housing several thousand monks by the time of Pachomius's death. His sister Maria led the first women's community across the Nile. Each monastery was governed by a superior under the overall authority of Pachomius and his successors, and the whole system was coordinated through regular general assemblies and visitations.

Pachomius drew up a Rule — the earliest known monastic rule for communal life — regulating prayer, manual labour, meals, dress, and discipline. The monks worked as weavers, basket-makers, agricultural labourers, and craftsmen, and the monasteries became economically self-sufficient, selling their produce in nearby towns. Literacy was required: a monk who could not read was taught to do so, and each monk was given books to study.

Death and legacy

Pachomius died on 9 May 348, during an epidemic that also carried off many of his monks. He was succeeded first by his disciple Theodore of Tabennisi. His Rule was translated into Latin by Jerome in 404 and strongly influenced later Western monasticism, including the Rule of Saint Benedict. Basil the Great drew on Pachomian practice in forming his own monastic rules in the East. Pachomius is venerated as a saint in the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and Roman Catholic traditions.
