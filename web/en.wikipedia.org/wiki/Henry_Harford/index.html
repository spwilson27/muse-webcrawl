Henry Harford (1758 – 1834) was the last proprietary owner of the British colony of Maryland. The illegitimate son of Frederick Calvert, 6th Baron Baltimore, he inherited the Calvert family's proprietary rights and estates on his father's death in 1771, when he was thirteen, although — because of his illegitimacy — he could not inherit the title of Baron Baltimore.

Inheritance and the Revolution

The Calverts' charter granted the family near-feudal authority over Maryland, including land rents (quitrents) and governmental appointments. Harford, still a minor, never visited the colony. When the American Revolutionary War broke out, the new state of Maryland confiscated the proprietary estates of British subjects, and in 1780 the Maryland authorities seized Harford's lands and revenues outright.

Claims for compensation

After independence, Harford petitioned both the Maryland legislature and the British government for restitution or compensation, arguing that he had lost an enormous fortune — the rents from millions of acres. Maryland refused to return the lands but, over decades of negotiation, the affair was entangled with the wider settlement of loyalist claims and the state's debt to the Bank of England. In the 1830s Maryland finally agreed to a financial settlement: the state paid Harford's heirs substantial compensation under agreements connected with the so-called "Harford claim", which became a long-running item in Maryland's public finances.

Legacy

Harford County, Maryland — created in 1773 from Baltimore County — was named in his honour while he was still the young proprietor. His story illustrates the peculiar survival of feudal proprietary rights into the age of revolution, and the way revolutionary governments dealt with the property of absentee British landlords.
