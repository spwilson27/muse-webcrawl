Chorus (jazz)


Placeholder image: a jazz combo on stage

In jazz, a chorus is one complete cycle through the chord progression and form of a tune. Whereas in popular song the word "chorus" usually means the refrain, in jazz usage the chorus is the repeated structural unit over which soloists improvise: a performance typically consists of the melody ("head") followed by a series of improvised choruses and a final restatement of the head.

Form

Common chorus lengths include the 12-bar blues chorus and the 32-bar AABA or ABAC form of the Great American Songbook standard. A soloist is said to "take a chorus" (or two, or more) when improvising over one such cycle; band members and listeners keep track of their place by counting choruses against the tune's form.

Usage

Jazz musicians speak of "trading choruses" or "trading fours" (alternating shorter units), of the "top of the chorus" (the first bar of a new cycle), and of playing "chorus after chorus" in extended solos, as in celebrated long performances by John Coltrane or Charlie Parker. Arrangers may assign entire written choruses to sections of a big band within an arrangement.
