Hives

Urticaria
Other names: Hives, nettle rash
Symptoms: Raised, itchy, red welts
Causes: Allergic reactions, infections, stress, often unknown
Treatment: Antihistamines

Hives, known medically as urticaria, is a skin condition characterised by raised, itchy, red welts (wheals) that appear suddenly and usually resolve within 24 hours in any given spot, though new ones may appear elsewhere. The name derives from Urtica, the genus of nettles, whose stings produce similar lesions.

Causes

Urticaria results from the release of histamine and other mediators from mast cells in the skin, increasing vascular permeability. Triggers include allergic reactions to foods, medications or insect stings; infections; physical stimuli such as pressure, cold, heat, sunlight or vibration (physical urticarias); and stress. In many cases, especially chronic cases, no cause is identified.

Classification

Acute urticaria lasts less than six weeks and is often due to allergy or infection. Chronic urticaria persists beyond six weeks and is frequently spontaneous (chronic spontaneous urticaria), in many patients associated with autoimmune mechanisms. Swelling of deeper tissues, called angioedema, may accompany hives and can be dangerous if it affects the airways, as in anaphylaxis.

Treatment

Management centres on avoiding identified triggers and on second-generation antihistamines, which may be used at higher than standard doses. Refractory chronic cases may respond to omalizumab (anti-IgE therapy) or immunosuppressants such as ciclosporin. Short courses of corticosteroids are sometimes used for severe acute episodes; emergency treatment with adrenaline is required when hives form part of anaphylaxis.
