Balto-Slavic languages

The Balto-Slavic languages form a branch of the Indo-European language family traditionally comprising the Baltic and Slavic language groups. Most historical linguists accept that Baltic and Slavic descend from a common Proto-Balto-Slavic ancestor spoken after the breakup of Proto-Indo-European, though a minority view holds that their similarities arose from prolonged contact between separate branches.

Shared features

Evidence for common descent includes numerous shared lexical items not found elsewhere in Indo-European, common accentual innovations such as Hirt's law and Winter's law, the development of syllabic resonants, and shared morphology in pronouns and verb endings. Proto-Balto-Slavic is reconstructed with a pitch-accent system whose reflexes survive in Lithuanian, Latvian, Serbo-Croatian, and Slovene.

Subdivision and history

The group divides into Baltic (Lithuanian, Latvian, and extinct Old Prussian and others) and Slavic (including Russian, Polish, Czech, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, and Serbo-Croatian). The homeland is usually placed in the forest zone of Eastern Europe, associated by many scholars with Corded Ware-derived cultures of the 3rd–2nd millennia BCE. Slavic expanded dramatically in the 6th–7th centuries CE, while the Baltic area contracted under German, Slavic, and Finnic pressure.

Debate

The "Balto-Slavic problem" concerns whether shared features reflect a period of common development or millennia of areal convergence. The mainstream view accepts a Proto-Balto-Slavic stage, differing over its duration and internal branching; computational phylogenetic studies generally support the clade.
