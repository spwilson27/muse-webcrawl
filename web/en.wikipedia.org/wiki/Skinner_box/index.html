A Skinner box, formally an operant conditioning chamber, is a laboratory apparatus used to study animal behaviour and learning. Developed by psychologist B. F. Skinner at Harvard in the 1930s, it isolates a subject — usually a rat or a pigeon — in a sound- and light-controlled enclosure where responses (such as pressing a lever or pecking a key) can be automatically reinforced or punished and precisely recorded.

Structure

A typical chamber contains one or more manipulanda (a lever for rats, an illuminated response key for pigeons), a feeder that delivers food pellets or grain, stimulus lights and a tone generator, and a floor of metal bars through which mild electric shock can be delivered. Responses are tallied by cumulative recorders, producing the characteristic learning curves of Skinner's research programme.

Research uses

Using the box, Skinner mapped schedules of reinforcement — fixed-ratio, variable-ratio, fixed-interval and variable-interval — showing that the pattern and timing of rewards powerfully shape the rate and persistence of behaviour. Variable-ratio schedules (as in gambling) produce the highest, most extinction-resistant response rates. The paradigm underpins work on operant conditioning, shaping, extinction, conditioned reinforcement and, later, behavioural pharmacology and the experimental analysis of behaviour.

Criticism and cultural references

Critics from Chomsky onward questioned extrapolating from boxed animals to human language and cognition, and the device has become a cultural shorthand for manipulative reward systems — applied to slot machines, social-media feeds and free-to-play video games that exploit variable-ratio reinforcement. Skinner himself never called it a "Skinner box" and deplored the rumour that he had raised his daughter in one; Deborah Skinner was raised with an "air crib," a climate-controlled enclosure unrelated to the conditioning chamber.
