Lipopolysaccharide

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), also known as endotoxin, is a large molecule consisting of a lipid and a polysaccharide found in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. It consists of three parts: the O-antigen (a repeating glycan polymer), a core oligosaccharide, and lipid A, the hydrophobic anchor that carries most of the molecule's endotoxic activity.

Biological effects

LPS is a potent activator of the innate immune system. It is detected by the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)–MD-2 complex on macrophages and other cells, triggering release of cytokines such as TNF-α and interleukin-1. Small amounts provoke fever; large amounts released during Gram-negative sepsis can cause endotoxic shock, a leading cause of death in intensive care units.

Structure and variation

The O-antigen varies greatly between strains and serves as the basis of serotyping (for example, E. coli O157). Bacteria that lose the O-antigen are termed "rough"; those retaining it are "smooth". Lipid A structure varies between species, which accounts for differing potency — the lipid A of some symbionts and environmental bacteria is a weak agonist or even an antagonist of human TLR4.

Detection and removal

Because LPS is heat-stable and ubiquitous, pharmaceutical manufacture uses the Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay, based on the clotting reaction of horseshoe-crab blood, to test injectable products for endotoxin contamination. Depyrogenation requires dry heat far exceeding ordinary autoclaving.
