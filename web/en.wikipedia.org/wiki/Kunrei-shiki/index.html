Kunrei-shiki (訓令式, "Cabinet-order style") is a romanization system for transcribing the Japanese language into the Latin alphabet. It is the system officially promulgated by the Japanese government and is taught in Japanese elementary schools. Its closely related variant Nihon-shiki shares the same general principles; Kunrei-shiki was standardised internationally as ISO 3602.

History

Kunrei-shiki derives from Nihon-shiki (1885), devised by the physicist Aikitu Tanakadate to follow the regular structure of the kana syllabary rather than English phonetics. A cabinet order of 1937 adopted a modified form as the official system; after the Allied occupation promoted Hepburn romanization, a 1954 cabinet order restored Kunrei-shiki as official while allowing Hepburn in certain international contexts. It remains the system used for Japanese elementary-school instruction and is the basis of the ISO standard.

Principles and differences from Hepburn

Kunrei-shiki maps each kana to a fixed roman spelling, preserving the kana table's symmetry. Thus し is si (Hepburn: shi), ち is ti (chi), つ is tu (tsu), ふ is hu (fu), じ is zi (ji), づ is du (zu) and ぢ is di (ji). Syllabic ん is written n (or n' before vowels and y), long vowels are marked with a circumflex (ô, û) rather than Hepburn's macron, and the particle を is written wo, は as ha and へ as he following their kana spellings in prescribed contexts.

Usage

Although Hepburn dominates road signs, passports and popular usage, Kunrei-shiki is standard in Japanese linguistic scholarship, elementary education, certain official gazetteers and in structured data applications (including the ISO and library standards) where its one-to-one correspondence with kana is an advantage.
