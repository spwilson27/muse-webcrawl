Visual field

The visual field is the "spatial array of visual sensations available to observation in introspectionist psychological experiments", or more simply the total area in which objects can be seen in peripheral vision while the eye is focused on a central point. In medicine, the normal human visual field extends to approximately 60 degrees nasally, 100 degrees temporally, 60 degrees superiorly and 75 degrees inferiorly for each eye.

Normal limits

The visual field is limited by the anatomy of the face: the brow restricts the superior field, the cheek the inferior, and the nose the nasal field. Sensitivity is greatest at the centre (the fovea) and declines toward the periphery. There is a natural blind spot roughly 12–15 degrees temporal to fixation, corresponding to the optic disc, where the optic nerve exits the eye.

Visual field defects

Defects (scotomas) reveal the location of lesions along the visual pathway. Characteristic patterns include:

  • Bitemporal hemianopia — loss of the temporal halves of both fields, classically from a pituitary tumour pressing on the optic chiasm.
  • Homonymous hemianopia — loss of the same half of the field in both eyes, from a retrochiasmal lesion.
  • Arcuate scotomas and nasal steps — typical of glaucoma.
  • Central scotoma — seen in optic neuritis and macular disease.
  • Tunnel vision — constricted fields in advanced glaucoma or retinitis pigmentosa.

Testing

Fields are assessed by confrontation testing at the bedside or formally by perimetry — manual Goldmann perimetry or automated static perimetry (e.g. the Humphrey field analyser) — which maps sensitivity across the field and is essential in monitoring glaucoma.
