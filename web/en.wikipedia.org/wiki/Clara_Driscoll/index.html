Clara Driscoll

Clara Driscoll (April 2, 1861 – November 6, 1944), born Clara Pierce Wolcott in Tallmadge, Ohio, was an American designer who headed the Women's Glass Cutting Department at Tiffany Studios in New York. She is now recognised as the designer of many of the firm's most celebrated leaded-glass lamps, including the Dragonfly, Wisteria, and Peony shades, which for decades were attributed to Louis Comfort Tiffany himself.

Life and career

Driscoll studied art in Cleveland and at the Metropolitan Museum Art Schools in New York before joining Tiffany Studios in 1888. She managed the department of women glass cutters — the "Tiffany Girls" — who selected and cut the glass for lamps, windows, and mosaics. A surviving correspondence with her family, uncovered by scholars in the early 2000s and published in the 2006 book A New Light on Tiffany, documented her central role in designing the nature-inspired lampshades that became icons of American decorative art. Her Wisteria lamp, with nearly 2,000 pieces of glass, was among the most complex the studio produced.

Driscoll left and rejoined the studio several times; company policy required women to leave upon marriage, and she departed permanently after marrying in 1909. Her contributions, like those of other Tiffany women designers, were long obscured because the firm's products were marketed under Tiffany's name. A major 2007 exhibition at the New-York Historical Society, A New Light on Tiffany: Clara Driscoll and the Tiffany Girls, restored public recognition of her work.

Namesakes

Clara Driscoll should not be confused with Clara Driscoll (1881–1945), the Texas philanthropist and businesswoman known as the "Savior of the Alamo" for purchasing and preserving the Alamo mission in San Antonio, who is the subject of a separate article.
