The Comics Code Authority (CCA) was formed in 1954 by the Comics Magazine Association of America as an alternative to government regulation of comic books, following public concern and Senate hearings on alleged links between comics and juvenile delinquency. Comics submitted to the CCA were reviewed against a strict code; those approved bore the "Comics Code Seal" on their covers, and distributors generally refused to carry unapproved titles.

The Code

The 1954 code forbade depictions of graphic crime and horror — the words "horror" and "terror" were banned from titles — required that good always triumph over evil, prohibited disrespect of authority figures, and barred vampires, werewolves, ghouls and zombies. It effectively ended EC Comics' acclaimed horror and crime lines and reshaped the industry toward superhero and children's titles. The code was revised in 1971, partly after Marvel published an anti-drug Spider-Man storyline without approval at the government's request, and again in 1989.

Decline

From the 1980s, the direct market of comic speciality shops allowed publishers to bypass the seal. Marvel abandoned the code in 2001 in favour of its own ratings; DC Comics and Archie Comics, the last adherents, dropped it in 2011, leaving the CCA defunct.
