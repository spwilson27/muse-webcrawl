Egtved Girl

The Egtved Girl's oak coffin, National Museum of Denmark

The Egtved Girl (c. 1390–1370 BC) was a young woman of the Nordic Bronze Age whose exceptionally preserved burial was discovered in 1921 at Egtved, Denmark. Her clothing and grave goods are among the best-known finds of prehistoric Europe, displayed at the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen.

The burial

The girl was laid in a hollowed oak-trunk coffin inside a burial mound. Acidic conditions dissolved most bone but preserved her hair, nails, clothing and skin traces. She wore a short woolen tunic and a knee-length corded skirt of woolen strings, with a bronze belt disc decorated with spirals, a comb of horn, and bronze ornaments — an outfit that shocked and fascinated the 1920s public. Beside her lay the cremated remains of a child of about five or six, and offerings including a bucket of fermented drink.

Scientific studies

Dendrochronology dates the coffin's oak to 1370 BC. Strontium isotope analysis published in 2015 suggested the girl was not born in Denmark but likely in southwest Germany (the Black Forest region), and that she travelled repeatedly between the two regions in her last two years — evidence of long-distance Bronze Age mobility and alliance networks. Later analyses of the wool have debated parts of this picture.
