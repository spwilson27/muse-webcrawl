The Anaconda Plan is the name applied to a strategy outlined by Union General-in-Chief Winfield Scott in the early stages of the American Civil War. It proposed to defeat the Confederacy primarily by blockade: a naval blockade of Southern ports combined with a thrust down the Mississippi River to split the Confederacy in two, thereby squeezing — like an anaconda — the South into submission with minimal bloodshed.

Background

Scott, elderly and aware that building a trained army would take time, set out the plan in letters of May 1861. He expected the blockade and river campaign, supported by Unionist sentiment within the South, to end the rebellion without a costly invasion. The plan was ridiculed in the press of the day, which demanded an immediate march "On to Richmond", but its two pillars — the blockade proclaimed by President Lincoln and the Mississippi River campaigns culminating in the capture of Vicksburg in July 1863 — became central features of Union strategy as the war developed.

Assessment

Historians have debated how far the Anaconda Plan was a coherent adopted strategy versus a label retrospectively applied to policies that evolved piecemeal. The blockade, though porous at first, grew increasingly effective and strangled the Confederate export economy, while control of the Mississippi isolated Texas, Arkansas and Louisiana from the rest of the Confederacy. The famous cartoon map "Scott's Great Snake", published in Cincinnati in 1861, fixed the anaconda image in public memory.
