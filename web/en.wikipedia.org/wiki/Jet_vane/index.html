Jet vane

Jet vanes are small fins placed in the exhaust stream of a rocket engine to provide steering control. By deflecting the hot exhaust gases, a jet vane produces a sideways force that can pitch, yaw or roll the vehicle. They are one of the oldest forms of rocket guidance, used on the German V-2 and on many early ballistic missiles.

Jet vanes mounted inside a rocket nozzle, deflecting the exhaust flow.

Principle

A jet vane is mounted on a pivot just below the nozzle exit plane. An actuator rotates the vane a few degrees into the exhaust jet, so the flow is deflected and the reaction force turns the missile. Four vanes, arranged in pairs, give full three-axis control; differential deflection produces roll. Because the vanes sit directly in the exhaust, they must withstand extreme temperatures — early vanes were made of graphite, which erodes slowly in the hot gas stream.

Advantages and drawbacks

Jet vanes work at any speed, including the first moments after launch when aerodynamic fins are useless, and they do not depend on the vehicle's velocity. Their main drawbacks are thrust loss — the vanes obstruct the exhaust and cause drag — and erosion, which limits burn time. Modern rockets generally use gimballed engines or thrust-vectoring nozzles instead, but jet vanes remain in use on some tactical missiles where simplicity matters.

History

The V-2 used graphite jet vanes coupled to gyroscopic guidance, a design refined at Peenemünde. After the war, American and Soviet engineers copied the arrangement for the first generation of guided missiles. Later designs moved the vanes outside the nozzle or replaced them with movable nozzle sections, but the basic idea survives in some short-range systems.
