David John Chalmers (born 20 April 1966) is an Australian philosopher and cognitive scientist specialising in the philosophy of mind and consciousness. He is a professor of philosophy and neural science at New York University, and co-director of the Center for Mind, Brain, and Consciousness. He previously held a chair at the Australian National University.

Education and career

Chalmers studied mathematics at the University of Adelaide and went to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, completing a doctorate under the supervision informed by the Oxford philosophy of mind tradition; he wrote his dissertation on consciousness and cognition. He held positions at the University of Arizona, where he directed the Center for Consciousness Studies' scholarly programme, before moving to the ANU and then NYU.

The hard problem of consciousness

In his 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" and his book The Conscious Mind (1996), Chalmers distinguished the "easy problems" of consciousness — explaining discrimination, integration, reportability and control of behaviour — from the "hard problem": why and how physical processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all. He argued that conscious experience may be a fundamental feature of the world, not fully reducible to physical facts, and defended a form of property dualism he calls "naturalistic dualism".

Philosophical zombies and conceivability

Chalmers is known for the philosophical zombie argument: a being physically identical to a conscious person but lacking inner experience seems conceivable, and — he argues — metaphysically possible, which would show that consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical. The argument, descending from Descartes and Saul Kripke, remains one of the most debated in philosophy of mind.

Other work

With Andy Clark, Chalmers co-authored "The Extended Mind" (1998), arguing that cognitive processes can extend into notebooks, instruments and other parts of the environment. His later work includes Constructing the World (2012), a defence of a generalised scrutability framework, and Reality+ (2022), on virtual reality and digital metaphysics. He also co-founded the PhilPapers archive and the PhilSurveys project, and helped organise the original 1994 Tucson conference Towards a Science of Consciousness.
