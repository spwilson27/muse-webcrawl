Pistol shrimp

A pistol shrimp of the genus Alpheus, showing the greatly enlarged snapping claw.
Scientific classification
Kingdom:Animalia
Phylum:Arthropoda
Class:Malacostraca
Order:Decapoda
Family:Alpheidae

Pistol shrimp, also known as snapping shrimp, are shrimp of the family Alpheidae, characterised by having asymmetrical claws, the larger of which is capable of producing a loud snapping sound. Other common names for animals in the group include snapping shrimp and pistol shrimp.

The family is diverse and worldwide in distribution, consisting of about 1,119 species within 38 or so genera. The two most prominent genera are Alpheus and Synalpheus, with species numbering well over 250 and 100 respectively in each case. Most snapping shrimp dig burrows and are common inhabitants of coral reefs, submerged seagrass flats, and oyster reefs. While most genera and species are found in tropical and temperate coastal and marine waters, Betaeus inhabits cold seas and Potamalpheops is found only in freshwater caves.

Snapping

The pistol shrimp may be most notable for its ability to create a cavitation bubble that is capable of stunning or killing small fish. The larger claw is composed of two parts, a "hammer" part that moves backward into a right-angled position when cocked, and a fixed part. As the joint flexes, the hammer snaps into a depression in the fixed part, shooting a stream of high-pressure liquid at great speed.

The "snapping" effect is produced by the formation and collapse of a cavitation bubble. As the water jet shoots out, the surrounding water is pushed away by the resultant pressure and a bubble is formed. The implosion of this bubble can generate a sound of up to 218 decibels, temperatures approaching 4,700 °C (8,500 °F) in the collapsing hotspot, and even a brief flash of light — an effect known as sonoluminescence. The sound is so loud that competing colonies of pistol shrimp interfere with sonar used by submarines.

Ecology

Some pistol shrimp species share burrows with goby fish in a mutualistic symbiotic relationship. The burrow is built and tended by the pistol shrimp, and the goby provides protection by acting as a lookout for danger. When both are out of the burrow, the shrimp maintains contact with the goby using its antennae. The goby, having the better eyesight, signals to the shrimp when it feels threatened by flicking its tail several times. The pistol shrimp then retreats into the burrow, followed by the goby.

Until recently, the only social shrimp in the sea were thought to be species of Synalpheus, which live inside sponges and exhibit eusocial behaviour, with a single breeding queen in a colony.
