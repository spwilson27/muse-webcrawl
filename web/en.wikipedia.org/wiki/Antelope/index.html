Antelope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The term antelope refers to numerous species of even-toed ruminant mammals of the family Bovidae that are indigenous to Africa and Eurasia. Antelope comprise a wastebasket taxon — they are not a formal clade, but the term groups the many bovid species that are neither cattle, sheep, nor goats. There are about 91 species, most of them native to Africa.

Characteristics

All antelope have long, slender legs, powerful hindquarters, and (in most species and usually in males) permanent, unbranched horns of keratin over a bony core, unlike the annually shed antlers of deer. Sizes range from the tiny royal antelope, about 25 cm at the shoulder, to the giant eland, which can weigh nearly a tonne. Most are built for speed and endurance to escape predators such as lions, leopards, and cheetahs; the springbok also displays "pronking" — stiff-legged bouncing leaps.

Diversity and habitat

Antelopes occupy habitats from savanna and steppe to desert and rainforest. Familiar examples include gazelles, impala, wildebeest, hartebeest, oryx, kudu, and the saiga of Central Asia. The North American pronghorn is often called an antelope but belongs to its own family, Antilocapridae.

Conservation

Many species have declined from hunting and habitat loss; the scimitar oryx became extinct in the wild (and has since been reintroduced), and the saiga suffered catastrophic die-offs, though several antelope populations, notably in southern African game reserves, remain abundant.
