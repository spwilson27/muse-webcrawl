Chinese Exclusion Act

The Chinese Exclusion Act was a United States federal law signed by President Chester A. Arthur on May 6, 1882, prohibiting all immigration of Chinese labourers. Building on the earlier Page Act of 1875, which banned Chinese women, the act was the first significant federal law restricting immigration into the United States, and the first to bar a specific ethnic group. It was initially intended to last ten years but was renewed and strengthened, made effectively permanent in 1902, and not repealed until the Magnuson Act of 1943.

Background

Chinese immigrants came to California in large numbers during the Gold Rush and later built much of the western portion of the First Transcontinental Railroad. Economic downturn in the 1870s fuelled anti-Chinese agitation, led by Denis Kearney's Workingmen's Party, which blamed Chinese workers for low wages. Violence against Chinese communities erupted in episodes such as the Los Angeles Chinese massacre of 1871 and the Rock Springs massacre of 1885. The Angell Treaty of 1880 had already allowed the United States to restrict Chinese immigration, paving the way for the act.

Provisions and enforcement

The act suspended immigration of Chinese skilled and unskilled labourers for ten years, required Chinese residents to carry certificates of residence, and barred Chinese immigrants from naturalised citizenship; merchants, teachers, students, travellers and diplomats were nominally exempt. The Scott Act (1888) and Geary Act (1892) tightened enforcement, and the act was extended indefinitely in 1902. Enforcement gave rise to extensive documentary inspection at ports such as Angel Island in San Francisco Bay, and to evasion strategies including the use of "paper son" identities.

Repeal and legacy

The Magnuson Act of 1943 repealed exclusion as a wartime gesture toward China, then a US ally, though it set a token annual quota of 105 Chinese immigrants; broader equality in immigration law came only with the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965. In 2011 and 2012, the US Senate and House of Representatives formally passed resolutions expressing regret for the Chinese Exclusion Act and related laws.
