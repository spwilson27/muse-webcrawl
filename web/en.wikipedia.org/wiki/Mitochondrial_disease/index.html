Mitochondrial disease

Mitochondrial diseases are a group of genetic disorders caused by dysfunction of the mitochondria, the organelles that generate most of a cell's chemical energy in the form of ATP. Because mitochondria are present in nearly all cells, the diseases can affect many organ systems, particularly those with high energy demands such as the brain, muscles, heart, and eyes.

Causes

Mitochondrial diseases may result from mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which is inherited maternally, or from mutations in nuclear DNA encoding mitochondrial proteins, which follow ordinary Mendelian inheritance. The severity of mtDNA disorders often depends on heteroplasmy — the proportion of mutated mitochondria within a cell or tissue.

Examples

  • MELAS — mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes
  • Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) — sudden vision loss, usually in young men
  • Leigh syndrome — a severe neurological disorder of infancy and childhood
  • Kearns–Sayre syndrome and chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia
  • MERRF — myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines clinical assessment, blood and cerebrospinal fluid lactate levels, muscle biopsy, and genetic testing. There is no cure for most mitochondrial diseases; management is largely supportive and symptomatic, although certain vitamin and cofactor supplements are used in some cases and mitochondrial replacement therapy has been developed to prevent transmission of mtDNA disease.
