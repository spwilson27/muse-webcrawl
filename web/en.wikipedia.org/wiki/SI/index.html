The International System of Units, internationally known by the abbreviation SI (from French Système international d'unités), is the modern form of the metric system and the world's most widely used system of measurement. Established in 1960 and maintained by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM), it is built on seven base units, from which all other units are derived coherently.

Defining constants

Since the revision that took effect on 20 May 2019, the SI has been defined by fixing the numerical values of seven constants of nature: the speed of light in vacuum (c), the Planck constant (h), the elementary charge (e), the Boltzmann constant (k), the Avogadro constant (NA), the hyperfine transition frequency of caesium-133 (ΔνCs), and the luminous efficacy of 540 THz radiation (Kcd). The 2019 revision retired the last physical artefact definition, that of the kilogram, which had been based on the International Prototype of the Kilogram.

Derived units and prefixes

Derived units with special names include the newton (force), pascal (pressure), joule (energy), watt (power), volt (electric potential), hertz (frequency), becquerel and gray (radioactivity), and sievert (dose equivalent). Decimal prefixes from quecto- (10−30) to quetta- (1030) — the extremes added in 2022 — scale units by powers of ten; familiar examples are kilo-, centi- and milli-.

Governance and adoption

The SI is governed under the Metre Convention of 1875 by the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), advised by the International Committee (CIPM) and administered by the BIPM near Paris. It is the legal system of measurement in nearly every country; the notable exception in everyday use is the United States, where customary units predominate outside science and industry, although US units are themselves legally defined in terms of SI.
