Debt-to-GDP ratio

[Image: World map coloured by government debt as a percentage of GDP]

In economics, the debt-to-GDP ratio is the ratio between a country's government debt (a stock measured at a point in time) and its gross domestic product (GDP, a flow measured over a year). It is a widely used indicator of a government's ability to service its debt: a low ratio suggests an economy that produces enough output to repay its debts, while a high ratio signals greater fiscal risk.

Interpretation

The ratio changes according to the relationship between the interest rate on government debt, the growth rate of GDP, and the government's primary balance. If the growth rate exceeds the effective interest rate, the ratio can fall even with modest deficits; if interest exceeds growth, surpluses are needed to stabilise it. Economists debate whether particular thresholds (such as the 90% level discussed by Reinhart and Rogoff, or the EU's Maastricht reference value of 60%) have general significance.

Examples

Japan has long had the highest ratio among advanced economies, exceeding 250% of GDP, most of it held domestically and by the Bank of Japan. Greece's ratio peaked around 200% during and after its debt crisis beginning in 2009, prompting debt restructuring. The United States federal debt held by the public plus intragovernmental holdings totals over 100% of GDP. Several countries, including Singapore, run high gross ratios offset by large government assets.
