Folsom culture

PeriodPaleo-Indian, c. 10,900–10,200 years ago (approx.)
RegionGreat Plains of North America
Type siteFolsom, New Mexico
Signature artefactFolsom point (fluted spear point)

The Folsom culture is a Paleo-Indian archaeological culture of the North American Great Plains, dating to shortly after the better-known Clovis culture. It is named for the town of Folsom, New Mexico, where in 1926–1927 a distinctive fluted projectile point was found in situ between the ribs of an extinct bison (Bison antiquus) — a discovery that conclusively proved humans had lived in the Americas during the late Pleistocene.

Archaeology

Folsom points are smaller and more delicately made than Clovis points, with a long channel flake ("flute") removed from nearly the full length of each face. Folsom hunters were highly mobile and appear to have specialised in hunting bison, including large kill sites where herds were driven and slaughtered, alongside smaller game and plant foods. Sites are distributed across the High Plains from the Dakotas and Montana to Texas and New Mexico.

Significance

The Folsom discovery, following the Blackwater Draw finds of Clovis material, pushed back accepted dates for human settlement of the Americas and laid the foundations of Paleo-Indian archaeology.
