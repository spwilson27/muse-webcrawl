Niobrara Formation

The Niobrara Formation, also called the Niobrara Chalk, is a geologic formation in North America deposited between roughly 87 and 82 million years ago during the Coniacian, Santonian and Campanian stages of the Late Cretaceous. It consists of two structural units: the Smoky Hill Chalk Member overlying the Fort Hays Limestone Member. The chalk formed beneath the Western Interior Seaway, the great inland sea that covered the middle of the continent.

Extent and stratigraphy

The formation is exposed across the Great Plains, from Montana and the Dakotas through Nebraska, Kansas and Colorado and into New Mexico. It rests on the Carlile Formation and is overlain by the Pierre Shale. The Fort Hays Limestone is a relatively pure, cliff-forming chalk, while the Smoky Hill member alternates chalky shales and limestones with thin bentonite seams that allow precise dating.

Paleontology

The Niobrara is world-famous for its fossils of marine life collected since the 1870s, notably by Othniel Charles Marsh and later by George F. Sternberg. Its quarries in western Kansas have produced mosasaurs such as Tylosaurus, plesiosaurs, the giant fish Xiphactinus (including a celebrated "fish-within-a-fish" specimen), pterosaurs including Pteranodon and Nyctosaurus, flightless diving birds such as Hesperornis and Ichthyornis, turtles, and ammonites. Monument Rocks in Kansas are erosional remnants of the Smoky Hill chalk.

Economic importance

In the subsurface, the fractured Niobrara chalk is an unconventional oil and gas reservoir, most notably in the Denver–Julesburg Basin of Colorado and Wyoming, where horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing made it a major shale play in the twenty-first century.
