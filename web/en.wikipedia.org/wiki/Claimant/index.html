Claimant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A claimant is a person or party who initiates a civil claim in a court or tribunal, or who asserts a right or demand against another person or institution — for example, a person claiming social-security benefits, an insurance payout, or asylum. In the civil courts of England and Wales, "claimant" replaced "plaintiff" in April 1999 when the Civil Procedure Rules, following Lord Woolf's Access to Justice reports, modernised legal terminology to make it more intelligible to lay users.

Usage

The opposing party in a civil case is the defendant (or respondent in some tribunals). In the United States the initiating party is usually still called the plaintiff, though "claimant" is standard in specific contexts such as bankruptcy (a creditor filing a proof of claim), workers' compensation, and claims against government agencies. In international law, claimant states or individuals assert claims before bodies such as the Iran–United States Claims Tribunal or investor-state arbitration panels.

Burden of proof

As the party bringing the claim, the claimant generally bears the burden of proving the elements of the case on the balance of probabilities in civil matters. A claimant must also have standing (or locus standi) — a sufficient connection to the matter — and must bring the claim within any applicable limitation period.
