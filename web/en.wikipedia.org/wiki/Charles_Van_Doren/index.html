Charles Lincoln Van Doren (February 12, 1926 – April 9, 2019) was an American writer and editor who was involved in a television quiz show scandal in the 1950s. After World War II and through the 1950s, he was a leading figure in American cultural life, appearing on television as a champion contestant on the quiz show Twenty-One. His popularity came to an abrupt end in 1959 when it was revealed that the show's producers had supplied him with the answers in advance.

Background

Van Doren came from a distinguished literary family: his father, Mark Van Doren, was a Pulitzer Prize-winning poet and Columbia University professor, and his uncle Carl Van Doren was a noted biographer. Charles earned degrees from St. John's College in Annapolis and a doctorate in English from Columbia, and taught English at Columbia.

The Twenty-One scandal

Beginning in November 1956, Van Doren won a string of victories on NBC's Twenty-One, defeating champion Herb Stempel and winning $129,000. He became a national celebrity, appeared on the cover of Time, and was hired by NBC as a cultural correspondent on the Today show. When defeated contestant Stempel accused the show of being rigged, investigations followed, culminating in a grand jury and congressional hearings. On November 2, 1959, Van Doren testified before Congress and admitted that he had been given questions and answers beforehand. He resigned from NBC and Columbia and pleaded guilty to second-degree perjury.

Later life

Van Doren later worked for Encyclopædia Britannica, where he rose to the position of vice president, and co-edited The Annals of America. With Mortimer J. Adler he wrote the bestseller How to Read a Book (revised edition, 1972). He also wrote A History of Knowledge (1991). The scandal was dramatized in Robert Redford's 1994 film Quiz Show, in which Van Doren was played by Ralph Fiennes. He lived in quiet retirement in Connecticut and died in 2019 at the age of 93.
