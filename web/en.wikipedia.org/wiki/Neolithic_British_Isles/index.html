Neolithic British Isles

The Neolithic in the British Isles refers to the period of British, Irish, and Manx history spanning roughly 4100 to 2500 BC, when farming — domesticated wheat, barley, cattle, sheep, and pigs — spread to the islands from continental Europe, replacing the hunter-gatherer way of life of the Mesolithic. The period is marked by the construction of monumental architecture, including chambered tombs, causewayed enclosures, and stone circles such as Stonehenge's earliest phases, Avebury, and the monuments of Orkney.

Arrival of farming

Agriculture reached Britain around 4100 BC, centuries after it spread through continental Europe. Ancient DNA studies indicate that the new farmers were largely migrants descended from Anatolian farming populations who travelled via the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts, and that they contributed the great majority of the ancestry of Neolithic Britons, with limited admixture from local Mesolithic hunter-gatherers. Ireland's farming began around the same time, possibly slightly earlier in some regions.

Society and monuments

  • Early Neolithic (c. 4100–3400 BC): long barrows and chambered tombs, causewayed enclosures, polished stone axes traded over long distances, carinated bowl pottery.
  • Middle Neolithic (c. 3400–2900 BC): cursus monuments, passage graves such as Maeshowe and Newgrange (in Ireland), early stone circles; Grooved Ware pottery first appears in Orkney.
  • Late Neolithic (c. 2900–2500 BC): great henge monuments — Avebury, Durrington Walls, the first stages of Stonehenge — and the sophisticated settlement of Skara Brae in Orkney.

The sophistication of Orkney's monuments, including the Ness of Brodgar complex, has prompted suggestions that some practices and styles spread from the north southward. Society appears to have been organised in kinship-based communities without strong evidence of centralised chiefs, though the scale of monuments implies substantial coordinated labour.

End of the Neolithic

Around 2500 BC the arrival of Bell Beaker culture and metalworking began the transition to the Bronze Age; genetic evidence shows a further major population turnover associated with Beaker-using groups of steppe-related ancestry, who replaced about 90% of Britain's Neolithic ancestry within a few centuries.
