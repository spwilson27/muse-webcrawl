The Migration Period, also known as the Barbarian Invasions, was a period in European history (roughly 375–568 AD, sometimes extended 300–800) during which many peoples — Goths, Vandals, Franks, Angles, Saxons, Lombards, Slavs, Avars and others — migrated across Europe, entering and helping to transform the declining Western Roman Empire.

Course

Pressure from the Huns from about 375 pushed Gothic groups across the Danube (376); Gothic victory at Adrianople (378) killed Emperor Valens. In 406 Vandals, Alans and Suebi crossed the frozen Rhine; Rome was sacked by Visigoths (410) and Vandals (455). Attila's Huns were checked at the Catalaunian Plains (451). By 476 the last western emperor was deposed; Germanic kingdoms — Visigothic Spain, Ostrogothic then Lombard Italy, Frankish Gaul, Anglo-Saxon England — replaced Roman rule in the west.

Causes and interpretation

Historians debate climate change, population growth, steppe pressure, Roman pull factors and internal decay. Modern scholarship stresses ethnogenesis and negotiated settlement as much as invasion. The period reshaped Europe's linguistic and political map, founding the early medieval world.
