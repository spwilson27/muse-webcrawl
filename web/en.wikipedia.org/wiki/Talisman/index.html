Talisman

A talisman is any object ascribed with religious or magical powers intended to protect, heal, or otherwise benefit its owner. Talismans are often portable objects — rings, pendants, stones, coins, or inscribed slips of parchment or paper — carried on the person or fixed in a place. The word comes through French and Spanish from Arabic ṭilasm (طلسم), itself from Greek telesma (τέλεσμα), "a consecrated object" or "initiation rite."

Talisman versus amulet

Writers on magic often distinguish the talisman from the amulet: an amulet is generally a passive protective object, frequently of natural material, while a talisman is deliberately manufactured and charged or consecrated — often inscribed with characters, divine names, verses, or magic squares at an astrologically chosen moment — to attract a specific power or influence (such as the planetary talismans of the Picatrix and other grimoires).

In religious traditions

Islamic ta'wiz contain Quranic verses worn for protection; Jewish tradition includes mezuzot, tefillin, and hamsa charms; Christians have worn cross pendants, scapulars, and medals of saints; Hindu kavacha and Buddhist amulets (such as Thai votive tablets) serve similar functions. Talismanic shirts inscribed with verses were worn by Ottoman soldiers. In China, fu talismans (符) written by Daoist priests are burned, worn, or pasted at thresholds. Official religious authorities have often been ambivalent, alternately tolerating talismans as devotion or condemning them as superstition.
