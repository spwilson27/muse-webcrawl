Mauveine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Mauveine was the first synthetic organic dye, made in 1856.

Mauveine (also known as aniline purple and Perkin's mauve) is a synthetic organic chemical dye. It was the first dye to be produced synthetically, and its accidental discovery in 1856 by the 18-year-old English chemist William Henry Perkin is generally taken as the founding event of the modern synthetic dye industry.

Discovery

While working in his home laboratory during the Easter holidays of 1856, Perkin attempted to synthesise quinine by oxidising aniline with potassium dichromate. Instead of the colourless product he expected, he obtained a dark precipitate that dissolved in alcohol to give a brilliant purple solution. Realising its potential, Perkin patented the dye and, with his father and brother, built a factory at Greenford in Middlesex to manufacture it. The colour became fashionable after Queen Victoria wore a mauve dress to the Royal Exhibition of 1862.

Chemistry

Mauveine is not a single compound but a mixture of several closely related phenazinium dyes, the principal components being mauveine A (two methyl groups) and mauveine B (three methyl groups), with mauveine B2 (an isomer) and mauveine C (four methyl groups) also present. Modern analysis has confirmed the structure of these components, which form by oxidative coupling of aniline and toluidines.

Legacy

The commercial success of mauveine launched the synthetic dye industry and, through it, much of the modern chemical industry: the same research tradition later produced synthetic pharmaceuticals, including aspirin and the sulfonamide antibiotics. Perkin, who became wealthy from the dye, later turned to research on magnetic rotation and founded the Perkin Medal for contributions to applied chemistry.
