Fovea centralis

Fovea centralis
Part ofMacula lutea of the retina
DiameterAbout 1.5 mm
ContentsDensely packed cone photoreceptors; no rods or blood vessels
FunctionHigh-acuity central vision; colour vision

The fovea centralis is a small depression or pit at the centre of the macula lutea of the retina of the eye. It is the region of sharpest visual acuity, densely packed with cone photoreceptor cells and responsible for detailed central vision used in reading, driving and recognising faces.

Anatomy

About 1.5 mm across, the fovea contains the highest density of cones anywhere in the retina — in its innermost part, the foveola, more than 150,000 cones per square millimetre — and no rods. The overlying layers of the retina and its blood vessels are displaced to the sides, forming the foveal pit and minimising light scattering. Because each foveal cone connects nearly one-to-one with a ganglion cell, spatial resolution is maximal.

Clinical significance

The fovea is essential to the visual tasks of daily life; damage from macular degeneration, diabetic disease or a macular hole disproportionately impairs reading and fine vision even when peripheral vision remains intact. Clinicians image the fovea with optical coherence tomography, which reveals its layered structure.
