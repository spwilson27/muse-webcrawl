Lăutari

The lăutari are traditional Romanian musicians, historically mostly Roma, who performed — and still perform — at weddings, feasts, and other celebrations in Romania and Moldova. The name derives from the lăută, an early lute-like instrument once central to their bands, though the violin later became the emblematic lead instrument.

History and repertoire

From at least the 16th century, lăutari — often enslaved Roma musicians on boyar estates until emancipation in the 19th century — were the principal bearers of Romanian folk and urban popular music. A typical ensemble (taraf) combines violin, cimbalom (țambal), double bass, accordion, clarinet, and the pan flute (nai). Their repertoire spans slow doinas, dance tunes (hore, sârbe), ballads, and virtuoso display pieces, marked by ornamentation, flexible rhythm (parlando rubato), and improvisation.

Legacy

Famous lăutar dynasties and performers influenced classical composers: Franz Liszt and Béla Bartók drew on their music, and George Enescu's Romanian Rhapsodies echo lăutărească style. Bands such as Taraf de Haïdouks brought lăutari music to international audiences in the late 20th century.
