Solubility equilibrium

Solubility equilibrium is a type of dynamic equilibrium that exists when a chemical compound in the solid state is in chemical equilibrium with a solution of that compound. The solid may dissolve unchanged, undergo dissociation, or react with another component of the solution. The equilibrium is described quantitatively by the solubility product, Ksp, which governs the maximum concentration of ions that can coexist in a saturated solution.

A saturated solution in dynamic equilibrium with its solid solute.

The solubility product

For a salt AxBy dissolving according to AxBy(s) ⇌ xA(aq) + yB(aq), the solubility product is Ksp = [A]x[B]y, with the concentration of the pure solid taken as constant and omitted. For example, calcium fluoride, CaF2, has Ksp = [Ca2+][F−]2 ≈ 3.9 × 10−11 at 25 °C. Comparing the ion product Q with Ksp predicts whether a precipitate will form: if Q > Ksp, precipitation occurs until the solution is saturated.

Common-ion effect

The solubility of a sparingly soluble salt decreases when a soluble salt containing one of its ions is added, a consequence of Le Chatelier's principle known as the common-ion effect. Conversely, solubility can increase through complex formation, as when silver chloride dissolves in aqueous ammonia by forming the diamminesilver(I) complex, or through increases in ionic strength (the diverse-ion effect).

Factors affecting solubility

Temperature, pH (for salts of weak acids or bases), particle size, and the presence of complexing agents all affect solubility equilibria. The dependence of solubility on pH is important in qualitative inorganic analysis, in the formation of kidney stones and dental enamel demineralisation, and in environmental chemistry, including the dissolution of limestone by acid rain and the formation of stalactites and stalagmites in caves through calcium carbonate equilibria.

Applications

Solubility equilibria underpin gravimetric analysis, water treatment, pharmaceutical formulation (where drug solubility affects bioavailability), and industrial crystallisation processes.
