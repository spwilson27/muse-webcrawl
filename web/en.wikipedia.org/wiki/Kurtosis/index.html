Kurtosis

In probability theory and statistics, kurtosis (from Greek: κυρτός, kyrtos or kurtos, meaning "curved, arching") is a measure of the "tailedness" of the probability distribution of a real-valued random variable. Like skewness, kurtosis describes a particular aspect of a probability distribution. There are different ways of quantifying kurtosis for a theoretical distribution, and corresponding ways of estimating it from a sample from a population.

Definition

The standard measure of kurtosis, originating with Karl Pearson, is the fourth standardised moment: E[(X−μ)4] / σ4. Excess kurtosis subtracts 3, the kurtosis of the normal distribution, so that the normal has excess kurtosis zero. Distributions with excess kurtosis greater than zero are called leptokurtic (heavier tails and a sharper peak than the normal), those with negative excess kurtosis platykurtic (lighter tails), and those with zero mesokurtic. Despite the common description of kurtosis as "peakedness", modern treatments emphasise that kurtosis is primarily a measure of tail extremity — the propensity to produce outliers — rather than the shape of the centre of the distribution.

Examples

The logistic distribution has excess kurtosis 1.2; the Laplace distribution 3; Student's t distribution has excess kurtosis 6/(ν−4) for ν > 4 degrees of freedom, diverging as ν approaches 4; the uniform distribution has excess kurtosis −1.2, the minimum possible being −2 for a symmetric two-point (Bernoulli with p = ½) distribution. In finance, leptokurtic asset returns are the familiar "fat tails" that make extreme losses more frequent than a normal model predicts.
