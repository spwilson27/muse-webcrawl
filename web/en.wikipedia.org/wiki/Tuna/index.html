Tuna

A tuna is a saltwater fish belonging to the tribe Thunnini, a subgrouping of the Scombridae (mackerel) family. The Thunnini comprise 15 species across five genera, ranging greatly in size from the bullet tuna (about 35 cm) to the Atlantic bluefin tuna (up to about 4.6 m and over 680 kg). Tunas are fast, powerful predators of the open ocean and among the most commercially important fish in the world.

Biology

Tunas have streamlined, torpedo-shaped bodies built for speed; yellowfin have been recorded at bursts above 75 km/h. Unlike most fish, tunas are partially warm-bodied: a countercurrent heat-exchange system (the rete mirabile) keeps their muscles and organs warmer than the surrounding water, supporting sustained cruising in cold seas. Tunas are ram ventilators and must swim constantly to breathe.

Species

The "true tunas" of the genus Thunnus include the albacore, yellowfin, bigeye, Pacific bluefin, Atlantic bluefin and southern bluefin. The skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis) accounts for the largest share of the world canned-tuna catch.

Fisheries and conservation

Tuna fisheries are worth tens of billions of dollars annually; tuna is eaten canned, as steak, and as sashimi, with prime bluefin commanding record auction prices in Japan. Heavy demand led to severe overfishing of bluefin stocks in the twentieth century, prompting international management through regional fisheries bodies; some populations have begun to recover under quota regimes. Concerns also include bycatch of dolphins, sharks and turtles, and mercury levels in large predatory species.
