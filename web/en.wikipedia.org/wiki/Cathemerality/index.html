Cathemerality

Cathemerality, sometimes called metaturnality, is an animal behaviour pattern in which the organism is irregularly active at any time of day or night, with a few hours of sleep interspersed between bouts of activity across the 24-hour cycle. It is one of the three main temporal activity patterns, contrasting with nocturnality (activity mainly at night) and diurnality (activity mainly in the daytime).

Occurrence

The term was coined by the primatologist Ian Tattersall in 1987, initially to describe the activity of certain lemurs of Madagascar, such as the mongoose lemur and the true lemurs (Eulemur), which shift their activity between day and night across the seasons. Cathemeral behaviour has since been reported in other mammals, including some large carnivores and ungulates, and is thought to be an ancestral, flexible state from which specialised diurnal and nocturnal patterns can evolve.

Causes

Cathemerality appears to be driven by a mix of factors: avoiding predators, reducing competition, thermoregulation (being active at night in the hot, dry season and by day in cooler months), and exploiting food resources whenever they are available. Moonlight levels, temperature, and rainfall all influence the timing of activity bouts in cathemeral lemurs. Because the pattern is flexible, cathemerality is regarded as an adaptive compromise in environments where the costs of strict day- or night-activity are high.
