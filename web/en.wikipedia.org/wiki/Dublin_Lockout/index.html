Dublin Lockout

The Dublin Lockout was a major industrial dispute between about 20,000 workers and 300 employers that took place in Dublin, Ireland, from 26 August 1913 to 18 January 1914. It is often viewed as the most severe and significant industrial dispute in Irish history. Central to it was the workers' right to unionise, opposed by employers led by businessman William Martin Murphy against the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) of Jim Larkin.

Background

Dublin's working class endured some of the worst slum conditions in Europe. Larkin's ITGWU, founded in 1909, organised unskilled workers and won strikes through sympathetic action. Murphy, owner of the Dublin United Tramways Company and the Irish Independent, determined to break the union, demanded employees renounce the ITGWU. When tram workers struck during Horse Show week, Murphy and allied employers locked out union members across the city.

Events

On 31 August 1913 ("Bloody Sunday"), police baton-charged a banned rally on Sackville Street, injuring hundreds; two workers died of injuries. The Irish Citizen Army was formed to protect workers. British trade unions sent food ships, but funds dwindled, and by January 1914 starving workers drifted back to work. James Connolly and Larkin nevertheless preserved the union movement, which later flourished.
