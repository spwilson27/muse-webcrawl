Apotropaic magic

Apotropaic magic (from Greek apotrepein, "to ward off") is a type of magic intended to turn away harm or evil influences, as in deflecting misfortune or averting the evil eye. Apotropaic observances may include particular protective actions, the wearing of amulets and talismans, and the marking of buildings and objects with protective symbols.

Examples

Common apotropaic devices across cultures include the evil eye bead (nazar) of the Mediterranean and Middle East, the hamsa hand, horseshoes hung over doors, gargoyles and grotesques on churches, witch marks and daisy wheels carved into the timbers of early modern English buildings, and the concealing of shoes and other objects within walls and chimneys. In ancient Egypt, apotropaic wands carved with protective deities were used in childbirth rituals; in ancient Greece, phallic herms and the gorgoneion served similar functions.

Interpretation

Anthropologists interpret apotropaic practices as responses to perceived supernatural threats, particularly at liminal points — doorways, windows, chimneys, and thresholds — where a household was thought vulnerable. Many such customs persist today in folk form, such as knocking on wood, crossing fingers, or touching a lucky charm.
