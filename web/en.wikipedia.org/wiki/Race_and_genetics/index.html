Race and genetics

Researchers have studied the relationship between race and genetics since the sequencing of the human genome — how human population structure, ancestry and genetic variation relate to socially defined racial categories.

Genetic variation

Humans are genetically very similar: any two people share about 99.9% of their DNA. Most variation occurs within populations rather than between them — a finding first quantified by Richard Lewontin in 1972 and confirmed by later studies, which attribute roughly 85–93% of variation to within-population differences. A. W. F. Edwards argued in 2003 ("Lewontin's fallacy") that correlations across many loci nevertheless make ancestry statistically inferable, and clustering studies (such as Rosenberg et al., 2002) find that genetic clusters roughly correspond to major geographic regions.

Consensus and debate

The prevailing scientific view is that race is primarily a social construct rather than a biological taxonomy: genetic variation is largely clinal (gradual with geography), clusters depend on sampling, and racial categories do not map onto discrete genetic divisions. At the same time, genetic ancestry is real and is used in medicine and forensics — for example in estimating disease risk allele frequencies and biogeographic ancestry — though professional bodies caution against treating race itself as a biological variable. Geneticists and anthropologists have issued statements opposing the misuse of genetics to support racial supremacy or claims of innate group differences in traits such as intelligence, for which no genetic evidence exists.
