Laser printing

Laser printing is an electrostatic digital printing process. It produces high-quality text and graphics by repeatedly passing a laser beam back and forth over a negatively charged cylinder called a photoreceptor drum to define a differentially charged image. The drum then selectively collects electrically charged powdered ink (toner), and transfers the image to paper, which is heated to permanently fuse the text and imagery.

History

The process is based on xerography invented by Chester Carlson. Gary Starkweather at Xerox adapted a Xerox copier in 1969 to create the first laser printer. The Xerox 9700 (1977) was an early commercial product; the HP LaserJet 8ppm of 1984, using a Canon engine, brought laser printing to desktop users, and together with the Apple LaserWriter (1985) and PageMaker helped launch desktop publishing.

Process

  1. Charging — the drum receives a uniform electrostatic charge.
  2. Exposing — a laser discharges points where the image should be.
  3. Developing — toner adheres to the charged (or discharged) areas.
  4. Transferring — toner moves to paper.
  5. Fusing — heat and pressure melt toner into the paper.
  6. Cleaning — excess toner and charge are removed.

Resolution and speed

Resolution is measured in dots per inch (dpi); 600–1200 dpi is typical. Colour laser printers use four toners (cyan, magenta, yellow, black). Compared with inkjet printing, lasers are faster for text and produce sharp, water-resistant output, though photo quality historically favoured inkjets.
