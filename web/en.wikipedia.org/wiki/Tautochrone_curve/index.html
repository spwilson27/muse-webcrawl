Tautochrone curve

A tautochrone curve (from Greek tauto, "same", and chronos, "time") is the curve for which a frictionless particle sliding under uniform gravity to the curve's lowest point takes the same time regardless of its starting position. The tautochrone is a cycloid — the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling circle — with the cusp at the top and the vertex at the bottom.

History

The problem was solved by Christiaan Huygens, who published the result in his Horologium Oscillatorium (1673), having found it in 1659. Huygens also proved that the cycloid is the evolute of a cycloid, and exploited this in clock design: he suspended a pendulum bob from a cord constrained by cycloidal "cheeks", so that the bob itself traced a cycloid and its period was independent of amplitude — a truly isochronous pendulum, at least in theory. The tautochrone problem was later solved by Newton in the Principia and prompted the brachistochrone problem posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1696 — whose answer is the same curve. Niels Henrik Abel's mechanical problem (1823), finding a curve from a given descent-time function, led to one of the first integral equations and an early application of fractional calculus.

Property

For a cycloid generated by a circle of radius r, the descent time to the bottom from any point is π√(r/g), where g is the acceleration due to gravity. The result holds because the tangential acceleration along the curve is proportional to the arc length remaining to the vertex, reproducing simple harmonic motion exactly.
