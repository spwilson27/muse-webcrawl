The Siege of Singara was an engagement of the Roman–Persian Wars fought in the 340s AD, when the Sasanian king Shapur II besieged the Roman fortress-city of Singara in northern Mesopotamia during the emperor Constantius II's eastern wars. The date is disputed—344 is traditional, with 348 also proposed—and ancient sources disagree about the outcome; the Persian night attack appears to have been repulsed with heavy loss, and the episode is chiefly known from the confused accounts of the battle that followed near the city.

Background

Singara, near modern Sinjar in Iraq, was one of the principal Roman strongholds on the Mesopotamian frontier, refortified under Diocletian and manned by legionary forces. After war resumed between Rome and Persia in 337, Shapur II campaigned repeatedly against the Roman fortresses of the region—Nisibis was besieged three times (337, 346 and 350)—seeking to recover territories ceded by Narseh in 299.

The siege and battle

According to the surviving epitomes and church historians, Shapur assaulted Singara and the fighting developed into a confused night engagement around the walls and the Persian camp. The Romans, sallying or counter-attacking at dusk, reportedly captured the son of Shapur and slaughtered his guards, a humiliation later embroidered in Syriac and Persian tradition. Persian accounts claimed a success; Roman panegyric claimed a victory. In fact the campaign changed nothing strategically: Shapur withdrew, and the frontier stalemate persisted until Julian's invasion of 363.

Legacy

The episode illustrates the difficulty of reconstructing the fourth-century eastern wars, whose chief narrative source, Ammianus Marcellinus, begins only in the 350s. Singara itself remained Roman until the settlement following Julian's death, when Jovian ceded the trans-Tigrine provinces and, soon after, the region passed under Persian control.
