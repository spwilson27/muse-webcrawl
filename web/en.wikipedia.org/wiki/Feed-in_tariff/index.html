Feed-in tariff

Feed-in tariffs guaranteed above-market prices for renewable electricity, driving rapid growth of solar and wind power.

A feed-in tariff (FIT) is a policy mechanism designed to accelerate investment in renewable energy technologies. It normally provides renewable electricity generators — from household rooftop solar owners to commercial wind farms — with a guaranteed, above-market price for the power they feed into the grid, under long-term contracts typically lasting 15–25 years.

Design

Common design elements include a guaranteed grid connection, a fixed tariff per kilowatt-hour differentiated by technology, project size and sometimes location, and a purchase obligation on the utility or grid operator. Costs are usually spread across all electricity consumers through a surcharge. Many schemes include scheduled tariff reductions ("degression") for new installations to reflect falling technology costs and to contain expenditure.

History

The idea traces to the United States' Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA) of 1978, which obliged utilities to buy power from qualifying small generators at avoided cost. Germany's Electricity Feed-in Act (1991) and especially its Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG, 2000) became the most influential model, offering technology-specific tariffs for 20 years. The German scheme is credited with creating mass markets for wind turbines and photovoltaic modules and driving steep global cost declines. Similar laws were adopted in Spain, France, Ontario (Canada), Japan after the 2011 Fukushima disaster, and dozens of other jurisdictions.

Debate and evolution

Supporters argue FITs provide the revenue certainty needed for capital-intensive projects and democratise energy production. Critics point to high consumer surcharges where tariffs were set too generously — Spain's solar boom and subsequent retroactive cuts being a cautionary case. As renewables reached cost parity with conventional generation, many countries shifted from administratively set tariffs toward competitive auctions, feed-in premiums over market prices, and contracts for difference.
