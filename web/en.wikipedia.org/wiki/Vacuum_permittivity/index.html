Vacuum permittivity

Vacuum permittivity, commonly denoted ε0 (pronounced "epsilon nought" or "epsilon zero"), is the value of the absolute dielectric permittivity of classical electromagnetism's reference medium, the classical vacuum. It quantifies how strongly electric fields are "permitted" to form in response to electric charges, and enters Coulomb's law for the force between two point charges.

Role in electromagnetism

In Coulomb's law the electrostatic force between charges q1 and q2 separated by r is F = q1q2 / (4πε0r²). Together with the vacuum permeability μ0, ε0 fixes the speed of electromagnetic waves in vacuum, c = 1/√(ε0μ0), a cornerstone of Maxwell's theory and special relativity. The permittivity of a material is written as the product ε = εrε0, where εr is its relative permittivity.

Definition and the 2019 SI revision

Before 2019, ε0 had the exact defined value 1/(μ0c²) = 8.854187817... × 10⁻¹² F/m, following from the former definitions of the ampere and the metre. Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, which fixed the elementary charge e, ε0 is no longer exact: it must be determined experimentally through the fine-structure constant α, via ε0 = e²/(2αhc).
