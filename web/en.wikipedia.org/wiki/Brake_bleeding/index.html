Brake bleeding

Brake bleeding is the procedure performed on hydraulic brake systems whereby the fluid lines (pipes and hoses) are purged of any air bubbles. This is necessary because, while the brake fluid is an incompressible liquid, air bubbles are compressible gas and their presence in the brake system greatly reduces the hydraulic pressure that can be developed.

Procedure

Bleeding is typically done at bleeder screws fitted to each brake caliper or wheel cylinder, beginning with the wheel farthest from the master cylinder. Common methods include the two-person pump-and-hold method, pressure bleeding using a pressurized reservoir, and vacuum bleeding, which draws fluid through the bleeder screw. The brake fluid reservoir must be kept topped up during the process to avoid drawing in more air.

Safety

Properly bled brakes produce a firm pedal; a spongy pedal is a classic sign of remaining air. Because brake fluid is hygroscopic and corrosive to paint, care is taken in handling it, and old fluid is replaced at recommended intervals.
