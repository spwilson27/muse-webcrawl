Southern Pacific Transportation Company

The Southern Pacific (reporting mark SP), later the Southern Pacific Transportation Company, was an American Class I railroad network that operated in the Western United States. Founded in 1865, it grew into one of the largest railroads in the country, at its height operating more than 13,000 miles of track in 13 western states. It was acquired by the Union Pacific Corporation in 1996, ending 131 years of independent operation.

History

The Southern Pacific was incorporated in 1865 by a group of San Francisco businessmen, but control soon passed to the "Big Four" — Collis P. Huntington, Leland Stanford, Mark Hopkins, and Charles Crocker — who also built the Central Pacific, the western half of the first transcontinental railroad. The two roads were combined under common control, and the Southern Pacific absorbed lines throughout California, Oregon, Nevada, Arizona, and Texas, including the Texas and New Orleans Railroad and later the St. Louis Southwestern ("Cotton Belt").

Under presidents such as E. H. Harriman, the railroad was modernised and expanded, operating celebrated passenger trains including the Sunset Limited, the Coast Daylight, and the Lark. Southern Pacific was also a pioneer in dieselisation, intermodal piggyback service, and unit trains, and it developed large landholdings and communications ventures; the name of its internal telecom network, SPRINT, derived from "Southern Pacific Railroad Internal Networking Telephony".

After a failed merger attempt with the Santa Fe in the 1980s — rejected by the Interstate Commerce Commission, resulting in the railroad being sold to Rio Grande Industries — the weakened Southern Pacific was purchased by Union Pacific in 1996 amid an industry-wide consolidation. Congestion following the merger caused the "UP meltdown" of 1997–98 in the Gulf Coast region.

Legacy

Remnants of the Southern Pacific remain visible across the West: the Coast Line in California, the Sunset Route to Texas, surviving depots, and preserved locomotives such as the famous Daylight 4-8-4 No. 4449. Its reporting mark and some corporate entities survived on paper within Union Pacific for years after the operating merger.
