A horse archer is a cavalryman armed with a bow and arrows, able to shoot while riding. Light horse archery, developing from the Eurasian steppe in the early first millennium BC, defined warfare across much of Asia and Eastern Europe for over two thousand years, from the Scythians and Assyria's mounted bowmen to the Mongol armies of the 13th century, and it persisted in parts of Asia into the age of gunpowder.

Equipment and technique

The classic weapon was the recurved composite bow, built of wood, horn, and sinew — short enough to use on horseback yet powerful, and shot with a thumb draw typically aided by a thumb ring. Riders shot at the moment in the gallop when all four hooves left the ground, to steady the aim, and could shoot in any direction, including backwards — the famous "Parthian shot". Each warrior carried one or more quivers and several spare horses on campaign.

Tactics

Horse archers harassed enemies with arrow showers from beyond the reach of infantry, avoided close combat, and used the feigned retreat — sometimes lasting days — to draw pursuers into ambush. At the Battle of Carrhae (53 BC), Surena's Parthian horse archers, resupplied with arrows by camel train, destroyed Crassus's Roman army. Mobility also made steppe armies formidable strategically: Mongol tumens could cover hundreds of kilometres in days and coordinate across fronts using relay messengers.

Peoples and traditions

Mounted archery was the specialty of the Scythians, Sarmatians, Parthians, Xiongnu, Huns, Avars, Magyars, Pechenegs, Cumans, Seljuk and Ottoman Turks, Mongols, Manchus, and the Mamluks of Egypt, and was also developed independently in Japan, where the samurai began as mounted archers (yabusame remains a ceremonial art) and in North America by Plains peoples such as the Comanche after the adoption of the horse. Sedentary states responded with their own horse-archer corps, crossbow and longbow defences, tercios, and eventually firearms and, in China's case, vast anti-steppe logistics.

Decline

Firearms gradually displaced the war bow between the 16th and 18th centuries; mounted archery last saw major battlefield use in the 18th century (e.g., by Manchu and Mongol bannermen and steppe peoples) before surviving as sport and ceremony in Mongolia, Japan, Korea, and modern mounted-archery revivals.
