Finnegans Wake

Author: James Joyce
Language: English (idiosyncratic)
Published: 4 May 1939
Publisher: Faber and Faber; Viking Press

Finnegans Wake is a novel by Irish writer James Joyce. It was published in 1939 and is known for its experimental style, difficult language, and use of stream of consciousness. Joyce spent 17 years writing the work, which he called "Work in Progress" before publication. The book is written in a largely idiosyncratic language that blends standard English lexical items with neologisms, portmanteau words, and punning allusions in many languages, creating a deliberately dense and polyphonic prose.

Structure and content

The narrative of Finnegans Wake has been described as a dream, often attributed to the publican H. C. Earwicker ("Here Comes Everybody") of Chapelizod, Dublin, his wife Anna Livia Plurabelle (ALP), their twin sons Shem and Shaun, and their daughter Issy. The book consists of four parts, modelled loosely on Giambattista Vico's cyclical theory of history, and its last sentence is left unfinished, completing itself in the opening words of the first page — "riverrun, past Eve and Adam's, from swerve of shore to bend of bay" — so that the book forms a cycle.

The title derives from the Irish-American street ballad "Finnegan's Wake", about a hod-carrier who falls from a ladder and revives at his own wake when whiskey is spilled on him; Joyce dropped the apostrophe so that the title also suggests "Finnegans wake" — all the Finnegans (all mankind) awakening.

Composition and reception

Joyce composed the work in Paris between 1922/23 and 1939, publishing fragments in journals such as transition. Samuel Beckett, William Carlos Williams and others contributed essays to the 1929 volume Our Exagmination Round His Factification for Incamination of Work in Progress defending it. Initial reviews were mixed to baffled, but the book has come to be regarded as one of the most significant works of modernist literature, and it has generated a large scholarly literature and reading groups devoted to deciphering it. Its ten "thunderwords" of one hundred letters each and its multilingual puns exemplify Joyce's technique.
