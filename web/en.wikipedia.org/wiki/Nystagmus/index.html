Nystagmus

Nystagmus is a condition of involuntary (or, in rare cases, voluntary) eye movement. It is characterised by repetitive, uncontrolled movements of the eyes, which may be side to side (horizontal), up and down (vertical), or rotary (torsional). The movements reduce visual acuity and depth perception and can affect balance and coordination. The word comes from the Greek nystagmos, "nodding" or "drowsiness".

Classification

Nystagmus is typically described by its waveform. In jerk nystagmus the eyes drift slowly in one direction and then snap back with a fast corrective saccade; by convention the condition is named after the direction of the fast phase. In pendular nystagmus the eyes oscillate at roughly equal speed in both directions, like a pendulum.

Causes

Some nystagmus is physiological: optokinetic nystagmus stabilises images when watching a moving scene (as from a train window), and vestibular nystagmus is produced by rotation of the head or by caloric stimulation of the ear. Pathological nystagmus may be congenital — infantile nystagmus often appears in the first months of life and is associated with conditions such as albinism and congenital cataracts — or acquired later through disorders of the vestibular system, brainstem or cerebellar lesions, multiple sclerosis, stroke, certain medications, or alcohol and drug intoxication. The rapid eye movements seen when a suspect is tested in a horizontal gaze nystagmus test are used by police as one indicator of intoxication.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause where possible. Options include corrective lenses, certain medications such as gabapentin or memantine for acquired forms, botulinum toxin injections, and eye muscle surgery (such as the Kestenbaum procedure) to shift the "null point" — the gaze angle at which the nystagmus is weakest — into the primary position.
