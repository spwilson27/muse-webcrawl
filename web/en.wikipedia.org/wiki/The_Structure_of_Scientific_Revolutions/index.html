The Structure of Scientific Revolutions is a 1962 book by the American philosopher and historian of science Thomas S. Kuhn. It is one of the most cited academic books of all time and a landmark in the history, philosophy and sociology of science. Kuhn challenged the then-prevailing image of science as a steady, cumulative acquisition of knowledge, arguing instead that scientific fields undergo periodic "paradigm shifts".

Summary

Kuhn described a cycle: a scientific community practises "normal science" within a shared paradigm — a constellation of theories, methods and exemplary problem-solutions. Puzzles that resist solution accumulate as anomalies; when anomalies provoke a crisis, the field may undergo a scientific revolution in which a new paradigm replaces the old. Successive paradigms are, in Kuhn's term, incommensurable: their adherents partly talk past one another, using key terms in different ways. Classic examples include the Copernican revolution and the transition from Newtonian mechanics to relativity.

Influence

The book popularised the term "paradigm shift", which entered general usage far beyond the philosophy of science. A 1969 postscript clarified Kuhn's notion of a paradigm as a "disciplinary matrix" and "exemplars". The work influenced the strong programme in the sociology of scientific knowledge and provoked extensive debate with Karl Popper, Imre Lakatos and others at a famous 1965 London colloquium.
