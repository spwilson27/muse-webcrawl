White clover (Trifolium repens), also known as Dutch clover or Ladino clover, is a herbaceous perennial plant in the bean family Fabaceae. A native of Europe and Central Asia, it has been widely introduced worldwide as a forage crop and is now common in lawns and pastures throughout temperate regions.

Description

The plant has creeping stems (stolons) that root at the nodes, allowing it to spread and persist under grazing and mowing. Its leaves are trifoliate, each leaflet marked with a pale crescent, and its flowers are white or pinkish, borne in dense rounded heads and rich in nectar for bees.

Cultivation and uses

White clover is one of the most important pasture legumes in temperate agriculture. In symbiotic association with Rhizobium bacteria in its root nodules, it fixes atmospheric nitrogen, improving soil fertility for companion grasses. It is highly palatable and nutritious for livestock, tolerates close grazing, and is a major honey plant. It is also used as a living mulch and a lawn component, though it can become weedy.

Cyanogenesis

Some strains of white clover contain cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin and lotaustralin) that release hydrogen cyanide when the leaves are damaged, a defence against herbivores. The frequency of cyanogenic plants varies geographically, declining in colder climates.
