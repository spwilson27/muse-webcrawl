Mental capacity

Capacity is assessed decision by decision, not globally.

Mental capacity is the ability of a person to make a particular decision at a particular time. It is a legal and clinical concept central to medical law and ethics: adults are generally presumed to have capacity, and interventions in the lives of those who lack it must be justified, proportionate and — under modern statutes — made in the person's best interests.

Legal frameworks

In England and Wales, the Mental Capacity Act 2005 codifies the common law. Its five statutory principles state that a person must be assumed to have capacity unless established otherwise; that all practicable steps must be taken to help them decide; that an unwise decision does not itself prove incapacity; that acts done for a person lacking capacity must be in their best interests; and that the less restrictive option must be preferred. A person lacks capacity if, because of an impairment of or disturbance in the functioning of the mind or brain, they cannot understand, retain, use or weigh information relevant to the decision, or communicate their decision.

Other jurisdictions have analogous schemes, such as Ontario's Substitute Decisions Act and Health Care Consent Act, and Scotland's Adults with Incapacity (Scotland) Act 2000.

Assessment

Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific: a person may be able to decide what to eat but not whether to sell a house, and capacity may fluctuate with illness, delirium or medication. Assessment accordingly focuses on the functional abilities listed above rather than on diagnosis alone. Serious consequences flow from a finding of incapacity: others — attorneys under lasting powers of attorney, court-appointed deputies, doctors, or courts — may lawfully decide on the person's behalf.

Related concepts

Mental capacity is distinct from competence in the criminal-law sense, from the "Gillick competence" standard applied to minors in medical consent, and from mental disorder: many people with mental illnesses retain capacity for most decisions, and the Mental Health Act 1983 addresses detention rather than capacity as such.
