Carrier testing, or carrier screening, is a type of genetic testing used to find out whether a healthy person carries a copy of a gene variant that could cause a genetic disorder in their children if their partner carries a variant in the same gene. It is most often offered before conception or in early pregnancy, and increasingly screens for hundreds of conditions at once (expanded carrier screening).

How it works

Most conditions screened are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern: a carrier has one working and one non-working copy of a gene and is usually unaffected, but if both parents are carriers of variants in the same gene, each child has a 1-in-4 (25%) chance of inheriting both variants and being affected. Screening uses a blood or saliva sample analysed by DNA sequencing or genotyping panels. Classic screening panels cover cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy and fragile X syndrome; population-specific screening has long been offered for Tay–Sachs disease in Ashkenazi Jewish communities and for thalassaemias and sickle cell disease in Mediterranean, African and Asian populations.

Implications

If both partners are found to be carriers, options include natural conception with prenatal diagnosis (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis), in-vitro fertilisation with preimplantation genetic testing, donor gametes, or adoption. Guidelines from bodies such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommend that carrier screening be offered to all pregnant patients and those planning pregnancy. Because no test detects every variant, a negative result reduces but does not eliminate risk ("residual risk"), and genetic counselling is recommended to explain results.
