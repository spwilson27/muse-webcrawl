Röstigraben

Röstigraben (German pronunciation: [ˈrøːstiˌɡraːbən], literally "rösti ditch" or "rösti trench") is a humorous term used to denote the cultural and linguistic boundary between the German-speaking and French-speaking parts of Switzerland. Rösti is a potato dish associated with the German-speaking cantons, while Graben means a ditch or trench — also a punning reference to the trenches of the First World War, during which tensions between the language groups, who sympathised with different belligerents, became acute.

The language border

The divide runs roughly along the Saane/Sarine river, through the bilingual canton of Fribourg and the bilingual city of Biel/Bienne, and along the crest of the Bernese Alps. West of it lies the Romandy (Suisse romande), where French is spoken; east of it, German-speaking Switzerland. The border has been largely stable since the early Middle Ages.

Political and cultural significance

The term is often invoked when referendum results diverge between the language regions. A famous example was the 1992 vote on joining the European Economic Area, approved by most French-speaking voters but rejected by the German-speaking majority, which decided the national outcome. French speakers are generally perceived as more open to state intervention and to European integration, while German speakers are seen as more fiscally conservative — stereotypes that Swiss commentators both use and criticise. A similar, joking divide with Italian-speaking Ticino is sometimes called the Polentagraben, after polenta.

Despite the jokes, the Röstigraben is usually presented as a source of national diversity rather than a genuine threat to Swiss unity; Switzerland's federal structure, with cantons grouped by language, largely manages the differences institutionally.
