Omission (law)

An omission is a failure to act, and in law it generally cannot ground criminal liability unless the defendant was under a legal duty to act. This reflects the common law's reluctance to compel positive action: there is ordinarily no offence of failing to rescue a stranger, however morally blameworthy the failure appears.

Duties to act

A duty to act may arise in several situations:

  • Statutory duty — for example, duties to file tax returns or to stop and report a road traffic accident.
  • Contractual duty — in R v Pittwood (1902) a railway crossing keeper who failed to close a gate was liable for the resulting death.
  • Relationships — parents owe duties to their children; in R v Gibbins and Proctor (1918) a father and partner who starved a child were guilty of murder.
  • Voluntary assumption of responsibility — in R v Stone and Dobinson (1977) defendants who took in an elderly relative and failed to care for her were guilty of manslaughter.
  • Creation of a dangerous situation — in R v Miller (1983) a squatter who accidentally started a fire and then simply went back to sleep was guilty of arson, because having created the danger he was obliged to act.
  • Public office — misconduct in public office can be committed by omission.

Civil law systems

Many civil-law jurisdictions impose a general duty to rescue: Germany's unterlassene Hilfeleistung and France's non-assistance à personne en danger make it an offence to fail to render reasonable assistance in an emergency.
