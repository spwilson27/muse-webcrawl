Canfield ocean

A Canfield ocean is an anoxic, sulfidic (euxinic) ocean proposed to have characterised much of the Proterozoic Eon, roughly 1.8 billion to 800 million years ago. It is named after American geologist Donald Canfield, who proposed the model in 1998.

The model

Unlike earlier ideas that the deep ocean became fully oxygenated after the Great Oxidation Event, Canfield argued that atmospheric oxygen was sufficient to weather sulfide minerals on land, delivering sulfate to the oceans, where sulfate-reducing bacteria produced hydrogen sulfide. The result was an ocean with an oxygenated surface layer overlying sulfidic deep waters — in contrast to the iron-rich (ferruginous) oceans of the Archaean.

Consequences

Sulfide scavenges trace metals such as molybdenum and iron needed for nitrogen fixation, which may have limited primary productivity and delayed the evolution and diversification of complex eukaryotic life during the so-called "Boring Billion". The eventual oxygenation of the deep ocean in the Neoproterozoic may have helped enable the rise of animals.

Evidence

Evidence comes from iron speciation and sulfur and molybdenum isotope records in Proterozoic sedimentary rocks, though later work suggests sulfidic waters were patchier than the original model proposed.
