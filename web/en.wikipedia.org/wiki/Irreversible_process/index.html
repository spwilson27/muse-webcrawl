Irreversible process

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Free expansion is a classic irreversible process.

In thermodynamics, an irreversible process is a process that cannot be reversed to return both the system and its surroundings to their original states. During an irreversible process the total entropy of system plus surroundings increases, in accordance with the second law of thermodynamics. All natural processes are irreversible to some degree; the reversible process is an idealised limit, approached when a process is carried out infinitely slowly (quasi-statically) without dissipative effects.

Sources of irreversibility

Irreversibility is generated by finite gradients and friction-like effects: heat transfer across a finite temperature difference, unrestrained (free) expansion of a gas, mixing of different substances, viscous dissipation and turbulence, electrical resistance, inelastic deformation, chemical reactions proceeding at finite rates, and hysteresis. Each of these produces entropy — quantified, in engineering thermodynamics, by the Gouy–Stodola theorem, which equates lost work potential to the ambient temperature times the entropy generated.

Examples

When a gas in an insulated container expands into an evacuated chamber, no work is done and no heat is exchanged, yet the process is irreversible: restoring the gas to its original volume requires compressing it, expending work and rejecting heat to the surroundings. Similarly, heat flowing spontaneously from a hot body to a cold one never flows back unaided; a refrigerator must consume work to reverse it. Diffusion, combustion, and the shattering of a glass are everyday irreversible processes.

Reversible limit

A reversible process changes the system through a succession of equilibrium states and produces no entropy. Heat engines operating reversibly (the Carnot cycle) set the maximum possible efficiency between two temperatures; real engines fall short in proportion to their internal irreversibilities. The concept also matters in chemistry, where the maximum non-expansion work obtainable from a reaction equals its Gibbs free energy change only along a reversible path.

Microscopic interpretation

Statistically, irreversibility reflects the overwhelming probability of macroscopic evolution toward states of higher multiplicity, even though the underlying microscopic laws are (nearly) time-symmetric — the puzzle posed by Loschmidt's paradox and addressed by Boltzmann's H-theorem and modern fluctuation theorems.
