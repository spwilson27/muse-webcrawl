Weregild

Weregild (also spelled wergeld, wergild or weregeld; from Old English wer "man" and geld "payment, money") was a value placed on every being and piece of property in the law codes of Germanic peoples and of early medieval England. If a person was killed, the killer (or his family) had to pay the fixed sum to the victim's kin; injuries likewise attracted fixed tariffs depending on the body part and the rank of the injured party. The system was designed to end or prevent blood feuds by substituting a money payment for revenge.

The system

Weregild sums varied sharply with social rank. In the laws of Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602), the earliest surviving English law code, a ceorl's (freeman's) life was valued at 100 shillings, while a nobleman commanded 300 shillings; Welsh law had its own equivalent, galanas. Kings commanded enormous sums, and the wergeld of a person of high rank who was killed could ruin a kindred. Separate fines, such as the bót for injuries and the manbót paid to a lord for killing his dependent, operated alongside the wergeld paid to the family.

Decline

As royal justice and centralised courts expanded after the Norman Conquest, private composition for homicide declined, homicide became a plea of the Crown punished capitally, and weregild faded from English practice, though similar institutions persisted longer in Scandinavia, the Low Countries and Ireland (as éraic).
