Greensill lobbying scandal

The Greensill lobbying scandal was a political scandal in the United Kingdom that emerged in 2021 concerning the activities of the financial firm Greensill Capital and the access it enjoyed to government, in particular through former prime minister David Cameron, who had been employed as an adviser to the company after leaving office.

Background

Greensill Capital, founded by the Australian financier Lex Greensill, specialised in supply-chain finance, lending money to companies against their unpaid invoices. Greensill had earlier worked as an adviser to the UK government during Cameron's premiership, developing a scheme under which pharmacies were paid early for prescriptions dispensed to the National Health Service. After leaving Downing Street in 2016, Cameron became an adviser to Greensill Capital in 2018, holding share options potentially worth millions of pounds.

The lobbying

In 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the economy, Greensill sought access to the Bank of England's Covid Corporate Financing Facility and to government-backed loan schemes. Cameron lobbied on the firm's behalf, sending text messages to Chancellor of the Exchequer Rishi Sunak and emails to other ministers and officials. The Treasury released the messages under freedom of information requests; Sunak replied that he had "pushed the team" to explore how Greensill might participate, although officials ultimately rejected the proposals.

Collapse and fallout

Greensill Capital collapsed into administration in March 2021, exposing billions of pounds of loans, particularly those connected to the metals magnate Sanjeev Gupta's GFG Alliance. The scandal prompted renewed debate about the rules governing lobbying by former ministers and officials, the revolving door between government and business, and the work of the Advisory Committee on Business Appointments. Several reviews followed, including one by lawyer Nigel Boardman commissioned by the government, and Cameron gave evidence to parliamentary committees, saying he had not broken any rules but accepting that communications with ministers should be conducted through formal channels.
