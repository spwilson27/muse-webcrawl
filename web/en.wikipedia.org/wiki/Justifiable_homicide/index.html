The concept of justifiable homicide in criminal law is a defence to culpable homicide (criminal or negligent homicide). Generally, a homicide can be justified if it prevents greater harm to the innocent. A homicide may be considered justified if it is done to prevent a very serious crime, such as rape, armed robbery, manslaughter, or murder. The victim must pose an imminent threat of causing death or grave bodily injury, or of committing a serious felony. [Image: A courthouse, where claims of justifiable homicide are adjudicated]

Justifiable homicide is distinct from excusable homicide: a justified act is one that the law affirmatively approves under the circumstances, while an excused act (for example, a killing by a person lacking criminal responsibility) is wrongful but not punished. Unlike excuses, justification negates the wrongfulness of the act itself, so an assistant to the act may also be blameless, and the person attacked has no right to resist a justified use of force.

Common-law tradition

In the common-law tradition, categories of justifiable homicide have historically included killing in the execution of lawful authority (such as carrying out a death sentence), killing to prevent an atrocious felony or to arrest a fleeing felon, and killing in self-defence or in defence of one's home. Modern statutes have narrowed some older categories — for instance, the United States Supreme Court in Tennessee v. Garner (1985) restricted deadly force against fleeing suspects to those posing a significant threat of death or serious injury.

Self-defence and defence of others

The most commonly invoked ground is self-defence: a person who reasonably believes they face an imminent threat of death or serious bodily harm may use deadly force in response. Jurisdictions differ on requirements of proportionality, imminence, and reasonable belief, and on whether a duty to retreat applies before deadly force may be used; "stand your ground" laws in many US states remove any such duty in places the defender is lawfully present. The "castle doctrine" gives special protection to force used against intruders in the home in many jurisdictions.

Law enforcement and state-sanctioned killings

Killings by police officers and other officials in the lawful performance of their duties, such as to stop an imminent deadly threat, are generally treated as justifiable homicide, as are killings in lawful warfare by combatants and executions carried out under a valid sentence — though the classification of executions varies in jurisdictions that have abolished capital punishment.

Statistics

In the United States, the FBI's crime reporting records several hundred justifiable homicides by private citizens and by law-enforcement officers each year. Definitions and reporting completeness vary, and criminologists note that cases ruled justifiable by police are rarely re-examined unless prosecuted.
