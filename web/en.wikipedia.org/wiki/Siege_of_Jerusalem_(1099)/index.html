Siege of Jerusalem (1099)

[Image: medieval miniature of crusaders assaulting the walls of Jerusalem with siege towers, July 1099]

The Siege of Jerusalem took place from 7 June to 15 July 1099 during the First Crusade. The climax of the crusade, it ended with the crusaders storming the city — then held by the Fatimid Caliphate of Egypt — and massacring much of its Muslim and Jewish population, an atrocity that has echoed in historical memory ever since.

The siege

The crusader army, perhaps 12,000–13,000 strong including non-combatants, arrived before Jerusalem on 7 June 1099. The Fatimid governor Iftikhar al-Dawla had expelled the city's Christians and poisoned wells outside the walls. A first assault failed for lack of ladders and timber; relief came when Genoese ships reached Jaffa and were dismantled to build siege towers and engines. Encouraged by the priest Peter Desiderius, who reported a vision of the dead bishop Adhemar, the crusaders processed barefoot around the walls on 8 July, fasting, as priests blew trumpets in imitation of Joshua at Jericho.

The storming and massacre

On the night of 13–14 July the attackers shifted Godfrey of Bouillon's great tower against the northeastern wall; on 15 July his men gained the wall and opened the gates. In the capture, the crusaders killed indiscriminately: chroniclers described blood running in the streets and at the Temple Mount, and the Jewish population that had taken refuge in a synagogue was burned inside it. Modern historians estimate the dead in the thousands, while noting that some inhabitants were spared, ransomed or expelled, including the governor and his guard, who were escorted out by Raymond of Toulouse.

Aftermath

On 22 July Godfrey of Bouillon was chosen ruler of the city, taking — by tradition — the title Advocate of the Holy Sepulchre rather than king. A Fatimid relief army was defeated at Ascalon in August, securing the conquest, and the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem was established, lasting until 1187.
