Transposition cipher

TypeClassical cipher
OperationReordering (permutation) of plaintext units
ExamplesScytale, rail fence, columnar transposition
Contrasted withSubstitution cipher

In cryptography, a transposition cipher (also called a permutation cipher) is a method of encryption in which the positions held by units of plaintext are shifted according to a regular system, so that the ciphertext constitutes a permutation of the plaintext. Unlike a substitution cipher, the letters themselves are unchanged — only their order is rearranged — so the ciphertext has exactly the same letter frequencies as the plaintext.

A message written into a grid and read off by columns

Examples

The ancient Spartan scytale wrapped a strip of parchment around a rod and wrote along its length; unwound, the strip showed scrambled letters that could only be read on a rod of the same diameter. The rail fence cipher writes the message in a zigzag across several "rails" and reads it off row by row. In columnar transposition, the message is written in rows into a grid whose columns are ordered by a keyword, then read off column by column in the keyword's order; double transposition applies the process twice and was used for serious field ciphers.

Use and cryptanalysis

Transposition ciphers were widely used militarily: the German ADFGX cipher of the First World War combined fractionation with transposition, and route and grille ciphers served various armies into the twentieth century. Because letter frequencies are preserved, pure transposition is vulnerable to anagramming — trying rearrangements until recognizable patterns of the language appear, a task well suited to computers. In modern cipher design, permutation remains a fundamental operation: block ciphers such as the Data Encryption Standard and AES combine transposition with substitution to achieve diffusion and confusion.
