Photographic plate

Photographic plates preceded photographic film as a capture medium in photography, and were still used in some communities until the end of the 20th century. They consist of a rigid support, typically a sheet of glass, coated with a light-sensitive emulsion of silver salts suspended in gelatin or collodion.

A glass photographic plate in its holder.

History

The first practical plates were the wet collodion plates introduced by Frederick Scott Archer in 1851. These had to be coated, exposed and developed while still wet, requiring photographers to carry a portable darkroom. Dry gelatin plates, commercialised in the 1870s and 1880s, could be manufactured in factories, stored, and developed long after exposure, and they remained the standard medium for professional photography until flexible roll film took over in the early twentieth century.

Scientific use

Plates survived longest in science, above all in astronomy. Glass plates are dimensionally stable, can cover a wide field of view, and can be archived for a century or more. Large surveys such as the Harvard College Observatory plate collection — more than half a million plates — record the sky back to the 1880s and are still used to study long-term changes in variable stars. Digitisation projects such as DASCH (Digital Access to a Sky Century at Harvard) have scanned much of this archive. Photographic plates were also used in particle physics, spectroscopy, and X-ray crystallography.

Decline

By the end of the twentieth century, charge-coupled devices (CCDs) had displaced plates in astronomy because of their far higher quantum efficiency, linearity, and immediate digital output. Specialist plate manufacture ended in the early 2000s, though old stock and small-scale coating continue for artistic and historical-process photography.
