Michael Pupin

Born: Mihajlo Idvorski Pupin, 9 October 1858, Idvor, Austrian Empire
Died: 12 March 1935, New York City
Known for: Pupin (loading) coil; long-distance telephony
Notable work: From Immigrant to Inventor (1923)

Michael Idvorski Pupin (Serbian Cyrillic: Михајло Идворски Пупин; 9 October 1858 – 12 March 1935) was a Serbian-American physicist, physical chemist and philanthropist, long a professor at Columbia University. He is best known for his numerous patents, including the loading coil that greatly extended the range of long-distance telephone lines.

Life

Pupin was born in the village of Idvor in the Banat, then in the Austrian Empire, and emigrated to the United States in 1874, arriving almost penniless. He worked in factories while studying at night, won admission to Columbia College, and graduated in 1883. After doctoral studies in Berlin under Hermann von Helmholtz, he returned to Columbia to teach mathematical physics and electromechanics, building one of America's first strong physics departments.

Work

His most famous invention, patented around 1900, was the systematic placing of inductive loading coils at intervals along telephone cables — "pupinisation" — which sharply reduced distortion and attenuation of voice signals; AT&T bought the rights for a large sum. He also worked on X-ray imaging, devising a fluorescent screen technique that shortened exposure times, and on electrical resonance and carrier wave systems. Pupin was a founding member of the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA), the forerunner of NASA, and a campaigner for Serbian interests after the First World War.

Autobiography and legacy

His autobiography From Immigrant to Inventor (1923) won the Pulitzer Prize for Biography in 1924 and became a classic immigrant success story. Columbia's physics building, Pupin Hall, is named in his honour, as is the Pupin Medal awarded by the university's engineering alumni.
