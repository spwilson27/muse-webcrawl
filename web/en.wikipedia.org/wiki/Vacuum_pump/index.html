Vacuum pump

A vacuum pump is a device that draws gas molecules from a sealed volume in order to leave behind a partial vacuum. The first vacuum pump was invented in 1650 by Otto von Guericke, whose air pump enabled the famous Magdeburg hemispheres experiment. Vacuum pumps are used in a great many industrial and scientific processes wherever an environment with pressure much lower than atmospheric is required.

Types

Vacuum pumps can be broadly categorized according to their operating principle:

  • Positive displacement pumps expand a cavity, seal it, and exhaust it to atmosphere — including rotary vane, piston, scroll and diaphragm pumps.
  • Momentum transfer (molecular) pumps, such as turbomolecular and diffusion pumps, use high-speed jets or blades to impart momentum to gas molecules, driving them toward the exhaust; they achieve high and ultra-high vacuum but must be backed by another pump.
  • Entrapment pumps capture gases in a solid or adsorbed state — cryopumps, getter pumps and ion pumps among them.

Performance

Pumps are rated by ultimate pressure and pumping speed. Pressures are commonly measured in pascals or torr, and regimes range from rough vacuum (down to about 100 Pa) through high vacuum to ultra-high vacuum below 10−7 Pa. Two-stage rotary vane pumps typically reach around 0.001 mbar, while turbomolecular pumps backed by a roughing pump reach far deeper vacua.

Applications

Vacuum pumps serve in semiconductor fabrication, thin-film coating, freeze-drying of food and pharmaceuticals, mass spectrometry, vacuum packaging, and brake servos and other automotive systems. Many processes require combinations of pumps staged from atmosphere down to the target pressure.
