Meat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Assorted cuts of beef, pork and poultry]

Meat is animal tissue, often skeletal muscle, that is eaten as food. Humans have hunted, farmed, and scavenged animals for their meat since prehistoric times. The establishment of settlements in the Neolithic Revolution allowed the domestication of animals such as chickens, sheep, rabbits, pigs, and cattle. This eventually led to their use in meat production on an industrial scale in slaughterhouses.

Meat is mainly composed of water, protein, and fat. It is edible raw but is normally eaten after it has been cooked and seasoned or processed in a variety of ways. Unprocessed meat will spoil or rot within hours or days as a result of infection with and decomposition by bacteria and fungi.

Meat is important to the food industry, economies, and cultures around the world. There is nevertheless much public debate about the ethics of eating animals, the environmental impact of meat production, and the health effects of consumption.

Etymology

The word meat comes from the Old English mete, which originally referred to food in general. In modern usage, "meat" can be restricted to the flesh of mammals, excluding fish and sometimes poultry — as in the expressions "meat and fish" — though in broader usage it covers animal flesh generally. Specific terms exist for the meat of particular animals, many derived from Norman French after 1066: beef (cattle), pork (pig), mutton (sheep), veal (calf) and venison (game).

History

Consumption of meat by early hominins is evidenced from at least 2.6 million years ago by cut-marked bones at archaeological sites. Hunting and later pastoralism and animal husbandry spread worldwide. Selective breeding transformed livestock over the last few centuries, and industrialisation in the 19th and 20th centuries brought refrigeration, large-scale feedlots, and global trade in meat products.

Production

World meat production has risen steadily; poultry is the most consumed meat globally by some measures, followed by pork and beef. Global production of meat in the early 2020s was on the order of 350–360 million tonnes per year. Animals are typically raised, transported, and slaughtered under national regulations governing welfare and food safety; meat is then cut, processed and packaged.

Composition and nutrition

Meat is a source of complete protein, providing all essential amino acids, and of vitamin B12, iron (including highly bioavailable haem iron), zinc and other nutrients. Fat content varies widely by species, cut, and production method. Processed meats include products such as sausages, ham, bacon and salami, often cured, smoked or salted.

Types

Common categories include red meat (beef, pork, lamb), poultry (chicken, turkey, duck), seafood (fish and shellfish), and game. "Cultured" or cell-cultivated meat, produced from animal cells in vitro, entered commercial sale in a few jurisdictions in the 2020s.

Environmental impact

Livestock production accounts for a significant share of global greenhouse-gas emissions — commonly estimated at roughly 14.5% of anthropogenic emissions — as well as large land and water use, particularly for ruminants. Impacts vary greatly between production systems.

Health

Moderate consumption of unprocessed meat is a normal part of many diets, but high consumption of red and processed meat has been associated with increased risks of colorectal cancer and cardiovascular disease. In 2015 the WHO's IARC classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A).
