Coase theorem

The Coase theorem, first articulated by economist Ronald Coase in his 1960 article "The Problem of Social Cost", holds that where property rights are well defined and transaction costs are negligible, private bargaining between parties will lead to an efficient allocation of resources regardless of the initial assignment of rights — that is, an externality can be internalised through negotiation. The label "theorem" was coined by George Stigler; Coase himself emphasised that the real world is characterised by positive transaction costs, in which case the initial allocation of rights and the design of institutions do matter.

A classic Coasean example: a factory and its neighbour.

Statement and example

Coase's canonical example involves a factory whose smoke damages a neighbouring laundry, or a rancher whose cattle stray onto a farmer's crops. If bargaining is costless, the parties will agree on the activity level that maximises their joint surplus: if the right to pollute is worth more to the factory than clean air is to the laundry, the factory will pay for the right; otherwise the laundry can pay the factory to abate. The efficient outcome is the same either way, though the distribution of wealth differs.

Criticism and significance

Critics note that transaction costs, holdout and free-rider problems, wealth effects, and asymmetric information mean the theorem's conditions rarely hold for large-scale problems such as air pollution, where millions of parties are affected. The theorem nevertheless transformed economics and law by shifting attention to transaction costs, property rights, and institutional design, and it underlies arguments for market-based environmental policies such as tradable emissions permits. Coase received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1991.
