Mechanotransduction

In cellular biology, mechanotransduction (mechano + transduction) is any of various mechanisms by which cells convert mechanical stimuli into electrochemical and biochemical activity. This process is fundamental to a number of senses and physiological processes in the body, including proprioception, touch, balance and hearing.

Mechanisms

A central role is played by mechanosensitive ion channels, membrane proteins that open or close in response to forces such as membrane tension, generating ion fluxes. The best characterised examples include the Piezo channels (Piezo1 and Piezo2), whose discovery by Ardem Patapoutian's group contributed to the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and channels of the TRP family.

Cells also sense mechanical properties of their surroundings through integrin-based focal adhesions that link the extracellular matrix to the cytoskeleton. Forces transmitted through these adhesions activate signalling pathways — such as FAK, Rho/ROCK and the YAP/TAZ transcriptional regulators — that control gene expression, cell proliferation, differentiation and migration. Substrate stiffness, for example, can direct stem cells toward bone, muscle or neural lineages.

Clinical relevance

Defects in mechanotransduction contribute to muscular dystrophy, polycystic kidney disease, deafness, chronic pain, and the progression of cancer, in which stiffening tumour tissue alters cell behaviour. Mechanotransduction also underlies bone remodelling in response to load.
