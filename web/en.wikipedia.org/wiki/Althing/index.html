Althing

The Althing (Icelandic: Alþingi, "general assembly") is the supreme national parliament of Iceland. Founded at Þingvellir ("Thing Fields") in AD 930, it is one of the oldest surviving parliamentary institutions in the world. The modern Althing sits in Reykjavík and has 63 members, elected every four years by proportional representation.

History

Norwegian settlers established the Althing during the Icelandic Commonwealth as a national assembly of chieftains (goðar) meeting for two weeks each summer. It had both legislative and judicial functions: laws were recited from the Law Rock (Lögberg) by the Lawspeaker (lögsögumaður), and disputes were heard in courts. It was at the Althing in AD 1000 that Iceland peacefully adopted Christianity, by the arbitration of the lawspeaker Thorgeir Thorkelsson.

When Iceland submitted to the Norwegian crown in 1262–64, the Althing lost legislative power and became chiefly a court. It was abolished by royal decree in 1800, then re-established in 1843 as an advisory body — meeting in Reykjavík from 1844 — and regained legislative authority with the constitution of 1874. Full sovereignty followed in 1918, and the Republic of Iceland was proclaimed at Þingvellir in 1944.

The modern parliament

Since 1991 the Althing has been unicameral; it previously sat as two chambers. Elections use six multi-member constituencies with compensatory seats. Iceland's parliament is noted for high female representation and, in 2009, sat as the first national legislature with women in the majority of a governing coalition's seats. The Speaker (forseti Alþingis) presides over sittings in the Parliament House, built in 1881.

Þingvellir

The original assembly site at Þingvellir, in a rift valley where the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates drift apart, is now a national park and UNESCO World Heritage Site. Icelanders celebrated the Althing's millennium there in 1930 and its 1100th anniversary in 2030 was anticipated by national commemoration planning.
