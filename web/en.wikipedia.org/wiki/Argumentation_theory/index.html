Argumentation theory

Argumentation theory is the interdisciplinary study of how claims should be made, supported, attacked, and evaluated through reasoning. It examines the logical, dialectical, and rhetorical aspects of argument as it occurs in everyday discourse, law, science, and public debate, rather than only in formal logic.

Structure of an argument in Toulmin's model: claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier and rebuttal.

History

The study of argumentation dates to classical antiquity: Aristotle's Topics and Rhetoric, and the sophists' teaching of disputation, laid the foundations for later work. In the twentieth century, interest revived with Chaïm Perelman and Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca's The New Rhetoric (1958), which treated argumentation as addressed to audiences, and Stephen Toulmin's The Uses of Argument (1958), which proposed a layout of arguments based on field-dependent warrants rather than formal syllogisms.

Approaches

Toulmin model

Toulmin analysed arguments into a claim supported by grounds (data), connected by a warrant, which may itself require backing; conclusions are typically qualified (e.g., "presumably") and subject to rebuttal conditions.

Pragma-dialectics

The pragma-dialectical theory of Frans H. van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst models argumentation as a critical discussion aimed at resolving a difference of opinion, governed by rules for reasonable discussion whose violations constitute fallacies.

Informal logic

Informal logic studies arguments in natural language, developing criteria (relevance, sufficiency, acceptability of premises) for evaluating everyday reasoning, in contrast to formal deductive logic.

Argumentation schemes

Douglas Walton and others catalogued recurring patterns of defeasible reasoning — argument from expert opinion, from analogy, from consequences, and so on — each associated with critical questions that test its strength.

Applications

Argumentation theory informs legal reasoning, deliberative democracy, science communication, education (critical thinking), and artificial intelligence, where computational models of argument (such as abstract argumentation frameworks deriving from Phan Minh Dung's work) are used for non-monotonic reasoning and multi-agent dialogue.
