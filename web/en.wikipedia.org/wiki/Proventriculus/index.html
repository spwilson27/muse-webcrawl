Proventriculus

The proventriculus is part of the digestive system of birds. An analogous organ exists in invertebrates and insects. In birds it is the glandular first chamber of the stomach, lying between the crop (oesophagus) and the gizzard, where digestion begins with the secretion of hydrochloric acid and the enzyme pepsin.

Anatomy and function

The proventriculus is a spindle-shaped widening of the digestive tract. Its walls contain glands that secrete gastric juice; the pH of its contents is typically around 3–4. Food passes from the crop into the proventriculus, where it is mixed with acid and enzymes, and then moves to the muscular gizzard (ventriculus), which grinds it mechanically. In many birds, partially digested food moves back and forth between the two chambers several times to maximise chemical and mechanical breakdown.

Variations

The size and development of the proventriculus vary with diet. In fish-eating birds and raptors it is large and distensible, forming a substantial storage and digestion chamber, whereas in seed-eating birds it is relatively small, the gizzard being the dominant chamber. In some petrels and other seabirds, the proventriculus produces a rich stomach oil used to feed chicks and as a defensive secretion. The organ is also of veterinary significance: dilation of the proventriculus ("proventricular dilatation disease") is a serious wasting disorder of parrots caused by avian bornavirus.

In invertebrates

In insects and other arthropods, the term proventriculus refers to a chamber of the foregut, often armed with teeth or plates, that grinds food or acts as a valve regulating its passage into the midgut — in blood-feeding insects it is a site where pathogens such as Yersinia pestis can block the gut.
