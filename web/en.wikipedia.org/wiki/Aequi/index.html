The Aequi (also called Aequicoli in the hills) were an Italic people of central Italy who occupied the mountainous country east of Rome around the upper valleys of the Anio and Tolenus rivers and Lake Fucinus. Together with the Volsci, they were the most persistent enemies of the early Roman Republic, raiding into Latium for much of the 5th and 4th centuries BC.

Wars with Rome

Roman tradition records near-annual Aequian invasions in the 5th century BC. The most famous episode is the siege of a Roman army on Mount Algidus in 458 BC, which led, according to legend, to the appointment of Cincinnatus as dictator and his victory over the Aequi within days. Rome planted colonies such as Alba Fucens (303 BC) and Carsioli to contain them. Defeated in the Second Samnite War era, the Aequi were finally subdued in 304 BC by the consuls Publius Sempronius Sophus and Publius Sulpicius Saverrio, who are said to have taken 31 or 41 of their towns in fifty days; they received Roman citizenship without the vote (civitas sine suffragio) and were soon fully incorporated.

Language and remains

Aequian is known from only a handful of short inscriptions and is classified with the Osco-Umbrian (Sabellic) branch of Italic. Surviving remains include impressive polygonal ("Cyclopean") fortification walls at hill towns in the Aniene valley. The Roman poet Ovid, from nearby Sulmo, and place names such as Carsoli and the Val Roveto region preserve traces of the Aequian world.
