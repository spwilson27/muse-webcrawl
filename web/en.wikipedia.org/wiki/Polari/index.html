Polari (from Italian parlare, "to talk") is a form of slang or cant historically used in Britain by actors, circus and fairground showmen, professional wrestlers, merchant navy sailors, criminals, sex workers, and the gay subculture. It served as a secret language, allowing speakers — particularly gay men at a time when homosexuality was illegal in the United Kingdom — to communicate and identify one another without being understood by outsiders.

Common Polari words include bona (good), omi (man), palone (woman), vada (look), and naff (bad).

Vocabulary and origins

Polari drew on Romani, Italian, Yiddish, Cockney rhyming slang, back slang (reversed words, as in ecaf for face), and the older thieves' cant known as Parlyaree. Words that passed into mainstream English include naff, camp, butch, drag (in its clothing sense), and possibly mince and zhoosh.

Decline and legacy

Polari was popularised for mainstream audiences by the characters Julian and Sandy, played by Hugh Paddick and Kenneth Williams in the BBC radio comedy Round the Horne (1965–1968). After the partial decriminalisation of homosexuality in England and Wales in 1967, the need for a secret code faded, and some gay activists of the 1970s rejected Polari as demeaning. It has since been documented by linguists such as Paul Baker and survives in fragments within drag and LGBT culture.
