Heap leaching

A heap leach pad: crushed ore is stacked on an impermeable liner and irrigated with leach solution.

Heap leaching is an industrial mining process used to extract precious metals, copper, uranium, and other compounds from ore via a series of chemical reactions that absorb specific minerals and re-separate them after their division from other earth materials. Similar to in situ mining, heap leach mining differs in that it places the ore on a liner to facilitate extraction. Heap leaching is widely used because it allows low-grade ores — which would be uneconomical to process by conventional milling — to be treated profitably.

Process

The mined ore is usually crushed into small chunks and heaped on an impermeable plastic or clay-lined leach pad. The ore is irrigated with a dilute alkaline cyanide solution (for gold and silver), or with sulphuric acid (for copper and uranium), which percolates through the heap and dissolves the target metal. The metal-laden ("pregnant") solution drains from the base of the heap into a pond, from which it is pumped to a recovery plant. After the metal is stripped out — by activated carbon adsorption, zinc precipitation (the Merrill–Crowe process), or solvent extraction and electrowinning — the barren solution is reconstituted and recycled onto the heap.

Gold and silver

In gold heap leaching, a dilute sodium cyanide solution (typically 0.05–0.1% cyanide) is sprayed or dripped over the heap. The cyanide dissolves gold and silver into solution as metal-cyanide complexes. Recovery from solution is most commonly by adsorption onto activated carbon (carbon-in-column), followed by stripping and electrowinning to produce doré.

Copper

Copper oxide and secondary sulphide ores are leached with dilute sulphuric acid. The copper-rich solution is treated by solvent extraction, in which an organic extractant selectively binds copper, and electrowinning, which plates out high-purity copper cathode. Bioleaching, using bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans that oxidise sulphide minerals, is an important variant, particularly for low-grade sulphide heaps.

Uranium and other metals

Uranium ores have been heap leached with both acid and alkaline solutions. Nickel laterite and other ores have also been tested or treated by heap leaching on a commercial scale.

Environmental concerns

Heap leaching has been criticised for its environmental risks. Cyanide solutions are toxic, and failures of liners or pond overflows can release contaminated solutions to surface water and groundwater. Notable accidents include the 2000 Baia Mare cyanide spill in Romania, associated with a gold processing facility, which contaminated the Tisza and Danube rivers. Acid mine drainage from spent sulphide heaps, and the large volumes of residual cyanide and metals left in spent ore, require long-term management and site closure care. Modern operations use multiple liners, leak-detection systems, and solution containment designed for extreme rainfall events.
