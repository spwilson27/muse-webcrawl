Cervical vertebrae

[Image: The seven cervical vertebrae of the human neck, lateral view]

In tetrapods, cervical vertebrae (singular: vertebra) are the vertebrae of the neck, immediately below the skull. In humans there are seven, designated C1 to C7 — the same number found in almost all mammals, from giraffes to mice, with sloths and manatees among the few exceptions. Cervical vertebrae are the smallest of the movable vertebrae and are distinguished by a foramen (hole) in each transverse process, through which the vertebral artery, vertebral vein and sympathetic nerves pass.

The atlas and axis

The first two cervical vertebrae are specialised. The atlas (C1), named for the Titan who held up the sky, has no body or spinous process; it articulates with the occipital condyles of the skull and permits the nodding "yes" movement. The axis (C2) bears the odontoid process (dens), a pivot around which the atlas rotates for the shaking "no" movement. The seventh vertebra, the vertebra prominens, has a long, easily felt spinous process.

Function and clinical notes

Together the cervical vertebrae support the head, protect the spinal cord and vertebral arteries, and give the neck its mobility. They are vulnerable to injury in whiplash and falls; fractures of the dens and "hangman's fracture" of C2 are clinically important, as is cervical spondylosis (degenerative disease) causing neck pain and nerve root compression.
