"Jack and the Beanstalk" is an English fairy tale. It appeared as "The Story of Jack Spriggins and the Enchanted Bean" in 1734 and as Benjamin Tabart's moralised version in 1807. The most famous telling is that by Joseph Jacobs in English Fairy Tales (1890), which is believed to give the story's best-known form. The tale is classified as Aarne–Thompson type 328, stories about a boy who steals a giant's treasure.

Plot

Jack, a poor country boy, lives with his widowed mother and their dairy cow. When the cow stops giving milk, Jack is sent to market to sell her. On the way he meets a bean dealer who offers him five magic beans in exchange for the cow, which Jack accepts. His mother, furious, throws the beans out of the window and sends Jack to bed without supper.

During the night the beans grow into an enormous beanstalk reaching into the clouds. Jack climbs it and arrives at a land high in the sky, where he finds the castle of a giant (often an ogre). The giant's wife lets Jack in, but when the giant returns he smells the boy — uttering the famous lines "Fee-fi-fo-fum, I smell the blood of an Englishman" — and Jack hides. Over three visits, Jack steals a bag of gold coins, a hen that lays golden eggs, and a magic harp that sings by itself. On the third theft the harp cries out, waking the giant, who pursues Jack down the beanstalk. Jack calls for an axe and chops the stalk down; the giant falls to his death. Jack and his mother live happily ever after on the riches.

Origins and analysis

The story of a boy climbing to a world above the sky has analogues in many cultures and may have roots going back thousands of years. Its precise origin is unknown; folklore researchers have noted parallels in Norse mythology and in other tales of plant ladders to the sky. Moral readings differ sharply: in Jacobs's version Jack is a hero avenging his father's murder by the giant, while other versions present him simply as a fortunate thief, a tension later retellings have emphasised or subverted.

Adaptations

The tale has been adapted countless times in pantomime (where it is a staple of the British Christmas season), children's books, animation — including Walt Disney's Mickey and the Beanstalk (1947) — and films such as Jack the Giant Slayer (2013). The phrase "Jack and the Beanstalk" has become a byword for a reckless bargain that pays off spectacularly.
