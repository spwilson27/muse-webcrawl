The Klondike Gold Rush was a mass migration of prospectors to the Klondike region of Yukon in north-western Canada between 1896 and 1899. Gold was discovered there by local miners on 16 August 1896; when news reached Seattle and San Francisco the following year, it triggered a stampede of an estimated 100,000 people hoping to make their fortunes.

Discovery

The discovery claim on Bonanza Creek (then called Rabbit Creek), a tributary of the Klondike River, is credited to George Carmack, his Tagish wife Kate Carmack (Shaaw Tláa), her brother Skookum Jim and their nephew Dawson Charlie. Word of the rich strike spread slowly over the winter; in July 1897 two steamships docked in San Francisco and Seattle laden with gold, and newspapers ignited "Klondike fever" across North America.

The stampede

Most stampeders sailed to the Alaskan ports of Skagway or Dyea and crossed the coastal mountains by the White Pass or the steeper Chilkoot Trail, then built boats at Lake Bennett to float down the Yukon River to Dawson City. The North-West Mounted Police famously required each prospector to carry a year's supply of food — about a tonne of goods — partly to prevent starvation. Only about 30,000–40,000 of the 100,000 who set out reached the Klondike, and only a few hundred became rich; the best claims had already been staked by local miners before the rush began.

Decline and legacy

Dawson City swelled to some 30,000 people in the summer of 1898, briefly becoming the largest Canadian city west of Winnipeg, then emptied just as fast when gold was found at Nome, Alaska in 1899. Large-scale corporate dredging continued for decades. The rush was immortalised in the writings of Jack London, the poems of Robert W. Service, and Charlie Chaplin's film The Gold Rush (1925). Its routes and sites are preserved today by Klondike Gold Rush national historical parks in both Canada and the United States, and in 2023 the Klondike's Tr'ondëk-Klondike cultural landscape was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
