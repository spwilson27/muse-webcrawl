The law of Turkey is a civil law system based on various European legal systems, particularly the Swiss Civil Code and the German Commercial Code, adopted during the early years of the Republic as part of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk's programme of secularisation and westernisation. The Ottoman Empire's earlier mixed system — combining Islamic law, customary law and imported codes — was comprehensively replaced between 1924 and 1937.

The current framework rests on the Constitution of 1982, substantially amended by referendums in 2010 and 2017, the latter converting Turkey to a presidential system. International agreements duly put into effect carry the force of law and, in matters of fundamental rights, prevail over conflicting statutes.

History

Reform began in the Tanzimat era (1839–76), when the Ottoman state enacted commercial, penal and land codes influenced by French models alongside the Mecelle, a codification of Hanafi civil jurisprudence. The Republic abolished the caliphate and the sharia courts in 1924, adopted the Swiss Civil Code and Italian-influenced penal code in 1926, and introduced women's suffrage and laicism into the constitutional order.

Principal codes

The Turkish Civil Code of 2001 (Law No. 4721) replaced the 1926 code while retaining its Swiss architecture, improving the status of women in family law. A new Turkish Penal Code (Law No. 5237) entered into force in 2005, and a new Code of Obligations (Law No. 6098) and Commercial Code (Law No. 6102) followed in 2012. Criminal and civil procedure are governed by codes of 2004 and 2011 respectively.

Judiciary

The judiciary is organised into judicial, administrative and (historically) military branches. The Court of Cassation is the final court for civil and criminal matters; the Council of State is the highest administrative court; and the Constitutional Court reviews the constitutionality of laws and, since 2012, hears individual applications alleging violations of fundamental rights. Judges and prosecutors are overseen by the Council of Judges and Prosecutors.
