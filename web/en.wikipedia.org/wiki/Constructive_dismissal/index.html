Constructive dismissal

In labour law, constructive dismissal, also called constructive discharge or constructive termination, occurs when an employee resigns because the employer has created a hostile work environment, fundamentally changed the terms of employment, or otherwise breached the contract of employment in a way that entitles the employee to treat themselves as dismissed. Because the resignation is legally regarded as a dismissal by the employer, the employee may claim remedies such as compensation for unfair dismissal or damages for wrongful dismissal, even though formally it was the employee who resigned.

Legal test

In the United Kingdom, the classic formulation derives from Western Excavating (ECC) Ltd v Sharp [1978]: the employer must be guilty of a repudiatory breach of contract — conduct showing that it no longer intends to be bound by an essential term — the employee must resign in response to that breach, and the employee must not delay too long and thereby affirm the contract. Any fundamental breach suffices; the employee need not show that the employer deliberately sought to drive them out, although tribunals examine whether the employer's conduct, judged objectively, destroyed the relationship of trust and confidence implied into every contract of employment (see Malik v Bank of Credit and Commerce International).

Typical examples

Conduct that has been held capable of amounting to constructive dismissal includes unilaterally cutting pay or demoting the employee, imposing unreasonable changes in duties or workplace, sustained bullying or humiliation by management, failure to address grievances, and subjecting the employee to intolerable working conditions ("last straw" cases, where a series of incidents cumulatively breaches the implied term). By contrast, mere unreasonableness on the employer's part is not enough; a serious breach of contract must be shown.

Other jurisdictions

Canadian courts apply a similar test: whether a reasonable person in the employee's position would view the employer's conduct as substantially altering an essential term of employment, or as showing that the employer no longer intends to be bound. In the United States, "constructive discharge" is recognised under federal statutes such as Title VII and the Age Discrimination in Employment Act where conditions were so intolerable that a reasonable person would have felt compelled to resign; it affects issues such as eligibility for unemployment benefits.
