Cappuccino

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A cappuccino with rosetta latte art in a white ceramic cup]

A cappuccino is an espresso drink composed in roughly equal thirds: a shot of espresso, steamed milk, and a deep cap of airy milk foam, usually in a small (150–180 ml) cup, sometimes dusted with cocoa. Its name comes from the Capuchin friars, whose brown habits matched the drink's colour — the same comparison that named the cappuccino's Viennese ancestor, the Kapuziner. Legend adds Marco d'Aviano, a Capuchin present at the 1683 Battle of Vienna, who supposedly tempered captured Turkish coffee with milk and honey; the documentable story begins with espresso machines in early-20th-century Italy.

Rules, real and invented

Italian custom confines cappuccino to the morning: milky coffee after 11 am — and heaven forbid after a meal — marks the tourist, a food rule rooted in ideas about milk and digestion that Italians enforce with theatrical horror. Within the cup, cafés distinguish wet (more milk, less foam) from dry, and the foam itself changed with microfoam technique: silky, glossy, pourable foam that carries latte art, rather than the stiff meringue of older machines. Greece drinks its own freddo cappuccino, shaken cold with a foam cap, through the Mediterranean summer.

Spread

Cappuccino conquered the Anglophone world in the 1980s–90s as café culture's gateway drink — large, sweet-adjacent, forgiving — then shrank back toward the Italian format in specialty shops. It remains a test of a café's milk work, and a canvas: the rosetta poured in its foam announces the barista before the first sip.
