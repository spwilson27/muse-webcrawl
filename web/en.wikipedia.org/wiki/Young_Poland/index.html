Young Poland

Young Poland (Polish: Młoda Polska) was a modernist period in Polish visual arts, literature and music, covering roughly the years 1890 to 1918. One of the major manifestations of Polish national consciousness, it flourished in the period of the Partitions of Poland, promoting values of decadence, neo-romanticism, symbolism, impressionism and art nouveau.

The term was coined in 1898 by the critic Artur Górski in a series of articles published in the Kraków newspaper Życie ("Life"). Young Poland developed as a reaction against the positivism that had dominated Polish letters after the failed January Uprising of 1863–64, whose adherents had subordinated art to social utility. The younger generation, centred on Kraków and the periodical Życie under Stanisław Przybyszewski, proclaimed the autonomy of art — "art for art's sake" — while simultaneously charging it with an almost mystical national mission.

Literature

The movement's literary programme embraced neo-romanticism and symbolism, drawing on the earlier Polish Romantics and on Scandinavian and French models. Leading figures included the poets Kazimierz Przerwa-Tetmajer, Jan Kasprowicz and Leopold Staff; the dramatist and painter Stanisław Wyspiański, whose 1901 play The Wedding (Wesele) is a central work of Polish theatre; and the novelists Stefan Żeromski and Władysław Reymont, whose novel The Peasants (Chłopi) later earned the Nobel Prize in Literature (1924). Gabriella Zapolska wrote naturalistic dramas exposing bourgeois hypocrisy, while the young Bolesław Leśmian developed a distinctive philosophical poetry only fully appreciated later.

Visual arts

In painting, Young Poland artists such as Jacek Malczewski, Józef Mehoffer, Stanisław Wyspiański, Leon Wyczółkowski, Olga Boznańska and Wojciech Weiss combined symbolism, landscape painting rooted in the Polish countryside, and the decorative line of art nouveau (called secesja in Poland). Stained glass and poster art flourished, notably in the designs of Mehoffer and Wyspiański for Kraków churches and public buildings.

Music

The composer Karol Szymanowski is regarded as the leading musical figure of Young Poland, alongside Mieczysław Karłowicz, Ludomir Różycki and Apolinary Szeluto, who formed the informal group known as the "Young Poland in Music" publishing company around 1905.

Legacy

The movement ended with the restoration of Polish independence in 1918. Its fusion of modernist experiment with national themes set the terms for much of interwar Polish culture, and its paintings remain among the most reproduced images of Polish art.
