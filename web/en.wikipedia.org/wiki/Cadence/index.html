Cadence

[Image placeholder: A perfect authentic cadence (V–I) notated in C major]

In Western musical theory, a cadence (Latin: cadentia, "a falling") is the end of a phrase in which the melody or harmony creates a sense of full or partial resolution. A harmonic cadence is a chord progression of two or more chords that ends a phrase or movement; cadences function as the punctuation marks of tonal music.

Types of cadence

  • Perfect authentic cadence (PAC): V–I (or V–i in minor), with both chords in root position and the tonic in the highest voice of the final chord — the strongest sense of closure.
  • Imperfect authentic cadence (IAC): an authentic progression weakened by inversion, a substituted chord (such as vii°6 in place of V), or a non-tonic highest note.
  • Half cadence (HC): a phrase ending on V, often approached from I, ii or IV, leaving the music sounding unfinished — a musical question mark.
  • Plagal cadence: IV–I, the "Amen" cadence of hymnody.
  • Deceptive (interrupted) cadence: V moving to an unexpected chord, most often vi, thwarting the expected resolution.
  • Phrygian half cadence: in minor, iv6–V, with its characteristic semitone descent in the bass.

History

The term and concept descend from Renaissance polyphony, where cadences (clausulae) were formulaic voice-leading patterns at phrase endings. In Baroque and Classical practice cadences became structural signposts articulating phrases, periods and whole movements; the Classical period in particular is organised around paired phrases ending in half and perfect authentic cadences. Later composers from Wagner onward stretched, evaded and multiplied cadences, but the underlying principle — a conventionalised gesture of ending — remains fundamental to tonal form.

Other uses

By extension, "cadence" denotes rhythmic patterns of fall and resolution in speech and verse, and is used in cycling (pedalling rate), running, military drill (marching tempo and call-and-response songs) and business (recurring review cycles).
