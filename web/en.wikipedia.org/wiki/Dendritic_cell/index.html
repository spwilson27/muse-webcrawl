Dendritic cell

Dendritic cell

SystemImmune system
FunctionAntigen presentation; activation of T cells; bridge between innate and adaptive immunity

Dendritic cells (DCs) are antigen-presenting cells of the mammalian immune system, named for their branching, tree-like (Greek: dendron, tree) projections. Their main function is to process antigen material and present it on their cell surface to T cells, acting as messengers between the innate and the adaptive immune systems. They are present in tissues in contact with the external environment — the skin (where a specialised type is called the Langerhans cell), the nose, lungs, stomach and intestines — and can also be found in an immature state in the blood.

Function

Immature dendritic cells patrol tissues and continuously sample their surroundings by phagocytosis and macropinocytosis. When they encounter a pathogen and its associated molecular patterns, they mature: they reduce antigen capture, migrate via lymphatics to draining lymph nodes, and greatly increase expression of MHC molecules and co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86. In the lymph node they present processed antigen to naive T lymphocytes, providing the activation signals needed to launch a primary adaptive immune response. Dendritic cells also help maintain tolerance to self-antigens, and defects in this function contribute to autoimmunity.

Types

The two major lineages are conventional (myeloid) dendritic cells — subdivided into cDC1, specialised for cross-presentation to CD8+ T cells, and cDC2, which chiefly activate CD4+ T cells — and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, potent producers of type I interferons during viral infection. Monocyte-derived dendritic cells arise during inflammation. Follicular dendritic cells of lymphoid follicles, despite the name, are of stromal rather than haematopoietic origin and function in presenting antigen to B cells.

History and medical use

Dendritic cells were identified in mouse spleen by Ralph Steinman and Zanvil Cohn at Rockefeller University in 1973; Steinman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery (announced the day he died). Clinically, dendritic cells are exploited in cancer immunotherapy: sipuleucel-T (Provenge), approved in 2010 for prostate cancer, is prepared from a patient's own antigen-presenting cells. DC-based vaccines and DC-targeting strategies remain active research areas, as do the cells' roles in HIV transmission, allergy and transplant tolerance.
