Front crawl

The front crawl or forward crawl, also known as the Australian crawl or American crawl, is a swimming stroke usually regarded as the fastest of the four primary strokes. Because of this, it is almost universally the stroke used in freestyle swimming competitions, and is sometimes referred to simply as "freestyle".

Technique

The swimmer lies face down in the water, propelling themselves with alternating overarm strokes while the legs perform a flutter kick — a continuous up-and-down movement from the hips. The arms provide most of the propulsion: each hand enters the water ahead of the shoulder, catches, pulls through beneath the body and recovers above the surface while the body rolls from side to side.

Breathing is coordinated with the arm cycle; the swimmer turns the head to the side, typically every second or third stroke, to inhale in the trough created by the bow wave, then exhales into the water.

History

Overarm swimming was demonstrated in London in 1844 by two Ojibwe swimmers, Flying Gull and Tobacco, but was initially considered ungentlemanly in Britain. John Arthur Trudgen popularised a hand-over-hand stroke in the 1870s, and the addition of the flutter kick in Australia around the turn of the 20th century — associated with the Cavill family — produced the modern front crawl.

Competition

Front crawl dominates freestyle events from 50 metres to 1500 metres at the Olympic Games. Because FINA's freestyle rules permit any stroke, virtually all competitors use front crawl, its efficiency having been refined by swimmers such as Johnny Weissmuller, Dawn Fraser, Ian Thorpe, Michael Phelps and Katie Ledecky.
