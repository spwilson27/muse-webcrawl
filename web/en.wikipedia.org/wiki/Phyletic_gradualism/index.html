Phyletic gradualism

Phyletic gradualism
FieldEvolutionary biology, palaeontology

Key ideaEvolution proceeds slowly and steadily within lineages (anagenesis)

Phyletic gradualism is a model of evolution which theorises that most speciation is slow, uniform and gradual. When evolution occurs in this mode, it is usually by the steady transformation of a whole species into a new one through a process called anagenesis. In this view no clear line of demarcation exists between an ancestral species and a descendant species, unless splitting occurs.

History

The gradualist picture derives from Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), which emphasised the accumulation of small variations over immense spans of time, and from the uniformitarian geology of Charles Lyell. For much of the 20th century gradualism was the default assumption of the modern synthesis: populations evolve by the slow shifting of gene frequencies, and the fossil record's gaps were attributed to its incompleteness.

Relation to punctuated equilibrium

In 1972 Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould proposed punctuated equilibrium, arguing that most species exhibit long periods of morphological stasis interrupted by geologically rapid episodes of branching speciation. The term "phyletic gradualism" was largely coined in this debate as the contrasting pole. Gould later emphasised that the two models describe different scales and that neither excludes the other entirely.

Evidence and debate

Some fossil lineages, such as certain planktonic foraminifera and bryozoans, do show gradual trends consistent with phyletic gradualism, while many others show stasis-and-punctuation patterns. Modern work generally treats gradual change and punctuated change as endpoints of a continuum, with tempo varying by lineage and environment. At the molecular level, gradual accumulation of neutral mutations remains a core mechanism regardless of morphological tempo.
