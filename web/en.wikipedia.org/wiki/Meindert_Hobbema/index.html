Meindert Hobbema (baptised 31 October 1638 – 7 December 1709) was a Dutch Golden Age painter of landscapes, specialising in views of wooded countryside with winding paths, water mills, ponds and cottages. He was the most accomplished pupil of Jacob van Ruisdael, the leading Dutch landscape painter of the period, and in the 19th and 20th centuries his reputation rose until he was regarded as one of the finest landscape artists of the Dutch school.

Life

Hobbema was born in Amsterdam, the son of a carpenter. Orphaned as a child, he lived for a time in an orphanage. Around 1655 he became the pupil of Jacob van Ruisdael, recently returned from Haarlem, and travelled with him through the eastern Netherlands and into Germany, sketching the scenery that would fill both men's paintings. In 1668 Hobbema married Eeltje Vinck and obtained a well-paid post as a wine gauger (inspector of imported wine) for the Amsterdam magistracy. From this time his output of paintings fell sharply, though his greatest works date from after his appointment — a pattern that has led art historians to debate the chronology and attribution of parts of his oeuvre.

Work

Hobbema's characteristic pictures show sunlit woodland scenes: tall trees flanking a road or sandy track, water mills beside still ponds, travellers and animals small within the scene, and a luminous sky occupying much of the canvas. His most famous painting, The Avenue at Middelharnis (1689, National Gallery, London), is a late masterpiece of almost geometric perspective: a straight avenue of pollarded alder trees recedes between fields and gardens toward the village church tower. Other well-known works include The Water Mill (Rijksmuseum) and the wooded landscapes in the Wallace Collection and the Mauritshuis.

Reputation

Although Hobbema was known in his lifetime, his fame is largely posthumous. English collectors of the 18th and 19th centuries prized his pictures highly, and many of his best works are still in British and American collections. In the early 20th century some paintings long attributed to Ruisdael were reassigned to Hobbema, and the boundary between the two artists' work remains a subject of connoisseurship.
