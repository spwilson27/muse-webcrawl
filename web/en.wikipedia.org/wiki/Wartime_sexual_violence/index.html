Wartime sexual violence

Wartime sexual violence
Legal statusWar crime; crime against humanity under international law
Key instrumentsGeneva Conventions (1949), Rome Statute (1998)

Wartime sexual violence is rape or other forms of sexual violence committed by combatants during armed conflict, war, or military occupation, often as a deliberate strategy — sometimes described as rape used as a weapon of war. It includes sexual slavery, forced prostitution, forced pregnancy, and other assaults against civilians and prisoners. Although historically widespread and frequently underreported, such violence is now recognised in international law as a war crime and, when part of a widespread or systematic attack, a crime against humanity.

History

Sexual violence has accompanied warfare since antiquity and was long treated as an expected spoil of victory or as a private matter rather than a prosecutable crime. During the Second World War, mass rapes were committed by several armies, and the Imperial Japanese military operated a system of so-called "comfort women" — women and girls forced into sexual slavery in occupied territories. Despite the scale of such crimes, they received little attention at the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials.

Modern conflicts and recognition

The wars in the former Yugoslavia in the 1990s, particularly the systematic use of rape against Bosniak women, and the 1994 Rwandan genocide, in which sexual violence was used extensively against Tutsi women, transformed international understanding. The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia and the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda prosecuted sexual violence explicitly; the 1998 Akayesu judgment in Rwanda was the first to recognise rape as an instrument of genocide. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (1998) codified rape, sexual slavery, enforced prostitution, forced pregnancy, and enforced sterilisation as war crimes and crimes against humanity.

Causes and effects

Scholars point to strategic motives — terrorising and displacing populations, ethnic cleansing, destroying community bonds — as well as opportunity, breakdown of discipline, and entrenched gender inequality. Consequences for survivors include physical injury, sexually transmitted infections including HIV, unwanted pregnancy, and severe psychological trauma, compounded by stigma and ostracism. Male victims, long overlooked, are increasingly recognised in research and law.

Prevention and prosecution

Prevention efforts include UN Security Council Resolution 1820 (2008), which recognised sexual violence as a tactic of war and a threat to international security, the office of the UN Special Representative on Sexual Violence in Conflict, and national and international prosecutions, though impunity remains common.
