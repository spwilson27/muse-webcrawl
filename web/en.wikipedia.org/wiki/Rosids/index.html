Rosids

Rosa, a member of the rosids

The rosids are a large clade of flowering plants, containing about 70,000 species, more than a quarter of all angiosperms. The clade is divided into 16 to 20 orders, depending on circumscription and classification. These orders, in turn, together comprise about 140 families. Rosids along with asterids are the two largest groups of eudicots.

Classification

The name "rosid" is based on the name "Rosidae", which had usually been understood to be a subclass. In the APG IV system, the rosids are an unranked clade within the eudicots. Fossil rosids are known from the Cretaceous period. Molecular clock estimates indicate that the rosids originated in the Aptian or Albian stages of the Cretaceous, between 125 and 99.6 million years ago.

Orders

The rosids consist of two principal subgroups: the fabids (eurosids I), including Fabales (legumes), Rosales (roses, elms, figs), Fagales (oaks and beeches), Cucurbitales, Malpighiales (willows, flax, euphorbs) and others; and the malvids (eurosids II), including Brassicales (cabbages, papaya), Malvales (mallows, cacao, balsa), Sapindales (citrus, maples, lychee) and Myrtales (eucalypts, pomegranate). The orders Vitales (grapes) and Saxifragales stand near the base of the clade in most analyses.

Economic importance

Rosids include many of humanity's most important plants: staple crops such as beans and soy (Fabales), fruits including apples, strawberries, citrus, mangoes and melons, timber trees such as oaks, teak and mahogany, and fibre and oil crops including cotton, flax and rapeseed.
