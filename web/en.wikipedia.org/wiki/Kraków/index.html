Kraków (Polish: [ˈkrakuf] ⓘ; in full the Royal Capital City of Kraków) is the second-largest and one of the oldest cities in Poland, situated on the Vistula River in the Lesser Poland region. Founded on a settlement dating back to the Stone Age and developed around Wawel Hill, Kraków was the capital of the Kingdom of Poland from 1038 to 1596 and a leading centre of Polish academic, cultural and economic life; it was a member of the Hanseatic League and the seat of the Jagiellonian University (founded 1364), one of the world's oldest universities. Its Old Town, centred on Europe's largest medieval market square, was among the first sites inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1978. Kraków is now Poland's principal tourist destination and a major hub for business services and technology.

History

Legend attributes the city's founding to the mythical ruler Krakus, said to have slain the Wawel Dragon. Kraków became a bishopric in 1000 and capital under Casimir I the Restorer in 1038. It was devastated by Mongol invasions (1241, 1259, 1287) and refounded under Magdeburg law in 1257 with its great grid-planned market square. Under Casimir III the Great the city flourished: Wawel Castle was rebuilt, the university founded (1364), and the Jewish town of Kazimierz established nearby. The Jagiellonian era (1386–1572) was a golden age of learning and art — Nicolaus Copernicus studied here in the 1490s. In 1596 Sigismund III Vasa moved the royal residence to Warsaw, ending Kraków's role as capital. The city suffered in the Swedish Deluge, was partitioned to Austria in 1795, was a free city republic (1815–1846), and then remained in Austrian Galicia until 1918. In the Second World War it became the seat of Nazi Germany's General Government under Hans Frank; most of Kraków's Jews were murdered at Płaszów and Auschwitz, an hour west. Post-war communist planners built the steel town of Nowa Huta on its edge, later a centre of Solidarity resistance.

Main sights

  • Main Market Square (Rynek Główny) — with the Cloth Hall (Sukiennice), Town Hall Tower and St Mary's Basilica, famous for its wooden altarpiece by Veit Stoss and the hourly hejnał bugle call.
  • Wawel Castle and Cathedral — coronation and burial place of Polish monarchs.
  • Kazimierz — the historic Jewish quarter, revived since the 1990s, with synagogues and the Remuh cemetery.
  • Barbican and St Florian's Gate — remnants of the medieval walls.
  • Schindler's Factory museum in Zabłocie.
  • Kościuszko Mound (1820–23), one of several artificial mounds.

Culture

Kraków is widely regarded as Poland's cultural capital: it hosts the Jagiellonian University and numerous other institutions, the National Museum (with Leonardo da Vinci's Lady with an Ermine, held by the Czartoryski Museum), the Słowacki Theatre, and festivals including the Jewish Culture Festival in Kazimierz. It was European Capital of Culture in 2000 and a UNESCO City of Literature from 2013. Poets Czesław Miłosz and Wisława Szymborska, both Nobel laureates, made the city their home, as did Pope John Paul II, who served as its archbishop before his election in 1978.

Modern Kraków

The city's population is roughly 800,000, with a metropolitan area of around 1.7 million. Since 1989 it has become a leading Central European centre for shared services, IT and start-ups (sometimes dubbed a Polish "Silicon Valley"), while tourism draws many millions of visitors a year. Kraków John Paul II International Airport at Balice is Poland's second-busiest.
