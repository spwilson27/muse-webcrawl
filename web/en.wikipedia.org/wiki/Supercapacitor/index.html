A supercapacitor (SC), also called an ultracapacitor or electric double-layer capacitor (EDLC), is a high-capacity capacitor whose capacitance value is much higher than that of conventional capacitors — typically thousands of times greater than an electrolytic capacitor of similar volume — while having lower voltage limits. Supercapacitors bridge the gap between electrolytic capacitors and rechargeable batteries: they store far more energy per unit volume than conventional capacitors and can charge and discharge far faster, and over many more cycles, than batteries.

Principle of operation

Unlike a conventional capacitor, a supercapacitor has no conventional solid dielectric separating its electrodes. Instead, charge is stored in an electric double layer that forms at the interface between a porous electrode — usually activated carbon with an enormous internal surface area, on the order of thousands of square metres per gram — and an electrolyte. When a voltage is applied, ions in the electrolyte accumulate at the electrode surfaces, separated from the electronic charge in the electrode by only a few ångströms, producing very large capacitance. In pseudocapacitors, fast reversible redox reactions at the electrode surface add a further faradaic contribution.

History

The double-layer phenomenon was described scientifically in the nineteenth century by Hermann von Helmholtz. The first modern patent was filed by Howard I. Becker of General Electric in 1957, and the Standard Oil Company of Ohio (Sohio) developed and licensed practical carbon-based devices in the 1960s and 1970s. NEC commercialised the devices under the name "supercapacitor" from 1978 for computer memory backup, and Panasonic's "Goldcap" followed. From the 1990s, high-power supercapacitors entered transport and industrial applications.

Characteristics

Individual cells are limited to roughly 2.3–2.75 V, so modules connect cells in series. Specific energy (around 5–15 Wh/kg) is well below that of lithium-ion batteries, but specific power (often exceeding 10 kW/kg) is much higher, and cycle life can exceed one million charge–discharge cycles with little degradation. Self-discharge is considerably faster than in batteries.

Applications

Supercapacitors are used for memory backup, regenerative braking and acceleration assistance in hybrid and electric vehicles and trams, pitch control in wind turbines, uninterruptible power supplies, camera flashes, and energy harvesting systems. Some city buses ("capabuses") run largely on supercapacitor storage recharged at stops.
