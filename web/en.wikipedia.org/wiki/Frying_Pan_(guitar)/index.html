Frying Pan (guitar)

The "Frying Pan" is the nickname of the Rickenbacker A-22, generally regarded as the first commercially successful electric guitar. A lap steel guitar made of cast aluminium, it was developed by George Beauchamp and Adolph Rickenbacker and manufactured by the Ro-Pat-In Corporation (later Rickenbacker) beginning in 1932. Its small circular body and long neck gave it the appearance of a frying pan.

Design

The instrument's body and neck were cast in one piece from aluminium. Its pickup — the essential innovation — consisted of a pair of large horseshoe magnets arching over the strings above a coil, a design patented by Beauchamp (granted in 1937 after a long application process). The strong magnetic field and simple construction produced a loud, clear signal suitable for the Hawaiian-style lap steel playing then popular in American music.

History

Beauchamp, a vaudeville musician and promoter of National steel guitars, sought amplification so that the guitar could be heard in dance bands. With engineer Paul Barth and toolmaker Harry Watson the design was developed, and Adolph Rickenbacker, whose company made metal guitar bodies, provided manufacturing and finance. The A-22 and the wooden-bodied A-25 were sold from 1932, and the success of the electric lap steel demonstrated the commercial viability of the electric guitar, paving the way for electric Spanish-style guitars later in the decade.
