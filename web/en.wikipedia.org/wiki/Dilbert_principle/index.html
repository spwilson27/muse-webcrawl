Dilbert principle

The Dilbert principle is a concept in management developed by Scott Adams, creator of the comic strip Dilbert, which states that companies tend to systematically promote incompetent employees to management in order to limit the damage they can do. Adams first explained the principle in a 1995 Wall Street Journal article and expanded on it in his 1996 business book The Dilbert Principle, which became a bestseller.

Comparison with the Peter principle

The Dilbert principle is a counterpart of the Peter principle, formulated by Laurence J. Peter in 1969, which holds that employees are promoted on the basis of their success in their current role until they reach a level at which they are incompetent — their "level of incompetence" — where they remain. Adams argued that in practice incompetence is no barrier to promotion because the Peter principle presumes promotion is merit-based, whereas modern companies, he claimed, actively shuffle their worst performers into management positions where their idleness and poor decisions interfere least with the workers who actually produce value.

Criticism

The principle is satirical rather than a tested management theory, though it resonated widely with office workers during the 1990s. Management scholars note the argument is anecdotal: Adams drew on stories sent in by readers of the comic strip. Others point out that in reality bad managers can do enormous damage, so deliberately promoting incompetent staff into management would be counterproductive.
