Conveyancing

In law, conveyancing is the transfer of legal title of real property from one person to another, or the granting of an encumbrance such as a mortgage or a lien. A typical conveyancing transaction has two major phases: the exchange of contracts and completion, between which the legal title passes subject to registration.

[Image: signing property transfer documents at a conveyancer's office]

Process in England and Wales

Conveyancing is carried out by solicitors and licensed conveyancers. After an offer is accepted, the seller's conveyancer prepares a draft contract and property information forms; the buyer's conveyancer raises enquiries and conducts searches (local authority, drainage, environmental, and others), while the buyer's lender arranges any mortgage. Once both parties are satisfied, contracts are exchanged — at which point the transaction becomes legally binding and a deposit is paid — and on the completion date the balance is transferred, keys are released, and the transfer is registered at HM Land Registry. Stamp Duty Land Tax is payable on most purchases. Most land in England and Wales is registered, making title investigation a matter of examining the register; a shrinking residue of unregistered land requires examination of historic title deeds.

Other systems

In much of the United States the equivalent process is the real-estate closing or settlement, handled by attorneys, title companies, or escrow agents, with title insurance commonly used to manage risk. Torrens-system jurisdictions (Australia, New Zealand, parts of Canada) rely on a state-guaranteed register of title, simplifying conveyancing. Civil-law countries typically require a notary to authenticate the transfer deed.
