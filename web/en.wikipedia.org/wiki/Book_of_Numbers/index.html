The Book of Numbers (Greek: Ἀριθμοί, Arithmoi, "numbers"; Hebrew: בְּמִדְבַּר, Bemidbar, "In the wilderness") is the fourth book of the Hebrew Bible and of the Torah. Its English name comes from the two censuses of the Israelites with which it begins and later resumes; its Hebrew name reflects its setting in the wilderness between Egypt and the Promised Land.


The spies sent into Canaan

Content

The book opens at Mount Sinai with a census of the tribes (603,550 fighting men), the arrangement of the camp around the Tabernacle, and laws for priests, Levites, Nazirites and offerings. Israel then marches to Kadesh, where the episode of the twelve spies (chapters 13–14) decides the book's course: because the people believe the spies' fearful report and refuse to enter Canaan, the whole generation is condemned to die in the wilderness over forty years. The middle chapters record rebellions — Korah's revolt, Miriam and Aaron's challenge to Moses, Moses' own failure at Meribah, which bars him from the land — and further laws. The final section follows the new generation to the plains of Moab: Balaam's oracles blessing Israel, a second census, the war against Midian, the settlement of the Transjordanian tribes, and boundary and inheritance laws preparing for entry into Canaan.

Composition

Traditionally ascribed to Moses, Numbers is analysed by scholars as a composite of Priestly and older (Jahwist/Elohist) materials, reaching its final form in the Persian period. It combines narrative, census lists, itineraries and legislation — including uniquely preserved material such as the priestly blessing ("The LORD bless you and keep you…", 6:24–26), attested on silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom dating to the 7th–6th centuries BC, the oldest surviving biblical text fragments.
