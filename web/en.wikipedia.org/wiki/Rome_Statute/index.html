Rome Statute

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court is the treaty that established the International Criminal Court. It was adopted at a diplomatic conference in Rome on 17 July 1998 and entered into force on 1 July 2002, upon the 60th ratification. As of the mid-2020s around 125 states are parties. The Statute defines the Court's subject-matter jurisdiction — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression — its structure, and the criminal procedure of the first permanent international criminal court in history.

Design

The Statute's principles define modern international criminal law: complementarity (the Court acts only where national systems are unwilling or unable genuinely to proceed), individual responsibility without official-capacity immunity before the Court, a detailed Elements of Crimes, victims' participation and reparations (a Rome innovation), and activation of aggression jurisdiction (Kampala amendments, activated 2018) under restrictive conditions. Major absences — the United States, Russia, China, India and others are not parties — and withdrawals (Burundi 2017, the Philippines 2019) bound the Court's reach, and its dependence on state cooperation for arrests (the al-Bashir saga, arrest warrants unexecuted for years) is the Statute's structural weakness. Non-party Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine — investigated via Ukraine's ad hoc acceptance of jurisdiction — and warrants issued against sitting heads of state and government of non-parties have made the Rome system the arena of the era's accountability politics.
