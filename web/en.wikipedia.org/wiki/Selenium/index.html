Selenium

SymbolSe
Atomic number34
Group16 (chalcogens)
Atomic mass78.971 u
Melting point221 °C
Discovered1817, by Jöns Jacob Berzelius

Selenium is a chemical element with the symbol Se and atomic number 34. It is a nonmetal (a chalcogen, in the same group as sulfur and tellurium) with several allotropes, including a red amorphous form and a grey, semi-metallic crystalline form whose electrical conductivity increases in light — a photoconductivity exploited historically in photocells, light meters, and xerographic copier drums. It was discovered in 1817 by Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who named it from the Greek selene (moon), pairing it with the previously found tellurium (named for Earth).

Occurrence and production

Selenium is rare in Earth's crust and seldom forms its own minerals; it is obtained almost entirely as a by-product of electrolytic copper refining, from anode slimes, and from lead and other metal refining. World production is a few thousand tonnes per year, used in glassmaking (to decolorise glass and to make ruby-red glass), pigments, metallurgy, agriculture, and thin-film (CIGS) solar cells.

Biological role

Selenium is an essential trace nutrient for humans and animals: it is incorporated, as the amino acid selenocysteine, into antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidases and into thyroid-hormone-processing enzymes. The margin between need and excess is narrow. Deficiency is linked to Keshan disease (a cardiomyopathy seen in low-selenium regions of China) and impaired thyroid and immune function, while excess intake causes selenosis, with hair and nail loss and garlic-smelling breath. Adults require roughly 55 micrograms per day; Brazil nuts are an exceptionally rich source.
