Obligate parasite

An obligate parasite is a parasitic organism that cannot complete its life cycle without exploiting a suitable host. If it cannot obtain a host it fails to reproduce and dies. This contrasts with a facultative parasite, which can live and reproduce independently of a host but may adopt a parasitic lifestyle when the opportunity arises.

Dependence on the host

Obligate parasites depend on their hosts for a range of needs: nutrition, a protected microhabitat, dispersal between hosts, and cues that trigger development. Over evolutionary time, extreme dependence often leads to the loss of genes and structures no longer needed for free living — genome reduction is striking in many obligate intracellular bacteria, and viruses, which lack any independent metabolism, are obligate parasites of cellular life.

Examples

  • Viruses — all viruses must replicate inside host cells.
  • Head and body lice (Pediculus humanus) — feed and breed only on humans.
  • Plasmodium species — the malaria parasites require both mosquito and vertebrate hosts.
  • Parasitic plants such as dodder (Cuscuta) and broomrapes, which cannot photosynthesize adequately and draw water and nutrients from host plants.
  • Many fungi, including rusts and powdery mildews, that grow only on living host tissue.

Evolutionary questions

Obligate parasitism poses problems for study and control: organisms that grow only inside hosts are difficult or impossible to culture in the laboratory (as long was the case with the leprosy bacillus, Mycobacterium leprae), and their hosts may be required to complete complex, multi-stage life cycles. Eradication campaigns exploit this dependence — a parasite that absolutely requires humans, like the Guinea worm, can in principle be extinguished by breaking transmission for a single generation of the parasite.
