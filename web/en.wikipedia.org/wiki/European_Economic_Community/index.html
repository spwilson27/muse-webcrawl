European Economic Community

European Economic Community
Established:1 January 1958 (Treaty of Rome)
Dissolved:1 December 2009 (Treaty of Lisbon)
Successor:European Union / European Community (renamed 1993)

The European Economic Community (EEC) was a regional organisation created by the Treaty of Rome of 1957, which came into force on 1 January 1958. Its aim was to bring about economic integration, including a common market and customs union, among its member states: Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and West Germany. Often called the Common Market in English-speaking countries, it later expanded to twelve member states before being renamed the European Community in 1993 and absorbed into the European Union's framework in 2009.

History

The EEC was established alongside the European Atomic Energy Community (Euratom), complementing the earlier European Coal and Steel Community of 1951. Its customs union was completed by 1968, and the community developed common policies on agriculture, trade, and transport. Membership expanded in 1973 (Denmark, Ireland, and the United Kingdom), 1981 (Greece), and 1986 (Portugal and Spain). The Single European Act of 1986 set the objective of completing the single market by the end of 1992.

Institutions

The community's main institutions were the Commission, the Council of Ministers, the European Parliament (initially an appointed assembly), and the Court of Justice. Under the Merger Treaty of 1967 these institutions were shared with the other European Communities.

Legacy

The Maastricht Treaty of 1993 renamed the EEC the European Community and made it the central pillar of the newly founded European Union. The Treaty of Lisbon abolished the pillar structure in 2009, and the European Union succeeded the Community as a single legal entity.
