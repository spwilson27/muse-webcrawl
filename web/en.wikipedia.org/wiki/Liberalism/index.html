Liberalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberalism is a political and moral philosophy based on the rights of the individual, liberty, consent of the governed, political equality, the right to private property and equality before the law. Liberals espouse various and often divergent views on the scope of government depending on how they understand these principles, but generally support private property, market economies, individual rights (including civil rights and human rights), liberal democracy, secularism, the rule of law, freedom of speech and religion, and racial and gender equality.

History

Liberalism emerged from Enlightenment thought — Locke's theory of natural rights and government by consent, Montesquieu's separation of powers, and Adam Smith's economics — and through the American and French Revolutions. In the nineteenth century it drove constitutional government, free trade and expanding suffrage; John Stuart Mill's On Liberty (1859) is its classic statement of the harm principle. The Great Depression and the world wars produced "modern" or social liberalism, accepting state action on welfare and economic management, while the late twentieth century saw revivals of classical liberal and libertarian thought.

Variants

Classical liberalism emphasises limited government and market freedom; social liberalism adds a role for the state in securing substantive equality of opportunity; conservative liberalism and national liberalism blend liberal economics with conservative or national themes; and neoliberalism is a contested term for market-oriented policies prominent since the 1980s. Liberal parties govern or contend in most democracies, sometimes in alliance with conservatives, sometimes with social democrats.

Criticism

Liberalism is criticised from the left for tolerating economic inequality and from communitarian and conservative critics for excessive individualism; its universalist claims have also been challenged historically for coexisting with colonialism and exclusion. Supporters reply that liberal institutions — free elections, independent courts, free media — remain the most reliable protectors of individual freedom yet devised, and point to the post-1945 liberal international order.
