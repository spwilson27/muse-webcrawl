Auguste Deter (16 May 1850 – 8 April 1906) was a German woman whose case became the first documented description of what is now known as Alzheimer's disease. Her physician, Alois Alzheimer, studied her symptoms at the Frankfurt asylum and, after her death, examined her brain, reporting the characteristic plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in 1906.

Auguste Deter in 1902.

Case history

Deter was admitted to the Städtische Anstalt für Irre und Epileptische in Frankfurt on 25 November 1901, aged 51. In the preceding year she had developed memory loss, disorientation, delusions of jealousy toward her husband Karl, and difficulty writing. Alzheimer's recorded examination includes her famous exchange: asked to write her name, she managed "Mrs." and then said, "I have lost myself, so to say" ("Ich habe mich sozusagen verloren"). Her condition progressed to total dementia and she became bedridden, dying in 1906 of sepsis from infected bedsores.

Neuropathology

Alzheimer examined Deter's brain using the Bielschowsky silver stain and described numerous amyloid plaques and dense neurofibrillary tangles throughout the cortex — the pathological hallmarks still used to define the disease. He presented the case in Tübingen in November 1906; Emil Kraepelin named the condition "Alzheimer's disease" in his 1910 textbook.

Rediscovery

Deter's medical file, long thought lost, was rediscovered in the Frankfurt archives in 1996, and her histological slides were re-examined. Later genetic studies of preserved tissue found no mutation in the presenilin genes sometimes responsible for early-onset cases, making her an example of sporadic early-onset disease.
