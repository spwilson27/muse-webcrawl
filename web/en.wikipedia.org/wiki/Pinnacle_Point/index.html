Pinnacle Point is a complex of coastal caves and rock shelters on a rocky headland near Mossel Bay on the southern coast of South Africa. Excavations since 2000 have revealed some of the earliest evidence for the behaviour of modern humans, dating to about 170,000–165,000 years ago.

Archaeological significance

During the glacial period known as Marine Isotope Stage 6, when much of humanity may have been reduced to a small population, the occupants of the Pinnacle Point caves exploited coastal resources. The site (especially caves PP13B and PP5-6) has yielded the earliest known evidence for the systematic consumption of shellfish, the use of red ochre pigment, and the heat treatment of silcrete stone to improve tool-making — technologies dated to roughly 164,000 years ago. Later levels contain small bladelet tools and pigments from at least 71,000 years ago.

Research

The excavations, led by Curtis Marean of Arizona State University and Peter Nilssen, form part of the SACP4 project combining archaeology with a detailed palaeoclimate record. The findings support the theory that the resource-rich Cape coast served as a refuge in which key modern human behaviours developed before populations expanded out of Africa.

Protection

Pinnacle Point was declared a provincial heritage site, and in 2024 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of "The Emergence of Modern Human Behaviour: The Pleistocene Occupation Sites of South Africa".
