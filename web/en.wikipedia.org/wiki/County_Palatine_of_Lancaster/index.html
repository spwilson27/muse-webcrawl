County Palatine of Lancaster

The palatinate jurisdiction associated with the Duchy of Lancaster.

The County Palatine of Lancaster is the legal designation of the county palatine historically exercised by the Dukes of Lancaster and, since 1399, by the English (later British) monarch in right of the Duchy of Lancaster. A county palatine was a territory whose lord exercised royal prerogatives—holding his own courts, appointing sheriffs and justices, and pardoning offences—that elsewhere belonged to the Crown.

History

Lancashire became a county palatine in 1351 when Henry of Grosmont was created Duke of Lancaster and granted palatine jurisdiction, partly to reward his service and to secure the northern border. The palatinate passed to John of Gaunt and, when his son became Henry IV in 1399, merged with the Crown—but by Henry's decree the Duchy of Lancaster was held separately from the crown lands, an arrangement that persists: the monarch is still styled Duke of Lancaster, and loyal toasts in Lancashire are traditionally drunk to "The King, Duke of Lancaster".

Institutions

The palatinate had its own Court of Chancery and common-law courts sitting at Preston and Lancaster, and its own seal. Most of the separate jurisdiction was absorbed into the national court system by the Courts Act 1971, but survivals remain: the Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster (a cabinet office), the Duchy's administration of bona vacantia (ownerless property) within the palatine boundaries, and its role in certain ecclesiastical and magisterial appointments in Lancashire, Greater Manchester, and Merseyside.

Modern extent

The historic palatine county covered a larger area than the modern ceremonial county of Lancashire, including the Furness district (now in Cumbria) and the areas now in Greater Manchester and Merseyside. The Duchy of Lancaster remains a private estate of the sovereign, administering landed and financial assets distinct from the Crown Estate.
