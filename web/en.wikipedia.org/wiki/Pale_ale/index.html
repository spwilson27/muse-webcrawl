Pale ale

Pale ale is a golden to amber coloured beer style brewed with pale malt. The term first appeared in England around 1703 for beers made from malts dried with high-carbon fuels — typically coke — which resulted in a lighter colour of beer than the brown ales popular at that time. Different brewing practices and hop quantities have resulted in a range of tastes and strengths within the pale ale family.

History

Coke had been used for roasting malts since 1642, but it was only around the beginning of the 18th century that the resulting pale malts were used to brew what became known as pale ale. By 1784 advertisements for "pale ale" appeared in the Indian press, and Burton-on-Trent brewers such as Bass and Allsopp became famous for their pale ales exported to India — the origin of the heavily hopped India pale ale (IPA), which survived the sea voyage thanks to its hop content and strength.

Styles

Main varieties include British bitter and English pale ale; the golden, hop-forward American pale ale (APA), popularised by Sierra Nevada Pale Ale (1980) and a foundation style of the craft beer movement; Belgian pale ale; and strong pale ales. Pale ales typically use top-fermenting ale yeast, warmer fermentation than lagers, and colour ranging from gold to copper.
