Hijra (South Asia)

In South Asia, hijras (Hindi: हिजड़ा) are a traditional community of people assigned male at birth — including eunuchs, intersex people and transgender women — who live in organised, all-hijra households led by a guru and who have a recorded history in the Indian subcontinent reaching back to antiquity, with mentions in texts such as the Kama Sutra and roles at Hindu and Mughal courts.

Society and religion

Hijra communities are structured around the guru–chela (teacher–disciple) relationship and are divided into houses with their own customs. Many hijras are devotees of Bahuchara Mata, a Hindu goddess, and traditionally earn a living by performing at births and weddings (badhai), where their blessings are believed to confer fertility, as well as by begging and sex work. Some undergo ritual emasculation (nirvana), though the practice is contested within the community and by health authorities.

Legal status

Under British rule, hijras were criminalised under the Criminal Tribes Act 1871 in parts of India. In the 21st century, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Nepal have all moved to recognise hijras and transgender people legally as a third gender: India's Supreme Court judgment in NALSA v. Union of India (2014) recognised transgender people as a third gender and affirmed fundamental rights, and Pakistan's courts and 2018 Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act granted recognition and protections. Despite legal progress, hijras continue to face poverty, stigma and violence.
