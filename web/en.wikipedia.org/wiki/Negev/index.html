The Negev (Hebrew: הַנֶּגֶב, ha-Negev; Arabic: النقب, an-Naqb) is a desert and semidesert region of southern Israel. It covers more than half of the country's land area — about 13,000 km² — but is home to only a small share of its population. The region's largest city and administrative capital is Beersheba; other cities include Eilat, Dimona, Arad, Rahat and Netivot.

Geography

The Negev forms an inverted triangle with the Mediterranean coast near Gaza in the northwest, the Dead Sea and Arabah valley to the east, and the Gulf of Aqaba at its southern tip. It features rugged mountains, wadis, and unique huge erosion cirques called makhteshim, the largest being Makhtesh Ramon. Annual rainfall ranges from about 300 mm in the northwest to under 50 mm near Eilat.

History

The Negev was home to Nabataean caravan cities such as Avdat and Mamshit on the Incense Route, and in biblical tradition to Abraham's wells at Beersheba. After 1948 Israel promoted settlement and development under David Ben-Gurion's vision of "making the desert bloom"; the region hosts Ben-Gurion University, the Negev Nuclear Research Center near Dimona, military bases, and solar energy plants at Ashalim. The status and land rights of the Negev Bedouin, many living in unrecognized villages, remain a major social and political issue.
