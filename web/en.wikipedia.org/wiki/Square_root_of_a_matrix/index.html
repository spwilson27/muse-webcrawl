Square root of a matrix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A matrix B with B² = A is a square root of A

In mathematics, the square root of a matrix extends the notion of square root from numbers to matrices. A matrix B is said to be a square root of a matrix A if the matrix product BB equals A, written B² = A.

Existence and uniqueness

Unlike for positive real numbers, matrix square roots are generally neither unique nor guaranteed to exist. A positive-definite matrix has exactly one positive-definite square root, and this principal square root is widely used. However, a general matrix may have many square roots — even infinitely many — or none at all: for example, a 2×2 matrix with two distinct positive eigenvalues can have four square roots, while certain matrices with negative or zero eigenvalues, or with defective Jordan structure, may have no square root.

An invertible matrix has a square root if and only if, in its Jordan form, the blocks corresponding to negative eigenvalues occur in pairs of equal size. Every non-singular matrix has at least one square root that is a polynomial in the original matrix.

Positive semidefinite matrices

If A is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, it can be diagonalised as A = PDP−1 with D diagonal and non-negative; its principal square root is PD1/2P−1, obtained by taking the ordinary square root of each diagonal entry. This square root is itself symmetric positive semidefinite and is unique among such matrices. It appears in statistics (in the whitening of random vectors), in the polar decomposition of matrices, and in defining the Bures metric for quantum states.

Computation

Methods for computing matrix square roots include the Denman–Beavers iteration, the product form of Newton's method for the matrix sign function, and algorithms based on the Schur decomposition. The matrix sign function provides a unified approach: the square root can be extracted from the sign of a block matrix constructed from A.
