Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC /dɛk/), using the trademark Digital, was a major American company in the computer industry from the 1960s to the 1990s. The company was co-founded by Ken Olsen and Harlan Anderson in 1957. Olsen was president until he was forced to resign in 1992, after the company had gone into precipitous decline.

The company produced the influential PDP series of minicomputers, which made interactive, departmental computing affordable, and the VAX line of 32-bit superminicomputers with the VMS operating system. DEC was a leading vendor of computer systems, including computers, software, and peripherals, and its PDP and successor VAX products were the most successful of all minicomputers in terms of sales.

History

Founding and the PDP line

DEC was founded with $70,000 of venture capital from American Research and Development Corporation. Its first products were electronic modules; its first computer, the PDP-1 (Programmed Data Processor-1), was delivered in 1960. The PDP-8 (1965) is generally credited with creating the minicomputer market, costing a fraction of contemporary mainframes, and the PDP-11 (1970) became one of the most popular 16-bit machines ever built.

VAX and peak

The VAX-11/780, launched in 1977, extended the PDP-11 concept to 32-bit virtual memory computing and, with VMS and DECnet networking, dominated the midrange market through the 1980s. At its peak in the late 1980s DEC was the second-largest computer company in the world, employing more than 100,000 people.

Decline and acquisition

DEC was slow to respond to the rise of workstations, personal computers, and open Unix systems. Its 64-bit Alpha processor (1992) was technically acclaimed but arrived as the company posted enormous losses. DEC was acquired by Compaq in 1998 for about $9.6 billion, then the largest merger in the history of the computer industry; Compaq was in turn acquired by Hewlett-Packard in 2002.

Legacy

DEC's culture of engineering-driven, decentralised innovation shaped generations of computer designers; its alumni and alumni companies seeded much of the industry, and its machines hosted the development of Unix, C, the ARPANET, and early computer games such as Spacewar!
