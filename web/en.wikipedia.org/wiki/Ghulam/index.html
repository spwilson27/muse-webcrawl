Ghulam

Ghulam (Arabic: غلام, plural ghilman, غلمان) is an Arabic word meaning a young man, servant, or slave. In the history of the Islamic world the term acquired a specific institutional meaning: from the 9th century onward, ghilman were slave-soldiers, typically of Turkic, Caucasian, or Balkan origin, who were purchased or captured as boys, converted to Islam, and trained for service in the ruler's household guard and army.

History

The institution is associated particularly with the Abbasid Caliphate: Caliph al-Mu'tasim (r. 833–842) built up a corps of Turkish ghilman and founded Samarra as its garrison city, seeking troops loyal to his person rather than to tribal or regional factions. The system spread widely. Under the Samanids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks, ghilman formed the military elite, and talented slave-soldiers could rise to become generals and even founders of dynasties — the Ghaznavid Empire itself grew out of a Samanid ghulam's governorship.

Related institutions

The ghulam institution is closely related to, and often compared with, the Mamluks of Egypt and Syria, who institutionalised slave-soldier rule and eventually governed in their own right, and with the Ottoman Janissaries, recruited through the devshirme levy of Christian boys. In Safavid Persia, Shah Abbas I created a corps of ghulams drawn from Georgian, Armenian, and Circassian captives to counterbalance the Qizilbash tribal cavalry. Across these systems, slave origin paradoxically offered a route to the highest power, since ghilman owed everything to the ruler and (in theory) had no independent base of support.
