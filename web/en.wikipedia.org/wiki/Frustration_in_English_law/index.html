Frustration in English law

In English contract law, frustration is a doctrine that can discharge a contract when, after its formation, an unforeseen event makes performance impossible, illegal, or radically different from what the parties undertook. The doctrine is narrow: mere hardship, expense or inconvenience is not enough.

Development

Early common law enforced contracts absolutely (Paradine v Jane, 1647). The modern doctrine begins with Taylor v Caldwell (1863), where a music hall hired for concerts burned down and the court implied a condition that the contract depended on the hall's continued existence. The coronation cases, notably Krell v Henry (1903), extended frustration to destruction of the contract's foundation, while Herne Bay Steamboat Co v Hutton refused it where the purpose was not wholly defeated.

Tests and limits

Lord Simon's formulation asks whether the circumstances render performance "a thing radically different" from what was undertaken. Frustration cannot be self-induced (Maritime National Fish Ltd v Ocean Trawlers), cannot apply where the contract allocates the risk, and is excluded where the event was foreseeable and provided for. Leases were long thought immune, but the House of Lords accepted in principle that a lease could be frustrated, though rarely in practice.

Consequences

At common law, frustration discharges both parties automatically, but losses lay where they fell—sometimes harshly, as in Chandler v Webster. The Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 allows recovery of money paid before discharge and compensation for valuable benefits conferred, subject to the court's discretion.
