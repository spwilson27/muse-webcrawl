Transleithania

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transleithania (Hungarian: Lajtántúl, German: Transleithanien) was a term used in Austria-Hungary to denote the Hungarian part of the Dual Monarchy, that is, the lands of the Hungarian Crown lying "beyond" — east and south of — the Leitha River, which formed part of the historical border between Austria and Hungary. Its counterpart, the Austrian half of the empire, was known as Cisleithania.

Meaning and usage

The terms were never official names; they were convenient administrative and journalistic shorthand that came into use after the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 created the dualist state. Transleithania comprised the Kingdom of Hungary proper, the autonomous Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia within it, and — in looser usage — was associated with the whole Hungarian half of the Monarchy, which was governed from Budapest and formally styled the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen.

The Hungarian half had its own parliament (the Diet in Budapest), its own government and ministries for internal affairs, finance, education, justice, agriculture, and defence (the Royal Hungarian Honvéd army). Only foreign affairs, the joint army and navy, and the finances needed to support them were common to both halves, administered by joint ministers responsible to the Delegations of the two parliaments.

Territory and peoples

At its greatest extent before the First World War, Transleithania covered roughly 325,000 square kilometres with a population of about 20.9 million (1910 census). Ethnic Hungarians (Magyars) made up just under half the population; the rest included Romanians, Slovaks, Germans, Serbs, Croats, Ruthenians (Ukrainians), and others. The Magyarization policies of the Budapest government and the restricted franchise were persistent sources of tension with the non-Hungarian nationalities.

Dissolution

Transleithania ceased to exist with the collapse of Austria-Hungary at the end of the First World War in 1918. Its territory was divided by the Treaty of Trianon (1920) among the reduced Kingdom of Hungary and its neighbours: Romania, Czechoslovakia, the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (later Yugoslavia), and Austria.
