Bacillus anthracis

Bacillus anthracis is a Gram-positive, rod-shaped, spore-forming bacterium that causes anthrax, a serious disease of livestock and, occasionally, humans. Its hardy endospores can survive in soil for decades. The organism was the first bacterium conclusively shown to cause a disease: Robert Koch's 1876 work on its life cycle helped establish the germ theory of disease. Because of its potential as a biological weapon, it is a U.S. HHS select agent.

Biology and virulence

Vegetative cells are large rods (1–1.2 × 3–5 μm) that form chains and produce a unique capsule of poly-D-glutamic acid. Virulence depends on two plasmids: pXO1, encoding the three-component anthrax toxin (protective antigen, lethal factor and edema factor), and pXO2, encoding the capsule. The toxins disable immune cells and produce the oedema and shock characteristic of systemic anthrax. Three main clinical forms occur — cutaneous (the most common, usually survivable when treated), gastrointestinal and inhalational (the most lethal) — as well as injection anthrax in people who inject drugs.

History

Anthrax was a scourge of textile workers ("woolsorter's disease"). Koch's demonstration of its life cycle in 1876, and Pasteur's public vaccine trial at Pouilly-le-Fort in 1881, were landmarks of early microbiology. The bacterium figured in the 1979 Sverdlovsk accidental release and the 2001 anthrax letter attacks in the United States, which killed five people. Treatment relies on antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and doxycycline, with antitoxin for systemic cases; a vaccine (AVA) exists for at-risk groups.
