Apis (Egyptian mythology)

Apis (Ancient Egyptian: ḥp, Coptic: ϩⲁⲡⲓ Hapi) was an ancient Egyptian deity, worshipped in the form of a living bull at Memphis. In death it was assimilated to Osiris as Osiris-Apis, the origin of the Greco-Egyptian god Serapis.

In ancient Egyptian religion, Apis or Hapis (Ancient Egyptian: ḥp) was a bull deity that was worshipped in the Memphis region, identified as the son of Hathor, a primary deity in the pantheon. Initially, he was assigned a significant role in her worship, being sacrificed and reborn. Later, Apis also served as an intermediary between humans and other powerful deities, especially Ptah, whose "herald" and living manifestation the bull was considered to be.

The cult

The living Apis was a single bull, selected for special markings — black hide with particular white patterns, described by Herodotus and other classical writers — and housed in a temple at Memphis, where its movements were interpreted as oracles. When the Apis died it was mourned nationally, embalmed at great expense, and buried with full ceremony in the Serapeum of Saqqara, a vast catacomb of enormous granite sarcophagi excavated by Auguste Mariette in 1850. A new bull was then sought and installed amid public rejoicing.

History

The cult is attested from the earliest dynasties and flourished for over three thousand years. Under the Ptolemies, the deceased Apis, united with Osiris as Osiris-Apis, was hellenised as Serapis, a god deliberately promoted to unite Greek and Egyptian subjects. The cult of the living bull continued into the Roman period and faded in late antiquity.
