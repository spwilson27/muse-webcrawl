Woodpecker

Scientific classification

Kingdom: Animalia

Phylum: Chordata

Class: Aves

Order: Piciformes

Family: Picidae

Subfamilies: Jynginae (wrynecks), Picumninae (piculets), Picinae (true woodpeckers)

Woodpeckers are birds of the family Picidae, which also includes the piculets, wrynecks, and sapsuckers. Members of this family are found worldwide, except for Australia, New Guinea, New Zealand, Madagascar, and the extreme polar regions. Most species live in forests or woodland habitats, although a few species are known to live in treeless areas such as rocky hillsides and deserts. The family includes roughly 240 species.

Characteristics

Woodpeckers range from tiny piculets about 7 cm long to the great slaty woodpecker, up to 58 cm. Most have strong bills for drilling and drumming on trees, very long sticky tongues for extracting insects, and stiff tail feathers used as a prop against trunks. Their feet are zygodactyl — two toes facing forward and two backward — suiting them to climbing vertical surfaces. Many species have striking black-and-white plumage with red or yellow head markings, and the sexes are often similar in appearance.

Repeated hammering would give most animals concussion, but woodpeckers are protected by a suite of adaptations: a thick skull with spongy bone, a brain that fits snugly in the cranial cavity, powerful neck muscles, and a hyoid apparatus that wraps around the skull and acts as a shock absorber. Recent research has questioned how much shock absorption actually occurs, suggesting instead that the head and beak behave as a stiff hammer and that small brain size limits injury.

Behaviour and ecology

Woodpeckers feed mainly on insects and larvae extracted from bark and wood, plus fruit, nuts and tree sap; sapsuckers drill rows of shallow wells in bark to drink sap. They communicate by drumming — rapid pecking on resonant dead wood or other surfaces — and by loud calls. Most species excavate nest holes in dead or living trees, and their abandoned cavities are important shelter for many other animals. Woodpeckers are considered keystone species in many forests. Most are non-migratory and solitary or live in pairs, though the acorn woodpecker is famous for cooperative group living and communal granary trees.

Conservation

While many woodpeckers are common, forest-dependent species are threatened by logging and habitat loss. The ivory-billed woodpecker of North America and Cuba was long presumed extinct, though unconfirmed reports of its survival in the 2000s prompted extensive searches; the imperial woodpecker of Mexico is likewise probably extinct.
