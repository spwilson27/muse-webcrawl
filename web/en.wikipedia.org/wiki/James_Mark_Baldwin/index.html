James Mark Baldwin

James Mark Baldwin (January 12, 1861 – November 8, 1934) was an American philosopher and psychologist who was one of the founders of experimental psychology in Canada and the United States. He is known for the "Baldwin effect", a theory of how learned behaviour can influence evolution.

Career

Baldwin studied at Princeton under James McCosh and in Germany under Wilhelm Wundt. In 1889 he became professor of psychology at the University of Toronto, where he established one of the first psychology laboratories in North America. He later taught at Princeton and at Johns Hopkins University, where he founded the Psychological Review. He was a founding figure of the American Psychological Association and served as its president in 1897.

Ideas

In 1896 Baldwin proposed that an organism's ability to learn new behaviours could affect the evolutionary success of its descendants — a mechanism later named the Baldwin effect. His work in developmental psychology, describing stages of cognitive growth in children, influenced Jean Piaget. His three-volume Thought and Things (1906–1911) and his Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology were widely read.

Later life

Following a personal scandal in 1909, Baldwin resigned from Johns Hopkins and moved to France, where he spent the rest of his life, teaching in Paris and Mexico. He died in Paris in 1934.
