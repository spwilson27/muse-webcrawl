Nemesis, goddess of retribution, often shown with the wheel of fortune and a bridle of restraint.

In ancient Greek religion, Nemesis (Greek: Νέμεσις, "distribution of what is due", from the verb némein, "to give what is due") was the goddess who personified retribution for acts of hubris and the inescapable distributor of fortune, neither good nor bad, simply in due proportion. She directed human affairs so as to maintain equilibrium: happiness and unhappiness were measured out by her, and she punished those who had attained excessive good fortune or who had committed arrogance against the gods.

Mythology

Hesiod makes Nemesis a daughter of Nyx (Night), born alone or with Erebus. In the epic Cypria, Nemesis was pursued by Zeus, shape-shifting to escape him, and was eventually caught; in some versions she laid the egg from which Helen of Troy was hatched after mating with Zeus in the form of a swan — a story also told of Leda. In Aeschylus and later moralizing tradition, Nemesis works alongside Adrasteia ("the inescapable") and is closely associated with Themis and Dike (Justice) in punishing wrongdoing.

Cult and depiction

Her principal sanctuary was at Rhamnous in Attica, where her temple housed a famous statue carved, according to legend, from a block of Parian marble the Persians had brought to make a trophy of their expected victory — a supreme example of hubris punished, since they were defeated at Marathon. Nemesis was depicted as a solemn winged woman carrying a sword, whip, balance, measuring rod or bridle, sometimes with a wheel of fortune. At Smyrna she was worshipped in a dual form.

Legacy

The name "nemesis" has passed into English as a term for an implacable foe or agent of one's downfall. In Roman religion she was sometimes equated with Invidia (Envy). In modern astronomy her name was given to the Nemesis hypothesis — a conjectured companion star to the Sun proposed in the 1980s to explain periodic mass extinctions.
