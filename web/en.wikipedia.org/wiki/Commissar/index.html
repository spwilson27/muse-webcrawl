Commissar

A Soviet military commissar, 1941

Commissar (or sometimes Kommissar) is an English rendering of the Russian комисса́р (komissar), a title used for various officials in the Soviet Union and other communist states. The word derives, through French commissaire, from the medieval Latin commissarius, one entrusted with a commission.

People's Commissars

After the October Revolution of 1917, the heads of the ministries of the Soviet government were styled People's Commissars (Narodny Komissar), and the government itself was the Council of People's Commissars, chaired by Vladimir Lenin. The deliberately anti-bourgeois title was retained until 1946, when the commissariats were renamed ministries and their heads ministers.

Military commissars

In the Red Army, military commissars were political officers attached to units to ensure the loyalty of (often ex-tsarist) commanders and to conduct political education among the troops. Instituted in 1918, the system of dual command was abolished and restored several times — notably reimposed in July 1941 after the German invasion — before being finally replaced in October 1942 by full unitary command, with commissars converted into deputy commanders for political affairs (the origin of the zampolit). Similar political officer systems existed in other communist armies and, in a different form, in bodies such as the Chinese People's Liberation Army.

Other uses

"Commissar" is also used in English translations for police ranks in some countries (analogous to superintendent or commissioner), and the German Kommissar is a detective rank familiar from television crime series.
