Reflection seismology

Reflection seismology (also called seismic reflection) is a method of exploration geophysics that uses the principles of seismology to estimate the properties of the Earth's subsurface from reflected seismic waves. The method requires a controlled seismic source of energy, such as dynamite, a vibrator truck, or an air gun array, and sensitive receivers — geophones on land or hydrophones at sea — that record the returning energy.

Principle

When a seismic wave encounters a boundary between materials with different acoustic impedances (the product of density and wave velocity), part of the energy is reflected back toward the surface and part is transmitted deeper. By measuring the two-way travel time of reflections and the wave speed through the layers, geophysicists reconstruct the depth and geometry of the boundaries. Repeated measurements along survey lines are stacked into two-dimensional sections, or combined into three-dimensional volumes (3D seismic) that image the subsurface in detail.

Acquisition and processing

Marine surveys tow long streamer cables of hydrophones behind a vessel firing air guns; land surveys use grids of geophones and vibroseis trucks or shot holes. Raw recordings pass through a processing sequence including deconvolution, normal moveout correction, stacking, and migration, which collapses diffractions and places reflectors in their true positions. Interpretation then maps horizons, faults, and structures, and analyses amplitudes for indications of rock and fluid type.

Applications

Reflection seismology is the principal tool of the hydrocarbon industry for locating oil and gas traps, and it underpins most offshore exploration. It is also used in site investigation, groundwater and geothermal studies, carbon-storage monitoring, and academic research into crustal structure. Techniques such as AVO (amplitude versus offset) analysis and 4D (time-lapse) seismic extend the method to estimating fluid content and tracking changes in producing reservoirs over time.

History

The method grew out of experiments in the 1920s, notably by J. Clarence Karcher and colleagues in Oklahoma, and by the German company Seismos. Commercial surveys began in the 1930s and expanded enormously after the Second World War. The arrival of digital recording, common-midpoint stacking, and, in the 1980s, routine 3D surveys transformed seismic reflection into a high-resolution imaging science.
