Vermeil

Vermeil (pronounced /vərˈmeɪ/; French: [vɛʁˈmɛj]) is a style of gilding, specifically gold plated or gilt sterling silver. It has been used for jewellery, tableware, church plate and decorative objects since antiquity. In the United States, to be legally described as vermeil, an item must have a base of sterling silver (92.5% pure silver) coated or plated with gold of at least 10 karats and a thickness of at least 2.5 micrometres.

History

Gilt silver was prized in ancient and medieval metalwork and reached a high point in 18th- and 19th-century France, where Parisian goldsmiths produced celebrated vermeil services for royalty and nobility. Fire gilding, the traditional method using gold amalgam heated to drive off mercury, was dangerous to workers and was largely replaced by electroplating in the 19th century.

Uses

Vermeil is used for fine flatware, serving pieces, chalices, medals and jewellery. Because the gold layer is much thicker than in ordinary gold plating, vermeil is more durable, though the coating can still wear with use. Many Olympic gold medals are in fact made primarily of silver with a gold coating.
