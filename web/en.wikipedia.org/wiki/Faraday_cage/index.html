Faraday cage

A Faraday cage or Faraday shield is an enclosure formed by conductive material used to block electric fields. It is named after the scientist Michael Faraday, who described the effect in 1836. A Faraday cage operates because an external electric field causes the electric charges within the cage's conducting material to redistribute so that they cancel the field's effect inside the enclosure.

Principle

When an external electric field is applied to a closed conductor, free charges move within the material until the field inside the conductor is zero. Any excess charge resides entirely on the outer surface. Consequently the interior of a hollow conductor is shielded from external static and slowly varying electric fields. For electromagnetic waves, the cage is effective when the apertures in the conductor are much smaller than the wavelength of the radiation and the material is thick enough relative to the skin depth; a fine metal mesh therefore works in place of a solid sheet for most radio frequencies.

History

In 1836 Faraday confirmed the effect by lining a room with metal foil, applying high-voltage discharges to the outside, and showing with an electroscope that no charge existed inside. He had built on an observation by Benjamin Franklin in 1755, who lowered a cork ball on a silk thread into an electrified silver can and found the ball was not attracted to the inside walls.

Applications

Faraday cages protect sensitive electronic equipment from lightning and electrostatic discharge; the metal body of an aircraft or automobile acts as a Faraday cage protecting occupants from lightning strikes. MRI scanner rooms are shielded cages that keep external radio noise out. Microwave oven doors incorporate a perforated screen that blocks microwaves while letting light through. Shielded cables and "Faraday bags" used in digital forensics to isolate phones from networks apply the same principle, as do the linings proposed for buildings requiring electromagnetic security.
