Greenschist

Greenschists are metamorphic rocks that formed under the lowest temperatures and pressures usually produced by regional metamorphism, typically 300–450 °C (570–840 °F) and 2–10 kilobars. They take their name from their schistose (foliated) texture and the abundance of green minerals — chlorite, actinolite, and epidote — that give them their colour.

Composition and formation

Greenschists most commonly form from mafic igneous protoliths such as basalt and gabbro, whose olivine and pyroxene are converted during metamorphism to assemblages of chlorite, actinolite, albite, and epidote. Related rocks in the same grade include chlorite schist, talc schist, and serpentinite, depending on the parent rock.

Greenschist facies

The rock type lends its name to the greenschist facies, one of the principal metamorphic facies used to classify regional metamorphic terrains. Rocks metamorphosed at greenschist grade lie between the zeolite and prehnite–pumpellyite facies at lower grade and the amphibolite facies at higher grade. Vast Archaean "greenstone belts" — such as those of the Canadian Shield, the Pilbara and Yilgarn cratons of Australia, and the Barberton belt of South Africa — consist largely of greenschist-facies volcanic rocks and are major hosts of gold and base-metal deposits.

Use

Greenschist and related green schists have historically been used as building and roofing stone, and prehistoric peoples carved soft chlorite-rich varieties into vessels and ornaments.
