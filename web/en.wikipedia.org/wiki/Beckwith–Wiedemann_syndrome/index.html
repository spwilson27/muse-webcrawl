Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome

Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome (BWS) is a congenital overgrowth disorder, usually present at birth, characterised by an increased risk of childhood cancer and a group of congenital features. It is named after J. Bruce Beckwith and Hans-Rudolf Wiedemann, who described the condition independently in the 1960s. BWS affects roughly 1 in 10,000–14,000 newborns and is associated more often with assisted reproductive technologies than the general population.

Features

Characteristic features include large birth weight and length (macrosomia), an enlarged tongue (macroglossia), abdominal wall defects such as omphalocele, overgrowth of one side or parts of the body (hemihyperplasia), enlarged organs (visceromegaly), ear creases or pits, and neonatal low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia). No single feature is required for diagnosis, and scoring systems are used clinically.

Genetics

Most cases arise from epigenetic or genetic alterations affecting imprinted genes in two domains on chromosome 11p15.5, involving genes such as IGF2, H19, CDKN1C and KCNQ1OT1. Mechanisms include loss or gain of methylation at imprinting centres, paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 11, and, in a minority, inherited CDKN1C mutations. Most cases are sporadic.

Cancer risk and management

Children with BWS have an elevated risk (roughly 5–10%) of embryonal tumours, especially Wilms tumour and hepatoblastoma, mostly before the age of eight. Surveillance typically involves regular abdominal ultrasound and measurement of serum alpha-fetoprotein in early childhood. Management also addresses hypoglycaemia, feeding and airway problems related to macroglossia, and limb-length discrepancy from hemihyperplasia. Most affected individuals have normal intelligence and a normal lifespan.
