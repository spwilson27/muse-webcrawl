Family planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Family planning is the consideration of the number of children a person wishes to have, including the choice to have no children, and the age at which they wish to have them. It involves the use of contraception, fertility awareness, and other techniques to implement such plans, as well as treatment for involuntary infertility. Other aspects include sex education, prevention and management of sexually transmitted infections, and preconception care.

Family planning services are defined by the World Health Organization as educational, comprehensive medical, and social activities which enable individuals to determine freely the number and spacing of their children and to select the means by which this may be achieved.

Health and economic effects

Spacing pregnancies by at least two years is associated with lower risks of preterm birth and maternal complications. Access to contraception reduces unintended pregnancies and maternal mortality, and is linked to higher female educational attainment and earnings. The United Nations estimates that hundreds of millions of women worldwide have an unmet need for modern contraception.

Methods

Modern methods include long-acting reversible contraception (hormonal and copper intrauterine devices and implants), hormonal methods (combined and progestin-only pills, patches, rings, and injections), barrier methods such as condoms, fertility-awareness methods, sterilisation, and emergency contraception. Effectiveness varies widely between perfect and typical use.

History and policy

Organised family planning movements date to the early 20th century, with figures such as Margaret Sanger in the United States and Marie Stopes in Britain opening clinics amid legal controversy. The advent of the combined oral contraceptive pill in 1960 transformed practice. Government programmes have varied widely: some countries subsidise services broadly, while China's one-child policy (1979–2015) imposed coercive limits; international conferences, notably the 1994 Cairo International Conference on Population and Development, reframed family planning within reproductive rights and health.
