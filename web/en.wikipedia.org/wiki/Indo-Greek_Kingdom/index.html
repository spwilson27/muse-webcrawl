Indo-Greek Kingdom

The Indo-Greek Kingdom (sometimes called the Graeco-Indian Kingdom) was a Hellenistic kingdom in the northwestern Indian subcontinent, existing from about 200 BCE to about 10 CE. It arose when the Greco-Bactrian king Demetrius I of Bactria expanded south of the Hindu Kush, and over two centuries more than thirty Indo-Greek kings ruled territories spanning parts of modern Afghanistan, Pakistan and northern India. The kingdom was the easternmost outpost of Hellenistic civilisation and a conduit of artistic, religious and scientific exchange between the Mediterranean world and India.

History

After Demetrius's invasion (c. 200–180 BCE), the most celebrated Indo-Greek king was Menander I Soter (c. 165/155–130 BCE), who ruled from Sagala (Sialkot) and is remembered in Buddhist tradition as King Milinda, interlocutor of the monk Nagasena in the Milindapanha. Later rulers such as Apollodotus I, Antialcidas (whose ambassador Heliodorus erected the famous Besnagar pillar), Strato I and Hermaeus ruled progressively fragmented territories under pressure from Indo-Scythians, Indo-Parthians and the Yuezhi. The last Indo-Greek kingdoms, in the Punjab, fell around 10 CE.

Culture and legacy

Indo-Greek coinage — bilingual and bi-scriptual, using Greek and Kharoshthi legends and blending Greek deities with Indian symbols — is a primary source for the kingdom's history. The Indo-Greeks helped transmit Hellenistic astronomy and astrology to India (the Yavanajataka), and the Greco-Buddhist art of Gandhara, with its first anthropomorphic images of the Buddha, grew from this cultural fusion. Greek communities and artistic conventions survived in the region long after the kingdom's fall.
