Twin study

Twin studies are studies conducted on identical or fraternal twins. They aim to reveal the importance of environmental and genetic influences for traits, phenotypes, and disorders. Twin research is considered a key tool in behavioural genetics and in the nature-versus-nurture debate: because monozygotic (identical) twins share nearly 100% of their genes, while dizygotic (fraternal) twins share on average 50% of their segregating genes, a trait that is much more similar in identical than in fraternal twins is inferred to be influenced by heredity.

Methods

The classical twin design compares the concordance rates of monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twin pairs raised together. Under the equal environments assumption — that MZ and DZ pairs experience equally similar environments relevant to the trait — the excess similarity of MZ pairs estimates heritability, often using structural equation models (the ACE model: additive genetics, common environment, unique environment). Related designs include studies of twins reared apart, such as the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart directed by Thomas J. Bouchard from 1979, which reported striking similarities between separated identical twins in IQ, personality, and even habits; adoption studies; and the co-twin control method used in medicine and epidemiology.

Findings

Twin studies have produced heritability estimates for a wide range of human characteristics: roughly 0.5–0.8 for general cognitive ability in adulthood, about 0.4–0.6 for major personality traits, and substantial genetic contributions to risks of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, autism, height, and body mass index. The field's "three laws of behaviour genetics" (Eric Turkheimer, 2000) summarise the typical results: everything is heritable; the effect of shared family environment is usually smaller than the effect of genes; and a large portion of variation is explained by neither genes nor shared environment.

Criticisms

Critics have challenged assumptions such as equal environments (identical twins may be treated more alike, and twins share a prenatal environment), questioned the generalisability of twins to singletons, and argued that heritability statistics are often misread as measures of how "genetic" a trait is in an individual or as proof that intervention is futile. Historical twin research was also entangled with the eugenics movement and with Josef Mengele's experiments, a legacy that continues to shadow the field.
