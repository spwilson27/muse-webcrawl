Dance notation

Purpose: Recording and analysis of human movement
Major systems: Labanotation, Benesh Movement Notation, Beauchamp–Feuillet notation
First systems: 15th–18th centuries

Dance notation is the symbolic representation of human dance movement and form, using graphic symbols and figures, path mapping, numeral systems and letter and word symbols. It serves the same role for choreography that musical notation serves for music: preserving works, enabling restaging and analysis, and protecting choreographers' copyright.

Major systems

The most widely used system is Labanotation, published by Rudolf Laban in 1928, which records direction, level, duration and body part on a vertical three-line staff. Benesh Movement Notation, devised by Rudolf and Joan Benesh in the 1950s, plots the dancer's positions on a five-line stave viewed from behind and is used extensively by ballet companies. Earlier court dances were recorded in Beauchamp–Feuillet notation, published in 1701.

Uses

Notated scores allow ballets and modern works to be reconstructed decades after their premiere, sometimes by specialist notators and répétiteurs. Computer tools and motion-capture technology have supplemented traditional hand notation, but trained notators remain in demand at major companies and archives such as the Dance Notation Bureau in New York.
