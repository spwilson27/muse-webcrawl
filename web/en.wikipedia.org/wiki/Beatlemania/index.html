Beatlemania was the intense fan frenzy directed toward the English rock band the Beatles during the 1960s. The phenomenon was characterized by screaming fans — predominantly young women — at concerts and during public appearances, record-breaking sales, and unprecedented media attention. The term emerged in the British press in the autumn of 1963 and is often cited as an early landmark of modern celebrity and fandom culture.

Origins

The Beatles — John Lennon, Paul McCartney, George Harrison, and Ringo Starr — built a devoted following in Liverpool and Hamburg before signing with EMI's Parlophone label in 1962 under producer George Martin. Their singles "Love Me Do", "Please Please Me", and "She Loves You" climbed the British charts through 1963. Scenes of screaming, fainting fans at concerts that autumn prompted newspapers to coin "Beatlemania". The band's appearance on Sunday Night at the London Palladium in October 1963 and a royal variety performance the following month brought national attention.

The British Invasion

Beatlemania crossed the Atlantic in early 1964. "I Want to Hold Your Hand" reached number one in the United States, and on 7 February 1964 the band arrived in New York to thousands of screaming fans. Their performance on The Ed Sullivan Show two days later drew an estimated 73 million viewers. The Beatles' success opened the American market to other British acts in what became known as the British Invasion.

Touring years

Concerts during 1964–1966 were frequently inaudible over audience screaming; the band played through low-powered amplification to crowds in venues such as New York's Shea Stadium, where they performed before some 55,600 fans in August 1965 — then the largest concert audience ever recorded. Exhausted by touring, security problems, and controversies (including Lennon's "more popular than Jesus" remark in 1966), the Beatles gave their final commercial concert at Candlestick Park, San Francisco, on 29 August 1966, and thereafter worked only in the studio.

Legacy

Historians and sociologists regard Beatlemania as a turning point in popular culture: it demonstrated the purchasing power of teenagers, reshaped the music industry's marketing, and anticipated later fan phenomena. The term has since been applied generically to intense celebrity fandoms.
