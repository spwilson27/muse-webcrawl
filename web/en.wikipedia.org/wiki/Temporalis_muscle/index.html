Temporalis muscle

The temporalis muscle, also known as the temporal muscle, is one of the muscles of mastication (chewing). It is a broad, fan-shaped convergent muscle on each side of the head that fills the temporal fossa, superior to the zygomatic arch, so that it covers much of the temporal bone.

Temporalis muscle
Origin: Temporal lines on the parietal bone of the skull and the superior temporal surface of the sphenoid bone
Insertion: Coronoid process of the mandible
Artery: Deep temporal arteries
Nerve: Deep temporal nerves (from the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve)
Actions: Elevation and retraction of mandible

Structure

The temporalis arises from the temporal fossa and the deep surface of the temporal fascia. Its fibres converge as they descend, passing deep to the zygomatic arch to form a tendon that inserts onto the coronoid process and the anterior border of the ramus of the mandible. It is covered superficially by the temporal fascia, the auriculotemporal and superficial temporal vessels, and part of the temporoparietal fascia.

Function

The temporalis elevates the mandible, closing the jaw. Its posterior, horizontally oriented fibres retract the mandible, drawing the chin backwards. It is among the most powerful muscles of the head, and its action can be felt on the temple when clenching the teeth.

Clinical significance

Overuse or tension of the temporalis is associated with jaw pain and headaches in temporomandibular joint disorders, and the temple is a common site for tension-type headache pain. A biopsy of the temporal artery, taken through the region, is used in the diagnosis of giant cell arteritis, though the biopsied vessel is the artery rather than the muscle itself.
