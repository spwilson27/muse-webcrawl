Penny (English coin)

English penny
Value: 1/240 pound sterling (before 1971); 1/12 shilling
Composition: Silver (early), later copper and bronze
Years of minting: c. 785 – 1970 (pre-decimal)

Silver penny of Offa, king of Mercia

The English penny was a denomination of sterling coinage for over a thousand years, from its introduction around 785 until decimalisation in 1971. For much of its history it was the principal — and at times the only — coin in circulation in England, struck in silver, and the foundation of the pounds, shillings and pence (£sd) system in which 240 pennies made one pound.

History

The penny was introduced by Offa of Mercia, modelled on the Carolingian denier, as a broad, thin silver coin of about 1.3–1.5 grams. The system of 12 pennies to the shilling and 20 shillings to the pound, inherited from Charlemagne's monetary reforms, endured in Britain for nearly 1,200 years. Under the Anglo-Saxon kings the penny was struck at dozens of mints across the kingdom, its types changing frequently to sustain royal revenue from recoinages.

Through the medieval period the silver penny was progressively debased and reduced in weight. From 1797, large copper "cartwheel" pennies were struck, and from 1860 the penny was minted in bronze. The pre-decimal penny, with Britannia on its reverse, circulated until Decimal Day, 15 February 1971, after which it was replaced by the decimal new penny worth 2.4 old pennies.

Legacy

The penny's abbreviation "d" derives from the Latin denarius. It survives in the decimal penny (1p) and in countless idioms — "a penny for your thoughts", "spend a penny", "the penny drops".
