Paris in the 1920s

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

During the 1920s, Paris — known in French as the années folles, the "crazy years" — was the world's capital of art, literature, music and ideas. After the trauma of the First World War, the city experienced a cultural explosion fuelled by a favourable exchange rate that drew expatriate writers and artists, particularly from the United States, and by the ferment of movements including Surrealism, Dada and modernist jazz culture.

The expatriate community

American writers of the "Lost Generation" — the term popularised by Gertrude Stein — gathered in Paris: Ernest Hemingway, F. Scott Fitzgerald, Ezra Pound, T. S. Eliot (visiting), and others associated with Sylvia Beach's bookshop Shakespeare and Company, which published James Joyce's Ulysses in 1922. The cafés of Montparnasse — Le Dôme, La Rotonde, Le Select and La Coupole — served as salons and studios for artists such as Picasso, Modigliani, Chagall, Man Ray and Kiki de Montparnasse.

Music and nightlife

Jazz arrived with African-American musicians after the war and flourished in clubs in Montmartre and Montparnasse. Josephine Baker became a sensation at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in the Revue Nègre of 1925. The Ballets Russes continued under Diaghilev until his death in 1929, and composers of Les Six, along with Stravinsky and Ravel, defined musical modernism. Cabarets, dance halls and the music hall at the Folies Bergère and Moulin Rouge drew large crowds.

Art and ideas

André Breton published the first Surrealist Manifesto in 1924; Surrealism reshaped poetry, painting and film. The 1925 International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts gave Art Deco its name. Fashion changed dramatically under designers such as Coco Chanel, and architecture saw early modernist works by Le Corbusier, including the Villa Savoye begun at the decade's end.

Society and the end of the era

The decade saw the 1924 Summer Olympics in Paris, expansion of the Métro, and growth of mass media, cinema and radio. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the economic depression that reached France in the early 1930s ended the années folles.
