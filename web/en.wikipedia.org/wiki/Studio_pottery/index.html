Studio pottery

Studio pottery is pottery made by professional and amateur artists or artisans working alone or in small groups, making unique items or short production runs. Typically, all stages of manufacture are carried out by the same individual, from preparing the clay to forming, glazing, and firing. Studio pottery includes functional wares such as tableware and cookware, as well as non-functional, purely artistic forms.

History

The modern studio pottery movement emerged in the early 20th century as a reaction against industrial mass production, drawing on the ideals of the Arts and Crafts movement. In Britain, Bernard Leach (1887–1979), who had studied pottery in Japan, founded the Leach Pottery at St Ives, Cornwall, in 1920 and published A Potter's Book (1940), which became the movement's foundational text. Leach promoted a synthesis of Eastern and Western traditions, valuing simplicity, utility, and the maker's individual touch.

Important figures include the Japanese potters Shōji Hamada and the philosopher-craftsman Sōetsu Yanagi, founder of the mingei (folk craft) movement; Lucie Rie and Hans Coper, who brought a modernist sculptural sensibility; and in the United States, potters such as Peter Voulkos, who pushed clay toward abstract expressionist sculpture in the 1950s.

Characteristics

Studio pottery typically uses stoneware or porcelain fired at high temperatures, with glazes such as ash, celadon, tenmoku, and shino drawn from East Asian traditions. The movement emphasized the wheel-thrown form, visible tool marks, and honest materials, standing in contrast both to factory ceramics and to ornate decorative porcelain.
