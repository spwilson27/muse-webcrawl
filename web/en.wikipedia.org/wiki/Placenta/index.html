Placenta

The placenta is a temporary organ that develops in the uterus during pregnancy in most mammals, connecting the developing fetus to the uterine wall. It supplies the fetus with oxygen and nutrients, removes waste products, and produces hormones that sustain the pregnancy. The placenta is attached to the fetus by the umbilical cord and is expelled after birth as the "afterbirth".

Development and structure

The placenta begins to form when the blastocyst implants in the uterine wall, around the end of the first week after fertilisation. It derives from both fetal tissue (the chorion) and maternal tissue (the decidua). Chorionic villi — finger-like projections containing fetal capillaries — are bathed in maternal blood in the intervillous space, allowing exchange by diffusion. In the mature human placenta, the fetal side is smooth and bears the umbilical cord (normally with two arteries and one vein), while the maternal side is divided into lobes called cotyledons.

Functions

  • Respiration and nutrition: oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and vitamins pass from mother to fetus; carbon dioxide and wastes pass the other way. Maternal and fetal blood normally do not mix.
  • Endocrine: the placenta secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone, oestrogens and human placental lactogen, maintaining pregnancy and adapting maternal metabolism.
  • Immune: maternal IgG antibodies cross to the fetus, conferring passive immunity; the placenta also shields the fetus from the maternal immune system.
  • Barrier: it blocks many (not all) pathogens and substances; alcohol, nicotine and many drugs can cross.

Clinical aspects

Disorders include placenta praevia (placenta covering the cervix), placental abruption (premature separation), placenta accreta (abnormally deep attachment) and pre-eclampsia, which is associated with defective placental development. After delivery the placenta is examined for completeness, since retained fragments can cause haemorrhage or infection. In many cultures the placenta has ritual significance; eating it (placentophagy) is practised by most mammals but has no established benefit in humans.
