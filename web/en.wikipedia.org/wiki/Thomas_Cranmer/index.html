Thomas Cranmer (2 July 1489 – 21 March 1556) was a leader of the English Reformation and Archbishop of Canterbury during the reigns of Henry VIII, Edward VI and, briefly, Mary I. He annulled Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon, enabling the break with Rome; under Edward VI he compiled the first two editions of the Book of Common Prayer and reshaped the doctrine and worship of the Church of England along Protestant lines. Under the Catholic Mary I he was tried for treason and heresy and burned at the stake in Oxford, thrusting into the flames the hand that had signed his recantations.

Rise

Educated at Jesus College, Cambridge, Cranmer was a fellow until his marriage forced him to resign; after his first wife's death he was reinstated and ordained. He came to the king's attention in 1529 by suggesting that Henry's case for annulling his marriage be put to the universities of Europe rather than to the Pope. Employed on embassies — during one, to Germany in 1532, he secretly married Margarete, niece of the Lutheran reformer Osiander — he was unexpectedly chosen Archbishop of Canterbury in 1533. That May he declared Henry's marriage to Catherine void and validated the marriage to Anne Boleyn; when Anne fell, Cranmer annulled that marriage too. He supported the royal supremacy yet pushed reform cautiously under Henry, surviving the fall of his patron Thomas Cromwell and the conservative reaction of the Six Articles.

The Edwardian Reformation

Under the boy king Edward VI, with the protection of the regents Somerset and Northumberland, Cranmer moved decisively: the first Book of Common Prayer (1549) imposed a uniform English liturgy; the more thoroughly Protestant second book (1552) recast the communion as a memorial meal; the Forty-two Articles (1553) defined reformed doctrine, and his Defence of the True and Catholic Doctrine of the Sacrament (1550) attacked transubstantiation. His liturgical prose — much of it adapted from Latin sources — set the cadence of English worship and influenced the English language itself.

Downfall and death

Cranmer supported the attempt to place Lady Jane Grey on the throne in July 1553 and was attainted for treason when Mary I succeeded. After a show trial for heresy at Oxford, broken by imprisonment and the sight of his colleagues Latimer and Ridley burned, he signed a series of recantations of his Protestant beliefs. Yet at his execution on 21 March 1556, called on to reaffirm them publicly, he dramatically renounced the recantations instead — "my hand shall first be punished" — and held his right hand steadily in the fire as he died. His martyrdom, immortalised in Foxe's Book of Martyrs, made him the symbolic father of Anglicanism; the Pilgrimage of Grace had demanded his removal twenty years before.
