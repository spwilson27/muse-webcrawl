Christian Norberg-Schulz

Christian Norberg-Schulz (23 May 1926 – 28 March 2000) was a Norwegian architect, architectural historian and theorist, one of the most influential architectural thinkers of the later 20th century. Drawing on the phenomenology of Martin Heidegger and on Gestalt psychology, he developed a theory of architecture centred on place and the genius loci, the "spirit of place".

Life

Norberg-Schulz was born in Oslo and studied architecture at the ETH Zürich, graduating in 1949; among his teachers was Sigfried Giedion. He later studied in Rome and at Harvard, and took his doctorate in 1964. He practised architecture in Norway, designing houses including his own, and in 1966 became professor at the Oslo School of Architecture, where he taught until 1992 and served as a leading figure in Scandinavian architectural education. He edited the journal Byggekunst and was a member of CIAM circles in his early career.

Thought

His first major book, Intentions in Architecture (1963), applied Gestalt psychology and semiotics to architectural form. Existence, Space and Architecture (1971) introduced an existential concept of space based on human orientation and identification. In Genius Loci: Towards a Phenomenology of Architecture (1979) — his best-known work — he argued, following Heidegger's essay "Building Dwelling Thinking", that architecture's task is to concretise the genius loci, the distinctive character and atmosphere of a place, so that people can dwell meaningfully in the world. The trilogy concluded with The Concept of Dwelling (1985).

Norberg-Schulz also wrote major historical surveys, including Baroque Architecture (1971), Late Baroque and Rococo Architecture, Meaning in Western Architecture (1975) and Modern Norwegian Architecture. His phenomenology strongly influenced postmodern and regionalist architecture, and the notion of genius loci passed into general design discourse, though critics have charged his approach with nostalgia and essentialism.
