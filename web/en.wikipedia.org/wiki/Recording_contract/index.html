Recording contract

A recording contract (commonly called a record deal) is a legal agreement between a record label and a recording artist (or group) under which the artist makes recordings for the label to sell and promote, and the label pays the artist royalties from those sales. Record deals structure the economics of the music industry: labels typically fund recording, manufacturing, marketing, and distribution, recouping those costs from the artist's royalties before further royalties are paid.

A record deal binds an artist to deliver recordings in exchange for funding, distribution, and royalties.

Typical terms

Traditional contracts grant the label ownership of the master recordings, often in perpetuity, and exclusive rights to the artist's recording services for a term measured in albums plus option periods. Key provisions include the royalty rate (a percentage of revenue, historically calculated on the wholesale or retail price with numerous deductions), advances (prepayments recoupable against royalties), recording budgets, creative control clauses, and the "360 deal" variant — increasingly common since the 2000s — in which the label also takes a share of touring, merchandise, and endorsement income in exchange for broader investment.

Recoupment and criticism

Because advances and many production and marketing costs are recoupable from the artist's royalty share, many signed artists never "recoup" and receive no royalties beyond their advances, a long-standing source of disputes and criticism of major-label contracts. High-profile conflicts — from Prince's battle with Warner Bros. to Taylor Swift's re-recording of her early albums after her masters were sold — have kept contract terms in public debate. Some jurisdictions limit contract length; California's Labour Code §2855 (the "seven-year rule") famously underpinned Olivia Newton-John's and later artists' cases.

Modern alternatives

Streaming-era alternatives include licensing deals (the artist retains the masters and licenses them for a term), distribution deals, label-services arrangements, and fully independent self-release through digital aggregators, each trading lower advances for greater ownership and higher revenue shares.
