Genomic imprinting
MechanismDNA methylation at imprinting control regions
Genes involvedOver 100 known in humans (e.g. IGF2, H19)
DisordersPrader–Willi syndrome, Angelman syndrome, Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome

Genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon that causes genes to be expressed or not, depending on whether they are inherited from the female or male parent. Genes whose expression depends on parent of origin are said to be "imprinted". Imprinting occurs in mammals, some insects, and flowering plants.

Mechanism

Imprints are established in the germline by DNA methylation at imprinting control regions (ICRs), which differ between the egg and sperm. These methylation marks are erased and reset in each generation according to the sex of the individual, and are maintained through development, so that for an imprinted gene only one parental allele is active. The classic example is the insulin-like growth factor 2 gene (IGF2), expressed only from the paternal allele, alongside the maternally expressed non-coding RNA H19.

Disorders

Errors of imprinting on chromosome 15 cause two very different syndromes depending on the parent of origin: loss of paternal gene expression produces Prader–Willi syndrome, while loss of the maternal UBE3A gene produces Angelman syndrome. Imprinting errors also cause Beckwith–Wiedemann and Silver–Russell syndromes, and can arise after assisted reproduction. David Haig's kinship (conflict) theory explains imprinting as an evolutionary tug-of-war between paternal genes promoting growth and maternal genes restraining it.
