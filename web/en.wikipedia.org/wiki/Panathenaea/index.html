The Panathenaea (Ancient Greek: Παναθήναια) was the most important festival of ancient Athens, held every year in honour of Athena Polias, the city's patron goddess, and celebrated on a grander scale every four years as the Great Panathenaea. Tradition attributed its founding to Erichthonius or to Theseus, and the Athenian statesman Peisistratus is credited with reorganising and enlarging it in the 6th century BC.

The festival

The annual festival culminated on 28 Hekatombaion, regarded as Athena's birthday, with a great procession (the pompē) that wound through the city, up the Panathenaic Way to the Acropolis. Its sacred purpose was the presentation of a new robe (peplos), woven by Athenian women and girls, to the ancient wooden cult statue of Athena Polias. A hecatomb of cattle was sacrificed, and the meat distributed to the people.

Games and contests

The Great Panathenaea included athletic competitions modelled on the Olympic Games — foot races, wrestling, boxing, the pankration, pentathlon, and equestrian events — as well as musical and rhapsodic contests, including recitations of Homer. Victors received Panathenaic prize amphorae, distinctive black-figure (later red-figure style) jars filled with olive oil from the sacred olive trees of Athena; these amphorae continued to be produced in the black-figure technique long after it had fallen out of general use. Tribal contests unique to Athens included the euandria (a contest of manly excellence), boat races at Piraeus, and the torch race.

Legacy

The Panathenaic procession is widely believed to be the subject of the Parthenon frieze. The modern Panathenaic Stadium in Athens, built on the site of the ancient stadium used for the games, hosted the first modern Olympic Games in 1896.
