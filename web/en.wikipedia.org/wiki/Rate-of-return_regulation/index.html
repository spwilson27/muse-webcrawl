Rate-of-return regulation

Rate-of-return regulation
FieldRegulatory economics
Applies toPublic utilities and other natural monopolies
Revenue formulaR = O + (V − D) · r
Known weaknessAverch–Johnson effect (over-capitalisation)

Rate-of-return regulation is a method of regulating the prices charged by a monopoly — classically a public utility — by limiting the profit it may earn to a "fair" rate of return on its invested capital. A regulator, such as a public utility commission, sets overall revenues so that the firm recovers its operating costs plus a return on its rate base.

How it works

The firm's allowed revenue requirement is R = O + (V − D) · r, where O is operating expense, V is the value of capital assets, D is accumulated depreciation (so V − D is the rate base), and r is the allowed rate of return, set to approximate the firm's cost of capital. Prices are then set so total revenue equals R; changes go through formal rate cases.

Criticism and the Averch–Johnson effect

If the allowed return exceeds the true cost of capital, the firm profits by expanding its rate base, giving it an incentive to over-capitalise (the Averch–Johnson, or "gold-plating," effect). The system also offers weak incentives for cost efficiency: cost savings may simply be clawed back at the next rate case, though regulatory lag partially restores incentives.

Alternatives

Modern alternatives include price-cap regulation (RPI − X) and other incentive-based, or performance-based, ratemaking schemes widely adopted in Britain and elsewhere since the 1980s.
