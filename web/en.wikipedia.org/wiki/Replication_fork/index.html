Replication fork

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The replication fork: the parental DNA duplex is unwound and two new strands are synthesised, one continuously and one in fragments.

The replication fork is the Y-shaped structure formed when replicating DNA separates into two strands, which then serve as templates for new DNA synthesis. During DNA replication, the fork is created by enzymes known as helicases, which unwind the double helix at an origin of replication. The fork moves along the parental DNA as replication proceeds, and the ensemble of proteins working at it is collectively called the replisome.

Structure and operation

Because DNA strands are antiparallel and DNA polymerases synthesise only in the 5′ → 3′ direction, the two strands at the fork are made differently. The leading strand is synthesised continuously, in the same direction as fork movement, from a single primer. The lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously in short segments — Okazaki fragments — each requiring its own RNA primer laid down by primase; the primers are later removed and the fragments joined by DNA ligase. Single-strand binding proteins coat the unwound templates to prevent them from re-annealing or being damaged, and topoisomerases relieve the torsional stress that accumulates ahead of the fork.

Proteins at the fork

Key components include the helicase (in eukaryotes, the CMG complex built around MCM helicase), DNA polymerases — in eukaryotes principally Pol ε on the leading strand and Pol δ on the lagging strand, loaded by the sliding clamp PCNA — the clamp loader, primase–Pol α complex, RPA (the eukaryotic single-strand binding protein), and accessory factors that coordinate the two polymerases so that both strands are copied at roughly equal rates despite their different modes of synthesis.

Fork stalling and restart

Forks stall when they encounter DNA damage, tightly bound proteins, transcription machinery, or depleted nucleotide pools. Persistent stalling can produce fork collapse and double-strand breaks, a source of genome instability implicated in cancer and ageing. Cells stabilise stalled forks (for example via BRCA1/BRCA2 and the Fanconi anaemia pathway), protect newly synthesised DNA from nucleases, and restart replication by repriming, template switching or firing dormant origins. Tumour cells' dependence on these stress responses is exploited by some chemotherapies and by ATR/CHK1 checkpoint inhibitors.

Replicon and fork speed

In bacteria, a single origin fires two forks that race around the circular chromosome at roughly 1,000 bases per second, meeting in the terminus region. Eukaryotic chromosomes use many origins; each fork advances only about 50 bases per second, and replicons range from tens to hundreds of kilobases. Visualisation of forks by electron microscopy and DNA fibre analysis underlies much of the experimental evidence for fork structure.
