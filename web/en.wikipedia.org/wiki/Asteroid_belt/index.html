Asteroid belt

[Image: an artist's impression of the asteroid belt — countless rocky bodies orbiting between Mars and Jupiter, the Sun glowing at left]

The asteroid belt is a torus-shaped region in the Solar System, roughly between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, occupied by many irregularly shaped bodies called asteroids or minor planets. It contains millions of objects, but their total mass is less than that of the Moon; the largest member, Ceres, is about 940 km across and is classified as a dwarf planet.

The belt formed from the primordial solar nebula 4.6 billion years ago; Jupiter's gravity prevented the material from accreting into a planet and stirs the belt to this day.

Discovery and exploration

The first asteroid, Ceres, was discovered by Giuseppe Piazzi on 1 January 1801, followed rapidly by Pallas, Juno and Vesta. What was first thought to be a single planet proved to be a vast population of small bodies.

Spacecraft that have visited belt asteroids include NASA's Dawn, which orbited Vesta (2011–12) and Ceres (2015–2018), revealing Vesta's differentiated interior and Ceres' briny, ice-rich crust. Japan's and NASA's sample missions to near-Earth asteroids (Hayabusa2 to Ryugu, OSIRIS-REx to Bennu) studied related populations.

Composition and families

Asteroids are classified chiefly as C-type (carbonaceous, the majority), S-type (silicaceous) and M-type (metallic). Collisions group asteroids into families with similar orbits and compositions; gaps in the belt (Kirkwood gaps) occur at orbital resonances with Jupiter.

Despite popular depictions, the belt is mostly empty space — typical separations between asteroids are millions of kilometres, and spacecraft pass through safely.
