Passive smoking

Passive smoking is the inhalation of tobacco smoke by persons other than the intended "active" smoker. It occurs when tobacco smoke enters an environment, causing its inhalation by people within that environment. Exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke (also called environmental tobacco smoke, or secondhand smoke, SHS) causes disease, disability, and death in non-smokers.

Health effects

The scientific consensus, summarized in reports by the U.S. Surgeon General, the World Health Organization, and other bodies, is that passive smoking increases the risk of lung cancer in non-smokers by roughly 20–30 percent for those living with a smoker, and also increases the risk of coronary heart disease and stroke. In children, exposure is associated with sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), asthma exacerbations, respiratory infections, and ear infections.

Secondhand smoke contains more than 7,000 chemicals, including at least 70 known to cause cancer. Sidestream smoke — emitted from the burning end of a cigarette — contains higher concentrations of some toxicants than mainstream smoke exhaled by the smoker.

Policy responses

Beginning in the late 20th century, evidence about passive smoking drove smoke-free laws covering workplaces, restaurants, bars, and public transport in many countries. Ireland became the first country to implement a nationwide workplace smoking ban in 2004. Studies following such bans found measurable reductions in hospital admissions for heart attacks and improved respiratory health among hospitality workers.

Third-hand smoke

Researchers have also described "third-hand smoke" — residual nicotine and other chemicals left on indoor surfaces that can react to form additional pollutants — though its health significance is still being studied.
