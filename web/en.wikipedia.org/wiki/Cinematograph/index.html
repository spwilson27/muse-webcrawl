Cinematograph

A cinematograph (from the Greek kinema, movement, + graphein, to write/record) is a motion-picture apparatus that records and reproduces moving images. The term is most famously associated with the Cinématographe patented by the French brothers Auguste and Louis Lumière in 1895, a single lightweight device that functioned as camera, printer and projector.

History

Several inventors developed moving-picture machines in the early 1890s: Thomas Edison's Kinetoscope (1893) showed films to one viewer at a time, while Léon Bouly had used the name "cinématographe" for his device of 1892. The Lumière machine, built with engineer Jules Carpentier, ran 35 mm film with round perforations at 16 frames per second and could be hand-cranked anywhere, freeing filming from the studio.

First public screenings

The Lumières gave a first demonstration in March 1895 and, on 28 December 1895, the first commercial public screening at the Salon Indien of the Grand Café in Paris — a date often taken as the birth of cinema. Their programme included Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory and The Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat. Lumière operators (cinematographers) were soon sent worldwide to shoot and exhibit films, creating the first global catalogue of actuality films.

Legacy

The word survives in "cinema" and "cinematography", and in the titles of institutions such as the Cinémathèque française. Rival machines — Edison's Vitascope, the Bioscop of the Skladanowsky brothers, Robert W. Paul's Theatrograph — competed in the same years, but the Cinématographe's combination of portability and projection made it the defining instrument of cinema's first decade.
