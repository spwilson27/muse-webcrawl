Adaptive expectations

Under adaptive expectations, forecasts adjust gradually to past errors.

In economics, adaptive expectations is a hypothesis about how people form expectations about the future: they base them on what has happened in the past, adjusting their previous expectation by a fraction of the error they made. For inflation, for example, expected inflation next period equals expected inflation this period plus a proportion of the difference between actual and expected inflation.

Formulation

The rule can be written as xet = xet−1 + λ(xt−1 − xet−1), where λ (between 0 and 1) is the speed of adjustment. Equivalently, expectations are a geometrically weighted average of all past values, with weights declining into the past. The hypothesis was formalised in the 1950s, notably by Phillip Cagan in his study of hyperinflation, and by Marc Nerlove for agricultural supply.

Use and criticism

Adaptive expectations underpinned the expectations-augmented Phillips curve used by Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps to argue that there is no long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment: any attempt to hold unemployment below its natural rate produces accelerating inflation as expectations catch up. From the 1970s the approach was largely displaced by the rational expectations hypothesis, which holds that agents use all available information and do not make the systematic, repeated errors that adaptive expectations imply when policy changes.
