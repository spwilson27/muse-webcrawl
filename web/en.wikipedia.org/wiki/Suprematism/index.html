Suprematism

Suprematism (Russian: супремати́зм) is an early twentieth-century art movement, founded by the Russian artist Kazimir Malevich in 1915, focused on fundamental geometric forms — particularly the square and the circle — painted in a limited range of colours. It was one of the earliest and most radical developments in abstract art, rejecting the depiction of the visible world in favour of what Malevich called the "supremacy of pure artistic feeling."

Origins

Malevich developed Suprematism while working on designs for the Futurist opera Victory over the Sun (1913). He publicly launched the movement at the Last Futurist Exhibition of Paintings 0.10 in Petrograd in December 1915, where his Black Square was hung high across the corner of the room — the position traditionally reserved in Russian homes for religious icons. His manifesto From Cubism to Suprematism accompanied the exhibition.

Development

Early Suprematist works used coloured rectangles and other shapes floating on white grounds; Malevich then moved through a "white phase" culminating in Suprematist Composition: White on White (1918). After the Russian Revolution, Suprematist ideas spread through the UNOVIS group at Vitebsk, where El Lissitzky, who carried the style toward Constructivism and to the Bauhaus via his "Proun" works, was a colleague. Malevich also extended the system into three-dimensional models (arkhitektona) and applied decoration. Suppressed in the Soviet Union as Socialist Realism became official doctrine in the 1930s, Suprematism nonetheless profoundly influenced abstract art, design, and architecture worldwide.
