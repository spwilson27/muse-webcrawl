Mass–energy equivalence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The equation E = mc squared displayed in large type, as on the Taipei 101 commemoration]
E = mc², the most famous equation in physics.

Mass–energy equivalence is the relationship between mass and energy in a system's rest frame, where the two quantities differ only by a constant factor and the units of measurement: E = mc², where E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light in vacuum. Proposed by Albert Einstein in 1905 (in the short paper 'Does the inertia of a body depend upon its energy content?'), it is the most famous equation of special relativity — and arguably of all physics.

Because c² is enormous, a small mass corresponds to a vast energy: 1 kg ↔ 9 × 10¹⁶ joules (roughly 21 megatons of TNT). The equivalence explains the energy of nuclear reactions — fission and fusion convert a fraction of mass into energy, measured as the mass defect of nuclei — the annihilation of matter and antimatter, and why particle accelerators can create matter from energy. Conversely, energy itself has mass: a hot object, a charged battery and a compressed spring are all (minusculy) heavier than their cold, discharged, relaxed selves. Einstein stressed that mass is a measure of energy content, unifying two previously separate conservation laws into one.

Form and extensions

The full relativistic relation is E² = (pc)² + (mc²)², which also covers massless particles (photons, E = pc) and defines relativistic mass-energy bookkeeping in particle physics, where masses are quoted in GeV/c². The equivalence is distinct from claims of 'converting' mass to energy: both are the same conserved quantity viewed in different frames.
