The phrase "the best of all possible worlds" (French: le meilleur des mondes possibles) expresses the central doctrine of the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, set out in his Essais de Théodicée (1710): that the actual world, despite the evil and suffering it contains, is the best world God could have created.

Leibniz's argument

Leibniz held that an omniscient, omnipotent and wholly good God surveys infinitely many possible worlds and necessarily chooses the best among them. Evil is therefore not willed for its own sake but is an unavoidable concomitant of a world optimised overall — a defence of God's goodness known as a theodicy. The doctrine also relies on Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason: nothing exists or happens without a reason why it is so rather than otherwise.

Criticism and satire

The Lisbon earthquake of 1755, which killed tens of thousands on All Saints' Day, made Leibnizian optimism a target of Enlightenment criticism. Voltaire first attacked it in his Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne and then immortalised the satire in his novella Candide (1759), whose character Dr Pangloss — the "professor of metaphysico-theologico-cosmolo-nigology" — repeats that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds" in the face of mounting calamities. The phrase has since become a byword for naïve or complacent optimism, though scholars note that Leibniz's own position was more subtle than the caricature.
