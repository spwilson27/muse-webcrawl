Territorial waters

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Territorial waters, or a territorial sea, is an area of coastal water extending up to 12 nautical miles (22.2 km; 13.8 mi) from the baseline (usually the mean low-water mark) of a coastal state. The territorial sea is regarded as the sovereign territory of the state, although foreign ships are allowed the right of innocent passage through it, and this sovereignty also extends to the airspace above and the seabed below.

Legal basis

The modern regime is set by the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982). Article 3 provides that every state may establish a territorial sea up to a limit not exceeding 12 nautical miles, measured from baselines determined in accordance with the Convention. The coastal state's sovereignty (UNCLOS Article 2) is exercised subject to the Convention, including the right of innocent passage for ships of all states (Articles 17–26): passage that is continuous, expeditious and not prejudicial to the peace, good order or security of the coastal state. Submarines must navigate on the surface and show their flag.

History

The extent of territorial waters was long contested between maritime powers favouring narrow seas and coastal states claiming more. The early modern "cannon-shot rule", associated with Cornelius van Bynkershoek, set the limit at roughly 3 nautical miles — the range of shore artillery. Claims of 4, 6 and 12 miles persisted through the 20th century; the 1930 Hague conference and the 1958 and 1960 Geneva conferences failed to fix a universal breadth, and the 12-mile standard was finally settled at the Third UN Conference on the Law of the Sea (1973–1982).

Beyond the territorial sea

Beyond the territorial sea lie the contiguous zone (up to 24 nautical miles), in which the state may enforce customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws, and the exclusive economic zone (up to 200 nautical miles), in which the coastal state has sovereign rights over natural resources but not full sovereignty. Straits used for international navigation are governed by the more permissive transit-passage regime.

Disputes

Disputes over territorial waters arise where states claim historic bays or excessive straight baselines, where islands generate contested entitlements (as in the South China Sea), or where a state purports to require prior authorisation for warship passage. The United States, which has not ratified UNCLOS but accepts most of it as customary law, conducts freedom of navigation operations (FONOPs) challenging excessive maritime claims.
