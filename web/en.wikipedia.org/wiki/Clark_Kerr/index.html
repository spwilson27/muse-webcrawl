Clark Kerr

Clark Kerr (May 17, 1911 – December 1, 2003) was an American economist and academic administrator best known as the first chancellor of the University of California, Berkeley (1952–1958) and as the twelfth president of the University of California system (1958–1967). He was the principal architect of the California Master Plan for Higher Education of 1960 and coined the term "multiversity" in his influential 1963 book The Uses of the University. Time magazine featured him on its cover in 1960 as higher education's leading statesman.

Early life and career

Kerr was born in Stony Creek, Pennsylvania, and raised in a Quaker-influenced farming family. He studied at Swarthmore College, Stanford, and the University of California, Berkeley, where he earned his PhD in economics in 1939. During the Second World War he served as a labour mediator for the National War Labor Board and gained a reputation as a skilled arbitrator in industrial disputes. Joining the Berkeley faculty, he founded its Institute of Industrial Relations and, after the loyalty-oath controversy of 1949–51, became Berkeley's first chancellor in 1952.

University of California president

As UC president, Kerr oversaw enormous growth driven by the baby boom and California's population surge: enrolment climbed past 100,000, and new campuses were founded at San Diego, Irvine, and Santa Cruz, while Davis, Riverside, and Santa Barbara became general campuses. The 1960 Master Plan, negotiated with Governor Edmund "Pat" Brown, divided California public higher education into three segments — the University of California, the state colleges (now the California State University), and the community colleges — guaranteed a place for every qualified student, preserved tuition-free education for residents, and became a model studied worldwide.

Kerr's presidency ended amid the political storms of the 1960s. The Free Speech Movement at Berkeley in 1964 placed him between students, faculty, and politicians; in January 1967, shortly after Ronald Reagan became governor on a promise to "clean up the mess at Berkeley", the UC Regents dismissed Kerr — "fired with enthusiasm", Kerr quipped, echoing an earlier remark about leaving as he had arrived, "fired with enthusiasm".

Later life and legacy

After 1967 Kerr chaired the Carnegie Commission on Higher Education, whose reports shaped national policy debates, and served as its successor bodies' leader into the 1970s. He wrote extensively on industrial relations and the university, and in his nineties published a two-volume memoir, The Gold and the Blue (2001–2003). The Clark Kerr Campus of UC Berkeley is named in his honour. He died in El Cerrito, California, in 2003, aged 92.
