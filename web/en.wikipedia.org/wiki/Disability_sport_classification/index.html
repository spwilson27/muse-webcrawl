Disability sport classification is a system for grouping athletes with disabilities into categories ("sport classes") according to how much their impairment affects the activities fundamental to a particular sport. Its purpose is to minimise the impact of impairment on the outcome of competition, so that success is determined by skill, fitness, power and tactics rather than by the severity of disability.

History

Early disability sport, beginning with the Stoke Mandeville Games organised by Ludwig Guttmann in 1948, classified athletes by medical diagnosis — for example, by the level of a spinal cord lesion. From the 1980s and 1990s onward, the Paralympic movement shifted toward functional, sport-specific systems in which classification is based on the activity limitation an impairment causes in that sport, an approach formalised in the International Paralympic Committee's Athlete Classification Code (2007, revised 2015).

Process

Athletes are evaluated by trained classifiers — usually a panel including medical and technical classifiers — through physical and technical assessments and observation in competition. Each athlete receives a sport class and a class status (such as Confirmed or Review) indicating when re-evaluation is needed. Classification is mandatory and intentional misrepresentation of ability is a disciplinary offence.

Systems by sport

Each sport sets its own classes. Swimming uses S classes for freestyle, backstroke and butterfly and SB classes for breaststroke; athletics uses T classes for track and F classes for field events, with numbers indicating impairment type and severity; wheelchair rugby and basketball use point systems in which players carry individual point values and team totals on court are capped.

Criticism

Classification has been criticised for complexity, inconsistency between sports, and high-profile controversies — most notably the Sydney 2000 intellectual-disability basketball scandal, which led to the removal of intellectual impairment events until London 2012. Research continues into evidence-based classification.
