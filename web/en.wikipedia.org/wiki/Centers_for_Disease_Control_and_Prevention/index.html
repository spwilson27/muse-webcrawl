Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is the national public health agency of the United States. It is a federal agency under the Department of Health and Human Services, headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia.

History

Founded in 1946 as the Communicable Disease Center, growing out of wartime malaria control work in the southern United States, the agency expanded from infectious diseases into chronic disease, occupational health, injury prevention, environmental health and health statistics. Its name changed several times, becoming the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 1992 while retaining the initials CDC.

Mission and work

The CDC conducts disease surveillance, laboratory science, outbreak investigation — notably through the Epidemic Intelligence Service — and issues public health guidance. It played central roles in the eradication of smallpox, responses to HIV/AIDS, Ebola, and the COVID-19 pandemic, and publishes the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report.

Organization

Centers, institutes and offices cover infectious diseases, immunization and respiratory diseases, global health, and the National Center for Health Statistics. Most staff are based in Atlanta, with quarantine stations and offices across the US and abroad.
