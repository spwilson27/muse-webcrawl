A muezzin (Arabic: مؤذن, muʾadhdhin) is the person appointed at a mosque to recite the adhan, the Islamic call to prayer, five times a day, and the iqama, the second call that announces the prayer is about to begin. The muezzin is traditionally chosen for the quality and power of his voice and for his character; the office is one of the most recognisable features of daily life in Muslim communities, and the sound of the call from the minaret has shaped the soundscape of cities from Istanbul to Jakarta for over a millennium.

History

The institution dates to the first Muslim community in Medina after the Hijrah of 622 CE. According to hadith, after debate over how to summon the faithful — by bell, horn, or fire — a companion's dream fixed the human voice as the means, and Bilal ibn Rabah, a freed Abyssinian slave known for his beautiful, powerful voice, was appointed the first muezzin, calling the prayer from a rooftop. The minaret developed centuries later, and with it the muezzin's elevated station; in Ottoman mosques the muezzin climbed the minaret's stairs for each call, while large imperial mosques employed teams of muezzins who answered one another antiphonally from different minarets.

Duties and qualifications

Beyond the five daily calls (Fajr, Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, and Isha), the muezzin may announce the iqama inside the prayer hall, lead certain supplications, call special prayers such as funeral and eclipse prayers, and in some traditions summon the congregation before Friday prayer with an additional adhan. Jurists discuss qualifications including being Muslim, male in most traditions, of sound mind, trustworthy, punctual, and knowledgeable about prayer times — historically requiring familiarity with astronomy and shadow lengths. A muezzin should also know the rules for the modified dawn call, in which the phrase as-salatu khayrun min an-nawm ("prayer is better than sleep") is added at Fajr.

Modern practice

Since the mid-20th century the call has commonly been delivered through loudspeakers and, in many cities, centralised or recorded systems relay a single call to many mosques. Turkey's Diyanet and other religious authorities employ muezzins as salaried mosque officials, often jointly with the role of kayyim (caretaker). The adhan remains a matter of public debate in some non-Muslim-majority countries, where amplified calls may be regulated under noise ordinances, while in Muslim-majority cities it remains a defining feature of the urban day. Notable muezzins — including famous reciters at the Great Mosque of Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina — have become celebrated voices, and recordings of master muezzins are sold and streamed worldwide.

Etymology

The word derives from the Arabic verb adhdhana, "to announce or proclaim," from the root ʾ-dh-n associated with the ear and hearing. Turkish renders it müezzin; in South Asia the office is also known by names such as muazzin or, colloquially, bang-giver (from Persian bāng, a cry).
