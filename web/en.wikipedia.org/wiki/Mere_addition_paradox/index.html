Mere addition paradox

The mere addition paradox, also known as the repugnant conclusion, is a problem in ethics, identified by Derek Parfit and discussed in his book Reasons and Persons (1984). It concerns how to evaluate populations of different sizes and qualities of life, and it poses a central challenge to total utilitarianism and population ethics generally.

The paradox

Consider population A, a large group of people with very high quality of life. Compare A+ , which consists of the same people living slightly less well, plus an additional group of people whose lives are worth living but are much worse than those in A, and whose presence slightly lowers the average. Intuitively, A+ seems worse than A in one respect but merely adds lives worth living. Next compare A+ with B, an even larger population in which everyone is equal but at a quality of life only slightly above the A+ average. By a sequence of such steps, each apparently involving no worsening overall—or only the addition of lives worth living—one arrives at a vast population B in which every life is barely worth living.

Parfit states the resulting repugnant conclusion: "For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better even though its members have lives that are barely worth living."

Responses

Philosophers have responded by proposing average utilitarianism (which has its own counterintuitive implications), critical-level views that discount lives below a threshold, person-affecting restrictions, or by accepting the conclusion as merely counterintuitive rather than truly repugnant. Arrhenius's impossibility theorems show that no population axiology can simultaneously satisfy all of a set of seemingly plausible adequacy conditions, suggesting some intuitively troubling implication is unavoidable.
