François Mitterrand

François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (1916–1996) was a French statesman who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest-serving president of the Fifth Republic and its first from the Socialist Party. His two terms brought abolition of the death penalty, sweeping social reforms, European integration through the Maastricht Treaty, and grand Paris building projects.

François Mitterrand
Born: 26 October 1916, Jarnac, France
Died: 8 January 1996, Paris
President of France: 1981–1995
Party: Socialist Party

Early career

A prisoner of war who escaped German captivity, Mitterrand joined the Resistance and served as a minister in numerous Fourth Republic governments, including Interior and Justice. He narrowly lost the 1974 presidential election, became Socialist Party first secretary in 1971, and built the Union of the Left with the Communists.

Presidency

Elected in 1981, his government nationalised industries, raised the minimum wage, cut the work week, abolished capital punishment under Robert Badinter, and decentralised power; economic troubles forced a 1983 turn to austerity (“tournant de la rigueur”). He endured two “cohabitations” with right-wing governments, championed the Franco-German partnership and the euro, and commissioned grands projets including the Louvre Pyramid. Ill with cancer he concealed, he died months after leaving office.
