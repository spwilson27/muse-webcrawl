Wind power in Canada

Wind power in Canada has grown rapidly since the 1990s. By the early 2020s Canada had more than 16,000 megawatts of installed wind capacity, supplying roughly 6% of the country's electricity, concentrated in Ontario, Quebec and Alberta.

Wind power has a long history in Canada in terms of its initial development, with the growth of modern utility-scale wind farms concentrated in the provinces of Ontario, Quebec and Alberta. By the early 2020s, Canada had well over 16 gigawatts of installed wind-generating capacity — among the top ten national totals worldwide — supplying roughly 6 per cent of Canada's electricity demand.

History

Small windmills were common on prairie farms in the early 20th century. Modern development began with experimental turbines in the 1970s–1980s (such as the Éole Darrieus turbine in Quebec, once the largest vertical-axis turbine in the world) and the first wind farms in Alberta (Cowley Ridge, 1993) and Quebec (Le Nordais, 1998). Growth accelerated in the 2000s under provincial renewable-energy programs and federal production incentives.

Capacity by province

Ontario built the largest fleet under its Green Energy Act feed-in tariff program, passing 5,000 MW before the program was cancelled in 2018. Quebec — where Hydro-Québec mandated local content — exceeded 4,000 MW, centred on the Gaspé Peninsula. Alberta, with excellent prairie wind resources, became a leading market after 2019 under corporate power-purchase agreements. Wind farms also operate in every other province, including the large Wolfe Island and Prince Wind projects in Ontario and facilities in Prince Edward Island, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Manitoba, Saskatchewan and British Columbia.

Resources and outlook

Canada's best wind resources lie in the prairies, along the coasts, and around the Great Lakes and the St. Lawrence. Wind is the lowest-cost source of new electricity generation in much of the country, and further growth — together with battery storage, transmission interconnections and emerging offshore wind in Atlantic Canada — is expected as provinces phase out coal and pursue net-zero electricity grids.
