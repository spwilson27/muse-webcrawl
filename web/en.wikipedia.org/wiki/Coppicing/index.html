Coppicing

Coppicing is a traditional method of woodland management in which young tree stems are repeatedly cut down near ground level. In subsequent growth years, many new shoots emerge from the base, the stool, which after a number of years are harvested again, and the cycle begins anew. Typically, a coppiced woodland is harvested in sections, or coups, on a rotation. In this way, a crop is available each year somewhere in the woodland. A coppiced stool can live for many centuries.

Practice

Species commonly coppiced include hazel, sweet chestnut, ash, oak, alder, willow and hornbeam. Rotations vary with the product desired: hazel may be cut every 6–8 years for hurdles and thatching spars; sweet chestnut on 15–20 year rotations yields fence posts and poles. Products historically included firewood, charcoal, fencing, tool handles and material for wattle and daub.

Ecology

Coppicing creates a mosaic of light conditions that benefits woodland wildlife: the flush of light after cutting encourages ground flora such as bluebells and primroses, as well as butterflies and dormice. Many ancient coppice woodlands are now Sites of Special Scientific Interest in the United Kingdom. Neglected coppice grows out into high forest as the stools' stems mature.
