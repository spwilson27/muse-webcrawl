Micrometeorology

Micrometeorology is the branch of meteorology concerned with atmospheric phenomena on the smallest scales of space and time — typically less than about one kilometre horizontally and less than a day in duration — especially within the atmospheric boundary layer, the lowest part of the atmosphere directly influenced by the Earth's surface.

Subject matter

Micrometeorologists study turbulence and the turbulent fluxes of heat, moisture, momentum and gases between the surface and the air, the microclimates of fields, forests, cities and slopes, frost pockets and valley winds, and the dispersion of pollutants near their sources. The surface layer — the lowest tens of metres — follows similarity relationships described by Monin–Obukhov similarity theory, which relates turbulent fluxes to mean gradients of wind, temperature and humidity.

Methods

Field measurements rely on fast-response instruments such as sonic anemometers, infrared gas analysers and net radiometers mounted on towers. The eddy covariance technique, which computes fluxes directly from correlated fluctuations of vertical wind and the quantity of interest, underlies global flux networks such as FLUXNET, used to quantify carbon dioxide and water exchange of ecosystems. Results from micrometeorology feed into agriculture (irrigation and frost protection), air-quality regulation, wind-energy siting and the surface schemes of weather and climate models.
