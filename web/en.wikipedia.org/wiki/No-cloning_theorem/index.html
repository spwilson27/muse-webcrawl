No-cloning theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram showing a quantum state entering a copying machine with a cross through the second output]
Unknown quantum states cannot be copied.

In physics, the no-cloning theorem states that it is impossible to create an independent and identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state, a result with profound implications for Quantum computing and quantum information. It was proved in 1982 by William Wootters and Wojciech Zurek and independently by Dennis Dieks (in response to Nick Herbert's proposed superluminal communicator, whose flaw the theorem exposed); a version appears in a 1970 note by James Park.

The proof is one line of linearity: a unitary copier that clones two distinct states cannot clone their superposition. Consequences radiate outward: Quantum teleportation must destroy the original; quantum information cannot be backed up (hence the difficulty of quantum error correction, which must spread information without copying it); quantum money and Quantum key distribution are secure because an eavesdropper cannot copy the transmission; and the theorem harmonises with relativity — cloning would permit faster-than-light signalling via Quantum entanglement. Classical information can be cloned because classical states are effectively orthogonal; quantum states in general are not.

Approximate cloning

Imperfect cloning is possible and bounded (optimal universal cloning fidelity 5/6 for qubits — Bužek and Hillery, 1996): the limits define how much an eavesdropper can learn, setting the security thresholds of quantum cryptography. Probabilistic exact cloning of restricted state sets also exists, consistent with the theorem's 'arbitrary unknown state' clause.
