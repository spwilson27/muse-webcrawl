Science and technology studies (STS), also called science, technology and society, is an interdisciplinary field examining how science and technology are shaped by, and in turn shape, society, politics, and culture. Rather than taking scientific knowledge as a simple mirror of nature, STS studies how facts and artefacts are produced, stabilised, and contested within social settings.

Origins

The field grew from the sociology of science pioneered by Robert K. Merton, the history and philosophy crystallised by Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962), and the Edinburgh "strong programme" in the sociology of scientific knowledge, which insisted that true and false beliefs be explained symmetrically by social causes.

Major approaches

Laboratory studies of the 1970s and 1980s, such as Latour and Woolgar's Laboratory Life, used ethnographic methods to watch facts being constructed at the bench. Actor–network theory extended the analysis to networks of human and non-human actors. The social construction of technology (SCOT) programme showed how artefacts bear the imprint of relevant social groups, while feminist and postcolonial STS examined whose knowledge counts and with what consequences.

Applications

STS scholarship informs science policy, technology assessment, bioethics, and debates over expertise and public participation in decisions about climate change, medicine, and emerging technologies.
