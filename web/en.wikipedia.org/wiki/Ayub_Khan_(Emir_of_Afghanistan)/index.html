Mohammad Ayub Khan (1857 – 7 April 1914) was an Afghan prince of the Barakzai dynasty, son of Emir Sher Ali Khan, who briefly claimed the emirate during the Second Anglo-Afghan War. He is best remembered as the victor of the Battle of Maiwand on 27 July 1880, one of the worst defeats suffered by a British-Indian army in the 19th century, and is honoured in Afghanistan as a national hero — the "Victor of Maiwand".

Second Anglo-Afghan War

During the war Ayub Khan governed Herat. After his brother Yaqub Khan's deposition by the British and the murder of the British resident Cavagnari in Kabul, Ayub marched east from Herat in 1880. At Maiwand, near Kandahar, his forces overwhelmed a British-Indian brigade under General Burrows, inflicting severe casualties; the stand of the 66th Regiment and the defence of the baggage inspired the Kipling poem and the legend of the dog "Bobbie". The Afghan victory was immortalised in Afghan memory by the heroine Malalai, said to have rallied the troops with a verse and carried the standard.

Defeat and exile

Ayub's triumph was short-lived. After besieging Kandahar, he was decisively defeated on 1 September 1880 by General Frederick Roberts, fresh from his famous march from Kabul. Ayub fled to Herat and then to Persia, losing his claim as Abdur Rahman Khan consolidated power with British support. In 1888 he surrendered to the British in India and lived in pensioned exile, dying in Lahore in 1914. He must not be confused with the later Pakistani president Ayub Khan (1907–1974).
