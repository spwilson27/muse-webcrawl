Oxyrhynchus

Oxyrhynchus (Greek: Ὀξύρρυγχος, "sharp-nosed") is an ancient Egyptian city on the west bank of the Bahr Yussef, a branch of the Nile, about 160 km south-south-west of Cairo in modern Minya Governorate. Known in ancient Egyptian as Per-medjed, it was the capital of the 19th Upper Egyptian nome. The city achieved its modern fame not from standing monuments but from its rubbish dumps, where the dry Egyptian climate preserved an enormous cache of papyri — the Oxyrhynchus Papyri — which since 1897 have yielded lost works of Greek literature, early Christian texts and an unparalleled record of everyday life in Roman Egypt.

History

Per-medjed was sacred to the oxyrhynchus fish, a species of elephantfish worshipped locally and, according to Plutarch, blamed for eating a part of the body of Osiris; the Greeks named the city after it. Under the Ptolemies and Romans Oxyrhynchus grew into a prosperous provincial capital of perhaps 25,000–30,000 inhabitants, with temples, theatres, baths, a gymnasium and colonnaded streets, extensively documented by its own papyri. In the Byzantine period it became a significant Christian centre with many churches and monasteries, before declining after the Arab conquest of the 7th century. The modern town of el-Bahnasa stands nearby.

The papyri

Excavations by Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt for the Egypt Exploration Society began in 1897 and almost immediately struck enormous quantities of papyrus discarded in ancient rubbish mounds outside the town. Over the following seasons an estimated several hundred thousand fragments were recovered; the collection, now mainly in Oxford, numbers in the hundreds of thousands of items, of which only a few thousand have been published in the long-running series The Oxyrhynchus Papyri (begun 1898). Publication continues to this day, aided by multispectral imaging and by the Ancient Lives crowdsourcing project, which enlisted the public to transcribe fragments.

Contents of the finds

The literary finds recovered lost works or better texts of Pindar, Sappho (including, with later finds, substantially complete new poems), Sophocles (including the satyr play Ichneutae), Euripides, Menander, Callimachus and the historian Hellenica Oxyrhynchia, as well as the "Constitution of the Athenians" tradition of scholarship. Christian finds include early fragments of the Gospels, the sayings-collection known as the Gospel of Thomas, and the Shepherd of Hermas. The vast majority, however, are documentary: census returns, tax receipts, leases, letters, marriage contracts, petitions and shopping lists that have made Roman and Byzantine Egypt the best-documented society of the ancient world at the level of ordinary life.
