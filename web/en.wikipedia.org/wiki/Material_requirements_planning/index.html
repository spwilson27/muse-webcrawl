Material requirements planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Material requirements planning (MRP) is a production planning, scheduling and inventory control method used to manage manufacturing processes. An MRP system computes what materials and components are needed, in what quantities, and by when, in order to execute a master production schedule, and generates purchase and work orders timed so that materials arrive when needed.

Inputs and process

MRP depends on three main inputs:

  • The master production schedule (MPS) — what finished products are to be made, in what quantities and by what dates.
  • The bill of materials (BOM) — the structured list of components, subassemblies and raw materials that go into each product.
  • Inventory records — stock on hand, stock on order, lead times, lot sizes and safety stock for every item.

The system "explodes" demand for finished goods level by level through the bill of materials, nets gross requirements against available inventory and scheduled receipts, and offsets planned order releases by each item's lead time, producing planned orders for purchasing and production. Its underlying logic distinguishes dependent demand (components, whose demand derives from demand for their parents) from independent demand for finished products — the key insight behind the method, developed in the 1960s and popularised by Joseph Orlicky and others as computerised systems spread through manufacturing in the 1970s.

Benefits and limitations

MRP can reduce inventory investment, improve delivery performance and coordinate purchasing with production. Its output quality, however, depends entirely on the accuracy of bills of materials and inventory records, and classic MRP assumes fixed lead times and effectively infinite capacity; nervousness — large swings in planned orders from small schedule changes — is a known weakness.

Successors

MRP was extended into manufacturing resource planning (MRP II), which added capacity planning, shop-floor control and financial integration, and later into enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems covering the whole business. MRP logic remains embedded in virtually all modern ERP software.
