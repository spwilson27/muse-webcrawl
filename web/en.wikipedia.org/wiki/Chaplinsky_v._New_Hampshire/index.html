Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire

Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), is a decision of the United States Supreme Court that upheld a state law prohibiting abusive and insulting language directed at a person in public. The case created the "fighting words" doctrine, holding that certain categories of speech — including insults that by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace — fall outside the protection of the First Amendment.

Background

Walter Chaplinsky, a Jehovah's Witness, was distributing religious literature on a street in Rochester, New Hampshire, when a crowd gathered and a disturbance developed. When a city marshal warned him about the crowd's reaction, Chaplinsky called the marshal "a God damned racketeer" and "a damned Fascist." He was convicted under a state statute forbidding offensive or derisive words addressed to another person in a public place.

Decision

Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Frank Murphy affirmed the conviction. He listed categories of speech "of such slight social value as a step to truth that any benefit that may be derived from them is clearly outweighed by the social interest in order and morality," including the lewd and obscene, the profane, the libellous, and "insulting or 'fighting' words — those which by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace."

Legacy

Later decisions, including Cohen v. California (1971) and R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul (1992), narrowed the doctrine: the Court has not upheld a fighting-words conviction since Chaplinsky itself, and the doctrine now applies only to face-to-face insults likely to provoke an immediate violent response, applied without viewpoint discrimination.
