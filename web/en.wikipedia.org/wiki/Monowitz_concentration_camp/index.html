Monowitz concentration camp

LocationMonowice, German-occupied Poland
OperatedOctober 1942 – January 1945
Operated bySS, for IG Farben
PrisonersOver 30,000 registered; approx. 10,000+ died

Monowitz (also known as Monowitz-Buna or Auschwitz III) was a Nazi concentration camp and labor camp (Arbeitslager) run by the SS in German-occupied Poland from 1942 to 1945, built to supply slave labour to the Buna Werke, the synthetic rubber and fuel plant constructed by the German chemical conglomerate IG Farben near the village of Monowice (German: Monowitz), east of the main Auschwitz camp.

History

IG Farben chose the site in 1941 because of its proximity to coal, water, and the Auschwitz camp's supply of prisoners. The first prisoners arrived in October 1942. Monowitz was the largest of the Auschwitz subcamps and the first camp built with funding by a private company in cooperation with the SS. In November 1943 it became administratively separate as Auschwitz III, with the other subcamps placed under its authority.

Conditions were brutal. Prisoners worked on the construction of the Buna plant under a regime summarised by the expectation of working inmates to death within a few months; those too weak to work were selected and sent to the gas chambers at Birkenau. Of more than 30,000 prisoners registered at Monowitz, at least 10,000 are estimated to have died. The Buna plant itself never produced synthetic rubber before the war's end.

Prisoners and testimony

The best-known prisoner of Monowitz was the Italian writer Primo Levi, whose memoir If This Is a Man (also published as Survival in Auschwitz) describes life in the camp. Elie Wiesel was also held there with his father. The camp was evacuated in January 1945 ahead of the advancing Red Army, which liberated the area on 27 January 1945.

After the war

Executives of IG Farben were tried at Nuremberg in the IG Farben Trial (1947–48) partly for their role in exploiting camp labour. The site of Monowitz has no major memorial comparable to Auschwitz I or Birkenau; a memorial plaque stands in Monowice.
