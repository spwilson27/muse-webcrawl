The 1955 Constitution of Ethiopia, formally the Revised Constitution of the Empire of Ethiopia, was promulgated by Emperor Haile Selassie I on 4 November 1955, the silver jubilee of his coronation. It replaced the 1931 Constitution — Ethiopia's first written constitution — and remained the country's fundamental law until the monarchy was overthrown in 1974.

Background

After the restoration of the Empire following the Italian occupation (1936–41) and the federation of Eritrea with Ethiopia in 1952, Haile Selassie sought a modernised constitution that would present Ethiopia as a progressive state while preserving imperial authority. Foreign advisers, including American jurists, assisted Eritrean and Ethiopian officials in the drafting.

Provisions

The constitution declared the Emperor's person sacred and his power indisputable, tracing the imperial line to Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. It established a bicameral parliament with an elected Chamber of Deputies and an appointed Senate, universal adult suffrage (first exercised in the 1957 general election), and a catalogue of civil rights including freedom of speech, press, and assembly — though these were qualified by law and limited in practice. Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity remained the established religion, while freedom of worship was guaranteed to others.

End of the constitution

The document's democratic forms coexisted with an essentially autocratic system, and dissatisfaction with corruption, land inequality, and slow reform fed the unrest that produced the Ethiopian Revolution of 1974. The Derg suspended the constitution after deposing Haile Selassie in September 1974, formally abolishing the monarchy the following year.
