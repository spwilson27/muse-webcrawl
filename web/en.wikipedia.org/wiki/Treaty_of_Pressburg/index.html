Treaty of Pressburg

The Treaty of Pressburg was signed on 26 December 1805 between France and Austria at Pressburg (present-day Bratislava, Slovakia). It ended the War of the Third Coalition, following Austria's defeat at the Battle of Ulm campaign and the Franco–Bavarian victory over the combined Austro-Russian army at the Battle of Austerlitz (2 December 1805). A separate treaty of the same name, the Treaty of Pressburg of 1491, had settled an earlier Habsburg–Hungarian succession dispute.

The treaty was negotiated for France by Talleyrand and for Austria principally by Prince Johann I Joseph of Liechtenstein and Count Ignaz von Gyulai, after an armistice agreed on 6 December. For Austria it was a dictated peace: the harshest imposed on the Habsburgs by Napoleon to that date, and a decisive step in the dissolution of the old order in Germany and Italy.

Terms

Austria ceded its Italian possessions — Venetia, Istria and Dalmatia — to Napoleon's Kingdom of Italy, ending centuries of Habsburg presence on the Adriatic's eastern shore. In Germany, Austria gave up its remaining Swabian lands: the Vorlande went chiefly to Württemberg and Baden, while the Tyrol and Vorarlberg passed to Bavaria — a transfer bitterly resented in the Tyrol, where it later fed Andreas Hofer's revolt of 1809. Augsburg and Passau also went to Bavaria. Austria recognised the kings of Bavaria and Württemberg as fully sovereign monarchs, accepted the French-sponsored settlements in Italy (including Joseph Bonaparte's installation in Naples the following spring), withdrew from the Coalition, and paid France a war indemnity of 40 million gold francs.

The territorial losses cost Austria roughly a sixth of its population — around three million subjects — and a large share of its revenue, and removed Habsburg influence from Italy and from south-western Germany.

Consequences

Pressburg prepared the way for the end of the Holy Roman Empire. In July 1806 sixteen German princes, headed by Bavaria, Württemberg and Baden, formed the French-protected Confederation of the Rhine and seceded from the Empire; on 6 August 1806 Emperor Francis II abdicated the imperial crown and dissolved the Empire, ruling thereafter as Francis I of Austria. Prussia's alarm at this revolution in Germany helped provoke the War of the Fourth Coalition in the autumn of 1806.

Austria, humiliated but not destroyed, spent the years after Pressburg reforming its army under Archduke Charles and biding its time; in 1809 it would challenge Napoleon again, alone, in the War of the Fifth Coalition.
