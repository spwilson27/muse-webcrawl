Greco-Roman geography

Greco-Roman geography is the geographic knowledge of the ancient Greek and Roman world, from the earliest Greek accounts of the inhabited world (the oikoumene) through the great synthesis of Claudius Ptolemy in the 2nd century AD. Greek and Roman geographers described the lands around the Mediterranean, Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, and developed foundational concepts of latitude, longitude, climate zones, and map projections.

Early Greek geography

Geographic ideas appear in Homer and Hesiod, whose world was a disc surrounded by Ocean. Anaximander of Miletus (6th century BC) is credited with one of the first world maps. Hecataeus systematized travel accounts, and Herodotus incorporated extensive geographic description into his Histories. Eratosthenes of Cyrene (3rd century BC) coined the word "geography," drew a remarkably accurate estimate of the Earth's circumference, and constructed a world map with a grid of parallels and meridians. Hipparchus criticized and refined Eratosthenes' work and advanced the use of astronomical determination of positions.

Roman geography

Roman writers emphasized practical description of the empire. Strabo's Geographica (early 1st century AD), in 17 books, is the fullest surviving ancient geographical work. Pomponius Mela and Pliny the Elder compiled Latin accounts, and Roman road itineraries and the survey-based maps of Agrippa served administration and war. Tacitus and others extended knowledge of northern Europe.

Ptolemy and legacy

Claudius Ptolemy's Geography (c. 150 AD) listed coordinates for some 8,000 places and described map projections, summarizing earlier work such as that of Marinus of Tyre. Transmitted through Byzantine and Arabic scholarship, Ptolemy's work profoundly shaped medieval and Renaissance cartography after its translation into Latin in the early 15th century, even as its errors—including an underestimated circumference of the Earth—influenced explorers such as Columbus.
