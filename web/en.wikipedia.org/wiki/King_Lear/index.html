King Lear is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare, considered one of the greatest tragedies in English literature. It is based on the mythological Leir of Britain, a legendary king recorded by Geoffrey of Monmouth and others. The play, written between 1605 and 1606, depicts the consequences of the title character's decision to divide his kingdom among his three daughters according to their professions of love for him.

Plot

The ageing King Lear resolves to divide Britain among his daughters Goneril, Regan, and Cordelia, giving the largest share to whoever declares the greatest love. Goneril and Regan flatter him extravagantly; Cordelia, refusing to exaggerate, is disinherited and banished, and the loyal Earl of Kent is exiled for defending her. Lear is soon treated with contempt by Goneril and Regan, who strip him of his retinue. Rejected by both, Lear descends into madness on a storm-swept heath, accompanied by his Fool and the disguised Kent.

In a parallel subplot, the Earl of Gloucester is deceived by his illegitimate son Edmund into condemning his legitimate son Edgar, who flees disguised as the mad beggar "Poor Tom". Gloucester, blinded by Regan's husband Cornwall for aiding Lear, is guided by the disguised Edgar. Cordelia returns with a French army; Lear is restored to sanity in her care but their forces are defeated. Edmund orders their execution; Cordelia is hanged, and Lear dies of grief over her body. Edmund, Edgar, Goneril, and Regan all die as the kingdom passes to survivors Albany and Edgar.

Texts and performance history

The play survives in two substantially different early texts: the 1608 quarto and the 1623 First Folio version, which differ by hundreds of lines; modern editions often conflate them. From 1681, Nahum Tate's adaptation with a happy ending—in which Cordelia survives and marries Edgar—held the stage for over 150 years, until Shakespeare's tragic ending was restored in the 19th century. The role of Lear is considered one of the most demanding in drama.

Themes and criticism

Major themes include justice, authority, kinship, blindness and insight, nature, and nihilism. Samuel Johnson found Cordelia's death so painful he could not reread the ending for years. The play has inspired operas, films (including Akira Kurosawa's Ran, 1985), and countless stage productions, and is routinely ranked among Shakespeare's supreme achievements.
