End-user license agreement (EULA)

Type: Software licence / contract

Parties: Licensor (software vendor) and end user

Common forms: Shrink-wrap, clickwrap, browsewrap

Regulation: Contract law; consumer protection law (varies by jurisdiction)

An end-user license agreement (EULA) is a contract between the licensor of a software application and the purchaser, establishing the purchaser's right to use the software. Rather than selling the software itself, the vendor licenses it, and the EULA defines permitted and prohibited uses — typically restricting copying, redistribution, resale, and reverse engineering — together with disclaimers of warranty and limitations of liability.

Forms of acceptance

Historically, EULAs were presented as shrink-wrap licenses, whose terms were said to take effect when the buyer opened the packaging. With distributed software they evolved into clickwrap agreements, requiring the user to click "I agree" during installation or sign-up, and browsewrap, where terms posted on a website are deemed accepted by use. Courts have generally been more willing to enforce clickwrap than browsewrap terms, provided the user had reasonable notice and an opportunity to review.

Enforceability and criticism

The enforceability of EULAs has been litigated in cases such as ProCD v. Zeidenberg (US, 1996) and Step-Saver v. Wyse, and outcomes vary between jurisdictions; parts of the EU and some other consumer-protection regimes void certain unfair terms. EULAs are frequently criticised because they are long, legalistic, and almost never read — studies repeatedly show users accept them without review — while containing provisions such as binding arbitration clauses, bans on class actions, restrictions on benchmarking or criticism, and consent to extensive data collection. Free and open-source software licences (such as the GPL) are EULA-like instruments that instead grant users rights to use, study, modify, and share the software, subject to conditions.
