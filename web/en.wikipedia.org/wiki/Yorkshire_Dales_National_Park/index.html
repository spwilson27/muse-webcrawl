Yorkshire Dales National Park

Established1954 (extended 2016)
Area2,178 km² (841 sq mi)
LocationNorth Yorkshire, Cumbria and Lancashire, England
Highest pointWhernside, 736 m

The Yorkshire Dales National Park is a national park in northern England covering most of the Yorkshire Dales, a landscape of river valleys (dales) separated by windswept moorland fells in the Pennines. Designated in 1954, the park was extended westward in 2016 into the Orton Fells and Howgill Fells of Cumbria and Lancashire.

Landscape

The park's character derives from Carboniferous limestone and millstone grit geology: flat-topped fells, deep valleys, limestone pavements such as those above Malham Cove, caves and potholes (including the Gaping Gill system), waterfalls such as Aysgarth Falls and Hardraw Force, and valleys including Swaledale, Wensleydale (famous for its cheese), Wharfedale, and Ribblesdale. The highest summits — Whernside, Ingleborough, and Pen-y-ghent — form the Yorkshire Three Peaks. The patchwork of dry stone walls, stone field barns, and hay meadows reflects centuries of sheep and cattle farming.

History and communities

Evidence of occupation runs from Mesolithic hunters through Roman roads and Norse settlement — many place names are Old Norse in origin. The area was shaped by monastic estates (Fountains and Jervaulx abbeys), lead mining in the 18th and 19th centuries, and the arrival of the Settle–Carlisle Railway, whose Ribblehead Viaduct is an icon of the park. Market towns such as Hawes, Sedbergh, Grassington, and Kirkby Stephen serve as gateways.

Conservation and recreation

The park authority manages access, planning, and conservation; the area includes an International Dark Sky Reserve designated in 2020. Popular activities include walking the Pennine Way and Dales Way, climbing, caving, cycling (the park hosted the 2014 Tour de France Grand Départ), and visiting show caves at White Scar and Ingleborough. Roughly 23,000 people live within the park.
