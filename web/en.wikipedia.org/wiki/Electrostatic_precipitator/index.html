Electrostatic precipitator

An electrostatic precipitator (ESP) is a filterless device that removes fine particles, such as dust and smoke, from a flowing gas using the force of an induced electrostatic charge. Minimally impeding the flow of gases through the unit, and with collection efficiencies that can exceed 99 per cent, ESPs are widely used in power plants, cement kilns, steel mills, and other industrial processes to control particulate emissions.

Principle of operation

Flue gas passes between discharge electrodes held at high voltage (tens of kilovolts) and grounded collecting plates or tubes. A corona discharge around the wires ionizes the gas; the ions charge the suspended particles, which are then attracted to the collecting surfaces by the electric field and deposit there. Accumulated dust is periodically dislodged by rapping or washing the collectors and falls into hoppers below. Collection efficiency is described by the Deutsch–Anderson equation, which relates efficiency to collecting area, gas flow, and particle migration velocity.

History

Experimental smoke precipitation dates to the nineteenth century, but the first commercially successful system was developed by Frederick Gardner Cottrell, who installed a precipitator in 1907 to recover sulphuric acid mist and later applied the technology to smelter and cement-plant emissions; his patents funded the Research Corporation. The Cottrell precipitator became standard equipment at coal-fired power stations.

Limitations

Performance depends strongly on particle resistivity: very high-resistivity ash causes "back corona" that reduces efficiency, while low-resistivity particles are easily re-entrained. ESPs also produce small amounts of ozone and are ineffective for gaseous pollutants, which require scrubbers or other treatment.
