Nativism (politics)

Nativism is the political policy and ideology of promoting or protecting the interests of native-born or long-established inhabitants of a country over those of immigrants, typically through restrictions on immigration and opposition to the cultural influence of newcomers. The term derives from nineteenth-century American politics, where "Native American" parties — most famously the Know Nothings of the 1850s — campaigned against Irish Catholic and German immigration. Historians such as John Higham, in his classic Strangers in the Land (1955), defined nativism as intense opposition to an internal minority on the ground of its foreign connections.

United States

Early republic and Know Nothings

Anti-immigrant sentiment appeared as early as the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798. It crystallised in the 1840s–50s amid mass Irish and German immigration: secretive nativist societies coalesced into the American Party, the "Know Nothings", whose members answered inquiries by saying "I know nothing". The party elected mayors and governors and won a substantial congressional bloc in 1854 before collapsing over the slavery question.

Anti-Asian and restrictionist eras

The late nineteenth century saw nativism directed against Chinese immigrants, culminating in the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, the first U.S. law barring a nationality. In the 1890s the American Protective Association targeted Catholics, while the Immigration Restriction League pressed for literacy tests. Fears of southern and eastern European immigration, Jews, and radicals after the First World War produced the Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Immigration Act of 1924, which imposed national-origins quotas that sharply curtailed immigration for four decades. The second Ku Klux Klan of the 1920s combined racism with anti-Catholic and anti-Jewish nativism, and the internment of Japanese Americans in 1942 is often linked to similar currents.

Modern debates

After the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 abolished the quota system, nativist themes re-emerged in debates over illegal immigration from Latin America, English-only legislation, and, in the twenty-first century, in populist movements in the United States and Europe that frame immigration as a threat to jobs, welfare systems, security and national identity.

Nativism elsewhere

Comparable movements have arisen worldwide: anti-Chinese movements in Southeast Asia and white Australia policy in Australia; "sons of the soil" movements in India and parts of Africa that privilege indigenous ethnic groups; and contemporary European far-right parties opposing Muslim immigration. Scholars distinguish nativism from ordinary immigration restriction by its ideological claim that the nation should be inhabited exclusively by members of the native group and that non-native elements pose a fundamental threat.
