A non-binding referendum on Sweden adopting the euro was held on 14 September 2003. Swedish voters rejected the proposal by 55.9 percent to 42.0 percent, and Sweden — which has no formal opt-out but is obliged in principle to join the eurozone once it meets the convergence criteria — has ever since remained outside the single currency by deliberately not meeting the exchange-rate criterion.

Campaign

The "yes" side, backed by the governing Social Democrats under Prime Minister Göran Persson, most of the business community and several major parties, argued that membership would boost trade and influence. The "no" side — including the Left Party, the Greens, the Centre Party and dissidents within the governing party — emphasized democratic control of monetary policy, the welfare state, and the experience of Sweden's independent interest-rate policy. The campaign was overshadowed by the assassination of foreign minister Anna Lindh, a prominent "yes" campaigner, three days before the vote; campaigning was suspended, but the referendum went ahead as scheduled.

Result and aftermath

Only Stockholm and a few regions voted yes. Persson accepted the result and pledged not to revisit the question for the rest of the decade. Sweden consequently kept the krona and its independent central bank, the Riksbank, outside the ERM II mechanism. Opinion polling since has generally shown continued majority opposition to euro adoption, spiking against it during the eurozone debt crisis.
