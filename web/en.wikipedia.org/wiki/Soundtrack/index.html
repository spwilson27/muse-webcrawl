Soundtrack

A soundtrack is recorded sound accompanying and synchronised to the images of a book, drama, motion picture, radio program, television program, or video game. The term originally referred to the physical sound track on a film strip. In common usage it now most often means a commercial album of music as featured in a film or TV production, comprising the film score, source music and licensed songs, and, by extension, any recorded music accompanying a work — such as a video game soundtrack.

Types

Soundtracks are commonly divided into the score — original instrumental (and sometimes vocal) music composed for the production — and albums of pre-existing songs. Musical films and Bollywood productions typically release song albums; some soundtracks, such as those for The Sound of Music (1965), Saturday Night Fever (1977) and The Bodyguard (1992), rank among the best-selling albums of all time. Orchestral scores by composers such as Max Steiner, Bernard Herrmann, Ennio Morricone, John Williams and Hans Zimmer have become central to film culture.

History

The first commercially issued film soundtrack album is generally considered to be MGM's recording for Till the Clouds Roll By (1947). The LP era made soundtrack albums a major industry; Henry Mancini's Peter Gunn (1959) won the first Grammy for Album of the Year. Video game music developed its own soundtrack culture, from chiptune albums to orchestral releases for games such as Final Fantasy.

Terminology

The phrase "original soundtrack" (OST) is widely used, particularly in East Asian media, to denote the official album release of a production's music.
