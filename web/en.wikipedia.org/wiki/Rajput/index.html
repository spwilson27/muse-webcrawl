Rajput

Rajput (from Sanskrit rāja-putra, "son of a king") is a term for a large, multi-component cluster of castes, kin bodies and ruling lineages of northern and western India — above all of the region still called Rajasthan, "the land of kings" — who claim descent from the warrior-ruler (kshatriya) varna of Hindu tradition. From the early medieval centuries the Rajput clans — Chauhans, Rathores, Sisodias, Kachhwahas, Paramaras, Solankis, Chandelas and dozens more — founded the kingdoms that dominated north India between the fall of the Gupta and early medieval empires and the coming of the Delhi Sultanate and the Mughals, with whom they fought, negotiated, intermarried and, from Akbar's time, entered into a famous military-political partnership that made Rajput generals and governors pillars of the Mughal Empire while Mewar's Sisodia maharanas held out in celebrated defiance. Rajput courts produced a distinctive culture of honour — the jauhar and saka of besieged fortresses, bardic epics, miniature painting, and the great hill forts of Chittorgarh, Kumbhalgarh, Jaisalmer, Amber and Mehrangarh — and Rajputana's princely states survived under British paramountcy until their merger into Rajasthan after 1947. Rajputs today number in the tens of millions across Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh and neighbouring regions, and remain prominent in Indian public and military life.

Origins

The word "Rajput" as a collective identity crystallised between roughly the sixth and twelfth centuries CE. Historians describe a process — sometimes called "Rajputisation" — by which successful lineages of varied origin (indigenous warrior clans, Gurjara and other groups entering from the north-west, Brahmin and tribal chiefs raised by land grants) were woven into a single status community claiming kshatriya descent, equipped with genealogies tracing them to the solar (Suryavansha), lunar (Chandravansha) and fire-born (Agnivansha) dynasties of epic tradition — the Agnivansha myth, in which four clans were born from a sacrificial fire-pit on Mount Abu, was elaborated in court epics such as the Prithviraj Raso. By the twelfth century the Rajput kingdoms — the Chauhans of Delhi and Ajmer under Prithviraj III, the Gahadavalas of Kannauj, the Chandelas of Bundelkhand (builders of Khajuraho), the Paramaras of Malwa — were the principal powers of the north.

Sultanate and Mughal eras

The Turkish invasions broke this world: Prithviraj Chauhan's defeat by Muhammad of Ghor at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192) opened north India to the Delhi Sultanate, and Rajput resistance contracted to the fortresses of the west — Ranthambore, Chittor and Jalore fell to Alauddin Khalji in 1301–11 with massacres and, in Chittor's famous case, the jauhar of the royal women celebrated in the legend of Rani Padmini. Recovery came with Rana Hammir and the Sisodias of Mewar and, in the fifteenth century, Rana Kumbha. The Mughal Akbar transformed relations after 1562: matrimonial alliances with the Kachhwahas of Amber and others, high mansab ranks, and service across the empire made the Rajputs partners in Mughal rule; Man Singh of Amber became one of Akbar's greatest generals. Mewar alone stood out: Rana Pratap's refusal and defeat at Haldighati (1576) and guerrilla resistance thereafter made him the pre-eminent hero of Rajput honour, until a settlement with Jahangir in 1615 spared Mewar the humiliation of personal attendance at court. Under Aurangzeb relations soured again (the Rathore rebellion of 1679–1707), and in the eighteenth century Rajputana was squeezed between Mughal decline, Maratha exactions and, finally, British treaties.

Princely states and modern times

In 1817–18 the Rajput states accepted British paramountcy; as the princely states of the Rajputana Agency — Jaipur, Jodhpur, Udaipur (Mewar), Bikaner, Jaisalmer and fourteen others — they kept internal rule while the British took foreign affairs, and Rajput soldiers served the Empire in both world wars (the Jodhpur Lancers' charge at Haifa in 1918 is still honoured). At independence the states acceded to India and were merged, in stages between 1948 and 1956, into the state of Rajasthan; privy purses and titles were abolished in 1971. Rajput identity remains politically potent in the region — caste associations, electoral blocs, recurrent controversies over history and honour in film and public debate — and the forts and palaces of the Rajput courts, six of them UNESCO World Heritage Sites since 2013, anchor Rajasthan's identity as India's most visited heritage landscape.
