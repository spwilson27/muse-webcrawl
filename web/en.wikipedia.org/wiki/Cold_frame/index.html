Cold frame

In agriculture and gardening, a cold frame is a transparent-roofed enclosure built low to the ground and used to protect plants from adverse weather, primarily excessive cold or wet. The transparent top admits sunlight and prevents heat escape via convection that would otherwise occur, particularly at night. A cold frame acts as a miniature greenhouse and is often used to extend the growing season.

Construction

Traditionally a cold frame is a bottomless box of wood, brick, or concrete block with a sloping glazed lid (called a "light"), often an old window sash, hinged at the back so it can be propped open for ventilation on warm days. Modern frames use plastic or polycarbonate tops and aluminium or recycled-plastic sides. The frame is sited in a sunny, sheltered spot, ideally facing south in the northern hemisphere, with the soil inside enriched and levelled.

Uses

Gardeners use cold frames to:

  • Start seeds earlier in spring than is possible outdoors.
  • Harden off seedlings raised indoors or in a heated greenhouse before transplanting.
  • Overwinter cuttings, alpines, and marginally hardy plants, keeping them dry as well as frost-protected.
  • Grow salads and other hardy crops later into autumn and winter.

Temperature management is the main skill involved: frames can overheat rapidly in strong sun, so the lid is vented or opened during the day and closed at night. In very cold weather, insulating covers such as sacking or straw mats may be added over the glass.

History

Cold frames were a standard feature of the walled kitchen gardens of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, often built in long ranges against a wall and sometimes heated by fermenting manure in an attached hotbed. They remain a low-cost, low-energy staple of allotment and home gardening today.
