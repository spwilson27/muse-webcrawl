George Graham (1673 – 20 November 1751) was an English clockmaker and inventor, regarded as one of the greatest horologists of the eighteenth century. A protégé and successor of Thomas Tompion, he refined the precision pendulum clock to the point where it served as the standard timekeeper of science for nearly two centuries.

Career

Born in Cumberland, Graham came to London in 1688 and was apprenticed to clockmaker Henry Aske. In 1695 he joined the workshop of Thomas Tompion, the father of English clockmaking, married Tompion's niece, and eventually inherited the business. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1721 and became master of the Clockmakers' Company.

Inventions

Graham's principal contributions include the deadbeat escapement (c. 1715), which eliminated recoil in the pendulum's motion and greatly improved accuracy; the mercury pendulum (1721), which used a jar of mercury whose expansion compensated for the lengthening of the pendulum rod with temperature; and improvements to the cylinder escapement for watches. His precision regulators, accurate to fractions of a second per day, were supplied to observatories and used by astronomers including James Bradley.

Graham also built fine scientific instruments — orreries, quadrants and telescopes — and generously lent money and encouragement to John Harrison in his work on marine chronometers. He is buried in Westminster Abbey in the same grave as Tompion.
