Electron transport chain

An electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes and other molecules which transfer electrons from electron donors to electron acceptors via redox reactions (both reduction and oxidation occurring simultaneously) and couple this electron transfer with the transfer of protons (H+ ions) across a membrane. The electrons that are transferred from NADH and FADH2 to the ETC involve four multi-subunit large enzymes complexes and two mobile electron carriers. Many organisms use the electron transport chain to generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP) by oxidative phosphorylation.

The mitochondrial electron transport chain, with complexes I–IV embedded in the inner membrane.

Overview

The flow of electrons through the chain is an exergonic process. The energy from the redox reactions creates an electrochemical proton gradient that drives the synthesis of ATP. In aerobic respiration, the flow of electrons terminates with molecular oxygen as the final electron acceptor, which is reduced to water. In anaerobic respiration, other acceptors such as sulfate or nitrate are used.

The complexes

Complex I (NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase) accepts electrons from NADH and pumps four protons across the membrane. Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), part of the citric acid cycle, feeds electrons from succinate into the chain via FADH2 but does not pump protons. The lipid-soluble carrier ubiquinone (coenzyme Q) shuttles electrons to Complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex), which passes them to the mobile protein cytochrome c. Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) transfers the electrons to oxygen. The resulting proton-motive force drives ATP synthase (sometimes called Complex V), which phosphorylates ADP to ATP as protons flow back across the membrane, as described by Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory.

Yield

Complete oxidation of one molecule of glucose yields roughly 30–32 ATP in eukaryotes, most of it produced through oxidative phosphorylation powered by the electron transport chain.
