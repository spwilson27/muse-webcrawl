Monochrome photography

Monochrome photography is photography where each position on an image can record and show a different amount of light, but not a different color. It includes all forms of black-and-white photography, which produce images containing tones of neutral grey ranging from black to white. Other hues besides grey, such as sepia or cyan, can also be used in monochrome photography. In the contemporary world, monochrome photographs are mostly produced for artistic reasons or for certain technical imaging applications.

History

From the invention of photography in the 1830s until the commercialisation of colour processes in the early 20th century, all photography was monochrome by necessity. Processes such as the daguerreotype, calotype, and wet collodion produced images in shades of grey or a single tone. Even after colour film became widely available with Kodachrome in 1935, black-and-white remained dominant in photojournalism, fine art, and amateur photography for decades because of its lower cost, faster speed, and archival stability.

Techniques

Monochrome photographers manipulate tone through exposure, development, and the use of coloured lens filters, which lighten objects of their own colour and darken complementary colours — a red filter, for example, dramatically darkens blue skies. Toning processes such as sepia and selenium toning alter the image colour both for appearance and for permanence. In digital photography, monochrome images may be captured with dedicated sensors lacking a colour filter array, or converted from colour captures by mixing colour channels.

Artistic use

Photographers such as Ansel Adams championed black-and-white as an expressive medium, emphasising composition, texture, light, and form over colour. Monochrome remains widely used in fine-art, documentary, and street photography.
