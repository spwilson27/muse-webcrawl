Conservation of mass

[Hero image placeholder: Lavoisier's apparatus for the study of combustion]

In physics and chemistry, the law of conservation of mass or principle of mass conservation states that for any system closed to all transfers of matter and energy, the mass of the system must remain constant over time, as the system's mass cannot change, so the quantity can neither be added nor be removed. Therefore, the quantity of mass is conserved over time.

The law implies that mass can neither be created nor destroyed, although it may be rearranged in space, or the entities associated with it may be changed in form. For example, in chemical reactions, the mass of the chemical components before the reaction is equal to the mass of the components after the reaction. Thus, during any chemical reaction and low-energy thermodynamic processes in an isolated system, the total mass of the reactants must equal the mass of the products.

History

An important idea in ancient Greek philosophy was that "nothing comes from nothing." The first clear statement of the principle came from Mikhail Lomonosov in 1748. The law was later formulated and experimentally demonstrated by Antoine Lavoisier, whose careful quantitative experiments on combustion and calcination in the 1770s and 1780s established the principle in chemistry. Lavoisier stated in his 1789 Traité élémentaire de chimie that "in all the operations of art and nature, nothing is created; an equal quantity of matter exists both before and after the experiment."

Chemical reactions

The conservation of mass is fundamental to stoichiometry: chemical equations must be balanced so that the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides. In practice, very small mass changes occur in chemical reactions because of the mass–energy equivalence, but these defects are far too small to measure chemically.

Relativistic corrections

The concept of mass conservation is widely used in many fields such as chemistry, mechanics, and fluid dynamics. Historically, mass conservation in chemical reactions was primarily due to the fact that the loss in mass of reaction substances accounted for the mass of reaction products accurately enough. However, the principle is approximately true rather than exactly true: Einstein's theory of special relativity showed that mass and energy are interconvertible, so the strictly conserved quantity is mass–energy. In nuclear reactions, measurable amounts of mass are converted into energy.

Fluid mechanics

In fluid mechanics, mass conservation is expressed by the continuity equation, which states that the rate of change of mass within a control volume equals the net rate at which mass flows across its boundaries.
