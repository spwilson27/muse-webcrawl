Fuging tune

A fuging tune (often spelled fuguing tune) is a form of Anglo-American a cappella sacred choral music that flourished in the 18th century, first in England and then in the American colonies. Fuging tunes are typically settings of metrical psalm or hymn texts for four voices, and they take their name from a loose resemblance to the fugue: after an opening homophonic section, the voices enter one after another in imitation.

Structure

A typical fuging tune falls into two parts. The first section presents the opening lines of the text in block chords, all voices singing together. The second section introduces the remaining text with successive, overlapping entries — each voice part begins the phrase alone before the others join — producing a brief spell of counterpoint; the piece usually closes with a short homophonic passage. Composers prized the contrast between the sober opening and the animated "fuging" section, in which a voice part may be heard briefly on its own.

History

The form appeared in English parish church music and collections such as those of the mid-1700s, then crossed the Atlantic, where it was taken up by New England singing-school composers including William Billings, whose "Chester" and other tunes became staples of the repertory. Fuging tunes were printed in tunebooks, often in shape notes, and spread southward and westward; many survive in the Sacred Harp singing tradition, where they remain favourites at all-day singings. Well-known examples include Billings's "Chester," Daniel Read's "Russia," and "Northfield" by Jeremiah Ingalls.
