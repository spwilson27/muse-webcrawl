Blood–brain barrier

[Hero image: diagram of a cerebral capillary showing endothelial tight junctions, pericytes and astrocyte end-feet forming the blood-brain barrier]

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semipermeable border of endothelial cells that regulates the transfer of solutes and chemicals between the circulatory system and the central nervous system, while protecting the brain from harmful or toxic substances in the blood, and filtering harmful compounds from the brain back to the bloodstream.

Structure

The barrier is formed primarily by endothelial cells of the capillary wall, which are joined by tight junctions of extremely high electrical resistivity, together with pericytes embedded in the capillary basement membrane and astrocyte end-feet ensheathing the capillary. Unlike peripheral capillaries, brain endothelial cells lack fenestrations and show very low rates of transcytosis, so most molecules must cross transcellularly. Small lipid-soluble molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and ethanol diffuse freely; glucose and amino acids cross via specific transporters (for example GLUT1); and efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein actively pump many drugs out of the endothelium.

Function and clinical significance

The BBB maintains the stable ionic environment required for synaptic signalling and shields neural tissue from pathogens, antibodies and fluctuating plasma composition. Its selectivity is a major obstacle in pharmacology: the great majority of small-molecule drugs and nearly all large biologics cannot cross it, complicating treatment of brain tumours, infections and neurodegenerative disease. Strategies to overcome it include transient disruption with focused ultrasound or hyperosmotic mannitol, and designing drugs to exploit endogenous transporters (as with L-DOPA, which uses the LAT1 amino-acid transporter).

Breakdown and disease

Barrier breakdown occurs in stroke, multiple sclerosis, meningitis, trauma and some epilepsies, permitting entry of plasma proteins and immune cells that worsen injury. Conversely, specialised regions called circumventricular organs have fenestrated capillaries and no functional barrier, allowing the brain to sample blood-borne hormones.

History

The phenomenon was first noted by Paul Ehrlich in 1885, whose injected dyes stained peripheral tissues but not the brain; his student Edwin Goldman demonstrated the complementary effect in 1913. The anatomical basis in endothelial tight junctions was established by electron microscopy in the 1960s.
