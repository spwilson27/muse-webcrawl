Labor theory of value


Labor as the source of economic value

The labor theory of value (LTV) is a theory of value that argues the exchange value of a good or service is determined by the total amount of "socially necessary labor" required to produce it. It was developed by classical economists such as Adam Smith and David Ricardo and was adopted and transformed by Karl Marx, for whom it became the foundation of his critique of capitalism.

Classical formulation

Smith treated labor as the original purchase money of all commodities, distinguishing between the value of a good in early, pre-capitalist society — measured by the labor needed to obtain it — and its price in advanced societies, composed of wages, profit and rent. Ricardo sharpened the theory, arguing that the relative value of commodities is governed almost entirely by the relative quantities of labor embodied in them, with capital treated as stored-up labor.

Marxian development

In Das Kapital (1867), Marx distinguished a commodity's use value from its exchange value and argued that what makes exchange possible is that commodities share a common substance: abstract human labor. Value is measured by socially necessary labor time — the labor time required under normal conditions of production with average skill and intensity. From this, Marx derived his theory of surplus value: workers sell their labor power for its value but produce more value than that, the difference being appropriated by the capitalist as profit.

Criticism and decline

The marginal revolution of the 1870s, led by Carl Menger, William Stanley Jevons and Léon Walras, displaced the labor theory with subjective theories of value based on marginal utility. Critics also posed the "transformation problem" — how values based on labor time convert into prices of production. The labor theory remains influential in Marxian economics and in some heterodox schools.
