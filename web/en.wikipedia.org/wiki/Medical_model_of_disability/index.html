Medical model of disability

The medical model of disability, also called the individual model, understands disability as a problem of the person, directly caused by disease, trauma, or other health conditions, which requires medical care and intervention to correct or cure. It contrasts with the social model of disability, which locates disability in the barriers created by society.

Key features

Under the medical model:

  • Disability is seen as a deficit or abnormality within an individual.
  • The problem is diagnosed and treated by medical professionals.
  • Cure, rehabilitation, or adjustment of the individual is the goal.
  • Quality of life is measured by functional capacity and the reduction of symptoms.

The model underlies much of modern medicine and services such as rehabilitation, prosthetics, and special education assessment.

Criticism

Disability rights advocates have criticized the medical model for focusing on what is "wrong" with a person rather than on what the person needs, for encouraging a view of disabled people as objects of pity or charity, and for giving professionals excessive control over disabled people's lives. Critics argue that a person using a wheelchair is disabled not by their impairment but by stairs without ramps, and that medical interventions cannot remove social barriers.

Alternatives and syntheses

The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) attempts a biopsychosocial synthesis, combining medical and social elements. Other frameworks include the Nordic relational model and capability-based approaches.
