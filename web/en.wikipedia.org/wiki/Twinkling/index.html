Twinkling, also called scintillation, is a generic term for variations in apparent brightness, colour, or position of a distant luminous object viewed through the atmosphere. If the object lies outside the Earth's atmosphere, as in the case of stars and planets, the phenomenon is termed astronomical scintillation; for objects within the atmosphere, the phenomenon is called terrestrial scintillation. As one of the three principal factors governing astronomical seeing (the others being light pollution and clouds), atmospheric twinkling is defined as variations in illuminance only.

In simple terms, twinkling of stars is caused by the passage of starlight through the turbulent layers and varying densities and temperatures of the atmosphere, i.e. astronomical seeing. Small cells of air with slightly different refractive indices bend the light by slightly different amounts, so the apparent brightness and position of the star fluctuate rapidly, typically on timescales of milliseconds to seconds.

Explanation

The effect is most pronounced near the horizon, where starlight traverses a much longer path through the atmosphere, and for point sources such as stars. Extended sources such as planets subtend a larger angle, so the fluctuations from different parts of the disc average out and planets generally appear to shine steadily. Twinkling is stronger on nights of high atmospheric turbulence and is reduced at high-altitude observatories above much of the turbulent layer.

Twinkling should not be confused with the interstellar scintillation of radio sources such as pulsars, which is caused by irregularities in the interstellar plasma rather than by the Earth's atmosphere.

Mitigation

Astronomers mitigate twinkling and other seeing effects using adaptive optics, speckle imaging, and by siting telescopes at high, dry locations. Space telescopes such as the Hubble Space Telescope avoid the effect entirely by observing from above the atmosphere.
