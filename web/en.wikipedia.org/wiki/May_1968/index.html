May 1968

May 1968 (French: Mai 68) was a period of civil unrest in France marked by student protests, occupations and a general strike involving some ten million workers — about two-thirds of the French workforce — that brought the economy to a halt and briefly threatened the government of President Charles de Gaulle. It remains a defining cultural and political watershed in modern French history.

Background

Postwar France had expanded enormously: universities overflowed with the baby-boom generation squeezed into overcrowded, authoritarian institutions, while workers faced low wages and Taylorist factory discipline. Agitation began in March 1968 at the University of Paris at Nanterre, where students led by Daniel Cohn-Bendit protested over dormitory rules, the Vietnam War and university reform (the "Movement of 22 March").

Events

When Nanterre was closed and protests moved to the Sorbonne, police cleared the Sorbonne on 3 May; students responded by occupying it and building barricades in the Latin Quarter. The night of 10–11 May ("Night of the Barricades") saw fierce street fighting. Trade unions called a one-day general strike on 13 May; it escalated into factory occupations across France, beginning at the Sud-Aviation plant near Nantes and spreading to Renault's plants. De Gaulle briefly and secretly left France on 29 May to consult General Massu in Baden-Baden, then returned to dissolve the National Assembly and call elections. The Grenelle agreements raised wages but failed to end the strike immediately; work gradually resumed in June, and the Gaullists won a landslide in the June elections.

Legacy

May 1968 failed as a revolution but transformed French society: universities were reformed (the Faure law), wages rose, and attitudes toward authority, sexuality, education and the workplace shifted durably. Its slogans — "Sous les pavés, la plage" ("Under the paving stones, the beach"), "Soyez réalistes, demandez l'impossible" — became emblems of the era worldwide. De Gaulle resigned the following year after losing a referendum.
