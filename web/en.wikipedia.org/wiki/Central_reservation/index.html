Central reservation

The central reservation is the area that separates opposing lanes of traffic on a dual carriageway or motorway. In American and Australian English it is called the median (or median strip). Its purposes are to keep vehicles on opposing carriageways apart, prevent head-on collisions, provide a recovery and refuge area, and give space for barriers, drainage, lighting columns and road signs.

Design

Central reservations range from wide grassed medians tens of metres across on rural highways to narrow strips on urban roads protected by a barrier. On high-speed roads in the United Kingdom and many other countries, a safety barrier is standard: historically steel Armco-type barriers, and increasingly concrete step barriers, which better contain heavy goods vehicles crossing into opposing traffic. Cross-over incidents — vehicles breaching the central reservation — are among the most lethal highway accidents and drive much barrier design.

Terminology and variations

The term "central reservation" is standard in British highway engineering. Wide medians may be landscaped, used for bus rapid transit lanes or rail lines (as in some Chicago and Los Angeles freeways), or planted to reduce glare from oncoming headlights. Some reservations have emergency crossing points for services; others are continuous to prevent unauthorised U-turns. In some jurisdictions portions of the median are paved and hatched as a "flush median" to serve as a turning refuge.
