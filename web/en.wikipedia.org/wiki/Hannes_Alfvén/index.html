Hannes Olof Gösta Alfvén (30 May 1908 – 2 April 1995) was a Swedish electrical engineer and plasma physicist. He founded the field of magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) — the study of electrically conducting fluids interacting with magnetic fields — and won the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Louis Néel, "for fundamental work and discoveries in magnetohydrodynamics with fruitful applications in different parts of plasma physics".

Scientific work

After taking his doctorate at Uppsala in 1934, Alfvén built his career at the Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm. In 1942 he predicted the existence of electromagnetic–hydrodynamic waves — now called Alfvén waves — in which ions oscillate in response to a restoring force from magnetic field lines; initially doubted, they were confirmed experimentally within the decade and are fundamental to plasma physics and space science. His "frozen-in flux" theorem, adiabatic invariants and guiding-centre formalism underpin the study of the solar wind, magnetospheres, auroras and fusion plasmas.

Cosmology and later views

Alfvén championed a plasma cosmology in which electromagnetic forces shape the large-scale universe, and he remained a prominent critic of Big Bang cosmology throughout his life — views that put him at odds with the mainstream even as his plasma physics became indispensable. He also proposed early ideas about antimatter in the universe and wrote science fiction under the pen name Olof Johannesson, including the satirical The Great Computer (1966).

Later life

Frustrated by academic politics in Sweden, he spent much of his later career at the University of California, San Diego, campaigning also against nuclear power after the Three Mile Island accident. The Alfvén wave, the Alfvén radius and an asteroid, 1778 Alfvén, commemorate him.
