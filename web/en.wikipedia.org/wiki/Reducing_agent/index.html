Reducing agent

A reducing agent (also called a reductant, reducer, or electron donor) is an element or compound in a redox chemical reaction that "donates" an electron to an electron recipient (an oxidizing agent, oxidant, or oxidizer). In the course of the reaction, the reducing agent itself becomes oxidized, and the recipient becomes reduced.

In a redox reaction the reducing agent is oxidized as it donates electrons

Overview

Historically, reduction referred to the removal of oxygen from a compound; the modern electronic definition covers any reaction in which an atom, ion, or molecule gains electrons and its oxidation state decreases. A mnemonic sometimes taught is "OIL RIG": Oxidation Is Loss (of electrons), Reduction Is Gain.

Good reducing agents include many metals (sodium, zinc, aluminium, iron), hydrogen, carbon (as coke in smelting), and compounds containing hydride, such as lithium aluminium hydride and sodium borohydride, which are workhorse reductants in organic synthesis. Their strength is quantified by reduction potentials: the stronger a reducing agent, the more negative the standard reduction potential of its oxidized form.

Applications

  • Metallurgy – carbon and carbon monoxide reduce metal oxides in smelting, for example in the blast furnace production of iron.
  • Organic chemistry – hydride reagents reduce carbonyl groups; catalytic hydrogenation reduces alkenes and alkynes.
  • Biochemistry – NADH, NADPH, and glutathione act as reducing agents; glutathione and other antioxidants protect cells from oxidative damage.

Examples

In the thermite reaction, aluminium powder is a powerful reducing agent that strips oxygen from iron(III) oxide. In corrosion, iron acts as a reducing agent in forming rust, and more reactive metals such as zinc are used as sacrificial anodes to protect steel structures.
