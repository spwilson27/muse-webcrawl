Eastern barred bandicoot

Scientific namePerameles gunnii
FamilyPeramelidae (bandicoots)
Weight500–650 g (typical)
DistributionTasmania; reintroduced sites in Victoria, Australia

The eastern barred bandicoot (Perameles gunnii) is a nocturnal marsupial native to southeastern Australia, named for the pale bars across its hindquarters. It is a small, rabbit-sized animal with a pointed snout, large ears, soft grey-brown fur, and a short tail. Two populations are recognised: one in Tasmania, where the species remains comparatively widespread, and one on the mainland, which is critically endangered.

Ecology

Eastern barred bandicoots shelter by day in nests of grass in dense cover and emerge at dusk to forage in grasslands and open woodland. Using their keen sense of smell and long snout, they dig conical holes in search of insects, earthworms, grubs, roots and fungi. Females have one of the shortest gestation periods of any mammal — about 12 days — and can produce several litters a year when conditions are good, giving the species a high potential rate of recovery.

Conservation

The mainland population collapsed from habitat loss, predation by introduced red foxes and cats, and competition from rabbits, declining to a single remnant colony near Hamilton in Victoria by the late 1980s. Captive breeding, begun at that time, has supplied animals for reintroductions to fox-free sites, including fenced reserves and offshore islands such as Phillip Island and French Island. The Tasmanian population, though more secure, is also declining. The establishment of these reintroduced populations has since allowed the mainland population's status to be reassessed, though it remains highly conservation-dependent.
