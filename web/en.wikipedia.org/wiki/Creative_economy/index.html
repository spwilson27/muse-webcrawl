Creative economy

The creative economy is the part of the economy based on people's creativity, knowledge and intellectual property — the economic activity that arises from individual creativity, skill and talent and that generates wealth and jobs through the creation and exploitation of ideas. The concept encompasses the creative industries such as advertising, architecture, design, fashion, film, music, publishing, software and video games, as well as creative occupations across the wider economy.

Origins of the concept

The term "creative industries" was placed on the policy map by the United Kingdom's Department for Culture, Media and Sport in 1998, which defined them as industries originating in individual creativity, skill and talent with potential for wealth and job creation through intellectual property. Author John Howkins popularised the broader term "creative economy" in his 2001 book The Creative Economy, and Richard Florida's work on the "creative class" (2002) drew attention to the concentration of creative workers in successful cities. UNCTAD and UNESCO have since tracked world trade in creative goods and services and promoted the creative economy in development policy.

Scale and characteristics

Estimates by UNCTAD and UNESCO place the creative economy at several percent of global GDP and tens of millions of jobs worldwide, spanning both formal industries and large informal cultural sectors in developing countries. Its inputs are ideas rather than physical resources, and its outputs are protected — imperfectly — by copyright and other intellectual-property rights. Digital technologies have lowered production and distribution costs, enabling individual creators and small firms to reach global audiences, while also raising questions about precarious work, unequal earnings and the market power of platforms.
