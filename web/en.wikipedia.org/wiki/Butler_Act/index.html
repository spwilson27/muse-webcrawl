Butler Act

The Butler Act was a 1925 Tennessee law that made it unlawful "to teach any theory that denies the Story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals" in any state-funded educational institution. The Act became famous as the statute under which teacher John T. Scopes was prosecuted in the Scopes "Monkey Trial" of 1925.

Passage

The bill was introduced by John Washington Butler, a farmer and member of the Tennessee House of Representatives, amid the fundamentalist campaign against evolution led nationally by William Jennings Bryan. It passed the House by 75 votes to 5 and the Senate by 24 to 6. Governor Austin Peay signed it on March 21, 1925, remarking that he doubted it would ever be applied; the Act set a fine of between $100 and $500 but was understood by many supporters as a symbolic statement rather than a law to be actively enforced.

The Scopes trial

The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend any teacher willing to test the law. Civic leaders in Dayton, Tennessee, eager for publicity, recruited 24-year-old teacher and football coach John Scopes, who agreed to be charged — though he was unsure he had actually taught evolution. The July 1925 trial pitted Bryan, assisting the prosecution, against Clarence Darrow for the defense, and became a national spectacle. Scopes was convicted and fined $100, but the Tennessee Supreme Court later overturned the conviction on a technicality while upholding the Act's constitutionality.

Repeal and legacy

The Butler Act remained on the books, largely unenforced, until the Tennessee legislature repealed it in 1967. Similar anti-evolution statutes in other states were struck down by the US Supreme Court in Epperson v. Arkansas (1968), which held such laws violated the Establishment Clause. The Act and trial remain central episodes in the American debate over science, religion, and public education.
