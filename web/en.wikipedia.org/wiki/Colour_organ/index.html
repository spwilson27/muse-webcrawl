A colour organ is an instrument that produces coloured light in performance, typically controlled from a keyboard, intended to create a form of visual music analogous to audible music. Inventors mapped musical notes to colours and built devices that projected changing coloured lights onto a screen or into a space, sometimes accompanying sound and sometimes silent.

History

The idea of music for the eye dates to the ocular harpsichord of Louis Bertrand Castel in the 18th century. In the nineteenth century, Bainbridge Bishop patented a colour attachment for organs (1877), and Alexander Wallace Rimington built a colour organ and published Colour-Music: The Art of Mobile Colour (1911). Thomas Wilfred's Clavilux instruments of the 1920s refined the art of "lumia" — silent light compositions played live. The concept influenced abstract film, light shows and, later, electronic music visualisers.

Colour–note correspondences

There is no universally accepted mapping between notes and colours; composers such as Alexander Scriabin, who included a part for a colour keyboard (tastiera per luce) in his Prometheus: The Poem of Fire (1910), devised their own systems, often based on the circle of fifths. The appeal of the colour organ rests partly on synaesthesia — the cross-sensory experience some people have of perceiving sound as colour.
