Nucleon

In physics and chemistry, a nucleon is either a proton or a neutron: the two kinds of baryon that make up the atomic nucleus. Nucleons each have a mass of roughly one atomic mass unit (the proton about 1.00728 u and the neutron about 1.00866 u), spin 1/2, and are composed of three quarks — the proton of two up quarks and one down quark, the neutron of one up and two down quarks.

Properties

Within nuclear physics, protons and neutrons are treated as two states of a single particle related by the isospin symmetry, distinguished by the third component of isospin. The strong nuclear force binds nucleons together in nuclei, overcoming the electrostatic repulsion between protons. A free neutron decays into a proton, electron and antineutrino with a half-life of about ten minutes, whereas neutrons bound in stable nuclei do not decay. The interactions between nucleons are described phenomenologically by nucleon–nucleon potentials and, at a deeper level, by quantum chromodynamics.

Terminology

The mass number A of a nucleus is its total number of nucleons, and the atomic number Z counts its protons (nucleons with positive charge). The term was popularised in the decades after the neutron's discovery by James Chadwick in 1932 made clear that nuclei consist of protons and neutrons.
