The Autobahn (German plural: Autobahnen) is the federal controlled-access highway system in Germany. Officially termed Bundesautobahn ("federal motorway"), the network has a total length of about 13,200 kilometres, making it among the densest and longest motorway systems in the world. German autobahns have no federally mandated general speed limit for some classes of vehicles, though an advisory speed of 130 km/h (the Richtgeschwindigkeit) applies, and many sections carry permanent or temporary limits.

History

The first road of the type was the AVUS in Berlin, opened in 1921 as a racing and test track. The first public motorway between Cologne and Bonn was opened in 1932 by Konrad Adenauer. After 1933 the Nazi regime massively expanded construction under the Reichsautobahn programme, employing thousands and creating propaganda value as well as infrastructure; by 1942 about 3,800 km had been built. Post-war West Germany resumed construction, and after reunification in 1990 the network was extended and modernised in the former East.

Construction and rules

Autobahns feature grade-separated junctions, at least two lanes per direction separated by a median, gentle gradients, freeze-resistant concrete or asphalt surfaces, and emergency telephones and rest areas (Raststätten). Driving on the right and overtaking on the left is mandatory; stopping, reversing and U-turns are prohibited except in emergencies. Since 2005, trucks pay a distance-based toll (Lkw-Maut); passenger cars remain untolled after a proposed car toll was struck down by the European Court of Justice in 2019.

Speed limits debate

Roughly 70 per cent of the network has no permanent limit for cars, with limits imposed near cities, on dangerous stretches and for noise abatement. Proposals for a general 130 km/h limit recur in German politics, argued on safety and climate grounds and opposed on grounds of driver freedom and tradition; surveys suggest public opinion is closely divided. Despite unrestricted sections, German motorway fatality rates per kilometre are comparable to those of neighbouring countries.

Numbering

Routes are numbered A 1 to A 999: single- and double-digit routes of national significance run broadly north–south (odd) and east–west (even), while three-digit numbers denote regional routes whose first digit indicates the region.
