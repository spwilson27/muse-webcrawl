Collision

In physics, a collision is any event in which two or more bodies exert forces on each other in a relatively short time. Although the most common use of the word refers to incidents in which two or more objects collide with great force, the scientific use is precise: the interacting bodies must exert a force on one another, and the interaction need not involve physical contact — for example, the scattering of particles.

Types of collision

Elastic collision

An elastic collision conserves both momentum and total kinetic energy. Collisions between hard billiard balls approximate elastic collisions, as do interactions between gas molecules at ordinary temperatures.

Inelastic collision

An inelastic collision conserves momentum but not kinetic energy; some kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound or deformation. Vehicle crashes are largely inelastic. A perfectly inelastic collision is one in which the bodies stick together after impact.

Analysis

Collisions are analysed using the conservation of linear momentum: in an isolated system the total momentum before the collision equals the total after. The coefficient of restitution measures the bounciness of a collision, ranging from 0 (perfectly inelastic) to 1 (perfectly elastic).

Other uses

The word is applied metaphorically in many fields: in computing, a hash collision and a network packet collision; in transport safety, a traffic collision; in astronomy, the collision of galaxies and the impact events that shape planetary surfaces.
