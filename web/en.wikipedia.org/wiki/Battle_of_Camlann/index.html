Battle of Camlann

The Battle of Camlann (Gwaith Camlan or Battle of Camboglanna) is the legendary final battle of King Arthur, in which Arthur either died or was fatally wounded while fighting his rival, in most versions his son or nephew Mordred, who was himself killed in the same clash.

The last battle: Arthur and Mordred slay one another.

Early accounts

The earliest reference is in the 10th-century Welsh annals Annales Cambriae, which record for the year 537 (or 539): "The Battle of Camlann, in which Arthur and Medraut fell; and there was plague in Britain and Ireland." The entry gives no indication that Arthur and Medraut (Mordred) were enemies. Later Welsh tradition and Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136) elaborated the story: Arthur, returning from a continental campaign, meets Mordred — left as regent and usurper — at the river Camblam, identified with the Camel in Cornwall.

Later tradition

In French romance and in Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485), the battle takes place on Salisbury Plain and is triggered by accident: during a parley, a knight draws his sword to kill an adder, and the two armies, mistaking the gesture, fall on each other. Arthur kills Mordred with a spear but is struck a mortal blow to the head; he is borne away by barge to the Isle of Avalon, giving rise to the "once and future king" legend.

Historicity

No contemporary evidence confirms the battle. Proposed sites include Queen's Camel in Somerset, the River Camel in Cornwall, and the Roman fort of Camboglanna on Hadrian's Wall near Carlisle. Most historians treat Camlann as belonging to legend rather than record, though it may preserve a memory of a real 6th-century British battle.
