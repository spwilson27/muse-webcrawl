Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphate poisoning is poisoning due to organophosphates (OPs), a class of chemicals including many insecticides (such as parathion, malathion and chlorpyrifos) and nerve agents (such as sarin and VX). It is a major public-health problem worldwide: hundreds of thousands of deaths each year are estimated to result from pesticide self-poisoning, particularly in rural areas of low- and middle-income countries, in addition to occupational and accidental exposures.

Agricultural pesticide spraying is a common route of occupational exposure.

Mechanism

Organophosphates inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, which normally breaks down the neurotransmitter acetylcholine in synapses and neuromuscular junctions. Acetylcholine therefore accumulates, overstimulating muscarinic and nicotinic receptors. Over time the inhibited enzyme undergoes "aging" — loss of an alkyl group — after which it cannot be reactivated by antidotes.

Signs and symptoms

The classic picture is a cholinergic toxidrome. Muscarinic effects are often summarised by the mnemonic SLUDGE/DUMBELS: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal cramping, emesis, miosis (pinpoint pupils) and bronchorrhoea, with bradycardia. Nicotinic effects include muscle fasciculations, weakness, and paralysis; respiratory failure is the usual cause of death. Central effects include anxiety, confusion, seizures and coma.

Delayed syndromes can follow: the "intermediate syndrome" of proximal muscle and respiratory weakness arising one to four days after exposure, and organophosphate-induced delayed polyneuropathy appearing weeks later.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is mainly clinical, supported by measurement of red-cell acetylcholinesterase or plasma (pseudo)cholinesterase activity, which fall after significant exposure.

Treatment

Management consists of removing the source and decontaminating the patient, supportive airway and breathing care, and specific antidotes: atropine, which blocks muscarinic receptors and is titrated against drying of secretions and heart rate, and an oxime such as pralidoxime, which reactivates acetylcholinesterase if given before aging. Benzodiazepines are used for seizures and agitation.

Prevention

Regulation and restriction of the most hazardous pesticides, safer storage, protective equipment and substitution with less toxic pest-control methods have been shown to reduce deaths substantially in several countries.
