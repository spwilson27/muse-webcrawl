Arcadia (regional unit)

Arcadia (Greek: Αρκαδία, Arkadía) is one of the regional units of Greece. It is part of the administrative region of Peloponnese, situated in the central and eastern part of the Peloponnese peninsula. It takes its name from the mythological figure Arcas. Its capital is the city of Tripoli.

Geography

Arcadia is the largest regional unit of the Peloponnese. Its terrain is dominated by mountains, including Mount Mainalo (1,981 m), Mount Parnon and the Taygetus foothills, interspersed with upland basins such as the Tripoli plateau and the Megalopolis basin, long known for its lignite deposits and power stations. To the east the land descends to a narrow coastal strip on the Argolic Gulf, where towns such as Leonidio and Tyros lie. Major rivers include the Alfeios and the Ladon.

History

Ancient Arcadia was a landlocked, pastoral region whose inhabitants worshipped Pan and were regarded by other Greeks as aboriginal and rugged. Its cities, including Tegea, Mantinea and Orchomenus, were often rivals. In classical literature, beginning with Virgil's Eclogues and renewed in the Renaissance, "Arcadia" became a poetic ideal of unspoiled pastoral life — the origin of the modern word arcadian. The region played a notable role in the Greek War of Independence; Theodoros Kolokotronis, the war's most famous commander, came from Arcadia, and Tripoli (then Tripolitsa) was stormed by Greek forces in 1821.

Modern administration

The modern prefecture of Arcadia was created in the 19th century and became a regional unit under the 2011 Kallikratis reform, with five municipalities. Its economy rests on agriculture (olives, grapes, cherries), some industry around Megalopolis's power plants, and growing mountain and coastal tourism.
