Theodoros Kolokotronis (Greek: Θεόδωρος Κολοκοτρώνης; 3 April 1770 – 4 February 1843) was a Greek general and the pre-eminent leader of the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829) against the Ottoman Empire. He is widely regarded as the most important figure of the Greek revolution and is affectionately known as the "Old Man of the Morea" (ο Γέρος του Μοριά), the Morea being the traditional name of the Peloponnese peninsula.

Early life

Kolokotronis was born in Ramavouni, Messenia, into a family of klephts — mountain bandits who resisted Ottoman rule. His father, Constantine Kolokotronis, was a noted klepht leader who was killed by the Ottomans in 1780. Raised among the klepht bands of the Peloponnese, the young Theodoros learned the guerrilla warfare of the mountains. As a young man he served under the famous captain Zacharias Barbitsiotis and later sought his fortune at sea.

In 1806, fleeing Ottoman persecution, he escaped to the Ionian Islands, then under British control, where he enlisted in the British-sponsored Greek Light Infantry as an officer. His service gave him formal military training and contact with European armies, experience that proved invaluable in 1821.

Greek War of Independence

When the Greek revolution broke out in March 1821, Kolokotronis returned to the Peloponnese and quickly emerged as its foremost military leader. His victory at the Battle of Valtetsi in May 1821 and the subsequent capture of Tripolitsa, the Ottoman administrative capital of the Morea, in October 1821, secured the Peloponnese as the heartland of the revolution.

His greatest triumph came in the summer of 1822, when he annihilated the Ottoman expeditionary force of Dramali Pasha at the Battle of Dervenakia, trapping the Ottoman army in the narrow passes and destroying it. This victory saved the revolution at its most precarious moment.

Later life and legacy

Kolokotronis was repeatedly caught up in the civil conflicts between Greek factions, and was imprisoned by his rivals on the island of Hydra in 1825 — only to be released to face the invasion of Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt, against whom he waged a tenacious guerrilla campaign. After independence he supported Count Ioannis Kapodistrias and, following the count's assassination, became entangled in politics under King Otto. Accused of treason in 1834, he was sentenced to death but pardoned by the king, and he died in Athens in 1843.

His memoirs, dictated late in life, are a classic of modern Greek literature. His bronze helmet and image became enduring symbols of Greek independence, and his statue stands before the Old Parliament building in Athens.
