The Jazz Age was a period in the 1920s and early 1930s in which jazz music and dance styles gained worldwide popularity. The Jazz Age's cultural repercussions were primarily felt in the United States, the birthplace of jazz. Originating in New Orleans in the early 20th century, jazz largely derived from ragtime and other styles of African American music.

The period is often referred to in conjunction with the Roaring Twenties, and overlapped in significant cross-cultural ways with the Prohibition era. The movement was largely affected by the introduction of radio, which disseminated the music to a mass audience. The Jazz Age also spawned the popular dance styles of the era, including the Charleston, the Lindy Hop and the Black Bottom.

Background

The term "Jazz Age" had been used as early as 1922 by F. Scott Fitzgerald, who published a collection of short stories titled Tales of the Jazz Age that year. Fitzgerald is widely credited with popularising the term. Jazz had emerged from African American communities in New Orleans, combining African rhythms, blues, ragtime and brass band traditions. The 1917 recording by the Original Dixieland Jass Band is often cited as the first jazz record released.

Music

Key musicians included Louis Armstrong, whose virtuosic trumpet playing and scat singing transformed jazz into a soloist's art; Duke Ellington, whose orchestra at Harlem's Cotton Club produced sophisticated compositions; Jelly Roll Morton, Bessie Smith, King Oliver, Fletcher Henderson and Bix Beiderbecke. Radio broadcasts and phonograph records spread the music far beyond its cities of origin.

Culture

The era was associated with flappers, speakeasies, Art Deco design, and a loosening of social conventions, especially for young women. Harlem became a centre of African American culture during the Harlem Renaissance. Writers such as Fitzgerald, Ernest Hemingway and Langston Hughes chronicled or embodied the period.

End of the era

The Jazz Age is generally considered to have ended with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the onset of the Great Depression, which curtailed the recording industry and nightlife spending. Jazz itself continued to evolve into swing in the 1930s.
