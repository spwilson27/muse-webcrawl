Hamilton–Jacobi equation

In physics, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, named after William Rowan Hamilton and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, is an alternative formulation of classical mechanics, equivalent to Newton's laws, the Euler–Lagrange equation and Hamilton's equations of motion. It expresses the dynamics of a system in terms of a single scalar function, Hamilton's principal function S, and is particularly powerful for identifying conserved quantities and for making the connection between mechanics and wave optics — a connection that historically led Schrödinger to wave mechanics.

Formulation

For a system with Hamiltonian H(q, p, t), the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is the first-order, non-linear partial differential equation

∂S/∂t + H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) = 0,

where the generalised momenta are given by p = ∂S/∂q. If the Hamiltonian does not depend explicitly on time, one writes S = W − Et, with W Hamilton's characteristic function and E the (conserved) energy, obtaining the abbreviated (time-independent) equation H(q, ∂W/∂q) = E. A complete integral of the equation, depending on as many constants of integration as there are degrees of freedom, generates the full solution of the mechanical problem through differentiation — the trajectories are obtained from ∂S/∂α = β, where α, β are constants.

Separation of variables

The practical strength of the method lies in separation of variables: when the Hamilton–Jacobi equation separates in some coordinate system, the motion reduces to quadratures and the constants of the separated equations are constants of motion. The Kepler problem separates in spherical polar and parabolic coordinates, and the Hamilton–Jacobi method underlies the theory of action–angle variables used in celestial mechanics and the study of integrable systems.

Relation to optics and quantum mechanics

Hamilton developed his theory by analogy with geometrical optics: Hamilton's principal function plays the role of the eikonal, and surfaces of constant S may be pictured as wavefronts propagating through configuration space, with trajectories as their orthogonal "rays". Jacobi recast and completed Hamilton's work in the 1830s–40s. Erwin Schrödinger explicitly cited this analogy when, in 1926, he replaced the classical Hamilton–Jacobi equation with his wave equation, taking the action S to be proportional to the phase of a wave. In the semiclassical (WKB) approximation the Schrödinger equation reduces back to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, displaying classical mechanics as the short-wavelength limit of quantum mechanics.
