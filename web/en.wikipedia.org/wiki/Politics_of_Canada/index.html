Politics of Canada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Canada are those of a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy, the monarch represented federally by a governor general and in each of the ten provinces by lieutenant governors. The Parliament of Canada (the elected House of Commons and the appointed Senate) shares legislative power with provincial legislatures under the Constitution Acts 1867 and 1982; the prime minister and cabinet, drawn from the Commons, govern while they hold its confidence. First-past-the-post elections routinely produce majority governments on well under half the vote.

The party system has centred on the Liberals (the “natural governing party” of the twentieth century) and Conservatives, with the social-democratic NDP, the sovereigntist Bloc Québécois (federal voice of Quebec nationalism since 1991) and Greens; provincial party systems differ sharply. Recurring constitutional themes are federal–provincial bargaining (equalisation, health funding), Quebec's status (two failed independence referendums, 1980 and 1995; the Clarity Act 2000), Indigenous rights and reconciliation (s.35 of the Charter era constitution; the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, 2008–15), and the Senate's unreformed appointed character. Courts, empowered by the Charter in 1982, are central political actors.
