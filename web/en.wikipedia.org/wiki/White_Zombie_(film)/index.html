White Zombie is a 1932 American pre-Code horror film directed by Victor Halperin and produced by Victor and Edward Halperin. It stars Bela Lugosi as the Haitian voodoo master "Murder" Legendre, who commands an army of mindless zombies working in his sugar mill. The film is widely considered the first feature-length zombie film, and it established many conventions of the zombie genre before George A. Romero's later redefinition of the creature.

Plot

Young lovers Madeleine Short (Madge Bellamy) and Neil Parker (John Harron) arrive in Haiti to be married at the plantation of Charles Beaumont (Robert Frazer), who secretly desires Madeleine. Desperate to win her, Beaumont turns to Legendre (Lugosi), a voodoo priest who has raised corpses as labourers for his mill. Legendre gives Beaumont a potion that makes Madeleine appear dead; she is buried, her "death" mourned, and then exhumed and revived as a soulless zombie under Legendre's control. With the help of the missionary Dr. Bruner (Joseph Cawthorn), Neil searches for Madeleine, descending into Legendre's cliff-top castle. Legendre attempts to have Neil killed by his zombies and to keep Madeleine even from Beaumont, but in the climax Legendre is thrown from the cliff, his control breaks, and Madeleine's spirit returns.

Production

The film was adapted from elements of William Seabrook's 1929 travel book The Magic Island, which popularised Haitian Vodou and the zombie in American culture, and from Kenneth Webb's stage play Zombie. Produced independently on a small budget of roughly $50,000, it was shot in eleven nights at Universal Studios, reusing sets from Dracula (1931) and other productions. Lugosi, coming off his triumph in Dracula, was reportedly paid only about $800 for the role.

Reception and legacy

Reviews in 1932 were mixed, but the film was a commercial success for an independent production, and its reputation grew with revivals and television broadcasts. Critics have praised its atmospheric use of shadow, matte paintings and long takes. White Zombie is now regarded as a classic of early horror cinema and the foundational zombie film; its depiction of zombies as enslaved, trance-like labourers reflects the folklore of Haiti under American occupation. The heavy metal band White Zombie, fronted by Rob Zombie, took its name from the film. A loose sequel, Revolt of the Zombies, followed in 1936.
