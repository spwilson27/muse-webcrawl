Texas v. Johnson, 491 U.S. 397 (1989), is a landmark decision by the Supreme Court of the United States holding that burning the American flag as an act of political protest is symbolic speech protected by the First Amendment.

Background

In 1984, during a protest outside the Republican National Convention in Dallas, Gregory Lee Johnson burned an American flag to protest the policies of the Reagan administration. No one was physically injured, but Johnson was convicted under a Texas law prohibiting the desecration of a venerated object and sentenced to one year in prison and fined $2,000. The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals reversed the conviction on First Amendment grounds.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Justice William J. Brennan Jr., the Court affirmed. The majority held that Johnson's conduct was expressive, that the state's interests in preserving the flag as a symbol and preventing breaches of the peace did not justify suppressing the expression, and famously declared that the government may not prohibit expression simply because society finds the idea offensive.

Aftermath

Congress responded with the federal Flag Protection Act of 1989, which the Court struck down the following year in United States v. Eichman. Repeated efforts to amend the Constitution to permit flag desecration bans have failed, and Texas v. Johnson remains a leading precedent on symbolic speech.
