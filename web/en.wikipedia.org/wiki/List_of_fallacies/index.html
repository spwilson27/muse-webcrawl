A fallacy is the use of invalid or otherwise faulty reasoning in the construction of an argument. Fallacies are commonly divided into formal fallacies, which are errors in the logical structure of an argument, and informal fallacies, which arise from errors in the content or context of the premises.

Formal fallacies

  • Affirming the consequent — assuming that because the consequent is true, the antecedent must be true.
  • Denying the antecedent — assuming that because the antecedent is false, the consequent must be false.
  • Affirming a disjunct — concluding one disjunct is false because the other is true, when both may be true.
  • Fallacy of four terms — a syllogism using four distinct terms instead of three.
  • Illicit major / illicit minor — a term distributed in the conclusion but not in the premises.
  • Masked-man fallacy — substitution of identical designators fails in intensional contexts.
  • Base rate fallacy — ignoring prior probability when evaluating conditional evidence.

Informal fallacies

Faulty generalisations

  • Hasty generalisation — drawing a broad conclusion from a small or unrepresentative sample.
  • Cherry picking — selecting only evidence that supports one's claim.
  • False analogy — arguing from a comparison that is not relevantly similar.
  • Slippery slope — asserting a first step will inevitably lead to an extreme outcome.

Presumption

  • Begging the question (petitio principii) — assuming the conclusion in the premises.
  • False dilemma — presenting only two options when more exist.
  • Loaded question — a question containing an unjustified presupposition.
  • No true Scotsman — redefining a term to exclude a counterexample.
  • Straw man — misrepresenting an opponent's argument to make it easier to attack.

Relevance

  • Ad hominem — attacking the person instead of the argument.
  • Appeal to authority — claiming something is true because an authority said so.
  • Appeal to popularity (argumentum ad populum) — claiming something is true because many believe it.
  • Appeal to emotion — manipulating feelings rather than giving reasons.
  • Appeal to ignorance — claiming something is true because it has not been proven false.
  • Red herring — introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention.
  • Tu quoque — dismissing an argument by accusing the arguer of hypocrisy.
  • Genetic fallacy — judging something by its origin rather than its merits.

Causation

  • Post hoc ergo propter hoc — assuming that because B followed A, A caused B.
  • False cause — identifying a spurious cause of an effect.
  • Single cause — assuming one simple cause when many could be responsible.

Ambiguity

  • Equivocation — using a word with two meanings as if it had one.
  • Amphiboly — ambiguity arising from grammatical structure.
  • Composition — assuming what is true of the parts is true of the whole; its converse is division.
