Yamato people

The Yamato people (Japanese: 大和民族, Yamato minzoku) are the dominant native ethnic group of Japan. The term came into use around the late 19th century to distinguish the inhabitants of mainland Japan from other ethnic groups in the expanding Japanese Empire, such as the Ainu, Ryukyuans, Nivkh, and Orok.

Origins

The Yamato take their name from the Yamato region (present-day Nara Prefecture), seat of the early Japanese imperial court. Genetic and archaeological research generally holds that the modern Yamato population descends principally from Yayoi migrants who arrived from continental Asia beginning roughly in the first millennium BCE, mixing with the indigenous Jōmon population. A "dual structure" model proposes that this admixture was most pronounced in central Japan, while Ainu and Ryukyuan populations retain greater Jōmon ancestry.

History

The Yamato court progressively unified the Japanese archipelago between the Kofun and Asuka periods. During the Meiji era and the period of Japanese imperial expansion, the concept of a single Yamato people was promoted as part of nation-building, and assimilation policies were applied to Ainu and Ryukyuan populations. Post-war Japanese scholarship and public discourse have increasingly recognised Japan's internal ethnic diversity.
