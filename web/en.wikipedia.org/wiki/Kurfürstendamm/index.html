The Kurfürstendamm (locally called the Ku'damm, [ˈkuːˌdam]) is one of the most famous avenues in Berlin, Germany. The street takes its name from the former Kurfürsten (prince-electors) of Brandenburg. It runs for 3.5 kilometres from Breitscheidplatz, by the ruin of the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church, westward through Charlottenburg to Halensee, and is considered the Champs-Élysées of Berlin — the traditional centre of luxury shopping and entertainment in the western city.

History

The route began around 1542 as a raised causeway (Damm) of logs across marshy ground, used by the electors to ride to the Grunewald hunting lodge. In the 1880s, at Chancellor Bismarck's urging, it was developed into a grand 53-metre-wide boulevard modelled on Paris. By the 1920s the Ku'damm was the glittering heart of Berlin's nightlife and intellectual scene, lined with cafés (the Romanisches Café), theatres, cinemas, and cabarets.

Division and reunification

After the Second World War — which left the street heavily bombed — the Kurfürstendamm lay in West Berlin and was rebuilt as the showcase of the capitalist West: the first post-war department stores, neon advertising, the Europa-Center (1965), and the Hotel Kempinski defined the "Golden West". The Memorial Church, whose ruined spire was deliberately preserved as an anti-war memorial beside Egon Eiermann's new octagonal church (1961), anchors its eastern end. After German reunification in 1990, some retail gravity returned to the historic centre around Friedrichstraße, but the Ku'damm remained Berlin's premier luxury retail street, home to flagship boutiques of international fashion houses and to the nearby Kaufhaus des Westens (KaDeWe), continental Europe's largest department store, on Wittenbergplatz.

Today

The street remains a major shopping and tourist destination and a venue for events such as the Berlin Marathon and New Year celebrations relayed to Breitscheidplatz. Its side streets hold theatres including the Theater des Westens and the Komödie am Kurfürstendamm.
