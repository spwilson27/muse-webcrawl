Sun Tzu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bamboo-slip manuscript of The Art of War from the Yinqueshan Han slips]

Sun Tzu (544–496 BC, traditional dates) was a Chinese military general and strategist traditionally credited with The Art of War, the most influential treatise on strategy ever written. Traditionally a general of the state of Wu in the late Spring and Autumn period — Sima Qian's biography has him drill the king's concubines into soldiers and win Wu's wars against Chu — Sun Tzu's historicity has been doubted since the Song dynasty, and modern scholarship treats The Art of War as a Warring States-era text (4th century BC) possibly compiled over generations under his name. The discovery in 1972 of the Yinqueshan bamboo slips — including both The Art of War and the long-lost Military Methods of his descendant Sun Bin — confirmed the antiquity of the tradition while complicating its authorship.

The text

Thirteen chapters, some 6,000 characters: war as a matter of deception and calculation, of winning without battle ('subduing the enemy without fighting is the acme of skill'), of terrain, logistics, spies (the final chapter's five kinds of agent) and the commander as the state's fate. Unlike Clausewitz's battle-centric theory, Sun Tzu's is a theory of the whole contest — political, economic, psychological — which is why it escaped the barracks into boardrooms, courtrooms and sports. Its terse classical Chinese sustains endless reinterpretation; the standard English versions (Giles 1910, Griffith 1963) are themselves classics of translation.

The man and the doubts

Sima Qian's biography — the concubine-drilling anecdote (two favourites executed for giggling at drill, the king's protests overridden: 'the general in the field is not bound by his sovereign's orders') — is the foundation of the legend. The Zuo Zhuan's silence about Sun Tzu in Wu's attested wars (where Wu Zixu and Sun's putative contemporary generals appear) fuelled the scepticism; the bamboo slips proved the Sunzi tradition older than its doubters allowed, while their pairing with Sun Bin's separate work supported the two-Suns solution. Wu's sack of the Chu capital Ying (506 BC) stands as the campaign his tradition claims.
