Equal opportunity

Equal opportunity is a state of fairness in which individuals are treated similarly, unhampered by artificial barriers, prejudices, or preferences, except when particular distinctions can be explicitly justified. The intent is that important jobs, educational places and other goods should go to those "most qualified" — most likely to perform ably — and not be allocated because of arbitrary or irrelevant circumstances such as birth, upbringing, having well-connected relatives or friends, religion, sex, ethnicity, race, caste, or involuntary personal attributes such as disability, age, or sexual orientation.

Concepts

Philosophers distinguish formal equality of opportunity — careers open to talents under fair competition — from substantive or fair equality of opportunity, in which people with the same talent and ambition should have the same prospects regardless of social background. John Rawls placed fair equality of opportunity among the principles of justice, lexically prior to the difference principle. Critics and defenders debate how far the ideal extends: to merely removing legal discrimination, to compensating for unequal schooling and family resources, or to luck-egalitarian neutralisation of brute luck.

In law and policy

Equal opportunity is embodied in anti-discrimination law such as Title VII of the US Civil Rights Act of 1964, enforced by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), and equality legislation in the European Union and United Kingdom. It differs from equality of outcome, which concerns the distribution of final results rather than starting conditions, and is related to concepts including affirmative action, meritocracy and social mobility.
