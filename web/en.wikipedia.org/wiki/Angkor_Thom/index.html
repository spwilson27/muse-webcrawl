Angkor Thom (Khmer: "Great City") was the last and most enduring capital of the Khmer Empire, founded by King Jayavarman VII in the late twelfth century on the right bank of the Siem Reap River. The walled city covers about 9 square kilometres and contains some of the most famous monuments of Angkor, centred on the state temple of the Bayon.

Walls and gates

Angkor Thom is enclosed by a square laterite wall about 8 metres high and 3 kilometres on each side, fronted by a moat 100 metres wide. Five sandstone gates — one in each wall plus the Victory Gate on the eastern royal road — are each crowned with four giant faces and approached by causeways lined with 54 gods on one side and 54 demons on the other, together pulling the body of the serpent Naga in a re-enactment of the Hindu myth of the Churning of the Ocean of Milk.

Monuments

At the geometric centre stands the Bayon, Jayavarman VII's state temple, its towers carved with more than two hundred vast smiling faces. Around the royal plaza stand earlier and later monuments: the Baphuon and Phimeanakas temples, the royal palace enclosure, the Terrace of the Elephants — from which the king reviewed troops and ceremonies — and the Terrace of the Leper King.

History

The city was built over the earlier capital Yasodharapura after the Cham sack of 1177 demonstrated the need for fortification. It remained the Khmer capital, with later kings adding to it, until the court's gradual move south in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries under pressure from Siam and the shifting economics of trade. The city was never wholly abandoned, and Buddhist monks continued to worship in its temples.
