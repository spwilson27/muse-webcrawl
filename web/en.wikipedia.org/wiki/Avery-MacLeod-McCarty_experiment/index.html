Avery–MacLeod–McCarty Experiment

The Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment was a scientific experiment reported in 1944 by Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty. It demonstrated that DNA is the substance that causes bacterial transformation — the process, first described by Frederick Griffith in 1928, by which a dead virulent strain of pneumococcus bacteria could permanently transform a living harmless strain into a virulent one. The experiment was the first to suggest strongly that DNA, rather than protein, carries genetic information.

Background

Griffith's experiment had shown that some "transforming principle" from heat-killed virulent Streptococcus pneumoniae could change living non-virulent bacteria into the virulent type. At the time, most biologists assumed that proteins, with their greater chemical complexity, must be the carriers of heredity.

The experiment

Working at the Rockefeller Institute, Avery and his colleagues purified the transforming substance from large quantities of bacteria. They removed proteins with proteases, carbohydrates with other enzymes, and RNA with ribonuclease, in each case finding that transformation still occurred. When they applied an enzyme that destroys DNA (deoxyribonuclease), transformation was abolished. Chemical analysis showed the active material had the nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratio expected of DNA and not of protein.

Impact

The results were initially received with scepticism, since many scientists believed their DNA preparation might still contain protein contaminants. Nevertheless, the work paved the way for the Hershey–Chase experiment of 1952 and the determination of the structure of DNA by Watson and Crick in 1953, which together established DNA as the molecule of heredity.
