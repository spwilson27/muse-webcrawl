Lucian Freud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Lucian Freud's portrait of a reclining nude, painted in thick impasto in his studio]
Lucian Freud's portrait of a reclining nude, painted in thick impasto in his studio

Lucian Michael Freud (8 December 1922 – 20 July 2011) was a German-British painter and draughtsman, specialising in figurative art. The grandson of Sigmund Freud, he fled Nazi Germany for London in 1933 and became the leading figure of the 'School of London', painting unflinching, heavily impastoed portraits and nudes of people he knew — sitters enduring months of daily sessions — in a career spanning seven decades.

Career

Freud exhibited in the 1940s with precise, almost surreal early work (Girl with a Kitten, 1947); from the 1950s, standing to paint with coarse hogshair brushes, he developed the fleshy, sculptural style of his maturity. Largely overlooked by the conceptual mainstream, he was championed by the critic Robert Hughes ('the greatest living realist painter') and late in life commanded record prices and museum retrospectives in London, Paris and New York.

Sitters and works

His models were family (he acknowledged 14 children; estimates run higher), lovers, fellow artists — Francis Bacon was a close friend and early subject; later came David Hockney and Kate Moss — and the benefits supervisor Sue Tilley, whose Benefits Supervisor Sleeping (1995) sold in 2008 for US$33.6 million, then a record for a living artist. He painted the Queen (2001) to polarised reviews, and his self-portraits chart his own ageing unsparingly.

Style and legacy

Freud insisted his work was autobiographical — 'the people in my pictures are there because I want them there' — and that paint must work as flesh. His revival of intense observational figure painting influenced generations, even as critics debated the ethics of his studio power dynamics.
