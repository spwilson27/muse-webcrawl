Thyroid-stimulating hormone

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (also known as thyrotropin, thyrotropic hormone, or abbreviated TSH) is a pituitary hormone that stimulates the thyroid gland to produce thyroxine (T4), and then triiodothyronine (T3), which stimulates metabolism. TSH is a glycoprotein hormone produced by thyrotrope cells in the anterior pituitary gland, and it regulates the endocrine function of the thyroid.

Physiology

TSH release is stimulated by thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus and inhibited by negative feedback from circulating thyroid hormones. TSH binds its receptor on thyroid follicular cells, increasing iodide uptake, synthesis and release of T4 and T3, and growth of the gland.

Diagnostics

Measurement of serum TSH is the standard first-line test of thyroid function because TSH responds logarithmically to small changes in thyroid hormone levels. A high TSH with low T4 indicates primary hypothyroidism; a suppressed TSH with high T4/T3 indicates hyperthyroidism, as in Graves' disease, in which stimulating antibodies activate the TSH receptor. Subclinical hypothyroidism is defined by a raised TSH with normal T4. TSH assays are also used in newborn screening for congenital hypothyroidism and in monitoring levothyroxine replacement.

Structure

TSH is a heterodimer: a common alpha subunit shared with LH, FSH and hCG, and a specific beta subunit that confers biological specificity.
