Charles Martin Hall

Charles Martin Hall (December 6, 1863 – December 27, 1914) was an American inventor, businessman and chemist. He is best known for inventing, in 1886, an inexpensive method for producing aluminium by electrolysis, independently developed at the same time by Paul Héroult in France and known as the Hall–Héroult process. The process transformed aluminium from a precious metal into a cheap, widely used material, and Hall went on to co-found the company that became Alcoa.

Early life and invention

Hall was born in Thompson, Ohio, and graduated from Oberlin College in 1885. Encouraged by his chemistry professor Frank Fanning Jewett — who had studied under Friedrich Wöhler, the first to isolate aluminium — Hall set out to find an economical way to extract the metal from its ore. Working in a woodshed behind his family home, on February 23, 1886, he passed an electric current through a solution of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite and produced beads of metallic aluminium.

Alcoa

After patent disputes and initial difficulty attracting backing, Hall and partners founded the Pittsburgh Reduction Company in 1888, which began commercial production in Pittsburgh. The company later became the Aluminum Company of America (Alcoa). As hydroelectric power became available at Niagara Falls and elsewhere, the process drove the price of aluminium down dramatically.

Legacy

Hall left much of his fortune to Oberlin College. He received the Perkin Medal in 1911. The Hall–Héroult process remains the basis of virtually all primary aluminium production worldwide.
