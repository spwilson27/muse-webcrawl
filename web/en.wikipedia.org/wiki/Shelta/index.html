Shelta (also called Gammon or, by its speakers, Cant) is a language traditionally spoken by Irish Travellers. It is a mixed language whose grammar is chiefly based on English (and Hiberno-English) while much of its vocabulary is derived from Irish, often disguised by systematic transformations such as reversing syllables, swapping sounds and adding prefixes or suffixes. Shelta functions partly as a cryptolect — a means of private communication within the community.

Origins

Scholars debate Shelta's age and formation. It is commonly thought to have arisen when Irish-speaking Travellers shifted to English over the 18th and 19th centuries, retaining and disguising Irish-derived vocabulary. Some vocabulary may be older, possibly drawing on the secretive bardic and monastic word-play traditions of medieval Ireland. It was first documented for outside scholarship in the late 19th century, notably by the linguist John Sampson and the folklorist Charles Godfrey Leland.

Features

In Shelta, words of Irish origin are obscured: for example doris "door" from Irish doras with metathesis, and lackin "girl, woman" from cailín. Place names include Deri for Dublin. Word counts recorded by researchers run to a few thousand items, though active fluency has declined among younger Travellers.

Status

Shelta is considered endangered. It received attention in Irish cultural policy following the formal recognition of Irish Travellers as an ethnic group in 2017, and community-led efforts promote its teaching and documentation.
