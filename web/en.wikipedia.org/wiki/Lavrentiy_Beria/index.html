Lavrentiy Beria

Lavrentiy Pavlovich Beria (29 March 1899 – 23 December 1953) was a Soviet politician of Georgian origin, Marshal of the Soviet Union, and head of the Soviet secret police (the NKVD) under Joseph Stalin during the Second World War. He was one of the most powerful and feared figures of the Stalin era, directing mass repression in the Caucasus and beyond, overseeing the deportations of whole nationalities, and administering both the Gulag labour-camp system and the Soviet atomic bomb project. After Stalin's death in March 1953, Beria briefly became one of the ruling troika and launched surprising liberalising moves, but he was arrested in June 1953 by his rivals, tried in secret, and executed.

Rise in the Caucasus

Born to a Mingrelian peasant family in Georgia, Beria studied in Baku and joined the Bolsheviks in 1917. He entered the Cheka, the Soviet secret police, and rose through the security apparatus of the Transcaucasian republics, crushing opposition and overseeing purges in Georgia and Azerbaijan. As party chief of Georgia and then of the Transcaucasian federation in the 1930s, he combined terror with energetic economic administration and cultivated Stalin's favour, even writing a falsified history of Bolshevism in the Caucasus that glorified the young Stalin.

NKVD chief

In 1938 Stalin brought Beria to Moscow, first as deputy to Nikolai Yezhov and then, from November–December 1938, as head of the NKVD. Beria wound down the most chaotic phase of the Great Terror — a period later termed the "Beria thaw" — while continuing large-scale repression, including the 1940 Katyn massacre of Polish officers, conducted on his written proposal to Stalin. During the war he supervised the deportation of entire ethnic groups accused of collaboration (Volga Germans, Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars, and others) and ran the Gulag's wartime economy. After the war he directed the Soviet atomic bomb project, which exploded its first device in 1949.

After Stalin and fall

Following Stalin's death on 5 March 1953, Beria merged the security ministries under himself and pushed through an amnesty, moves to curb the Gulag, and a more liberal nationality policy, while apparently positioning himself for supreme power. His colleagues Nikita Khrushchev and Georgy Malenkov, fearing him, organised his arrest on 26 June 1953 with the help of Marshal Zhukov and other officers. Beria was secretly tried by a special court under Marshal Konev on charges including treason and terrorism, and shot on 23 December 1953, officially in the bunker of the Moscow Military District headquarters.

Legacy

Beria's name remains synonymous with the Stalinist terror apparatus. Historians continue to debate the sincerity of his post-Stalin reforms and the extent of the personal crimes — including documented accusations of sexual violence — attributed to him. His removal began the subordination of the secret police to the Communist Party that characterised the later Soviet Union.
