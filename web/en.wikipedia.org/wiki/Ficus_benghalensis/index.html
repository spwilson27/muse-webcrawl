Ficus benghalensis, commonly known as the banyan, banyan fig, and Indian banyan, is a tree native to the Indian subcontinent. It is famous for its unusual growth habit: branches send down aerial prop roots that thicken into woody trunks, allowing a single tree to spread over a vast area and appear as a small forest. It is the national tree of India.

Description

The banyan begins life as an epiphyte, germinating in the crevice of a host tree or building. As it grows, it produces aerial roots that descend to the ground and form supplementary trunks, eventually strangling the host. Mature trees reach 30 metres in height, with leaves 10–20 cm long, ovate and leathery. Like other figs, it bears small syconia (figs) that ripen red and are pollinated by a species-specific fig wasp.

Ecology

Banyans are keystone species in their habitats: their fruit feeds birds, bats, monkeys, and many other animals, often during seasons when little else is available. The wide canopy also provides shade and shelter for a whole community of organisms.

Notable specimens

The Great Banyan in the Acharya Jagadish Chandra Bose Indian Botanic Garden, Howrah, near Kolkata, is over 250 years old and covers roughly 1.9 hectares with thousands of prop roots; after its main trunk was removed in 1925 following cyclone damage, the tree survived as a clonal colony. Thimmamma Marrimanu in Andhra Pradesh is among the largest trees in the world by canopy area, covering about 5 hectares.

Cultural significance

The banyan is sacred in Hinduism, associated with longevity and knowledge; the Buddha is traditionally said to have attained enlightenment beneath a related fig, the Bodhi tree (Ficus religiosa). Village councils in India have long met under banyans, and the tree's name is thought to derive from banias — Gujarati merchants who traded in its shade.
