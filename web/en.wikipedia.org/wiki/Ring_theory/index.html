Ring theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, ring theory is the study of rings — algebraic structures in which addition and multiplication are defined and have similar properties to those of the integers. Ring theory studies the structure of rings, their representations, or modules, special classes of rings (group rings, division rings, universal enveloping algebras), as well as applications including number theory and algebraic geometry.

Definitions

A ring is a set equipped with two binary operations, addition and multiplication, such that the set is an abelian group under addition, multiplication is associative, and the distributive laws hold. Most modern authors also require a multiplicative identity (1 ≠ 0). A ring in which multiplication is commutative is a commutative ring; central examples are the integers ℤ, polynomial rings, and rings of algebraic integers.

Key concepts

  • Ideals — subsets absorbing multiplication; kernels of homomorphisms and the building blocks of quotient rings.
  • Factorization — prime and irreducible elements; unique factorization domains and their failures in number rings, which historically motivated ideal theory.
  • Modules — the representation theory of rings, generalizing vector spaces and abelian groups.
  • Special classes — fields, integral domains, principal ideal domains, Euclidean domains, Noetherian rings, Artinian rings, local rings and division rings.

History

Ring theory grew out of 19th-century work on algebraic number theory and invariant theory. Richard Dedekind introduced ideals to restore unique factorization in rings of algebraic integers; David Hilbert and later Emmy Noether placed the subject on an axiomatic footing — Noether's work in the 1920s is regarded as the foundation of modern commutative algebra. Noncommutative ring theory developed through the study of division algebras and culminated in results such as the Artin–Wedderburn theorem classifying semisimple rings.

Applications

Commutative ring theory is the algebraic language of algebraic geometry (the spectrum of a ring), while noncommutative rings appear in representation theory, operator algebras and mathematical physics.
