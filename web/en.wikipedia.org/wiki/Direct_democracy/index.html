Direct democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Direct democracy or pure democracy is a form of democracy in which the electorate decides on policy initiatives without elected representatives as proxies. This differs from the majority of currently established democracies, which are representative democracies. In direct democracy, citizens vote on laws and policies themselves, rather than choosing representatives to decide on their behalf.

History

The classical example is Athenian democracy of the 5th century BC, where adult male citizens assembled to legislate, served on juries and filled offices by lot (sortition). Direct institutions survived in Swiss cantonal assemblies (Landsgemeinden) and New England town meetings. In the modern era, the term usually refers to instruments within representative systems: referendums called by authorities, citizen-initiated initiatives and propositions (as in California and other US states), and recall elections letting voters remove officials mid-term. Switzerland combines all of these with frequent national votes on constitutional amendments, treaties and laws.

Arguments

Supporters claim direct democracy expresses popular sovereignty most faithfully, legitimises decisions and educates citizens. Critics warn of majority tyranny, low-information voting, manipulation by moneyed campaigns and demagogues, and voter fatigue; deliberative democrats propose citizens' assemblies chosen by lot as a middle way — experiments held in Ireland, France and elsewhere on constitutional and climate questions.
