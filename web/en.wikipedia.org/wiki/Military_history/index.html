Military history is the study of armed conflict in the past: its causes, conduct, consequences and the institutions that wage it. One of the oldest branches of historical writing — Thucydides' account of the Peloponnesian War remains a model — it ranges from the reconstruction of individual battles to the analysis of strategy, logistics, military technology, intelligence and the relationship between war and society.

Development of the discipline

Ancient and medieval writers such as Herodotus, Thucydides, Julius Caesar and Vegetius combined narrative with lessons for commanders; Sun Tzu's The Art of War distilled strategy into principles still studied today. In the nineteenth century, staff colleges and theorists such as Carl von Clausewitz (On War, 1832) and Antoine-Henri Jomini professionalised the field, and states began compiling official histories of their wars. The field expanded after 1945 with archival access and, later, the "war and society" turn, which brought in conscripts' experiences, gender, economics and culture.

Approaches

Military historians distinguish levels of war — tactics, operations, strategy and policy — and debate how military change occurs, as in the "military revolution" thesis concerning early modern Europe's gunpowder armies and state growth. The "face of battle" approach pioneered by John Keegan shifted attention from commanders' intentions to soldiers' experience. Critics of older, "drum and trumpet" history charged it with ignoring politics and society; contemporary practice typically integrates operational narrative with social and cultural context.
