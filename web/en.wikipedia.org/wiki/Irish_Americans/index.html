Irish Americans are citizens of the United States who have Irish ancestry, in whole or in part. Around 31 million Americans — close to one in ten — report Irish ancestry in census surveys, making Irish one of the largest reported ancestries in the country. The group comprises two historically distinct streams: Protestant "Scotch-Irish" (Ulster Scots) who arrived mainly in the 18th century, and the predominantly Catholic Irish who emigrated in vast numbers during and after the Great Famine of 1845–1852.

Immigration history

Irish emigrants arrived in America from the colonial period onward; Ulster Presbyterians settled the Appalachian backcountry and supplied numerous soldiers and frontiersmen. The great Catholic migration began in the 1820s canal- and railroad-building era and surged during the Famine, when more than a million Irish fled starvation and disease. Famine immigrants concentrated in northeastern port cities — Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore — and later Chicago, working as labourers, domestic servants, miners and factory hands. They faced virulent nativist hostility, epitomised by the Know-Nothing movement of the 1850s and "No Irish Need Apply" discrimination.

Rise in American life

Through the Catholic parish, the Democratic Party's urban political machines (such as New York's Tammany Hall), trade unions, the police and fire services, and the Church's schools, Irish Americans built institutions that carried them into the middle class by the early 20th century. Irish surnames came to dominate urban politics, the labour movement and the American Catholic hierarchy. John F. Kennedy's election in 1960 — a great-grandson of Famine immigrants — is often taken as the symbolic culmination of Irish-American acceptance; more than twenty US presidents have claimed some Irish ancestry.

Culture

Irish-American identity is celebrated annually on Saint Patrick's Day, whose great parades in New York (from 1762) and Boston are American institutions, and through Irish music, dance, pubs, sports clubs of the Gaelic Athletic Association, and organisations such as the Ancient Order of Hibernians. Irish-American writers — from Eugene O'Neill and F. Scott Fitzgerald to Mary Higgins Clark — occupy a central place in American letters. Relations with Ireland remain close: Irish-American lobbying influenced US policy during the Northern Ireland peace process, and Ireland is a favoured destination for American heritage tourism.

Demographics

Irish ancestry is reported most heavily in New England and the Mid-Atlantic; Massachusetts has the highest proportion of residents claiming Irish ancestry of any state. While early settlement was urban, Irish Americans are now distributed throughout the suburbs and across the country, and their rates of education and income are at or above national averages.
