The Line of Actual Control (LAC) is a notional demarcation line that separates Indian-controlled territory from Chinese-controlled territory in the Sino-Indian border dispute. The term was first used by Chinese premier Zhou Enlai in 1959 and refers to the de facto border resulting from the Sino-Indian War of 1962. It runs for about 3,488 km, although India and China disagree about its alignment in many places, and crucially, the two sides have never exchanged agreed maps of their respective perceptions of the entire line, except in the less-disputed middle sector.

Sectors and disputes

In the western sector, China controls Aksai Chin (about 38,000 km²), which India claims as part of Ladakh; the disputed boundary derives from rival British-era lines (Johnson and Macartney–MacDonald). In the middle sector (Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand), disputes are relatively small. In the eastern sector, China claims about 90,000 km² administered by India as Arunachal Pradesh, asserting that the 1914 McMahon Line — agreed between British India and Tibet at Simla — is invalid. China refers to the area as "South Tibet".

Agreements and incidents

The 1993 Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquility committed both sides to respect the LAC pending a final settlement, followed by confidence-building agreements in 1996, 2005 and 2013 (the Border Defence Cooperation Agreement). Nevertheless, patrols regularly face off where perceptions differ — notably at Depsang (2013), Chumar (2014) and Doklam plateau (2017, involving Bhutan). In June 2020, a violent clash in the Galwan Valley in Ladakh — fought with clubs and stones because firearms are restricted by agreement — killed 20 Indian soldiers and at least four Chinese soldiers, the first fatalities on the border since 1975. Massive troop deployments followed; partial disengagements were negotiated between 2021 and 2024, and a patrolling arrangement announced in October 2024 preceded a Modi–Xi meeting and gradual normalisation.
