History of San Francisco

San Francisco
Founded1776 (Mission San Francisco de Asís)

The history of San Francisco begins with the Ohlone peoples, the Spanish Presidio and Mission founded in 1776, and the explosive growth of the California Gold Rush, which turned the small settlement of Yerba Buena into one of America's great ports. Rebuilt after the 1906 earthquake and fire, the city became a centre of finance, counterculture and technology.

San Francisco occupies the tip of a peninsula between the Pacific Ocean and San Francisco Bay.

Spanish and Mexican eras

The area was inhabited by the Yelamu group of the Ohlone people. Europeans first sighted the bay in 1769 with the Portolá expedition. In 1776 Spain founded the Presidio of San Francisco and Mission San Francisco de Asís (Mission Dolores). After Mexican independence in 1821 the missions were secularised, and the small port settlement of Yerba Buena grew beside the anchorage. United States forces seized the town in 1846 during the Mexican–American War, and it was renamed San Francisco in 1847.

Gold Rush boom

The discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill in January 1848 triggered the Gold Rush. San Francisco's population exploded from about 1,000 in 1848 to some 25,000 by the end of 1849, as "forty-niners" arrived by sea and land. The city became the main supply port for the gold fields, spawning banks such as Wells Fargo, merchants like Levi Strauss, and a notorious Barbary Coast waterfront.

Late 19th century

The completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869, built with the labour of many Chinese immigrants, tied the city to the eastern United States. Cable cars, invented by Andrew Smith Hallidie in 1873, climbed its hills. Anti-Chinese agitation culminated in the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882. The city hosted the Panama–Pacific International Exposition in 1915.

1906 earthquake and rebuilding

On 18 April 1906 a major earthquake on the San Andreas Fault struck the city; the ensuing fires burned for days and destroyed over 80 per cent of the city, killing an estimated 3,000 people. San Francisco rebuilt rapidly. In the 1930s the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge (1936) and the Golden Gate Bridge (1937) were completed.

Postwar era

The United Nations Charter was signed in San Francisco in 1945. In the 1960s the Haight-Ashbury district became the epicentre of the hippie counterculture and the 1967 Summer of Love. The Castro district became a centre of the gay rights movement under Harvey Milk, assassinated in 1978. The 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake caused major damage. From the 1990s the dot-com boom and later the social-media era transformed the city's economy, driving housing costs to among the highest in the United States.
