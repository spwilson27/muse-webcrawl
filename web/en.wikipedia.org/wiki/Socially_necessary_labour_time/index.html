Socially necessary labour time

Socially necessary labour time is a central concept in Karl Marx's labour theory of value, defined in the first chapter of Capital (1867) as the labour time required to produce a commodity "under the conditions of production normal for a given society and with the average degree of skill and intensity of labour prevalent in that society".

Meaning

The value of a commodity is determined not by the time any individual producer takes, but by this social average. A slow or inefficient producer cannot claim extra value for extra hours; conversely, innovations that cut production time below the social average bring temporary extra profit until competitors adopt them, redefining what is socially necessary. Marx used the concept to explain competition, technical change and the equalisation of values across a market without reference to individual effort.

Interpretation and criticism

Scholars debate the exact relation between socially necessary labour time, market prices and the transformation problem, and how the definition changed in volume III of Capital, where demand conditions also enter. Mainstream economics rejects labour-embodied value in favour of marginal utility and supply-and-demand pricing, but the concept remains foundational in Marxian economics and in discussions of productivity and working time.
