Taste bud

LocationTongue, soft palate, epiglottis, esophagus
Cells per bud50–100 receptor and support cells
SystemGustatory system

Taste buds are clusters of taste receptor cells, also known as gustatory cells, located mainly on the tongue. The taste receptors are contained within the bumps on the tongue called papillae, and are also found on the roof of the mouth (soft palate), the epiglottis, the pharynx and the upper part of the esophagus. A human tongue contains roughly 2,000–8,000 taste buds, the number declining with age.

Structure

Each taste bud is an onion-shaped structure about 50–60 micrometres across, embedded in the epithelium and opening to the surface through a small taste pore. Within the bud are several cell types: Type I (glial-like support) cells, Type II receptor cells carrying G-protein-coupled receptors for sweet, bitter and umami compounds, Type III presynaptic cells responsive to sour stimuli, and basal cells that act as progenitors. Receptor cells bear microvilli projecting into the taste pore, where dissolved food chemicals make contact.

Transduction

Salty taste is mediated mainly by sodium channels; sour taste by proton channels such as OTOP1 in Type III cells. Sweet, bitter and umami stimuli bind receptors on Type II cells, triggering release of ATP, which activates afferent nerve fibers. Taste bud cells are continuously renewed from basal progenitors, with an average lifespan of about ten days.

Innervation

The anterior two-thirds of the tongue is innervated by the chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve (VII); the posterior third by the glossopharyngeal nerve (IX); buds on the epiglottis and palate by the vagus (X) and greater petrosal branches respectively. Fibers relay in the nucleus of the solitary tract before projecting to the thalamus and gustatory cortex.
