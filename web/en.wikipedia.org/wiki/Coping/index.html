Coping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For other uses, see Coping (disambiguation).

In psychology, coping is the conscious expenditure of effort to solve personal and interpersonal problems, and to seek to master, minimise or tolerate stress and conflict. The psychological coping mechanisms are commonly termed coping strategies or coping skills. The term was coined by Abraham Maslow in 1954, and the field was developed extensively through the work of Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman, whose transactional model framed stress as an appraisal of demands against perceived resources.

Types of coping strategies

Lazarus and Folkman distinguished two broad functions of coping. Problem-focused coping aims to change the source of stress: planning, seeking information, taking direct action or confronting the problem. Emotion-focused coping aims to regulate the emotional response: seeking social support, reappraisal, acceptance, relaxation, distancing or, in maladaptive forms, avoidance and denial. Later research added meaning-focused coping, in which people draw on beliefs and values to sustain well-being during prolonged adversity.

Strategies are also classified as adaptive or maladaptive. Adaptive coping — such as problem-solving, seeking support and positive reframing — is generally associated with better psychological outcomes, whereas avoidance, rumination, self-blame and substance use as coping are associated with worse outcomes, though the effectiveness of any strategy depends on the controllability of the stressor: emotion-focused strategies tend to work better when the situation cannot be changed.

Appraisal and coping

In the transactional model, primary appraisal evaluates whether an event is a threat, challenge or harm, while secondary appraisal evaluates available coping resources. Coping efforts then feed back into reappraisal. Coping self-efficacy — belief in one's ability to manage demands — is a strong predictor of which strategies are used and how well they work.

Health effects

Chronic reliance on avoidant coping is linked to anxiety, depression and poorer physical health, while active coping and social support buffer the effects of stress on the immune and cardiovascular systems. Interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy explicitly train more flexible repertoires of coping strategies.
