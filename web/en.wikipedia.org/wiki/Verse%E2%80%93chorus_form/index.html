Verse–chorus form

TypeSong structure
ElementsVerse, chorus (refrain), often bridge and pre-chorus
Common inPop, rock, country, R&B

Verse–chorus form is a musical form common in popular music in which verses and a repeating chorus alternate. The chorus — also called the refrain — contains the song's main musical and lyrical motif, usually including its title, and is designed to be its most memorable section, while the verses carry the narrative detail and change their lyrics on each repetition.

Structure

A typical pattern is verse–chorus–verse–chorus, often extended with a bridge (a contrasting middle section), a pre-chorus that builds tension before the chorus, and instrumental sections or a final repeated chorus. In contrast with the older AABA form, where the refrain returns with different verses between repetitions of the same music, verse–chorus songs use distinct music for verse and chorus.

History

The form grew out of blues, gospel and folk practices and became the dominant structure of rock and pop songwriting from the 1960s onward. Songwriters and producers often place the first chorus early — sometimes within the first minute — and some songs open with the chorus itself for immediate impact.
