Mental test

Mental test was a term coined by the American psychologist James McKeen Cattell in his 1890 paper "Mental Tests and Measurements," published in the journal Mind. It came to denote standardised procedures for measuring mental capacities — including intelligence, memory, perception, and reaction time — and helped found the field of psychometrics and psychological testing.

History

Cattell, influenced by Francis Galton's anthropometric programme, proposed a battery of tests measuring sensory discrimination, reaction time, memory, and other basic functions, hoping they would predict intellectual ability. W. Clark Wissler's 1901 correlational study found that such sensorimotor measures correlated poorly with academic performance, undermining the approach. Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon took a different path: their 1905 scale measured higher-level judgement, comprehension, and reasoning in children, and became the ancestor of modern intelligence tests such as the Stanford–Binet and the Wechsler scales.

Development and applications

During the First World War, Robert Yerkes and colleagues developed the Army Alpha and Beta tests to screen over a million American recruits, popularising group mental testing. In the decades that followed, mental tests spread to education, personnel selection, clinical assessment, and neuropsychology. The umbrella term "mental test" gradually fell out of use in favour of more specific terms — psychological test, psychometric test, intelligence test, aptitude test, and neuropsychological assessment.

Criticism

Mental testing has been criticised for cultural bias, for its historical misuse in the eugenics movement and in discriminatory immigration and education policies, and for reducing complex human abilities to single scores. Modern test standards emphasise reliability, validity, fairness, and appropriate use.
