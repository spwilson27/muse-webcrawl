Captive breeding

Captive breeding, also known as captive propagation, is the process of keeping and breeding plants or animals in controlled environments, such as zoos, aquaria, botanical gardens, and wildlife reserves, in order to maintain or restore populations. In conservation, captive breeding is used to produce individuals for reintroduction into the wild, to serve as an insurance population against extinction, and for research and public education. Programmes are coordinated through regional and global studbooks and plans such as the Association of Zoos and Aquariums' Species Survival Plans (SSP) and the European Endangered Species Programmes (EEP).

Methods and challenges

Effective captive breeding requires managing small populations to preserve genetic diversity: pedigree records (studbooks) are used to pair individuals so as to minimise inbreeding and mean kinship, and populations are exchanged between institutions. Other challenges include adaptation to captivity (genetic and behavioural), disease transmission between captive and wild populations, surplus animals, the cost and space limits of keeping large populations long-term, and the difficulty of teaching survival skills — finding food, avoiding predators — to animals destined for release. Techniques such as artificial insemination, cryopreservation of gametes ("frozen zoos"), cross-fostering, and puppet-rearing of chicks are used to address these problems.

Notable successes

  • California condor — reduced to 27 individuals by 1987, all taken into captivity; breeding at San Diego and Los Angeles zoos enabled reintroductions in California, Arizona, and Baja California, with the wild population passing 300 in the 2020s.
  • Arabian oryx — extinct in the wild by 1972; captive herds ("Operation Oryx", begun at Phoenix Zoo in 1962) supplied animals for reintroduction to Oman and other states; the first species to move from Extinct in the Wild back to Vulnerable.
  • Black-footed ferret — rediscovered in Wyoming in 1981; the last 18 animals formed the basis of a captive programme whose offspring have been released at multiple sites.
  • Przewalski's horse — bred from a small founder group in zoos and reintroduced to Mongolia from the 1990s.
  • Golden lion tamarin — zoo-bred animals from an international programme were reintroduced to Brazilian Atlantic forest reserves from 1984.
  • Giant panda — breeding breakthroughs at Chinese centres such as Chengdu and Wolong ended reliance on wild capture and support reintroduction trials.

Criticism and limitations

Critics note that only a fraction of threatened species can be kept in captivity, that captive populations can lose wild adaptations over generations, and that resources might be better spent protecting habitat. Conservation bodies such as the IUCN therefore treat captive breeding as one tool within integrated in situ and ex situ strategies, governed by the "One Plan" approach that manages wild and captive populations together.
