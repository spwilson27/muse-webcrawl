Malaria prophylaxis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Malaria prophylaxis is the preventive treatment of malaria, most commonly with antimalarial medication, intended to prevent the disease in travellers, military personnel, pregnant women and populations in endemic regions. Several medications are commonly used, including chloroquine, mefloquine, doxycycline, and the combination atovaquone/proguanil, the choice depending on local patterns of drug resistance and on the traveller's tolerance of side effects.

Medication regimens

Prophylactic drugs act either on the liver stage of the parasite (causal prophylaxis, e.g. atovaquone/proguanil and primaquine) or on the blood stages (suppressive prophylaxis, e.g. chloroquine, mefloquine, doxycycline). Regimens differ in when they must be started before travel and continued after leaving an endemic area: doxycycline and atovaquone/proguanil begin one or two days before travel, while chloroquine and mefloquine are usually started one to several weeks earlier; most must be continued for four weeks after return, atovaquone/proguanil for only seven days. No regimen is 100% effective, and febrile illness after travel to an endemic area must always be evaluated for malaria.

Non-drug measures and other strategies

Bite prevention — insecticide-treated bed nets, repellents such as DEET, protective clothing and screened accommodation — is a fundamental complement to medication. For resident populations, strategies include intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy and in children (IPTp/IPTc, now perennial malaria chemoprevention) and seasonal malaria chemoprevention in the Sahel. The RTS,S and R21 malaria vaccines are recommended by the WHO for children in endemic regions but are not a substitute for traveller chemoprophylaxis.
