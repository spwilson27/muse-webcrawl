Divination (from Latin divinare, "to foresee, to be inspired by a god") is the attempt to gain insight into a question or situation by means of an occultic, standardised process or ritual, or to foretell future events through signs and omens believed to encode messages from gods, spirits or fate. It has been practised in virtually every known culture, from Mesopotamian extispicy and Chinese oracle-bone inscriptions to Roman augury, West African Ifá, and modern tarot reading.

Divination differs from fortune-telling mainly in its ritual formality and integration into religious and state institutions — in many societies the diviner was a priestly official whose readings decided wars, marriages, harvests and laws.

History and traditions

In Mesopotamia, haruspicy — reading the livers of sacrificed sheep against clay liver models — guided royal decisions for two millennia and passed to the Etruscans and Rome. In Shang-dynasty China (c. 1200 BC), kings inscribed questions on ox bones and turtle plastrons, heated them, and read the cracks; these oracle bones preserve the earliest substantial Chinese writing. The Chinese I Ching (Book of Changes), consulted by casting yarrow stalks or coins, became a classic of Confucian and Daoist thought. Greek religion centred on oracles — Delphi's Pythia above all — while Rome institutionalised augury from bird flight and the inspection of sacred chickens before battles and elections. In Yoruba culture, Ifá divination with palm nuts and a vast corpus of memorised verses is recognised by UNESCO as intangible heritage.

Methods

Thousands of methods are catalogued, usually grouped as:

  • Sortilege / cleromancy — casting lots, bones, runes, cards (cartomancy, tarot) or sticks.
  • Observation of nature — augury (birds), astrology (celestial configurations), oneiromancy (dreams), pyromancy and hydromancy.
  • Scrying — gazing into reflective surfaces, crystal balls, mirrors or water.
  • Body reading — palmistry, physiognomy.
  • Inspired utterance — prophecy, mediumship, possession.
  • Geomancy and its derivatives — figures generated from random marks, spread across West Africa, the Islamic world and medieval Europe.

Attitudes of religions

Judaism and Islam formally prohibit most divination (Deuteronomy condemns soothsaying; Qur'anic teaching rejects fortune-telling) while historically tolerating or absorbing some practices; the Hebrew Bible itself records the Urim and Thummim and the casting of lots. Christianity inherited the prohibition — Augustine and Aquinas classed divination as superstition or worse — yet astrology flourished at medieval and Renaissance courts. Many indigenous and East Asian traditions continue divination as a normal part of religious life.

Scientific assessment

Controlled tests have found no predictive power in astrology, tarot or similar methods beyond chance; psychologists attribute their apparent accuracy to the Barnum effect, cold reading, confirmation bias and the human drive to find patterns. Anthropologists, however, study divination seriously as a decision-making and social technology — randomised outcomes can break deadlocks, spread risk and legitimise choices, as anthropologist Omar Khayyam Moore argued for Naskapi scapulimancy randomising hunting directions.
