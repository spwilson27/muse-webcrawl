Salomon van Ruysdael (c. 1600/1603 – buried 3 November 1670) was a Dutch painter of the Golden Age, one of the pioneers of the naturalistic Dutch landscape and the inventor, with Jan van Goyen and Pieter de Molijn, of the "tonal" river landscape. He was the uncle and an early influence of the more famous Jacob van Ruisdael.

Biography

He was born Salomon de Goyer in Naarden, the son of a prosperous furniture maker who moved the family to Haarlem. Around the 1620s he adopted the surname Ruysdael, after the castle of Ruysdael (Ruisdael) near Blaricum, from where the family originated; his brother Isaack, also a frame-maker and painter's father, followed suit, fathering Jacob van Ruisdael. Salomon became a member of the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1623 and served as an officer of the guild. He was a man of substance, dealing in property and other ventures alongside painting, and he was also involved in the manufacture of a novel type of dye-house or bleaching equipment he tried to patent. He died wealthy in 1670 and was buried in St. Bavo's Church, Haarlem.

Work

Ruysdael's earliest dated paintings, from 1626–27, are winter scenes and dune landscapes in the manner of Esaias van de Velde. From about 1630–31 he and Van Goyen developed the tonal style: broad, low-horizon river and estuary views built up in muted greys, greens, and browns with rapid, sketchy brushwork, in which water and sky occupy most of the picture surface and minute observations of boats, ferries, fishermen, and reflections animate the scene. His River Landscape with a Ferry (1639) in the Rijksmuseum is a celebrated example. In his later work of the 1650s and 1660s his palette grew richer and his compositions more classical, and he turned also to estuaries and seascapes, hunting still lifes, and a few winter scenes. More than 400 paintings are attributed to him, though attributions are complicated by the similarity of his manner to Van Goyen's and by his monogram, sometimes misread by later collectors.
