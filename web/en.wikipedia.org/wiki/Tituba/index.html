Tituba was an enslaved woman, owned by Samuel Parris of Salem Village, in the Massachusetts Bay Colony. She was one of the first three persons to be accused of practicing witchcraft during the Salem witch trials, which took place in 1692. She was brought from the Caribbean — likely Barbados — by Parris, who had lived there as a merchant before becoming a minister. Her ethnic background is debated by historians; contemporary records variously described her as "Indian," and later popular retellings often, probably erroneously, portrayed her as of African descent.

Accusation and confession

When Parris's daughter Betty and niece Abigail Williams began exhibiting strange fits in early 1692, suspicion fell on Tituba, who (according to later accounts) had told the girls folk stories and possibly performed a folk-magic counter-spell. Along with Sarah Good and Sarah Osborne, she was arrested on 29 February 1692. Under examination — and, by some accounts, after being beaten by Parris — Tituba gave an elaborate confession, describing pacts with the Devil, a tall man from Boston, black dogs, red cats, and flights on poles. Her confession, unlike the denials of others, validated the court's proceedings and helped fuel the ensuing witch hunt, in which 20 people were executed.

Later life

Tituba remained in jail for more than a year; a grand jury declined to indict her in May 1693. Because Parris refused to pay her jail fees, she was sold to an unknown party to cover the costs. Nothing further is known of her life. She has become a major figure in retellings of the Salem story, notably in Arthur Miller's play The Crucible (1953), in which she is portrayed as a Barbadian conjure woman, and in Maryse Condé's novel I, Tituba, Black Witch of Salem (1986).
