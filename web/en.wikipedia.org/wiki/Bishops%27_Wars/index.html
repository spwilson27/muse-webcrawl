Bishops' Wars
Date: 1639–1640
Location: Scotland and northern England
Belligerents: Scottish Covenanters vs Charles I of England
Result: Covenanter victory; Treaty of London (1641)
Consequence: recall of the English Parliament, prelude to the English Civil War

The Bishops' Wars (Latin: Bellum Episcopale) were two conflicts in 1639 and 1640 between Scotland and England, arising from King Charles I's attempt to impose Anglican-style reforms, including bishops and a new prayer book, on the Presbyterian Church of Scotland. The Scots responded with the National Covenant of 1638, abolished episcopacy, and raised an army under Alexander Leslie.

First Bishops' War (1639)

Charles assembled an army at York but, short of money and facing a well-organized Covenanter force, avoided battle. The Pacification of Berwick in June 1639 ended the first war inconclusively, with both sides agreeing to disband and to settle the dispute in a Scottish assembly and parliament — which promptly reaffirmed the abolition of bishops.

Second Bishops' War (1640)

Charles recalled the English Parliament — the Short Parliament — to raise funds, but dissolved it when it pressed grievances. The Scots invaded England, routed the king's forces at the Battle of Newburn in August 1640, and occupied Newcastle. By the Treaty of Ripon and then the Treaty of London (1641), Charles agreed to pay the Scots an indemnity and confirm their religious settlement. Needing money, he was forced to summon the Long Parliament, whose confrontations with the crown led directly to the English Civil War.
