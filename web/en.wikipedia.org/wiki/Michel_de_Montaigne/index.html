Michel de Montaigne

Michel Eyquem, Sieur de Montaigne (28 February 1533 – 13 September 1592), also known as the Lord of Montaigne, was one of the most significant philosophers of the French Renaissance. He is known for popularising the essay as a literary genre. His work is noted for its merging of casual anecdotes and autobiography with serious intellectual insight. Montaigne had a direct influence on numerous Western writers, and his massive volume Essais (in English, "Attempts" or "Trials") contains some of the most influential essays ever written.

Life

Montaigne was born at the family château near Bordeaux, in southwestern France, to a wealthy family of merchant descent recently ennobled. His father had him raised by a German tutor speaking only Latin, so that Latin became his first language. He studied law, served as a counsellor in the Bordeaux parlement, and formed his great friendship with the humanist Étienne de La Boétie, whose early death in 1563 profoundly marked him. Devastated by grief and by the French Wars of Religion then ravaging the country, he retired in 1571 to a tower of his château, where he began writing the Essais. He later travelled through Germany, Switzerland and Italy (1580–81), served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux, and acted as an occasional mediator between Henry III and Henry of Navarre (the future Henry IV).

The Essays

Published in stages (books I and II in 1580, book III in 1588, with endless manuscript additions until his death), the Essais take their author himself as their subject: "I am myself the matter of my book." Their 107 chapters range from "Of cannibals" — a strikingly relativist reflection on Brazilian peoples and European barbarity — to "Of experience", and from thumbs, smells and coaches to custom, education and repentance. Montaigne's sceptical motto, Que sçay-je?, is stamped on the beams of his study tower, alongside quotations from Sextus Empiricus, Lucretius and the Stoics.

Influence

Montaigne essentially created the modern essay and pioneered introspective autobiography. Shakespeare read him in John Florio's 1603 English translation; Descartes, Pascal, Rousseau, Emerson and Nietzsche all engaged deeply with his work. His humane scepticism and hatred of cruelty and fanaticism have made the Essais a perennial classic.
