Sonnet 18

Sonnet 18 is one of the best-known of the 154 sonnets written by English playwright and poet William Shakespeare. In the sonnet, the speaker asks whether he should compare the beloved to a summer's day, concluding that the beloved is more lovely and more temperate, and that poetry itself will preserve the beloved's beauty against the passage of time and death.

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer's lease hath all too short a date:
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
And often is his gold complexion dimm'd;
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd;
But thy eternal summer shall not fade
Nor lose possession of that fair thou owest;
Nor shall Death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
When in eternal lines to time thou growest:
So long as men can breathe or eyes can see,
So long lives this and this gives life to thee.

Structure

The poem follows the form of the Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a concluding rhymed couplet, written in iambic pentameter with the rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. It belongs to the "Fair Youth" sequence (Sonnets 1–126) and concludes the opening "procreation" group, turning from urging the youth to have children toward promising him immortality in verse.

Commentary

The first two quatrains catalogue the imperfections of summer — rough winds, brief duration, excessive heat, and inevitable decline. The third quatrain asserts that the beloved's "eternal summer" will not fade, and the couplet declares that the poem itself grants life for as long as it is read. Sonnet 18 is among the most frequently anthologised and quoted lyric poems in English.
