Digital rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital rights are those human rights and legal rights that allow individuals to access, use, create, and publish digital media or to access and use computers, other electronic devices, and telecommunications networks. The concept is particularly related to the protection and realisation of existing rights — such as the right to privacy and freedom of expression — in the context of digital technologies, above all the Internet. Many advocates and international bodies argue that the same rights people have offline must also be protected online.

Scope

Digital rights encompass a wide and contested set of issues:

  • Access — affordable Internet access and accessibility for people with disabilities; broadband increasingly treated as a basic service.
  • Freedom of expression — protection against censorship, content blocking, and Internet shutdowns.
  • Privacy and data protection — control over personal data, limits on surveillance, encryption rights.
  • Net neutrality — equal treatment of data traffic by network operators.
  • Access to knowledge and culture — copyright balance, open access, and the preservation of digital works; the term also borders on the critique of digital rights management (DRM), which can restrict lawful uses.
  • Algorithmic accountability and platform power — due process in automated decisions and content moderation.

Frameworks and law

In 2012 the UN Human Rights Council affirmed that the same rights protected offline must be protected online. Regional instruments include the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights, the GDPR (data protection) and the Digital Services and Digital Markets Acts. Several countries have adopted explicit digital-rights charters — Brazil's Marco Civil da Internet (2014) is a prominent example — and courts have recognised Internet access itself as a fundamental right in some jurisdictions, such as France's 2009 HADOPI decision by the Constitutional Council. Civil-society organisations including the Electronic Frontier Foundation (founded 1990), Access Now, and the Association for Progressive Communications campaign on these issues, and the annual RightsCon conference gathers the digital-rights community.

Debates

Controversies recur over encryption backdoors, state surveillance programmes revealed in the 2010s, "right to be forgotten" rulings, platform liability for user content, and the obligations of private companies that operate the infrastructure of public speech. The balance between protecting users from harm (fraud, harassment, disinformation) and preserving openness remains the central tension of digital-rights policy.
