Autogyro

Autogyro
Also called: Gyrocopter, gyroplane
First flight: 9 January 1923
Inventor: Juan de la Cierva

An autogyro (also called a gyrocopter or gyroplane) is a type of rotorcraft that uses an unpowered rotor in free autorotation to develop lift, while an engine-powered propeller provides thrust to maintain airspeed. Whereas a helicopter's rotor is powered for both lift and propulsion, the autogyro's rotor is turned only by the aerodynamic forces of the air flowing through it, so the aircraft must maintain forward speed and cannot hover or take off vertically.

History

The autogyro was invented by Spanish engineer Juan de la Cierva, whose first successful machine flew on 9 January 1923 at Getafe aerodrome in Spain. De la Cierva's key invention was the flapping hinge, which allowed rotor blades to flap and so made practical rotorcraft flight possible. His Cierva Autogiro Company licensed designs abroad, including to Harold Pitcairn in the United States, where autogyros were used for early airmail services and even landed on the White House lawn in 1931. The autogyro's development was overtaken by the practical helicopter in the late 1930s.

Characteristics

Autogyros are noted for their ability to fly very slowly without stalling and to land in very short distances by making steep, near-vertical descents. Most modern autogyros are small recreational or training aircraft with open or enclosed cockpits and pusher propellers; a pre-rotator spins the rotor before take-off, after which a short ground roll brings the rotor to flight speed. They are regulated as a distinct category of aircraft in many countries.

Records and uses

Autogyros have been flown for observation, agriculture, policing and sport. A replica of a Cierva autogyro appeared famously in popular culture, and "Little Nellie", a Wallis WA-116 autogyro, featured in the 1967 James Bond film You Only Live Twice.
