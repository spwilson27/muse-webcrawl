Segovia is a city in the autonomous community of Castile and León, Spain, capital of the province of the same name. Situated about 90 kilometres northwest of Madrid, on a rocky ridge between the Eresma and Clamores rivers at the foot of the Sierra de Guadarrama, it is famed for its remarkably preserved Roman aqueduct, its fairytale Alcázar, and one of the last great Gothic cathedrals built in Spain. In 1985 the old city and aqueduct were declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

The site was settled in Celtiberian and then Roman times; the aqueduct, probably built around the end of the 1st century AD, carried water some 15 kilometres from the mountains into the city through 167 soaring granite arches (in its elevated section) assembled without mortar. Segovia flourished in the Middle Ages as a centre of the wool trade and royal residence: it was in the Alcázar that Isabella I was proclaimed Queen of Castile in 1474. The city's cloth industry declined in the 17th century, but its monumental core survived largely intact.

Main sights

The Aqueduct dominates the Plaza del Azoguejo, rising nearly 29 metres at its highest. The Alcázar, a fortress-palace on a cliff at the confluence of the two rivers, was rebuilt in the 19th century after a fire, with the slate spires that give it its storybook profile — often said to have inspired the castles of animated films. The Cathedral (1525–1577), designed by Juan Gil de Hontañón and his son Rodrigo, is a late flowering of Spanish Gothic. The old Jewish quarter, Romanesque churches such as San Millán and San Martín, and the Casa de los Picos complete the historic centre. Segovia is also renowned for its roast suckling pig (cochinillo) and roast lamb.
