British Doctors Study

TypeProspective cohort study
Duration1951–2001 (50 years)
Participants34,439 male British doctors (initial cohort)
InvestigatorsRichard Doll, Austin Bradford Hill, later Richard Peto

The British Doctors Study was a landmark prospective cohort study that ran from 1951 to 2001 and provided some of the first convincing statistical evidence linking cigarette smoking to lung cancer and other diseases, as well as quantifying the health benefits of stopping smoking.

Background

In 1950, Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill published a case-control study showing a strong association between smoking and lung cancer, but critics argued that such retrospective designs could not establish causation. Doll and Hill therefore set out to follow a defined population prospectively. British doctors were chosen because they were easy to trace through the medical register, had reliable cause-of-death certification, and might be expected to answer questionnaires accurately.

Methods

In October 1951 questionnaires were sent to all registered doctors in the United Kingdom; about 41,000 replied, and the final male cohort numbered 34,439 (a smaller female cohort was also followed). Participants reported their smoking habits at intervals over the decades, and their deaths were tracked through the Registrar General's records.

Findings

Reports published at 10, 20, 40 and 50 years consistently showed that smokers died at substantially higher rates than non-smokers from lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart disease and many other causes. The 50-year report (Doll, Peto and colleagues, 2004) concluded that cigarette smokers died on average about 10 years earlier than lifelong non-smokers, but that stopping smoking at ages 30, 40, 50 or 60 gained about 10, 9, 6 or 3 years of life expectancy respectively.

The study is a classic of epidemiology and a milestone in public health, helping to change medical and public attitudes toward smoking across the world.
