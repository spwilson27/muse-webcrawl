The Dead Sea Transform (DST), also known as the Dead Sea Rift, is a series of faults that run for about 1,000 kilometres from the Marash triple junction in southern Turkey to the northern end of the Red Sea Rift in Egypt. It forms the boundary between the Arabian Plate to the east and the Sinai subplate of the African Plate to the west, with predominantly left-lateral (sinistral) strike-slip motion.

Geology

The transform began to form in the early Miocene, when the Red Sea began to rift and Arabia started moving away from Africa. Total left-lateral displacement across the fault system is estimated at about 105 kilometres. Along its length the fault steps and bends create a series of pull-apart basins, including the deep depressions occupied by the Sea of Galilee, the Dead Sea—whose shore is the lowest land on Earth—and the Gulf of Aqaba.

North of Lebanon the fault splays into several strands, including the Yammouneh fault, which passes through the Bekaa Valley, and the Serghaya and Rachaya faults, before linking with the East Anatolian Fault Zone near the Marash triple junction.

Seismicity

The Dead Sea Transform has generated many destructive historical earthquakes, including events in 31 BC, AD 363, 749, 1033, 1202, 1546, 1759, and 1837, which damaged cities such as Jerusalem, Damascus, Beirut, and Safed. Instrumentally recorded seismicity is comparatively moderate, but paleoseismic and historical studies indicate the fault is capable of earthquakes of magnitude 7 or greater, and the region's dense population places large numbers of people at risk.
