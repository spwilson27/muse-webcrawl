Solar radiation modification

Solar radiation modification (SRM), also known as solar geoengineering, refers to a range of proposed techniques that would reflect a small fraction of incoming sunlight back into space in order to reduce global warming. SRM methods would not reduce greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere, and so would not address ocean acidification or other effects of elevated carbon dioxide; they are studied as a potential supplement to emissions reductions and carbon dioxide removal, not a substitute.

Proposed SRM methods include stratospheric aerosol injection, marine cloud brightening, and space-based reflectors.

Proposed methods

The most studied method is stratospheric aerosol injection, in which reflective particles such as sulfates would be introduced into the stratosphere, mimicking the cooling effect observed after large volcanic eruptions such as the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo. Other proposals include marine cloud brightening, spraying sea-salt particles into low marine clouds to increase their reflectivity; cirrus cloud thinning; surface albedo modification of roofs, crops, or deserts; and space-based sunshades positioned near the Sun–Earth L1 Lagrange point.

Potential benefits and risks

Climate models suggest SRM could rapidly reduce global mean temperatures and some associated impacts such as extreme heat and sea-level rise. However, it could also alter regional precipitation patterns, deplete stratospheric ozone (in the case of sulfate injection), and produce uneven regional effects. A major concern is the "termination problem": if SRM were stopped abruptly after masking substantial warming, temperatures could rebound very quickly. SRM also raises difficult governance questions, since deployment by one country could affect the climate of all others, and its availability might weaken incentives to cut emissions — the so-called moral hazard argument.

Research and governance

Research has so far been dominated by computer modelling and small-scale process studies; proposed outdoor experiments, such as Harvard's Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment (SCoPEx) in Sweden, attracted controversy and were cancelled in 2024. Scientific assessments by bodies such as the US National Academies have recommended a cautious, governed research programme. International law does not specifically regulate SRM, though the Convention on Biological Diversity has called for restraint, and debates continue in forums such as the UN Environment Assembly.
