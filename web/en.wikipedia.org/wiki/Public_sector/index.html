Public sector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The public sector is the part of an economy composed of public services and public enterprises — organisations owned or controlled by the state at national, regional or local level. It includes government departments and agencies, public schools and hospitals, police and armed forces, social-security administration, and state-owned enterprises (from postal services and railways to sovereign wealth and industrial holdings in some countries). Its size is measured by government spending as a share of GDP (roughly 35–55 per cent across the OECD in recent decades, highest in France and the Nordic countries) and by public employment (roughly 15–25 per cent of total employment, again highest in Scandinavia).

What belongs in the public sector shifts historically: the post-1945 era nationalised utilities and industries in much of Europe; the 1980s–90s privatisation wave transferred them out; contracting-out blurred the boundary (private firms delivering public services under public finance and accountability). The sector's rationale rests on public goods, natural monopoly, externalities and equity; its critics cite monopoly inefficiency, producer capture and fiscal burden — the argument replayed in every era's reform programme, from New Public Management to post-crisis renationalisations.
