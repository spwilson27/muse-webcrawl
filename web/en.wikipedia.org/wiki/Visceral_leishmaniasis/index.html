Visceral leishmaniasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Visceral leishmaniasis, or kala-azar, is the most severe form of leishmaniasis.

Visceral leishmaniasis (VL), also known as kala-azar (Hindi for "black fever"), is the most severe form of leishmaniasis and, without diagnosis and treatment, is associated with high fatality. It is a disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Leishmania, principally Leishmania donovani and Leishmania infantum, transmitted by the bite of infected female phlebotomine sandflies.

Signs and symptoms

Signs and symptoms include fever lasting weeks to months, weight loss, fatigue, anaemia, and substantial swelling of the liver and especially the spleen (splenomegaly). Because the parasite attacks the immune system, the disease can be complicated by secondary infections. Without treatment, the case-fatality rate in developing countries can be as high as 100% within two years.

Epidemiology

Visceral leishmaniasis is endemic in parts of East Africa, South Asia and Brazil, among other regions. The World Health Organization estimates tens of thousands of new cases occur each year, and VL is considered a neglected tropical disease that disproportionately affects poor rural communities. Co-infection with HIV is a serious complication in some endemic areas.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by finding the parasite in samples of bone marrow, spleen or lymph node tissue, or by serological tests such as the rK39 immunochromatographic test. Treatment includes liposomal amphotericin B, miltefosine, paromomycin and pentavalent antimonials, used singly or in combination depending on the region. Control efforts also focus on sandfly vector control and rapid treatment of cases.
