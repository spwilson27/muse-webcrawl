Sir Alec John Jeffreys (born 9 January 1950) is a British geneticist who invented DNA fingerprinting at the University of Leicester in 1984, a discovery that transformed forensic science, paternity testing and immigration casework.

Discovery of DNA fingerprinting

Jeffreys was studying variation in human genes when, on 10 September 1984, an X-ray film from an experiment on minisatellite DNA revealed unexpectedly complex, individual-specific banding patterns. He immediately realised the patterns could identify individuals. His team termed the method "genetic fingerprinting" and published it in Nature in 1985, showing that hypervariable minisatellite regions of DNA produce profiles unique to each person (identical twins excepted).

Applications

The technique's first public success came in 1985, resolving an immigration dispute over a Ghanaian boy's family relationship. In 1986–87 it was used in the Leicestershire "Pitchfork" murders: DNA evidence first exonerated a suspect, Richard Buckland, and then — through the world's first mass DNA screening of local men — identified the murderer Colin Pitchfork, who was convicted in 1988. Modern forensic DNA profiling, now based on short tandem repeats amplified by PCR, descends directly from Jeffreys's work and underpins national DNA databases worldwide.

Honours

Jeffreys was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society at 36, knighted in 1994, and received the Royal Society's Royal Medal and Copley Medal, among many awards. He has been a prominent voice in debates over the retention of innocent people's DNA profiles.
