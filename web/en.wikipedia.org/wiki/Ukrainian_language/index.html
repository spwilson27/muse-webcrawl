Ukrainian (українська мова, ukrainska mova) is an East Slavic language of the Indo-European family, spoken primarily in Ukraine, where it is the sole state language. It is written in a 33-letter version of the Cyrillic alphabet. Ukrainian descends from the spoken language of Kievan Rus' and developed its modern literary form in the late 18th and 19th centuries, notably through the works of Ivan Kotliarevsky and Taras Shevchenko.

The Ukrainian alphabet has 33 letters, including the distinctive і, ї, є, and ґ.

History

The written tradition of the region began with Old East Slavic and Church Slavonic. Under the Russian Empire, Ukrainian publishing was restricted by the Valuev Circular (1863) and the Ems Ukaz (1876), which suppressed the language as a separate literary medium. It flourished nonetheless in Austrian Galicia. In the Soviet Union, an early policy of Ukrainization in the 1920s was reversed in the 1930s, and Russification pressures continued for decades. Since independence in 1991, and especially since 2014 and the full-scale Russian invasion of 2022, Ukrainian has consolidated its position, with many formerly Russian-speaking citizens switching to it.

Linguistic features

Ukrainian phonology retains features lost in Russian, such as the development of Proto-Slavic *o and *e into і in closed syllables (e.g., ніч, "night"). Its grammar has seven noun cases, including a vocative, three genders, and a synthetic future tense. Vocabulary is closest to Belarusian and shares substantial common ground with Polish, Slovak, and Russian; it is broadly mutually intelligible with Belarusian and partly with Russian.

Dialects and status

Dialects fall into northern, south-western, and south-eastern groups; the standard language is based mainly on the south-eastern (Poltava–Kyiv) dialects. A Ukrainian–Russian mixed speech, Surzhyk, is widespread. Ukrainian is also spoken by large diaspora communities in Canada, the United States, Poland, and elsewhere.
