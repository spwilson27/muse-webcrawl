British subject

British subject is a class of British nationality. Historically, "British subject" was the common status held by all persons owing allegiance to the British Crown throughout the British Empire. Since the British Nationality Act 1981 came into force on 1 January 1983, the term has designated a much narrower residual category of people who do not hold any other British or Commonwealth citizenship.

History

The concept of the subject originated in the feudal allegiance owed to the Crown, confirmed in Calvin's Case (1608), which held that a person born within the King's dominions owed allegiance and was entitled to protection. A common code of subject status applied across the Empire until the mid-twentieth century, when individual dominions began creating their own citizenships, beginning with Canada in 1946.

The British Nationality Act 1948 created the status of Citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies (CUKC), while providing that CUKCs and citizens of Commonwealth countries would share the common status of British subject (also styled Commonwealth citizen). As immigration controls were imposed by the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971, the practical rights of British subjects without CUKC status were progressively curtailed.

Current status

Under the 1981 Act, only two groups remained British subjects: persons who had been British subjects without another citizenship before 1983 (mainly connected with pre-1949 Ireland or the former British India), and certain persons born to British subject parents. The status is not automatically transmissible and will eventually disappear. British subjects do not have the right of abode in the United Kingdom, though those with a UK-born parent or grandparent, or five years' residence, may register as British citizens. Irish citizens in this category generally also hold Irish citizenship.
