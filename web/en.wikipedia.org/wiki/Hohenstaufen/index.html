The Hohenstaufen (also called the Staufer) were a dynasty of German kings and Holy Roman Emperors that reigned, with interruptions, from 1138 to 1254, and also ruled the Kingdom of Sicily from 1194. The dynasty takes its name from Staufen Castle on the Hohenstaufen mountain in Swabia. Its most famous members are Frederick I Barbarossa, Henry VI and Frederick II, whose conflict with the papacy and empire-building in Italy defined the dynasty's history.

Rise

The family rose in Swabia in the 11th century; Frederick I of Swabia (d. 1105) built the castle that gave the house its name and married Agnes, daughter of the emperor Henry IV. His son Conrad was elected king of Germany in 1138 as Conrad III, beginning the rivalry with the House of Welf. Conrad's nephew Frederick Barbarossa became king in 1152 and emperor in 1155, pursuing repeated campaigns to assert imperial authority in Italy and presiding over a flourishing court in Germany.

Apogee

Barbarossa's son Henry VI inherited the Sicilian crown through his wife Constance, uniting the empire and Sicily in 1194. His son Frederick II—raised in Palermo, king of Sicily from 1198, emperor from 1220—was the dynasty's most celebrated ruler: a patron of learning and law (the Liber Augustalis of 1231), founder of the University of Naples, and a figure of wonder to contemporaries ("stupor mundi"). His long struggle with the papacy, including excommunications and a crusade conducted under ban (1228–29), weakened the dynasty's position; in 1245 a papal council declared him deposed.

Fall

After Frederick II's death in 1250, his son Conrad IV reigned briefly (1250–1254). In Sicily, Frederick's illegitimate son Manfred was defeated and killed by Charles of Anjou at Benevento in 1266; Conradin, Conrad IV's young son and the last legitimate Hohenstaufen, invaded Italy and was captured and beheaded at Naples in 1268 after the Battle of Tagliacozzo. The duchy of Swabia dissolved, the empire entered the Great Interregnum, and Hohenstaufen claims survived only in the female line, notably through Constance of Sicily into the House of Barcelona.

Legacy

The Hohenstaufen era saw the height of medieval imperial power, the flowering of Middle High German courtly literature under princely patronage, and distinctive architecture (the Staufer castles and cathedrals). The legend that Barbarossa sleeps in the Kyffhäuser mountain, awaiting Germany's hour of need, later attached the dynasty to German national mythology.
