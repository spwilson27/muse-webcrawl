Dairy cattle (also called dairy cows) are cattle bred for their ability to produce large quantities of milk, from which dairy products are made. Dairy cattle generally belong to the species Bos taurus. The world's dairy herd numbered roughly 270 million cows in the early 21st century, producing more than 900 million tonnes of milk annually.

Breeds

The Holstein Friesian, developed in the Netherlands and northern Germany, is the dominant breed in industrialised dairying, prized for very high milk volume. The Jersey, from the Channel Island of Jersey, produces milk with exceptionally high butterfat content. Other important breeds include the Brown Swiss, Ayrshire, Guernsey and Milking Shorthorn. In South Asia, water buffalo and zebu breeds are major milk sources.

Management

Dairy cows must calve to begin lactation; a typical cycle involves annual calving, a lactation of about 305 days, and a dry period of around 60 days before the next calving. Cows are usually milked two or three times daily, by machine in a milking parlour or increasingly by automated robotic milking systems. Common housing systems include free-stall (cubicle) barns, tie-stalls, and pasture-based systems, which predominate in countries such as New Zealand and Ireland.

Feeding and health

High-producing cows require energy-dense rations of forage (grass, silage, hay) supplemented with concentrates. Important health problems include mastitis (udder infection), lameness, ketosis and milk fever. Selective breeding, increasingly aided by genomic selection, has dramatically raised yields since the mid-20th century, though it has also raised concerns about fertility and longevity.

Welfare and environment

Dairy farming is scrutinised for animal welfare issues such as early cow–calf separation and the fate of male calves, and for its environmental footprint: enteric methane emissions, manure management and land use. Milk production accounts for a significant share of global livestock greenhouse-gas emissions.
