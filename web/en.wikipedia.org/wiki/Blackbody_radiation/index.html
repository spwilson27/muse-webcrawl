Blackbody radiation

Blackbody radiation is the thermal electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body — an idealised opaque, non-reflective object — in thermodynamic equilibrium. The radiation has a specific spectrum and intensity that depend only on the body's temperature, and it is emitted equally in all directions (it is diffuse).

Planck's law

The spectral radiance of a black body at absolute temperature T is given by Planck's law, proposed by Max Planck in 1900. Planck derived the formula by postulating that electromagnetic energy could be emitted only in quantised packets — an assumption that launched quantum theory. At low frequencies Planck's law approaches the classical Rayleigh–Jeans law, and at high frequencies it approaches Wien's approximation, avoiding the "ultraviolet catastrophe" predicted by classical physics.

Related laws

Integrating Planck's law over all wavelengths gives the Stefan–Boltzmann law: the total power radiated per unit area is proportional to the fourth power of temperature. Differentiating it gives Wien's displacement law, which states that the wavelength at which emission peaks is inversely proportional to temperature — the reason hot objects change colour from dull red to white to blue as they are heated.

Applications

Stars radiate approximately as black bodies, so their colour indicates their surface temperature. The cosmic microwave background, at 2.725 K, is the most precise blackbody spectrum known in nature. Blackbody principles underlie pyrometry, infrared thermography, climate science (Earth's effective temperature) and the design of thermal radiation sources and calibration standards.
