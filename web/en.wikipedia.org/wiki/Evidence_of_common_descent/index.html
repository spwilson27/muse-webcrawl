Evidence of common descent

Evidence of common descent of living organisms has been discovered by scientists working in a variety of disciplines over many decades, demonstrating that all life on Earth comes from a single ancestor, the last universal common ancestor (LUCA). This forms an important part of the evidence on which evolutionary theory rests and demonstrates the process of natural selection and other mechanisms generating the diversity of life.

Evidence from comparative anatomy

Homologous structures — anatomical features in different species that share a common structural plan despite different functions, such as the pentadactyl limb of tetrapods (the human arm, the bat's wing, the whale's flipper and the mole's digging forelimb all contain the same arrangement of bones) — are explained by inheritance from a common ancestor. Vestigial structures, such as the human appendix and coccyx, the pelvic bones of whales and snakes, and the wings of flightless birds, are remnants of functional features in ancestors. Atavisms — rare reappearances of ancestral traits, such as hind limbs in whales or tails in human infants — further reveal this inherited developmental programme.

Evidence from molecular biology

All known life uses the same basic biochemistry: DNA and RNA as genetic material, the same (nearly universal) genetic code, the same 20 amino acids, and ATP as an energy currency — a pattern expected if all organisms descend from one ancestor. Comparing DNA and protein sequences allows scientists to construct phylogenetic trees that independently match trees derived from anatomy and the fossil record. Shared "errors", such as identical pseudogenes and endogenous retroviruses inserted at the same chromosomal positions in humans and chimpanzees, are particularly strong evidence, as is the fusion that produced human chromosome 2 from two ape chromosomes.

Evidence from biogeography

The geographical distribution of species matches evolutionary history: marsupials dominate in Australia, which was isolated for tens of millions of years; oceanic islands lack native land mammals and amphibians but host unique radiations of birds and insects descended from rare colonists, as with Darwin's finches in the Galápagos and lemurs in Madagascar. Continental drift explains matching fossils and species on now-separated southern continents.

Evidence from the fossil record

Fossils document the succession of life through geological time and include numerous transitional forms, such as Archaeopteryx (between dinosaurs and birds), Tiktaalik (between fish and tetrapods), and the rich series documenting the evolution of whales from land mammals and of horses from small multi-toed ancestors.

Observed evolution

Evolution has been directly observed in the laboratory and the field: in long-term experiments with E. coli, in the evolution of antibiotic and pesticide resistance, in documented speciation events, and in the rapid adaptation of populations such as Darwin's finches and peppered moths.
