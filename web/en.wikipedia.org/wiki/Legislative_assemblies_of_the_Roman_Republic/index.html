Legislative assemblies of the Roman Republic

The legislative assemblies of the Roman Republic were political institutions in ancient Rome. During the Roman Republic, the citizens exercised their sovereign popular will in three assemblies: the comitia centuriata (Centuriate Assembly), the comitia tributa (Tribal Assembly) and the concilium plebis (Plebeian Council). A fourth body, the archaic comitia curiata (Curiate Assembly), survived mainly for formal and religious acts. These assemblies elected magistrates, enacted laws (leges) and tried certain capital cases.

Procedure

Assemblies could meet only when summoned by a competent magistrate, who alone could propose measures. Citizens did not debate: after preliminary meetings (contiones) at which speeches were made, the assembly voted by groups — centuries or tribes — without amendment. Voting was by majority within each unit, and the units' votes were counted in a fixed order until a majority of units was reached, which often meant the votes of poorer citizens, concentrated in the last units, were never cast. Bribery and procedural obstruction were endemic in the late Republic.

The assemblies

The Centuriate Assembly, organised by wealth and age on a military basis in 193 centuries, elected consuls, praetors and censors, declared war, and heard appeals in capital cases. The Tribal Assembly, organised by the 35 territorial tribes, elected quaestors and curule aediles and passed many laws. The Plebeian Council, organised likewise by tribes but excluding patricians, elected tribunes and plebeian aediles and passed plebiscites, which bound the whole people after the lex Hortensia of 287 BC.

Decline

Under the Principate the assemblies lost their electoral functions to the Senate (from AD 14) and their legislative role withered as imperial legislation took precedence, though the forms persisted into the third century AD.
