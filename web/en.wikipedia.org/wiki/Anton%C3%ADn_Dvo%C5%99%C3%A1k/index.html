Antonín Leopold Dvořák (8 September 1841 – 1 May 1904) was a Czech composer of the Romantic era, the most celebrated Czech composer internationally. Drawing on the folk music of Bohemia and Moravia, he combined folk idioms with classical forms influenced by Brahms and Wagner.

Early life

Dvořák was born in Nelahozeves near Prague, the eldest son of an innkeeper and butcher. He learned violin and viola, played in village bands, and at 16 moved to Prague to study at the Organ School. He spent years as a viola player in the Provisional Theatre orchestra, much of it under Bedřich Smetana, while composing in private.

Career

His breakthrough came in the 1870s: Johannes Brahms championed his music, secured him a publisher, and the Slavonic Dances (1878) made him famous across Europe. Commissioned works poured in from England, where he conducted nine visits. From 1892 to 1895 he directed the National Conservatory of Music in New York, where he composed his Symphony No. 9, "From the New World", and the Cello Concerto in B minor.

Legacy

His works include nine symphonies, the operas Rusalka and Dimitrij, the "American" String Quartet, the Stabat Mater and the Requiem. Dvořák is regarded, with Smetana and Janáček, as one of the founders of a distinctively Czech national music.
