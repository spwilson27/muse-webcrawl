Council of Pisa

Council of Pisa
Date1409
LocationPisa, Italy
PurposeEnding the Western Schism
OutcomeDeposed both rival popes; elected Alexander V — producing a third papal line

The Council of Pisa was a council of the Roman Catholic Church held in 1409 in an attempt to end the Western Schism, in which rival popes at Rome and Avignon claimed the papacy. Convened by cardinals disaffected from both camps, it deposed both Pope Gregory XII of Rome and Antipope Benedict XIII of Avignon and elected a new pope, Alexander V — but, as neither deposed claimant accepted the verdict, the schism deepened from two lines to three.

Background

The Western Schism had split Latin Christendom since 1378. Pressured by the universities, clergy and secular rulers, cardinals from both obediences met at Livorno in 1408 and summoned a general council at Pisa for March 1409, grounding their action in the emerging conciliarist theory that a council could judge a pope in cases of heresy or schism.

The council

Some 24 cardinals and hundreds of prelates, theologians and ambassadors assembled. Both Gregory XII and Benedict XIII refused to attend and were declared contumacious, deposed as heretics and schismatics on 5 June 1409. The cardinals then entered conclave and elected the Greek Franciscan Peter Philargos, who took the name Alexander V. He died within a year and was succeeded by John XXIII of the Pisan line.

Aftermath

Because Gregory XII retained support in parts of Italy and Germany and Benedict XIII in Spain and Scotland, the Pisan settlement failed to restore unity, leaving three claimants. The impasse was finally resolved by the Council of Constance (1414–1418). The Roman Catholic Church later treated the Pisan popes as antipopes, and Pisa's legitimacy as a general council remained disputed — debated again in the twentieth century when Angelo Roncalli chose the name John XXIII, implicitly accepting the Pisan line's numbering.
