Pruning

Pruning is a horticultural and silvicultural practice involving the selective removal of certain parts of a plant, such as branches, buds, or roots. In modern arboriculture and fruit production, proper pruning is essential to plant health, safety and yield, while incorrect pruning can permanently damage or kill a tree.

Reasons for pruning

  • Health — removing dead, diseased or infested wood (the "three D's"), and improving airflow to reduce fungal disease.
  • Structure — training young trees to a strong framework, removing crossing or weakly attached branches.
  • Production — fruit trees and grapevines are pruned to balance vegetative growth with fruiting and to renew fruiting wood.
  • Safety and clearance — removing limbs that threaten buildings, paths, power lines or sight lines.
  • Appearance — shaping hedges, topiary and ornamental shrubs.

Techniques

Common cut types include heading cuts, which shorten a stem and stimulate branching; thinning cuts, which remove a branch back to its point of origin; and reduction cuts, which shorten a limb to a lateral branch of sufficient size. Tools range from hand secateurs and loppers to pruning saws and chainsaws for large limbs. For trees, cuts should be made just outside the branch collar so the tree can seal the wound; practices such as topping are widely condemned as harmful. Timing depends on species and purpose: many deciduous trees are pruned in dormancy, while flowering shrubs are often pruned just after blooming.

In forestry and agriculture

In plantation forestry, pruning lower branches produces clear, knot-free timber of higher value. Specialist forms include pollarding and coppicing (historical management systems), espalier training of fruit trees against walls, and the annual spur pruning of vineyards that underpins much of commercial viticulture.
