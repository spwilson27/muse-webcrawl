Acousto-optics

Acousto-optics is a branch of physics that studies the interaction between sound waves and light waves, in particular the diffraction of laser light by ultrasound in a medium. The periodic variations in refractive index produced by an acoustic wave act as a moving diffraction grating for light passing through the material.

History

The diffraction of light by ultrasonic waves was predicted by Léon Brillouin in 1922 and observed experimentally in the early 1930s by Peter Debye, F. W. Sears, Lucas, and Biquard, among others. Interest grew rapidly after the invention of the laser in 1960, which provided the intense, coherent light sources needed for practical devices.

Principles

When an acoustic wave propagates through a transparent medium, the associated pressure variations compress and rarefy the material, producing a periodic modulation of its refractive index. A light beam incident on this sound field is diffracted into discrete orders. In the Bragg regime, a single diffracted order dominates, and the diffraction angle and efficiency depend on the acoustic frequency and power. Two limiting cases are distinguished: Raman–Nath diffraction, producing multiple orders, and Bragg diffraction, producing essentially one.

Devices

Acousto-optic devices exploit these effects to control light by means of radio-frequency electrical signals applied to a piezoelectric transducer bonded to the crystal or glass:

  • Acousto-optic modulators — switch or modulate the intensity of a laser beam
  • Acousto-optic deflectors — steer a beam by varying the acoustic frequency
  • Acousto-optic tunable filters — select narrow wavelength bands from broadband light
  • Acousto-optic spectrum analyzers — map radio-frequency spectra onto optical patterns

Common interaction materials include fused silica, tellurium dioxide (TeO2), lithium niobate, and germanium for infrared wavelengths.

Applications

Applications include laser printing and scanning, optical signal processing, spectroscopy, laser Q-switching, telecommunications, and confocal microscopy.
