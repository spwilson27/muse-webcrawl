Neural network (machine learning)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A diagram of a multilayer perceptron with neurons and weighted connections]

An artificial neural network is a machine-learning model inspired by the structure and function of biological neural networks in animal brains. It consists of connected units called artificial neurons, which model the neurons in the brain. These are connected by edges, which model the synapses in the brain.

Each connection has a weight adjusted during training; neurons aggregate weighted inputs and apply an activation function. Networks with several 'hidden' layers are 'deep', and deep learning with such networks achieved breakthroughs in computer vision (2012, AlexNet), speech, and language (transformers, 2017).

History

The McCulloch–Pitts neuron (1943) and Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) began the field; Minsky and Papert's 1969 critique of single-layer perceptrons contributed to a first 'AI winter'. Backpropagation, popularised in 1986 (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams), enabled training of multilayer networks; a second winter followed until data, GPUs, and algorithmic refinements (ReLU activations, dropout, better initialisation) fuelled the 2010s deep-learning boom.

Architectures include feedforward networks, convolutional networks (images), recurrent networks and LSTMs (sequences), and transformers — the basis of modern large language models such as the GPT family.

Training and theory

Training minimises a loss function by gradient descent: forward propagation computes predictions, backpropagation computes gradients via the chain rule, and optimisers (SGD, Adam) update weights. The universal approximation theorem guarantees single-hidden-layer networks can approximate any continuous function on compact sets, though depth often matters in practice.
