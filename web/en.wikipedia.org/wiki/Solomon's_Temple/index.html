Solomon's Temple, also known as the First Temple, was the first Jewish temple in Jerusalem, according to the Hebrew Bible. It was built by King Solomon, son of David, on Mount Moriah in Jerusalem, and served as the religious centre of the Israelite kingdom and the resting place of the Ark of the Covenant. It stood for roughly four centuries before its destruction by the Babylonian king Nebuchadnezzar II in 586 BCE, following the siege of Jerusalem.

Biblical account

According to the Books of Kings and Chronicles, David purchased the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite as the temple site and gathered materials, but was not permitted to build the house himself. Solomon began construction in the fourth year of his reign, employing Phoenician craftsmen and materials supplied by Hiram I of Tyre, including cedar and cypress timber from Lebanon. The building took seven years to complete.

Architecture

The biblical description portrays a rectangular stone structure, about 60 cubits long, 20 wide and 30 high, lined inside with cedar and overlaid in part with gold. It was divided into an outer porch (ulam), the sanctuary or Holy Place (heikhal), and the innermost Holy of Holies (devir), a windowless cube of 20 cubits that housed the Ark beneath the wings of two great cherubim. Two free-standing bronze pillars, Jachin and Boaz, flanked the entrance, and the courtyard contained the bronze altar and the great basin known as the Molten Sea.

Ritual and history

The Temple was the sole authorised place of sacrificial worship and the destination of pilgrimage festivals. It was plundered several times, renovated under kings such as Joash and Josiah, and finally burned by the Babylonians in 586 BCE, after which the Ark disappears from the historical record. The site was rebuilt after the Babylonian exile as the Second Temple, completed around 516 BCE.

Archaeology

No remains of the First Temple itself have been excavated, as the Temple Mount is religiously and politically sensitive; related Iron Age structures and artefacts elsewhere in Jerusalem are often interpreted in light of the biblical description. The historicity and scale of the Solomonic building remain debated among scholars.
