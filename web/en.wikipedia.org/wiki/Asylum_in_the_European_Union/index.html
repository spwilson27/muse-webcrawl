Asylum in the European Union

Asylum in the European Union is governed by the Common European Asylum System (CEAS), a body of EU law that sets common standards for the qualification, reception and processing of people seeking international protection in the member states. The system rests on the 1951 Geneva Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and its 1967 Protocol, and on the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union, whose Article 18 guarantees the right to asylum.

Development

Cooperation on asylum began as an intergovernmental matter alongside the creation of the single market and the abolition of internal border controls under Schengen. The 1990 Dublin Convention established rules for determining which state is responsible for examining an application. The Treaty of Amsterdam (1999) brought asylum into the EU's legal framework, and the European Council at Tampere committed the Union to building a common system in two phases of harmonising legislation.

Main instruments

  • The Dublin III Regulation (No. 604/2013), which assigns responsibility for an application, generally to the member state of first entry, subject to family unity and other criteria.
  • The Qualification Directive, which defines who qualifies for refugee status or subsidiary protection and the rights attached to each.
  • The Asylum Procedures Directive, setting common procedural standards and guarantees.
  • The Reception Conditions Directive, laying down minimum standards for housing, food, healthcare and access to work for applicants.
  • The Eurodac Regulation, establishing an EU-wide fingerprint database for asylum applicants, used to apply the Dublin rules.

Agencies

The European Asylum Support Office (EASO), established in 2010 and based in Malta, was replaced and strengthened in 2022 by the European Union Agency for Asylum (EUAA), which provides operational and technical support to member states under pressure and issues country-of-origin information.

Criticism and reform

The system came under intense strain during the 2015–2016 European migrant crisis, when over a million applications were lodged in a single year. The Dublin rules' allocation of responsibility to frontline states such as Greece and Italy was widely criticised, relocation schemes proved divisive, and member states repeatedly failed to agree a permanent solidarity mechanism. After years of negotiation, the EU adopted a new Pact on Migration and Asylum in 2024, introducing mandatory but flexible solidarity, faster border procedures and a new screening and crisis framework.
