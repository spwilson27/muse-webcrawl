The Tanner scale (also known as the Tanner stages) is a scale of physical development in children, adolescents, and adults. Developed by the British paediatrician James M. Tanner, in collaboration with W. A. Marshall, from longitudinal growth studies published in 1969–1970, the scale defines five stages based on the development of secondary sex characteristics: breast development in females, genital development in males, and pubic hair growth in both sexes.

The stages

StageGeneral description
IPrepubertal — no sexual hair; childlike genitals/breasts
IIOnset of puberty — breast buds or testicular enlargement; sparse first pubic hair
IIIFurther growth of breasts/genitals; hair coarser and spreading
IVAdvanced development approaching adult form; hair adult in type but smaller area
VAdult development complete

Clinical use

Tanner staging is used to assess whether puberty is progressing normally, to investigate precocious or delayed puberty, and in growth and endocrine research. Typical timing varies; pubertal onset is generally considered normal between ages 8 and 13 in girls and 9 and 14 in boys. Assessment historically relied on examination with reference photographs or drawings, though self-assessment questionnaires are also used in research.

Limitations and criticism

The original standards were derived from a relatively small, ethnically homogeneous British cohort photographed at a single children's home, and secular trends toward earlier puberty mean the scale's timings are now treated as descriptive rather than normative. The clinical photography underlying the scale has also raised ethical discussion in the era of digital records.
