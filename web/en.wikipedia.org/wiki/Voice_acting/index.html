Voice acting

Voice acting is the art of performing a character or delivering information with the voice alone, for media in which the performer is not seen: animation, video games, radio drama, audiobooks, dubbing of foreign-language productions, advertising, narration, automated announcements and toys. Performers in this field are called voice actors or voice artists.

History

The profession began with radio: pioneers such as Reginald Fessenden made early voice broadcasts, and by the 1920s–30s radio drama had created the first voice stars. In animation, Mel Blanc — "the man of a thousand voices" — defined the craft at Warner Bros. as Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck and dozens more, and eventually won on-screen credit and higher status for the profession. Walt Disney himself voiced Mickey Mouse in the character's early years.

In Japan, voice acting (seiyū) developed into a distinct celebrity profession driven by anime, with specialised training schools and performers who also work as singers and idols. In the United States, the growth of television animation, then video games and audiobooks, expanded the field greatly from the 1980s onward.

Types

  • Character voices for animation and games, requiring sustained, distinctive characterisation.
  • Dubbing, matching translated dialogue to lip movements and performance in foreign productions, including anime and live-action film.
  • Narration and documentary work, including film trailers — a niche famously associated with Don LaFontaine.
  • Commercials and imaging for radio and television stations.
  • Audiobooks, ADR (automated dialogue replacement), announcements and voice assistants.

Process

Animation voice work is usually recorded before the pictures are finished ("pre-lay"), with actors performing to script and sometimes filmed for animators' reference; anime dubbing instead records to finished picture. Sessions are directed for tone, timing and consistency, and performers must protect their voices given the strain of shouting, creature noises and long sessions. The industry has been reshaped by home studios, remote recording, union agreements (such as SAG-AFTRA contracts), and debate over the use of artificial-intelligence voice synthesis.
