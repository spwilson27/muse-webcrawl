The blastocyst is a structure formed in the early development of mammals. It possesses an inner cell mass (embryoblast), which will form the embryo itself, enclosed by an outer layer of cells called the trophoblast, which will form part of the placenta, surrounding a fluid-filled cavity, the blastocoel.

Development

After fertilisation, the zygote divides into a solid ball of cells, the morula. Around the fourth to fifth day in humans, fluid accumulates inside, creating the blastocoel and reorganising the morula into the blastocyst of roughly 200–300 cells. The blastocyst then "hatches" from the protective zona pellucida and implants into the uterine wall, beginning around day 6–7. The equivalent stage in other vertebrates is the blastula.

Clinical significance

In in-vitro fertilisation, embryos are commonly cultured to the blastocyst stage (day 5) before transfer, because embryos reaching this stage have higher implantation potential and blastocyst culture allows better selection. The inner cell mass is the source of embryonic stem cells, first isolated from human blastocysts in 1998. Grading systems assess blastocyst quality by cavity expansion and the appearance of the inner cell mass and trophoblast.
