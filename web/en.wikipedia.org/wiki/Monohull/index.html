Monohull

A monohull is a type of vessel having only one hull, in contrast to multihulled vessels such as catamarans (two hulls) and trimarans (three hulls). Monohulls are by far the most common form of boat and ship design, used for almost all cargo ships, naval vessels, fishing boats and the majority of recreational craft.

Design and stability

A monohull floats by displacing a volume of water equal to its weight. Stability comes either from form — the shape of the hull and the shift of the centre of buoyancy as it heels — or from ballast placed low in the hull, typically in a keel on sailing yachts, which provides a righting moment. Ballasted monohull sailboats are self-righting: they can recover from a knockdown or even a complete capsize, unlike most multihulls.

Advantages and disadvantages

Monohulls offer large internal volume for a given length, straightforward construction and handling, and, for sailing craft, the ability to point high into the wind. Their drawbacks compared with multihulls include greater heel under sail, slower potential speed for light craft (displacement hulls are limited by hull speed), and a rolling motion some crews find uncomfortable. Racing classes that long mandated monohulls, such as the America's Cup, have in recent editions switched to foiling multihulls for speed.
