In mathematics, the total variation identifies several related concepts arising in measure theory, functional analysis and the theory of functions of a real variable. Most generally, it quantifies the overall "amount of change" of a function or a measure, summing both increases and decreases rather than netting them out.

Total variation of a function

For a real-valued function f on an interval [a, b], the total variation is the supremum, over all partitions of the interval, of the sum of absolute differences |f(xi+1) − f(xi)|. If this supremum is finite, f is said to be of bounded variation. For a differentiable function the total variation equals the integral of |f′| over the interval — that is, the arc length of the graph.

Every monotone function has bounded variation, and a classical theorem of Camille Jordan states that a function is of bounded variation if and only if it can be written as the difference of two monotone increasing functions.

Total variation of a measure

For a signed or complex measure μ on a measurable space, the total variation measure |μ| assigns to each set the supremum of the total mass |μ| can take over partitions of that set. The total variation norm of μ is |μ| evaluated on the whole space. The related total variation distance between two probability measures P and Q, defined as the supremum of |P(A) − Q(A)| over all events A (equivalently half the L¹ distance between their densities), is a standard metric in probability and statistics for comparing distributions.

Applications

  • Analysis: functions of bounded variation (BV) form an important function space; BV functions may have jump discontinuities but are differentiable almost everywhere.
  • Image processing: total variation denoising (the Rudin–Osher–Fatemi model) removes noise while preserving edges by minimising the total variation of the image.
  • Probability: total variation distance is used to bound convergence rates of Markov chains and to compare statistical experiments.
