A barcode is a method of representing data in a visual, machine-readable form. Initially, barcodes represented data by varying the widths and spacings of parallel lines (one-dimensional, or linear, codes); later they developed into two-dimensional patterns of rectangles, dots and hexagons, of which the QR code is the best known. Barcodes are read by optical scanners and are ubiquitous in retail, logistics, manufacturing, libraries and healthcare for automatic identification and data capture (AIDC).

History

The barcode was invented by Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver, graduate students at Drexel Institute of Technology, who patented a bullseye-style code in 1952 inspired in part by Morse code. Practical adoption awaited laser scanners and cheap computing. In 1973 the grocery industry in the United States adopted the Universal Product Code (UPC); the first retail item scanned, on 26 June 1974 at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio, was a pack of Wrigley's chewing gum. Europe adopted the compatible EAN system shortly after.

Symbologies

Linear symbologies include UPC-A/E, EAN-13, Code 39 (the first alphanumeric code, used in logistics and defence), and the high-density Code 128. Two-dimensional codes such as QR code (invented by Denso Wave in 1994), Data Matrix and PDF417 store far more data and include error correction, allowing them to be read even when partially damaged. Product barcodes are administered globally by GS1, which assigns company prefixes ensuring worldwide uniqueness of trade item numbers (GTINs).

Uses

Beyond supermarket checkouts, barcodes track inventory, parcels, rental assets, library books, patient wristbands and blood products, event tickets and manufacturing components. Camera-based scanning by smartphones has extended barcodes and QR codes into payments, marketing and authentication.
