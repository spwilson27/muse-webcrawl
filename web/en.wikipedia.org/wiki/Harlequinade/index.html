Harlequinade

A harlequinade is a comic theatrical entertainment in the British tradition, derived from the Italian commedia dell'arte. It formed the slapstick second part of the nineteenth-century English pantomime, telling the story of Harlequin and Columbine.

Origins

The form developed in early 18th-century London, notably through the dancer and theatre manager John Rich, who performed as Harlequin under the stage name "Lun". The wordless mime plot followed Harlequin, armed with a magic sword or bat (the slapstick), and his lover Columbine, pursued by her father Pantaloon and his servants, including the foolish Clown.

The transformation scene

In the mature pantomime, the harlequinade began with a spectacular transformation scene: characters from the opening fairy-tale portion were changed by a fairy into the harlequinade figures — the young lovers becoming Harlequin and Columbine, the obstructive father becoming Pantaloon, and a comic servant becoming Clown. The ensuing chase through trick scenery featured trapdoors, collapsing scenery, and visual gags.

Decline

Joseph Grimaldi, the most famous Clown of the early 19th century, shifted the balance of the entertainment toward the Clown's antics. By the late Victorian period the harlequinade shrank, and by the mid-20th century it had largely disappeared from British pantomime, surviving mainly in the dame-and-principal-boy conventions of modern panto.
