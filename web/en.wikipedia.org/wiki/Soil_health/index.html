Soil health is a state of a soil meeting its range of ecosystem functions as appropriate to its environment. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service defines it as "the continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans". The concept goes beyond soil fertility for crop production to include water infiltration and storage, nutrient cycling, carbon storage, disease suppression, and support of soil biodiversity.

Indicators

Soil health is assessed through physical indicators (structure, aggregate stability, infiltration, compaction), chemical indicators (pH, organic matter, nutrient levels), and biological indicators (microbial biomass, respiration, earthworm counts). Composite tests such as the Cornell Soil Health Assessment and the Haney test score soils against regional benchmarks; soil organic matter is widely treated as the master variable, influencing nearly all other properties.

Management principles

The widely promoted principles of soil-health management are: keep the soil covered; minimise physical and chemical disturbance (e.g. reduced tillage); keep living roots in the ground as much of the year as possible (cover crops); diversify crops and rotations; and, where appropriate, integrate grazing livestock. These are central to regenerative agriculture and to soil conservation programmes.

Significance

Soils hold more carbon than the atmosphere and all vegetation combined, so building soil organic matter is a climate-mitigation strategy, while degraded soils undermine food security and water quality. Interest in soil health has grown among farmers, researchers, and policymakers since the 2000s, reflected in initiatives such as France's "4 per 1000" proposal to raise soil carbon stocks annually.
