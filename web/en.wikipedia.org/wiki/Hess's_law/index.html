Hess's law

Hess's law of constant heat summation
Named afterGermain Hess (1802–1850)
Published1840
FieldThermochemistry
StatementTotal enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route between initial and final states

Hess's law of constant heat summation, or simply Hess's law, states that the total enthalpy change during a chemical reaction is the same whether the reaction takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final states are the same. It is a consequence of the fact that enthalpy is a state function and, more fundamentally, of the first law of thermodynamics (conservation of energy). The law was published in 1840 by the Swiss-born Russian chemist Germain Hess and is a foundation of thermochemistry.

Use

Hess's law lets chemists calculate enthalpy changes that cannot be measured directly. Standard enthalpies of formation of many compounds cannot be determined by direct synthesis — for example, carbon and hydrogen do not combine directly to give methane under measurable conditions — but the enthalpies of combustion of carbon, hydrogen and methane are easy to measure, and the enthalpy of formation follows by combining the equations in a Hess cycle:

ΔHreaction = Σ ΔHf(products) − Σ ΔHf(reactants)

The same method, using enthalpy cycles (closely related to the Born–Haber cycle for lattice enthalpies), yields enthalpies of reaction, hydration and atomisation used throughout physical and inorganic chemistry.

Example

The enthalpy of formation of carbon dioxide is obtained directly from burning carbon in oxygen. For methane, combustion data for the element pair and for CH4 are combined: the direct route C + 2H2 → CH4 must have the same enthalpy change as the indirect route in which the elements are burned and the products "un-burned", giving ΔHf(CH4) ≈ −75 kJ mol−1.
