Estrogens are a class of sex hormones responsible for the development and regulation of the female reproductive system and secondary sex characteristics. There are four major endogenous estrogens: estrone (E1), estradiol (E2), estriol (E3) and estetrol (E4). Estradiol is the predominant estrogen during the reproductive years, estrone predominates after menopause, and estriol is the main estrogen of pregnancy. Estrogens are present in all vertebrates and in both sexes, though at much higher levels in females of reproductive age.

Biosynthesis

Estrogens are synthesised from androgen precursors by the enzyme aromatase. In premenopausal women most estradiol is produced by the granulosa cells of the ovaries from androstenedione and testosterone supplied by the theca cells. During pregnancy the placenta produces large quantities of estriol. After menopause, estrogen production shifts largely to peripheral aromatisation in adipose tissue. Men produce small amounts of estradiol in the testes and by aromatisation, which is essential for bone maturation and the closure of the epiphyseal plates.

Mechanism of action

Estrogens diffuse across cell membranes and bind intracellular estrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ), which act as ligand-activated transcription factors regulating gene expression. Rapid, non-genomic effects are mediated by the membrane receptor GPER. Receptor distribution explains the hormone's wide-ranging effects on the uterus, breast, bone, liver, blood vessels, skin and brain.

Functions

Estrogens drive pubertal development of the female reproductive tract and breasts, regulate the menstrual cycle, and prepare the endometrium for implantation. They maintain bone density by restraining osteoclast activity — estrogen deficiency after menopause is a leading cause of osteoporosis — influence fat distribution, modulate cholesterol metabolism, and affect cognition and mood.

Medical uses

Synthetic and natural estrogens are used in combined oral contraceptives, in menopausal hormone therapy, and in feminising hormone therapy. Well-known preparations include ethinylestradiol and conjugated estrogens. Estrogen exposure also carries risks, including increased rates of venous thromboembolism and, depending on regimen and duration, effects on breast and endometrial cancer risk, which are weighed against benefits in clinical practice.

History

The existence of an ovarian hormone was demonstrated in the 1920s; Edward Doisy and others isolated estrone in 1929, and estriol and estradiol were characterised soon afterwards. Adolf Butenandt shared the 1939 Nobel Prize in Chemistry in part for work on sex hormones.
