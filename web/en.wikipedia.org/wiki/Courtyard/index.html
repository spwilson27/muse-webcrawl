Courtyard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A courtyard is an open area wholly or partly surrounded by walls or buildings, typically forming part of a larger building or complex. Courtyards are a recurring feature of architecture across many cultures and periods: they provide light, ventilation, security and a private outdoor space, and in hot climates they act as cool, shaded wells of air. The word derives from Old French court (an enclosed space) and "yard".

A colonnaded courtyard provides shade, ventilation and a private open-air room.

Historical forms

Ancient and classical

Courtyard houses appear from the earliest urban settlements in Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley. The Roman domus was organised around an atrium and often a peristyle garden court. In China the siheyuan — a compound of buildings around a rectangular courtyard — was the standard form of residence from the Ming dynasty onward. Islamic architecture made extensive use of courtyard houses and of the mosque courtyard (sahn), balancing privacy with climatic adaptation.

Medieval and Renaissance

Castles, monasteries (where the courtyard takes the form of the cloister), colleges and inns of court in medieval Europe were planned around courts. Renaissance palaces adopted the classical cortile, a regular, arcaded courtyard, while in Spain and Spanish America the patio became the organising centre of houses, palaces and civic buildings.

Functions

  • Climate: in hot, dry regions the courtyard traps cool night air and shades walls; fountains and planting add evaporative cooling.
  • Light and air: inner rooms of deep-plan buildings gain daylight and ventilation from the court.
  • Security and privacy: blank outer walls protect family life, a pattern central to traditional houses in the Middle East, North Africa and China.
  • Circulation and ceremony: cloisters, quadrangles and palace courts organise movement and formal gathering.

Modern usage

Courtyards remain common in schools, hospitals, hotels, housing blocks and office buildings, where they serve as light wells, gardens and social spaces. Urban designers value the courtyard block (perimeter block) for combining density with private open space. The term is also applied metaphorically and in place names, and "courtyard" housing is promoted in temperate climates for its shelter from wind and traffic noise.
