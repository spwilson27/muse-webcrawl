Waverley; or, 'Tis Sixty Years Since is a historical novel by Sir Walter Scott, published anonymously in 1814. It is generally regarded as the first historical novel in Western literature, and it launched Scott's enormously successful career as a novelist. The work is the first of the so-called Waverley Novels, a series Scott continued to publish anonymously for more than a decade, being known only as "the Author of Waverley."

Plot

The novel follows Edward Waverley, a young English officer and romantic dreamer, who is posted to Scotland in 1745. Through a series of accidents and friendships — including the Highland chief Fergus Mac-Ivor and his sister Flora — Waverley is drawn into the Jacobite rising of 1745, in which Charles Edward Stuart ("Bonnie Prince Charlie") attempts to regain the British throne for his father. Waverley is arrested for treason, escapes, joins the Jacobite army, and witnesses its defeat. Pardoned through the intercession of friends, he ultimately marries Rose Bradwardine and settles into a more sober vision of life.

Significance

Waverley established the template of the historical novel: a middling, somewhat passive hero moves between opposing cultures and historical forces, allowing the author to dramatise a moment of national transition — here, the clash between the modernising Hanoverian Lowlands and the feudal, clan-based Highlands whose world the '45 destroyed. The novel's portrait of Highland society, published within living memory of the rising, helped transform the Scottish Highlands in the public imagination from a zone of rebellion into a romantic landscape.

The novel was an immediate commercial success, selling out its first edition rapidly, and it influenced historical fiction across Europe and America, from Balzac and Pushkin to James Fenimore Cooper.
