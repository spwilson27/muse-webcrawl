Latent tuberculosis

Latent tuberculosis (LTB), also called latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), is a medical condition in which a person is infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the bacterium that causes tuberculosis (TB), but does not have active tuberculosis disease and cannot spread the infection to others. The bacteria live in the body without making the person sick.

About one-quarter of the world's population is estimated to be infected with M. tuberculosis, most of them with latent infection. People with latent infection have roughly a 5–15% lifetime risk of progressing to active TB, with the risk highest in the first two years after infection and in people with weakened immune systems, such as those with HIV.

Diagnosis

Latent tuberculosis is diagnosed with either the tuberculin skin test (Mantoux test) or interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), blood tests such as QuantiFERON. A normal chest X-ray and a negative sputum test distinguish latent infection from active disease. Tests cannot directly distinguish latent infection from active tuberculosis on their own.

Treatment

Treatment of latent infection greatly reduces the risk of progression to active disease. Regimens include isoniazid taken for six to nine months, rifampin for four months, or a once-weekly combination of isoniazid and rifapentine for three months. Shorter regimens have higher completion rates, an important consideration since many people start but do not finish longer courses.
