Balkan Peninsula

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Balkan Peninsula, known usually as the Balkans, is a geographical region in southeastern Europe with various geographical and historical definitions. It is bounded by the Adriatic Sea in the northwest, the Ionian Sea in the southwest, the Aegean Sea in the south, the Turkish straits in the east, and the Black Sea in the northeast. The northern border is often drawn along the Danube and Sava rivers. The region comprises all or parts of Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Greece, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Romania, Serbia, Slovenia and the European part of Turkey.

History and character

The Balkans were the cradle of Greek antiquity and a crossroads of Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman and Habsburg empires; the region's name comes from the Balkan Mountains of Bulgaria (from a Turkish word for wooded mountains). Ottoman rule lasted up to five centuries in some areas, and the national awakenings of the 19th and early 20th centuries produced a patchwork of new states, the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, and the Sarajevo assassination that sparked the First World War. In the 1990s the federation of Yugoslavia disintegrated in a series of wars — in Croatia, Bosnia and Kosovo — that brought ethnic cleansing back to Europe. Most Balkan states are now in, or seeking membership of, the European Union and NATO; Greece borders the region's south, and the peninsula's Black Sea coast faces the Black Sea.
