A cargo cult is a religious movement, usually arising among indigenous peoples in colonial situations, centred on the belief that manufactured goods ("cargo") sent by ancestral or spiritual beings to the colonisers rightfully belong to the colonised, and that ritual action will cause the cargo to be delivered to them instead. The term is most closely associated with Melanesian movements in the South Pacific from the late 19th century through the Second World War and after.

Characteristics

Followers observed Europeans and Americans receiving vast quantities of goods by ship and aircraft without visibly making them, and concluded that the cargo originated with the ancestors or gods and was being intercepted. Movements imitated the outward forms associated with the cargo's arrival: building wharves, warehouses and airstrips, marching with wooden rifles, raising flags, and wearing imitations of uniforms, in the expectation that the rituals would attract the cargo. Prophets typically promised that the foreigners would leave, the ancestors would return, and an age of abundance would begin.

The most famous surviving movement honours "John Frum" on the island of Tanna in Vanuatu, where followers still parade each 15 February and raise the flag of the United States. The Prince Philip movement, also in Vanuatu, venerated the Duke of Edinburgh as a divine figure.

Interpretation

Anthropologists debate whether "cargo cult" — a label applied by outsiders — fairly describes these movements; many see them as rational responses to colonial disruption, early forms of political organisation, and attempts to explain and access the material power of the colonisers. The term has passed into general usage metaphorically, for example in Richard Feynman's phrase "cargo cult science".
