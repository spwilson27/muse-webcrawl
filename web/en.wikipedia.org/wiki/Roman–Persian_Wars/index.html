The Roman–Persian Wars, also known as the Roman–Iranian Wars, were a series of conflicts between states of the Greco-Roman world and two successive Iranian empires — the Parthian and the Sasanian — lasting from 92 BCE, when Rome and Parthia first made contact, until 628 CE. They form one of the longest-running rivalries of the ancient world, fought chiefly over Armenia, Mesopotamia and the trade routes of the Near East.

Roman–Parthian conflicts

Early encounters were disastrous for Rome: Crassus's army was annihilated at Carrhae in 53 BCE, and Mark Antony's invasion of Media failed in 36 BCE. Trajan briefly conquered Mesopotamia in 114–117 CE and sacked Ctesiphon, but Hadrian abandoned the new provinces. Later emperors, including Lucius Verus and Septimius Severus, again captured Ctesiphon, yet Parthia survived until its overthrow by the Sasanians in 224 CE.

Roman–Sasanian conflicts

The Sasanian Empire was more centralised and aggressive. Shapur I defeated and captured the emperor Valerian at Edessa in 260 CE. Diocletian and Galerius restored Roman fortunes, gaining territory by the Peace of Nisibis (299). In the fourth century Shapur II waged long wars against Constantius II and Julian, whose invasion of Persia ended with his death in 363. The fifth century was comparatively peaceful.

The final war, 602–628

The last and greatest war began when Khosrow II invaded after the murder of the emperor Maurice. Persian armies conquered Syria, Egypt and much of Anatolia, reaching Constantinople in 626 in alliance with the Avars. The emperor Heraclius counter-attacked deep into Persia, winning the decisive Battle of Nineveh in 627; Khosrow was overthrown and killed, and peace restored the old borders. Exhausted, both empires were swiftly overrun by the Arab Muslim conquests of the 630s–650s.

Legacy

Seven centuries of warfare entrenched fortified frontiers (the limes), shaped diplomacy and military practice in both states, and left Armenia a perennial battleground and buffer kingdom.
