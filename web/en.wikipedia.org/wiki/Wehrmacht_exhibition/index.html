Wehrmacht exhibition

The Wehrmacht exhibition (German: Wehrmachtsausstellung) was a series of two travelling exhibitions organised by the Hamburg Institute for Social Research focusing on war crimes of the Wehrmacht, the regular armed forces of Nazi Germany, during the Second World War on the Eastern Front. The full title of the first exhibition was Vernichtungskrieg. Verbrechen der Wehrmacht 1941 bis 1944 ("War of Annihilation. Crimes of the Wehrmacht 1941 to 1944").

The first exhibition (1995–1999)

The first exhibition opened in Hamburg in March 1995 and toured 33 cities in Germany and Austria, attracting around 900,000 visitors. Using some 1,400 photographs and documents, it challenged the popular post-war myth of the "clean Wehrmacht" — the belief that the regular armed forces had not participated in Nazi crimes, which were instead blamed solely on the SS and other Nazi organisations. The exhibition provoked intense public debate, protests, and counter-demonstrations, including a bombing attack on the exhibition in Saarbrücken in 1999.

Criticism and withdrawal

Historians Bogdan Musiał and Krisztián Ungváry demonstrated that a number of photographs had been incorrectly attributed — some showed victims of the Soviet NKVD rather than of the Wehrmacht, or crimes by other forces. In November 1999 the Hamburg Institute withdrew the exhibition and appointed an independent commission of historians to review it. The commission confirmed errors in a small proportion of the image captions but upheld the exhibition's central thesis about Wehrmacht involvement in crimes.

The second exhibition (2001–2004)

A thoroughly revised second exhibition, Verbrechen der Wehrmacht. Dimensionen des Vernichtungskrieges 1941–1944 ("Crimes of the Wehrmacht. Dimensions of the War of Annihilation 1941–1944"), opened in Berlin in November 2001 and toured until 2004, with far fewer photographs and a greater emphasis on documents. It is widely credited with consolidating the scholarly consensus that the Wehrmacht as an institution was deeply complicit in the crimes of the Nazi regime.
