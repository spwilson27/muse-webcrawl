Asura

Asuras (Sanskrit: असुर) are a class of beings in Indic religions, described as power-seeking and often opposed to the devas (gods). In the earliest Vedic texts, asura could be an honorific applied to powerful deities such as Varuna, but in later Hindu mythology the asuras became the perennial adversaries of the devas, with whom they are said to be related through the sage Kashyapa. In Buddhism, asuras form one of the six realms of rebirth, characterised by pride, envy, and constant strife.

In Hinduism

Famous asuras of the epics and Puranas include Hiranyakashipu, slain by Vishnu's man-lion avatar Narasimha; Mahishasura, slain by the goddess Durga; Ravana, the antagonist of the Ramayana; and Bali, humbled by the dwarf avatar Vamana. The Samudra Manthana, the churning of the ocean of milk, is a joint enterprise of devas and asuras competing for the nectar of immortality (amrita). Scholars note the striking reversal in ancient Iranian religion, where ahura is the title of the supreme god Ahura Mazda, while daeva — cognate with Sanskrit deva — denotes demons.

In Buddhism

Buddhist cosmology places the asuras in a realm just below the devas of the Trayastrimsa heaven, with whom they wage perpetual war over the wish-fulfilling tree. Their condition — powerful yet consumed by jealousy and anger — is used to illustrate the suffering inherent in samsara. In East Asian Buddhism, asuras (Japanese: ashura) also appear as fierce guardian figures.
