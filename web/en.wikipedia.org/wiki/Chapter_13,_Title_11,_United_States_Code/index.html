Chapter 13, Title 11, United States Code

Chapter 13 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, codified at 11 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1330, provides for the adjustment of debts of an individual with regular income. Commonly called a "wage earner's plan," it allows a debtor to propose a court-supervised repayment plan over three to five years, rather than liquidating assets as in Chapter 7.

Chapter 13 plans are administered through the federal bankruptcy courts under a standing trustee.

Overview

Only individuals (not corporations or partnerships) with regular income and debts below statutory ceilings may file under Chapter 13. Upon filing, an automatic stay halts most collection actions, foreclosures and repossessions. A Chapter 13 trustee evaluates the case and disburses payments to creditors.

The repayment plan

The debtor proposes a plan, typically lasting three years (five for debtors above the state median income), under which future income is paid to the trustee for distribution. Secured debts such as mortgages may be cured of arrears over the plan's life, and the debtor keeps property — including a home — provided plan payments are made. Priority debts such as recent taxes and child support must be paid in full; general unsecured creditors receive at least as much as they would in a Chapter 7 liquidation. On completion of payments, remaining dischargeable debts are discharged.

Comparison with other chapters

Unlike Chapter 7 liquidation, Chapter 13 lets debtors stop foreclosure and catch up on secured debts, and it protects co-signers through a co-debtor stay. Chapter 11, by contrast, is used by businesses and high-debt individuals for reorganisation. A Chapter 13 case may be dismissed or converted to Chapter 7 if the debtor fails to make plan payments.
