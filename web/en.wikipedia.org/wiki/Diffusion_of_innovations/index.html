Diffusion of innovations is a theory that seeks to explain how, why and at what rate new ideas and technology spread through cultures and social systems. It was popularized by American communication theorist and sociologist Everett Rogers in his book Diffusion of Innovations, first published in 1962 and repeatedly revised. Rogers defined diffusion as the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system.

The model

Cumulative adoption over time typically follows an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve: adoption begins slowly among innovators (about 2.5% of a population), accelerates as early adopters (13.5%) — often respected opinion leaders — take it up, proceeds through the early majority (34%) and late majority (34%), and trails off with laggards (16%). Plotting adoptions per time period gives a bell-shaped curve. Rogers identified five attributes that govern the rate of adoption: relative advantage over what the innovation replaces, compatibility with existing values and practices, complexity (inversely), trialability and observability of results. The decision process itself moves through knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation and confirmation, and may end in rejection or later discontinuance. The intellectual roots lie in rural sociology, notably Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross's 1943 study of hybrid seed corn adoption among Iowa farmers, and in Gabriel Tarde's earlier sociological work on imitation.

Applications and criticism

The theory has been applied in public health, agriculture, education, marketing and technology management; Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm (1991) adapted it for high-tech marketing, arguing a "chasm" separates early adopters from the early majority. Critics note a pro-innovation bias (an assumption that innovations ought to spread), an individual-blame bias that attributes non-adoption to laggards rather than to structural constraints, and a tendency to treat the innovation as fixed rather than reinvented by users — a phenomenon Rogers himself later acknowledged.
