Cardiac glycoside

[Image placeholder: Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea), a natural source of cardiac glycosides]

Cardiac glycosides are a class of organic compounds that increase the output force of the heart and decrease its rate of contractions by inhibiting the cellular sodium–potassium ATPase pump. They consist of a steroid aglycone — a cardenolide (with a five-membered lactone ring) or a bufadienolide (with a six-membered ring) — linked to one or more sugar residues.

Sources

Cardiac glycosides occur naturally in plants including foxglove (Digitalis purpurea and D. lanata), oleander, lily of the valley, and Strophanthus species, and in the skin secretions of some toads (Bufo). Historically they were used as arrow poisons and rat poisons as well as medicines.

Medical use

Digoxin, isolated from Digitalis lanata, remains in clinical use for heart failure and for rate control in atrial fibrillation, although its role has diminished with the advent of safer alternatives. William Withering's 1785 account of the foxglove is a landmark in the history of pharmacology. Ouabain, from Strophanthus, is used mainly as a research tool.

Toxicity

Cardiac glycosides have a narrow therapeutic index. Overdose produces nausea, visual disturbances (including yellow-tinted vision), and potentially fatal arrhythmias; severe poisoning can be treated with digoxin-specific antibody fragments.
