Offences Against the Person Act 1861

Citation24 & 25 Vict. c. 100
Enacted1861
JurisdictionEngland and Wales; Northern Ireland
StatusAmended, partly in force

The Offences Against the Person Act 1861 (24 & 25 Vict. c. 100) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that consolidated the law of non-fatal offences against the person in England and Wales. Remarkably for its age, it remains the principal statute governing offences such as wounding and assault occasioning actual bodily harm, though it has long been criticised for its archaic language.

Key provisions

The best-known surviving sections are: section 18, wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent (maximum sentence: life imprisonment); section 20, unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm (maximum five years); section 47, assault occasioning actual bodily harm (maximum five years); and section 16, threats to kill. The Act also originally contained the provisions on abortion in sections 58 and 59, which remained the basis of abortion law in England and Wales until modified by the Abortion Act 1967, and which still govern abortion in Northern Ireland's legal history.

Criticism and reform

The Law Commission has repeatedly recommended replacing the Act, calling it "inefficient, outdated and obscure", with Victorian phrasing such as "maliciously" requiring layers of case law to interpret. Draft replacement bills were published in 1998 and again following a 2015 report, but the Act remained in force, with prosecutors and courts continuing to rely on its sections daily.

Related offences

Common assault and battery were made summary offences under section 39 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988, but the more serious offences in the 1861 Act remain the backbone of charging practice for violence in England and Wales.
