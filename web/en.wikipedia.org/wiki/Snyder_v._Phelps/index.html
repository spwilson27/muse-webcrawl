Snyder v. Phelps

A Westboro Baptist Church picket of the kind at issue in the case

Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), was a landmark United States Supreme Court case in which the Court held that the First Amendment protects the right of the Westboro Baptist Church to picket a military funeral, even though the protest targeted a deceased service member and caused emotional distress to his family.

Background

Members of the Westboro Baptist Church, led by Fred Phelps, had for years picketed military funerals to promote their view that God punishes the United States for tolerating homosexuality. In March 2006 they protested the funeral of Marine Lance Corporal Matthew Snyder in Westminster, Maryland, standing on public land about 1,000 feet from the church hosting the service, in compliance with police instructions. Snyder's father, Albert Snyder, saw only the tops of the signs at the funeral but later read their messages and the church's online "epic" about his son, and sued for intentional infliction of emotional distress, intrusion upon seclusion, and civil conspiracy. A jury awarded him about $10.9 million, later reduced.

Decision

On 2 March 2011 the Supreme Court ruled 8–1 for Phelps. Chief Justice John Roberts's majority opinion held that speech on matters of public concern, delivered in a public place, occupies the highest rung of First Amendment protection and cannot be punished on the ground that it is outrageous or distressing. Justice Samuel Alito dissented alone, arguing that the protest was a targeted attack on a private family.

Significance

The case reaffirmed broad protection for offensive speech on public issues and influenced later free-speech doctrine on tort claims based on speech. In response to funeral protests, Congress and many states enacted buffer-zone laws regulating protest near funerals rather than the speech itself, including the Honoring America's Veterans and Caring for Camp Lejeune Families Act of 2012.
