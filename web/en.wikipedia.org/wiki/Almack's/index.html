Almack's was a social club and assembly rooms in King Street, St James's, London, famous in Regency England as the most exclusive venue of the London season. Its weekly subscription balls were governed by a committee of lady patronesses whose decisions on admission amounted to a verdict of social acceptance or ruin.

History

The rooms were opened in 1765 by William Almack (a Scot who had inverted his real surname, Macall, to disguise it), who also founded the gentlemen's club that became Brooks's. Almack's offered a ballroom 100 feet long, gaming rooms, and supper rooms. Membership was by balloting, limited and jealously guarded.

The patronesses

During the Regency the patronesses — among them Lady Castlereagh, Lady Jersey, Lady Cowper, Lady Sefton, Lady Willoughby de Eresby and Princess Esterházy — issued non-transferable vouchers and enforced strict rules: doors closed at eleven o'clock (later eleven-thirty), so that even the Duke of Wellington was famously turned away for arriving late; dress requirements forbade trousers in favour of knee breeches; and the dances, refreshments and supper (bread, butter and cake) were deliberately modest. To be denied a voucher — or to lose one — could blight a young person's prospects; to receive one was the seal of fashion.

Decline

As the century wore on the assembly's exclusivity faded; in 1871 the rooms were renamed Willis's Rooms after a new owner. The building was demolished in 1903. Almack's survives in the novels of Georgette Heyer and other chroniclers of the Regency as the epitome of the marriage market of high society.
