Unruh effect


An accelerating detector in the vacuum registers a thermal bath of particles.
Unruh effect
Predicted byWilliam G. Unruh (1976); related work by Stephen Fulling (1973) and Paul Davies (1975)
FieldQuantum field theory, general relativity
FormulaT = ħa / (2πckB)
Related toHawking radiation

The Unruh effect (or sometimes the Fulling–Davies–Unruh effect) is a kinematic prediction of quantum field theory that an accelerating observer will observe a thermal bath of black-body radiation, whereas an inertial observer in the same region of spacetime observes no particles — that is, the very notion of a particle depends on the observer's state of motion.

Explanation

The effect means that the concept of a vacuum depends on the path of the observer through spacetime. An observer who accelerates uniformly through the Minkowski vacuum with proper acceleration a will find the vacuum to have a temperature

T = ħa / (2πckB)

where ħ is the reduced Planck constant, c the speed of light, and kB the Boltzmann constant. The effect is tiny: an acceleration of about 1020 m/s² is required to raise the temperature by 1 kelvin, making direct experimental confirmation extremely difficult.

History and relation to Hawking radiation

Stephen Fulling noted the observer-dependence of particles in 1973, Paul Davies extended the analysis in 1975, and William Unruh published the full treatment in 1976. The Unruh effect is closely related to Hawking radiation: an observer hovering just above a black hole's event horizon must accelerate enormously to avoid falling in, and the "thermal atmosphere" they perceive corresponds to the radiation that escapes to infinity as Hawking radiation.

Experimental status

The Unruh effect has not been directly observed because of the extreme accelerations required, though related phenomena — notably the dynamical Casimir effect and analogue models in condensed matter and atomic physics — have been studied as indirect tests. Proposed experiments using ultra-intense lasers and storage rings continue to be debated.
