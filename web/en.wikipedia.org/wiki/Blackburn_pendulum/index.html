Blackburn pendulum

A Blackburn pendulum drawing a harmonographic curve

A Blackburn pendulum is a device for demonstrating harmonic motion, invented by Professor Hugh Blackburn (1823–1909) of the University of Glasgow. It is a pendulum in which the bob is suspended by a V-shaped (bifilar) arrangement so that it oscillates along two different axes with two different periods. The resulting motion traces out curves known as Lissajous figures, making the pendulum a classic example of a harmonograph.

Construction and operation

In the Blackburn pendulum, the bob hangs from a string that splits into two branches of unequal effective length. Because the effective pendulum length differs between the two perpendicular directions of swing, the bob oscillates with two distinct frequencies at once. The superposition of the two simple harmonic motions produces a Lissajous curve whose shape depends on the ratio of the two frequencies and on the phase difference between them. With tuned length ratios, the bob traces lines, ellipses, circles, and more intricate closed patterns.

Harmonographs

Blackburn's pendulum was widely adopted in 19th-century physics lecture demonstrations and drawing instruments. Devices based on the same principle — one or two pendulums carrying a pen above a moving table — were sold as harmonographs and as toys, producing intricate geometric drawings that were popular in the Victorian era. The patterns also inspired artistic works and early studies of acoustics and vibration, since the same frequency ratios that generate consonant musical intervals generate simple, stable figures.
