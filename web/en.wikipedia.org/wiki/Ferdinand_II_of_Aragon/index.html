Ferdinand II of Aragon (Spanish: Fernando II; 10 March 1452 – 23 January 1516), called Ferdinand the Catholic, was King of Aragon from 1479 until his death and, by his marriage to Isabella I, co-ruler of Castile (as Ferdinand V) from 1474. Together the "Catholic Monarchs" (Reyes Católicos, a title granted by Pope Alexander VI) united the two great Iberian crowns, completed the conquest of Granada, sponsored Columbus's first voyage, and laid the foundations of Spain as a unified monarchy and global empire.

Reign

Ferdinand married Isabella in 1469 and helped secure her throne in the War of the Castilian Succession (1475–1479). The monarchs created the Spanish Inquisition (1478), conquered the Nasrid kingdom of Granada — surrendering on 2 January 1492 — and that same year issued the Alhambra Decree expelling Spain's Jews and financed Christopher Columbus's expedition, which reached the Americas in October 1492. Ferdinand, a master of diplomacy Machiavelli admired in The Prince, extended Spanish power in Italy, recovering Naples (1504) and annexing Navarre (1512).

Later life and succession

After Isabella's death in 1504, Ferdinand lost Castile to his daughter Joanna and her husband Philip of Burgundy, serving as regent after Philip's death in 1506. His second marriage, to Germaine of Foix, produced no surviving heir. He died in 1516 and was succeeded in both crowns by his grandson Charles I (later the Emperor Charles V). He is buried beside Isabella in the Royal Chapel of Granada.
