Templon

A templon (Greek: τέμπλον, "temple") is a feature of Byzantine church architecture: a barrier separating the sanctuary (bema) from the nave in an Orthodox church. Originally a low marble enclosure of posts and parapet slabs (chancel barriers), the templon developed over the medieval period into the tall, multi-tiered iconostasis familiar in Orthodox churches today, covered with icons.

History

Early Christian sanctuaries were screened by low cancelli. In Byzantine usage the screen became a colonnade with an architrave (epistyle) from which lamps and later panel icons were hung. From about the 11th century, icons were placed between the columns, and by the Palaiologan period (13th–15th centuries) the templon had become a full wall of icons — the iconostasis — with three doors: the central Holy Doors and two deacon's doors.

Structure

A developed templon consists of tiers: local icons flanking the Holy Doors (Christ, the Theotokos, the church's patron saint, John the Baptist forming the Deesis), a festival tier (dodekaorton) of the twelve great feasts, and above, apostles, prophets and a crucifixion. Curtains behind the doors veil the altar during parts of the liturgy.

Significance

The templon marks the boundary between the nave, representing the earthly church, and the sanctuary, representing heaven; its icons mediate between the two. Debate continues on how far the screen symbolised separation versus revelation.
