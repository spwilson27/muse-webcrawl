The 1975 Australian constitutional crisis, often referred to simply as the Dismissal, culminated on 11 November 1975 when Governor-General Sir John Kerr dismissed Gough Whitlam of the Australian Labor Party as prime minister and appointed the leader of the opposition, Malcolm Fraser, as caretaker prime minister. It is the most dramatic constitutional event in Australian history and the only time a governor-general has dismissed an elected government that retained the confidence of the House of Representatives.

Background

Whitlam's Labor government, elected in 1972 and re-elected in a double-dissolution election in May 1974, pursued an ambitious reform programme but was battered by economic turmoil (stagflation after the 1973 oil shock) and scandals, notably the "Loans affair," in which ministers sought unauthorised overseas loans. Although Labor held the House of Representatives, the Liberal–Country opposition controlled the Senate.

The supply crisis and dismissal

In October 1975 the opposition used its Senate numbers to defer passage of the appropriation ("supply") bills, aiming to force Whitlam to call an election. Whitlam refused, arguing that a government with the confidence of the lower house should govern. As funds ran out, Kerr — after consulting Chief Justice Sir Garfield Barwick, and without warning Whitlam — withdrew Whitlam's commission on 11 November 1975 and commissioned Fraser, who guaranteed supply and an immediate double-dissolution election. On the steps of Parliament House, Whitlam famously declared: "Well may we say 'God save the Queen', because nothing will save the Governor-General."

Aftermath

The Fraser caretaker government passed supply and, at the election of 13 December 1975, the Coalition won the largest majority in Australian federal history to that point. The crisis provoked lasting debate about the reserve powers of the governor-general, the Senate's power over supply, and the role of the Crown; it remains a touchstone of the Australian republic debate. The "Palace letters" — Kerr's correspondence with Queen Elizabeth II's private secretary — were released by the National Archives in July 2020 following a High Court ruling.
