Flavin adenine dinucleotide

In biochemistry, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) is a redox-active coenzyme associated with various proteins (flavoproteins), which is involved with several enzymatic reactions in metabolism. A flavoprotein is a protein that contains a flavin group, which may be in the form of FAD or flavin mononucleotide (FMN). FAD is derived from riboflavin (vitamin B2).

Structure and chemistry

FAD consists of an adenine nucleotide (adenosine monophosphate) joined through a pyrophosphate bridge to flavin mononucleotide, itself composed of riboflavin attached to a phosphate group. The redox-active part of the molecule is the isoalloxazine ring of the flavin, which can accept either one electron (forming the semiquinone radical FADH•) or two electrons and two protons (forming FADH2).

Role in metabolism

FAD serves as an electron carrier in key metabolic pathways. In the citric acid cycle, succinate dehydrogenase oxidises succinate to fumarate and reduces FAD to FADH2 within Complex II of the respiratory chain, yielding roughly 1.5 ATP per FADH2 oxidised — fewer than NADH because its electrons enter the chain at a later point. FAD is also the cofactor for acyl-CoA dehydrogenases in fatty acid beta-oxidation, for monoamine oxidases, and for the E3 component (dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase) of the pyruvate and alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complexes.

History

The flavin cofactors were characterised in the 1930s through work on the "old yellow enzyme" by Otto Warburg and others; Hugo Theorell separated the flavin from the protein and showed the coenzyme nature of the group, work contributing to his 1955 Nobel Prize.
