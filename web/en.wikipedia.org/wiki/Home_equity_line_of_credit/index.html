Home equity line of credit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A home equity line of credit (HELOC) is a revolving line of credit secured by the borrower's home equity. Like a credit card, a HELOC lets the borrower draw funds up to an approved limit, repay them, and draw again during the draw period; unlike a credit card, the loan is secured by the borrower's home, which is at risk of foreclosure if the borrower defaults.

How it works

The credit limit is usually set as a percentage of the home's appraised value minus the outstanding mortgage balance, commonly allowing total borrowing of up to 80–85 per cent of the home's value. Interest is charged only on the amount drawn. Most HELOCs have a draw period (often 5–10 years) during which the borrower may borrow and typically makes interest-only payments, followed by a repayment period (often 10–20 years) during which the balance must be repaid in instalments of principal and interest.

Interest rates

HELOCs almost always carry variable interest rates, typically indexed to a benchmark such as the prime rate plus a margin. This makes them cheaper than most unsecured credit when rates are low, but payments can rise substantially if interest rates increase or when the repayment period begins.

Uses and risks

Borrowers commonly use HELOCs for home improvements, education expenses or consolidating higher-interest debt. The main risks are the variable rate, the sharp payment increase at the end of the draw period, and the fact that the debt is secured against the home itself.
