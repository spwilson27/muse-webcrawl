North Ronaldsay sheep

Sheep grazing seaweed on the North Ronaldsay shore

The North Ronaldsay sheep is a rare breed of sheep originating on North Ronaldsay, the northernmost island of Orkney, Scotland. It is a member of the Northern European short-tailed sheep group and is remarkable for subsisting largely on seaweed.

The sheep dyke

Around 1832 the islanders built a drystone wall, the sheep dyke, roughly 13 miles (21 km) long around the coast, confining the sheep to the shore. The wall — one of the largest drystone structures of its kind — kept the sheep off the cultivated land and forced them onto a diet of kelp and other seaweeds exposed at low tide. The dyke is maintained by islanders, and a "sheep court" traditionally organised communal work and the roundup (punding) of the flock.

Characteristics

The sheep are small, hardy and slow-maturing, with fine wool in a wide range of natural colours including white, grey, brown and black. Their digestive systems have adapted to the seaweed diet, which is rich in minerals but low in copper; the breed is unusually efficient at absorbing copper, and consequently vulnerable to copper poisoning if fed ordinary sheep rations on inland pasture.

Conservation

The breed is classed as vulnerable by the Rare Breeds Survival Trust, with numbers in the low thousands. The dyke itself was badly damaged by storms in 2012, prompting restoration efforts. The island's sheep festival and volunteer "dyke" programmes have drawn attention to maintaining both the wall and the breed's unique management system.
