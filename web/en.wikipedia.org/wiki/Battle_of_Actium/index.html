Battle of Actium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The rostral column monument of Octavian at Nicopolis, decorated with captured ship prows]

The Battle of Actium was a naval battle fought on 2 September 31 BC in the Ionian Sea near Actium in Greece, between the forces of Octavian (the future Augustus), commanded at sea by Marcus Agrippa, and the combined fleet of Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII of Egypt. Antony's heavy warships, blockaded, diseased and short of crews, attempted to break out; in the fighting Cleopatra's squadron sailed through a gap and away, Antony followed with part of his fleet, and the rest surrendered or was captured within days — his land army capitulating a week later. Actium ended the civil wars of the Roman Republic and cleared Octavian's path to become Augustus, first emperor of Rome.

The battle and its problems

The sources — written under the victor's regime — frame the day as Cleopatra's treacherous flight dragging Antony after her; modern reconstructions suggest a planned breakout by a detachment, with the treasure and sails aboard, that the rest of the fleet failed to join once wind and battle turned. Antony's larger quinqueremes, undermanned after a malaria-ridden winter on short rations, could not match Agrippa's manoeuvrable liburnians; after hours of standoff and close fighting, the breakout of some sixty Egyptian ships and Antony's escape with a fraction of his fleet decided nothing but his personal survival.

Aftermath

Antony's abandoned army surrendered and was settled as veterans; Octavian pursued the pair to Egypt, where in August 30 BC Alexandria fell and Antony and Cleopatra died by suicide. Egypt was annexed as the emperor's personal province, its treasure funding Octavian's settlement. In 27 BC he took the name Augustus. Actium is thus conventionally the birthday of the Roman Empire — and its propaganda (Virgil's shield of Aeneas, Horace's odes) fixed the image of the battle for two thousand years.
