Surface weather analysis

Surface weather analysis is a special type of weather map that provides a view of weather elements over a geographical area at a specified time based on information from ground-based weather stations. Weather maps are created by plotting or tracing the values of relevant quantities such as sea level pressure, temperature, and cloud cover onto a geographical map to help find features such as pressure systems and frontal boundaries.

Surface analyses are used by meteorologists to locate and analyze synoptic-scale features such as high and low pressure centers, cold fronts, warm fronts, occluded fronts, and stationary fronts. The first weather maps in the 19th century were drawn well after the fact to help devise a theory on storm systems. With the advent of the telegraph, simultaneous surface weather observations became possible for the first time, and beginning in the late 1840s the Smithsonian Institution became the first organization to draw real-time surface analyses.

History

The use of frontal analysis on surface weather maps began in the United States in the late 1940s, following the pioneering work of the Bergen School of Meteorology in Norway in the 1910s. Norwegian cyclone model theory described the lifecycle of mid-latitude cyclones in terms of fronts and air masses. During the first decades the practice was often subjective, but the advent of computers has led to techniques of objective frontal analysis.

Station models

Surface observations are encoded in a compact notation known as the station model (or station plot), which packs temperature, dew point, wind speed and direction, sea level pressure and its tendency, present weather, and cloud cover into a small symbol cluster centered on the station's location. This allows a large amount of data to be displayed on a single map.

Isobars and isallobars

Lines of equal sea level pressure, called isobars, are drawn to reveal the pressure pattern. Closely spaced isobars indicate strong winds. Lines of equal pressure change over a given period, called isallobars, help identify where pressure is rising or falling most rapidly and hence where systems are strengthening or weakening.
