The Evolution of Cooperation is a 1984 book by political scientist Robert Axelrod that explores how cooperative behavior can emerge and persist among self-interested agents through repeated interactions. It grew out of Axelrod's famous computer tournaments of the iterated prisoner's dilemma and became a foundational text in game theory, evolutionary biology, and the social sciences.

The tournaments

Axelrod invited game theorists and other specialists to submit computer programs to play rounds of the iterated prisoner's dilemma against one another. In both of Axelrod's tournaments, the winning entry was the simple strategy "tit for tat", submitted by psychologist Anatol Rapoport: cooperate on the first move, then copy the opponent's previous move. Analyzing the results, Axelrod identified the properties of successful strategies: they should be nice (never defect first), retaliatory (punish defection), forgiving (return to cooperation after punishing), and clear (readily understood by opponents).

Argument and applications

Drawing on examples including the "live and let live" system of trench warfare in the First World War, Axelrod argued that cooperation can evolve "in a world of egoists without central control" when the shadow of the future is long enough — that is, when interactions are repeated and the participants value future encounters. The book develops principles for promoting cooperation among individuals, firms, and nations, including enlarging the shadow of the future, changing payoffs, and teaching reciprocity.

Influence

The book helped launch the modern study of the evolution of cooperation and reciprocal altruism, influenced evolutionary psychologists and economists, and inspired a large subsequent literature, including Axelrod's own The Complexity of Cooperation (1997). A revised edition was published in 2006 with a foreword by Richard Dawkins.
