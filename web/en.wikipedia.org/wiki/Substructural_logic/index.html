Substructural logic

Substructural logics are non-classical logics that lack one or more of the structural rules ordinarily assumed in classical and intuitionistic logic. In a sequent calculus, structural rules govern the manipulation of hypotheses rather than any logical connective: weakening (adding unused assumptions), contraction (using one assumption multiple times), and exchange (reordering assumptions). A logic missing at least one of these is called substructural.

Motivation

Restricting structural rules makes a logic sensitive to resources: each assumption must be used a controlled number of times and in a controlled order. This resource interpretation is valuable in computer science (tracking memory, state, and concurrency), in linguistics (where word order and resource consumption matter in grammatical frameworks such as the Lambek calculus), and in the philosophy of logic.

Principal examples

  • Linear logic (Girard, 1987) restricts both weakening and contraction, treating premises as resources that must be consumed exactly once; multiplicative and additive fragments arise from this discipline.
  • Relevance logic drops the unrestricted weakening rule, requiring premises to be genuinely relevant to the conclusion, thereby avoiding the paradoxes of material implication.
  • Affine logic permits weakening (resources may be discarded) but not contraction (resources may not be duplicated).
  • Ordered logic and the Lambek calculus additionally drop exchange, making inference sensitive to the order of premises.
  • Intuitionistic logic relative to the sequent calculus of classical logic restricts contraction/weakening on the right side — a mild substructural feature sometimes noted in this taxonomy.

Semantics and applications

Substructural logics admit algebraic semantics via residuated lattices and operational semantics in proof theory. Linear logic in particular has influenced programming-language design — its resource sensitivity underlies linear type systems and systems such as Rust's ownership discipline at an intuitive level — and has applications in quantum logic, concurrency, and the semantics of natural language.
