Spin–spin relaxation

In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), spin–spin relaxation is the decay of the transverse component of the magnetisation vector, characterised by the time constant T2. It is also called transverse relaxation.

Mechanism

After excitation, the nuclear spins precess in phase. Each spin experiences slightly different local magnetic fields produced by neighbouring spins and by molecular motion, so the spins gradually lose phase coherence and the transverse magnetisation decays exponentially with time constant T2. Unlike spin–lattice relaxation (T1), T2 involves no net energy transfer to the surroundings — it is an entropy-driven dephasing process.

Measurement and significance

T2 is measured with spin-echo sequences (Hahn echo, CPMG), which refocus dephasing from static field inhomogeneities; the remaining decay reflects true T2, while the observed free-induction decay gives the shorter T2*. Because T2 differs between tissues and fluids, T2-weighted MRI contrast is fundamental to clinical imaging, and T2 relaxometry is used in food science and petroleum well logging.
