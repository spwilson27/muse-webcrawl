Pi bond

In chemistry, pi bonds (π bonds) are covalent chemical bonds in which two lobes of an orbital on one atom overlap with two lobes of an orbital on another atom, with the electron density concentrated above and below the plane of the nuclei. The name derives from the Greek letter π, referring to p orbitals, since the orbital symmetry of the pi bond is the same as that of the p orbital when seen down the bond axis.

Properties

Pi bonds occur in multiple bonds: a double bond consists of one sigma bond and one pi bond, and a triple bond of one sigma and two pi bonds. A pi bond alone is weaker than a sigma bond between the same atoms, and the side-by-side overlap is less effective than the head-on overlap of sigma bonds. Crucially, rotation about a bond containing a pi component is strongly hindered, because twisting would break the p-orbital overlap; this rigidity gives rise to cis–trans (E/Z) isomerism in alkenes.

Examples and reactivity

Pi bonds are found in alkenes, alkynes, carbonyl compounds, and aromatic systems, where delocalised pi electrons spread over several atoms, as described by resonance and molecular orbital theory. The exposed electron density of pi bonds makes them reactive sites for electrophilic addition reactions, and pi bonding between transition metals and ligands (backbonding) is central to organometallic chemistry.
