Vascular plants (from Latin vasculum, "small vessel"), also called tracheophytes, are plants possessing specialized conducting tissues — the xylem, which transports water and minerals from the roots, and the phloem, which distributes the sugars produced by photosynthesis. These tissues allowed plants to grow tall, develop true roots, stems and leaves, and colonize dry land far from water. Vascular plants dominate nearly all terrestrial ecosystems and include the great majority of familiar plants.

Characteristics

The xylem's water-conducting cells, tracheids and vessel elements, are dead at maturity and reinforced with lignin, giving vascular plants structural support as well as conduction. The dominant generation in the life cycle is the diploid sporophyte, which is independent of the much-reduced gametophyte — a reversal of the pattern in bryophytes (mosses and liverworts), the non-vascular land plants.

Major groups

The living vascular plants fall into two great clades. The lycophytes (clubmosses, spikemosses and quillworts) are an ancient lineage with small leaves bearing a single vein. The euphyllophytes comprise the ferns and horsetails — seedless plants reproducing by spores — and the seed plants (spermatophytes), which reproduce by seed and include the gymnosperms (conifers, cycads, ginkgo, gnetophytes) and the flowering plants (angiosperms), by far the largest group.

Evolution

Vascular plants appear in the fossil record in the Silurian period, more than 420 million years ago; early forms such as Cooksonia were small, leafless, branching axes tipped with sporangia. By the Devonian period, forests of lycophytes, horsetails and early trees had transformed soils and the atmosphere, drawing down carbon dioxide and contributing to the formation of coal deposits during the Carboniferous. Seeds, flowers and fruit evolved later, enabling the eventual dominance of the angiosperms in the Cretaceous and Cenozoic.

Importance

Vascular plants underpin nearly all life on land: they fix the carbon eaten by terrestrial animals, produce most atmospheric oxygen via photosynthesis, stabilize soils, and drive the water cycle through transpiration. Human agriculture, forestry, medicine and construction depend almost entirely on vascular plants, particularly grasses, legumes and timber trees.
