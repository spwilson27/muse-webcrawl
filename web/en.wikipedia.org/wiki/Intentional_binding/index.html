Intentional binding

Intentional binding is a phenomenon in which the perceived time interval between a voluntary action and its sensory consequence is shortened—the action is perceived as occurring later, and its effect earlier, than they actually do. It is widely used as an implicit measure of the sense of agency.

Experimental paradigm

The effect was described by Patrick Haggard and colleagues in 2002 using a Libet clock paradigm: participants pressed a key that produced a tone after a fixed delay, and judged the times of the press and the tone. Voluntary actions paired with tones showed compressed perceived intervals, whereas involuntary movements (produced by transcranial magnetic stimulation) showed the opposite pattern—temporal repulsion.

Mechanisms

Binding is thought to arise from both predictive motor signals (efference copy used to anticipate the effect) and retrospective inference from the effect back to the action. It depends on the action being voluntary, on the outcome being predictable and attributable to the self, and is weakened when outcomes are delayed, negative, or caused by another agent.

Clinical and research relevance

Altered binding has been reported in schizophrenia (often exaggerated binding, linked to symptoms of delusions of control) and in other conditions affecting agency. The paradigm is used in cognitive neuroscience, philosophy of action, and studies of human–machine interaction to measure implicit agency without explicit report.
