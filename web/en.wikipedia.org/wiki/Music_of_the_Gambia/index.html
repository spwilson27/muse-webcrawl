Music of the Gambia

The music of the Gambia is rooted in the Mandinka griot tradition of the wider Mande world of West Africa, and is closely intertwined with the music of neighbouring Senegal and Mali. Though the Gambia is one of Africa's smallest countries, its musicians — above all its kora players — have had an outsized influence on the international perception of West African music.

Griot tradition

Hereditary musician-historians called jalolu (sing. jali; French: griots) have served Mandinka courts and patrons for centuries, preserving genealogies, epics such as that of Sunjata Keita, and praise songs, accompanied by the kora (a 21-string bridge-harp), the balafon (wooden xylophone) and the konting (lute). Gambian kora masters such as Amadu Bansang Jobarteh and Alhaji Bai Konte brought the instrument to Western audiences in the 1970s; Bai Konte's son Dembo Konte and the Gambian-based British musician Kausu Kuyateh continued the tradition. The Jobarteh family remains prominent, and Sona Jobarteh became the first female professional kora player from a griot family.

Popular music

Gambian popular music developed under strong Senegalese influence. The Super Eagles, formed in the 1960s, were the country's pioneering modern band, blending Wolof and Mandinka lyrics with funk and Afro-Cuban sounds; after converting to Islam the group re-formed as Ifang Bondi in the 1970s. Senegalese mbalax, driven by stars such as Youssou N'Dour, dominates Gambian radio and dance floors, while since the 1990s reggae, dancehall and Gambian hip-hop acts such as the Da Fugitivz and ST have developed a local scene.
