Coupling reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A coupling reaction in organic chemistry is a reaction in which two fragments are joined together, usually with the aid of a metal catalyst. The term is most commonly applied to reactions forming a new carbon–carbon bond, although carbon–heteroatom couplings are also well developed.

Types

In cross-coupling, two different partners are joined — typically an organometallic reagent (or alkene) and an organic halide or pseudohalide, catalysed by palladium or nickel. Landmark named reactions include the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling (organoboranes), Heck reaction (alkenes), Sonogashira coupling (alkynes), Negishi coupling (organozincs), Stille coupling (organotins), and the Buchwald–Hartwig amination forming C–N bonds. In homocoupling, two identical fragments dimerise, as in the Glaser and Ullmann couplings.

Importance

Cross-couplings transformed the synthesis of pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and materials; the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Richard Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi, and Akira Suzuki for palladium-catalysed cross-couplings.
