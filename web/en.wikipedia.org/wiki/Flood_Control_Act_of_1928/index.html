Flood Control Act of 1928

The Flood Control Act of 1928 (Public Law 70-391), also known as the Jones–Reid Act, was United States federal legislation enacted in response to the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, the most destructive river flood in American history. The Act authorized a comprehensive federal program of flood control works on the Mississippi River and its tributaries and marked a decisive shift of responsibility for flood control from local levee districts to the federal government.

EnactedMay 15, 1928
Public lawPub. L. 70-391
Executing agencyU.S. Army Corps of Engineers

Provisions

The Act adopted the "Jadwin Plan" prepared by Chief of Engineers Edgar Jadwin, authorizing some $325 million for levees, floodways, spillways, and channel improvements along the lower Mississippi — at the time the largest public works appropriation in U.S. history. Importantly, it ended the requirement that local interests share construction costs for Mississippi River flood control, and it protected the federal government from liability for flood damages caused by its works (a provision later central to litigation after Hurricane Katrina). Implementation, including the Bonnet Carré Spillway and the Atchafalaya Basin floodway system, was assigned to the Mississippi River Commission and the Army Corps of Engineers.

Legacy

The 1928 Act laid the foundation for later, broader flood control legislation, notably the Flood Control Act of 1936, which declared flood control a proper federal activity across the whole country. The system it authorized has prevented catastrophic repetition of the 1927 flood on the main stem of the Mississippi, though debates over its levee-centered approach and environmental effects continue.
