Invisible hand

[Image: an 18th-century market scene of merchants trading, illustrating Smith's metaphor]

The invisible hand is a metaphor introduced by Adam Smith for the unintended social benefits of individuals pursuing their own interests in a Market economy. A merchant who invests at home rather than abroad out of self-regard, Smith wrote, is 'led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention' — the public good.

Usage and interpretation

Smith used the phrase only three times in his published works (once each in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, The Wealth of Nations and an essay on astronomy), but in the 20th century it became shorthand for the claim that competitive markets coordinate millions of decisions into order without central direction — the intuition later formalised in the welfare theorems of neoclassical economics and popularised in arguments for free markets and Laissez-faire. Historians of thought stress that Smith himself hedged the idea with caveats about monopoly, merchants' conspiracies against the public, and necessary state functions; the modern slogan is both a development and a simplification of his position.
