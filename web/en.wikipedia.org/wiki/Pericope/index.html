Pericope

A pericope (from Greek perikopē, "a cutting around") is a set of verses from a text that forms one coherent unit or passage, especially a passage of Scripture selected for reading in public worship or for study. The plural is "pericopes" or "pericopae".

Use in worship

Collections of pericopes arranged for the liturgical year are called lectionaries. In Western Christianity, systems of epistle and gospel pericopes date to late antiquity and were standardised in the medieval Roman Rite; the Revised Common Lectionary now used by many churches assigns Old Testament, psalm, epistle and gospel readings in a three-year cycle. Jewish practice similarly divides the Torah into weekly portions (parashot), each a pericope in function.

Use in scholarship

In biblical studies, form criticism treated individual pericopes — for example pronouncement stories, parables and miracle accounts in the Gospels — as originally independent units of oral tradition that editors later assembled into the written Gospels. Identifying the boundaries, genre and "setting in life" (Sitz im Leben) of each pericope was central to the work of scholars such as Rudolf Bultmann and Martin Dibelius. The famous "pericope adulterae" (the woman caught in adultery, John 7:53–8:11) is so named because textual critics treat it as a discrete passage that was not part of the original text of John's Gospel.
