Isomer

In chemistry, isomers are molecules or polyatomic ions with identical molecular formulae — the same number of atoms of each element — but distinct arrangements of atoms in space, and consequently different properties. The word derives from the Greek isos ("equal") and meros ("part"). The concept emerged in the 1820s–30s when compounds such as silver fulminate and silver cyanate were found to have the same composition but different properties; Jöns Jacob Berzelius coined the term isomerism in 1830.

Types of isomerism

Structural (constitutional) isomers have the same molecular formula but different connectivity. They include chain (skeletal) isomers, such as the three pentanes; position isomers, such as 1-propanol and 2-propanol; and functional-group isomers, such as ethanol and dimethyl ether, which contain different functional groups.

Stereoisomers have the same connectivity but different three-dimensional arrangements. Geometric (cis–trans or E/Z) isomers arise from restricted rotation about double bonds or rings. Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, typically arising from a chiral centre; they have identical physical properties except for the direction in which they rotate plane-polarised light and their interactions with other chiral substances. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. The term epimer and anomer are used for diastereomers differing at one stereocentre, especially in carbohydrate chemistry.

Tautomers are structural isomers that interconvert readily, most commonly by migration of a hydrogen atom, as in keto–enol tautomerism. Conformational isomers (conformers) interconvert by rotation about single bonds and are usually not counted as distinct isomers.

Importance

Isomerism is crucial in biochemistry and medicine: enzymes are highly selective for particular isomers, so one enantiomer of a drug may be therapeutic while the other is inactive or harmful — thalidomide is the classic cautionary example, though its enantiomers interconvert in the body. The term is also used in nuclear physics for nuclear isomers, metastable excited states of atomic nuclei (see Island of stability).
