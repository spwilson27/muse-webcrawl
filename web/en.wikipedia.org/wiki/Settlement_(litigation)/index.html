Settlement (litigation)

In law, a settlement is a resolution between disputing parties about a legal case, reached either before or after court action begins. A collective settlement resolves a large group of similar claims, as in a class action or mass tort. The vast majority of civil cases in the United States are resolved by settlement rather than trial.

Settlement agreements

A settlement is typically embodied in a contract — a settlement agreement and release — under which the plaintiff agrees to dismiss claims, often with prejudice, in exchange for payment or other consideration, usually without an admission of liability and often with confidentiality provisions. Structured settlements pay damages over time, sometimes through annuities, and are common in personal-injury and minors' cases.

Process

Settlement may be negotiated directly, at a settlement conference before a judge, or through mediation or other alternative dispute resolution. Courts must approve settlements in class actions (Rule 23(e) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure), cases involving minors, and certain statutory claims, to ensure fairness.

Effects

A settlement generally ends the dispute without a judgment on the merits, creates no binding precedent, and may be enforced as a contract or, if retained by the court, as an order. Breach can lead to enforcement motions or a new action.
