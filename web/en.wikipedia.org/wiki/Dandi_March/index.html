Dandi March

The Dandi March, also known as the Salt March and the Salt Satyagraha, was an act of nonviolent civil disobedience in colonial India led by Mohandas K. Gandhi. The 24-day march lasted from 12 March 1930 to 6 April 1930, spanning about 385 kilometres (240 miles) from the Sabarmati Ashram near Ahmedabad to the coastal village of Dandi, in present-day Gujarat.

Background

Under British rule, Indians were forbidden to collect or sell salt, and were forced to buy it from the state at prices inflated by a heavy salt tax. Salt, a necessity of diet in India's climate, was an ideal symbol around which to mobilise mass resistance: the tax hurt the poorest Indians most.

The march

Gandhi set out with 78 followers, and thousands joined along the route while international journalists covered the growing procession. At dawn on 6 April 1930, after morning prayers, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore at Dandi, technically "producing" salt and breaking the law. He called on Indians everywhere to defy the salt laws.

Consequences

Mass civil disobedience spread across India: salt was made illegally on beaches, foreign cloth was boycotted, and some 60,000 people were imprisoned, including Gandhi on 5 May 1930. The march drew worldwide attention to the Indian independence movement and marked a turning point in its transformation into a mass movement; the subsequent Gandhi–Irwin Pact of 1931 saw Gandhi attend the Second Round Table Conference in London. In 2005 the route was commemorated as the National Salt Satyagraha Memorial at Dandi.
