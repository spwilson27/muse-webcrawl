Species reintroduction

Species reintroduction is the deliberate release of a species into the wild, from captivity or other areas where the organism is capable of survival, in a place where it formerly occurred but has become extinct or extirpated. It is a form of conservation translocation governed by IUCN guidelines, and aims to re-establish a healthy, self-sustaining population and restore ecological processes.

Wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995 after a 70-year absence.

Famous programmes

  • Grey wolf to Yellowstone (1995): Canadian wolves restored a top predator; claimed effects on elk behaviour and riparian vegetation popularised the concept of the trophic cascade.
  • California condor: reduced to 27 birds in 1987, all taken into captivity; captive-bred birds were released from 1992 in California, Arizona and Baja California, and the population now exceeds 500, though lead poisoning from ammunition remains the main threat.
  • Arabian oryx: extinct in the wild by 1972, reintroduced to Oman from 1982 — the first species to move from "Extinct in the Wild" back to "Vulnerable" on the IUCN Red List.
  • Peregrine falcon: captive breeding and "hacking" restored populations across North America after DDT-driven declines.
  • Eurasian beaver: reintroduced across Europe, including licensed and unlicensed returns to Britain, for its wetland engineering effects.
  • Przewalski's horse to Mongolia, and European bison to forests of Poland and beyond.

Methods and challenges

Programmes use "hard" releases (direct release) or "soft" releases with acclimatisation enclosures and supplementary feeding. Founders are usually sourced from captive breeding or wild donor populations, ideally genetically close to the lost population. Common causes of failure include poor habitat quality, unaddressed original causes of decline, predation and conflict with people, and low genetic diversity. Reviews find success rates vary widely between taxa; birds and mammals fare better than amphibians and invertebrates, though well-planned projects across groups can succeed.

Controversy

Reintroductions of large predators generate conflict with livestock farmers and hunters; unauthorised "guerrilla" releases (such as beavers in Scotland) create legal difficulties. Debates also include assisted colonisation beyond historic ranges under climate change, and de-extinction proposals.
