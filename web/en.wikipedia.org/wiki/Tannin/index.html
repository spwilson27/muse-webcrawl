Tannin

Tannins (or tannoids) are a class of astringent, polyphenolic biomolecules that bind to and precipitate proteins and various other organic compounds including amino acids and alkaloids. The term tannin comes from the use of oak and other bark in tanning animal hides into leather; by extension, the term is widely applied to any large polyphenolic compound containing sufficient hydroxyls and other suitable groups (such as carboxyls) to form strong complexes with various macromolecules.

Classification

Tannins are traditionally divided into two main groups:

  • Hydrolysable tannins — esters of gallic acid (gallotannins) or ellagic acid (ellagitannins) with a sugar core, usually glucose. They are readily hydrolysed by acids, bases, or enzymes into their constituent acids and sugars.
  • Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) — polymers of flavan-3-ol units such as catechin and epicatechin, linked by carbon–carbon bonds that resist hydrolysis. They are the dominant tannins in grapes, tea, and many barks.

Occurrence

Tannins are widely distributed in the plant kingdom, occurring in leaves, bark, wood, fruit, seeds, and roots. They are abundant in oaks, chestnuts, tea plants, grapes, persimmons, and the South American quebracho tree. In plants they serve as a defence against herbivory and microbial attack, deterring feeding through their bitter, astringent taste and their ability to bind dietary protein.

Astringency

The astringency of tannins — the dry, puckering mouthfeel produced by unripe fruit, red wine, and strong tea — results from tannins binding to proline-rich salivary proteins, causing them to aggregate and precipitate and reducing the lubricating quality of saliva. This same protein-binding behaviour underlies their use in tanning, where tannins cross-link collagen in animal hides, rendering the leather resistant to decay.

Uses

Leather production

Vegetable tanning with tannin-rich extracts of oak, chestnut, mimosa, and quebracho is one of the oldest methods of leather manufacture, producing firm, durable leather traditionally used for soles, saddlery, and bookbinding.

Wine and food

In winemaking, tannins from grape skins, seeds, and stems — supplemented by oak ageing — contribute structure, bitterness, colour stability, and ageing potential to red wines. Tannins also occur in tea, coffee, cocoa, and many fruits and nuts, and are studied as dietary antioxidants.

Other uses

Tannins have been used in traditional medicine as astringents, in dyeing and ink manufacture (iron gall ink is made from tannic acid and iron salts), as clarifying agents, and industrially in adhesives, drilling muds, and corrosion inhibition.

Health effects

Tannins can reduce the absorption of iron and some proteins from the diet when consumed in large quantities, but in ordinary dietary amounts they are considered safe and are investigated for antioxidant, antimicrobial, and potentially cardioprotective properties.
