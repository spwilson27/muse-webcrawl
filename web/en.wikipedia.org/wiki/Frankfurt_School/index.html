Frankfurt School
Founded1923 (Institute for Social Research)
Key figuresHorkheimer, Adorno, Marcuse, Benjamin, Fromm, Habermas

The Frankfurt School is a school of neo-Marxist interdisciplinary social theory associated with the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung), founded in 1923 at the University of Frankfurt am Main. Its members developed critical theory, an approach combining Marxism, psychoanalysis, and sociology to analyse the structures of advanced capitalist society.

History

The Institute, endowed by the grain merchant Felix Weil, took its influential direction under Max Horkheimer from 1930. Driven into exile by the Nazis in 1933, it relocated via Geneva to Columbia University in New York, returning to Frankfurt after the war. Key figures included Horkheimer, Theodor W. Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, Erich Fromm, Friedrich Pollock, and, closely associated, Walter Benjamin.

Major works and ideas

Horkheimer and Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947) argued that Enlightenment rationality had reverted to myth and domination, and coined the concept of the "culture industry" — mass-produced culture that pacifies consumers. Marcuse's One-Dimensional Man (1964) became a touchstone of the New Left. A second generation, led by Jürgen Habermas, recast the project around communicative action and the public sphere.

Influence

Frankfurt School concepts profoundly shaped sociology, media studies, and cultural studies, and the school remains a focus of political controversy, celebrated as a critique of domination and attacked as a source of cultural pessimism.
