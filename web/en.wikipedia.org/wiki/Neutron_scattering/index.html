Neutron scattering

Neutron scattering, the irregular dispersal of free neutrons by matter, can refer to either the naturally occurring physical process itself or the man-made experimental techniques that use it for investigating materials. Neutron scattering as a technique encompasses neutron diffraction, small-angle neutron scattering, neutron reflectometry, and inelastic neutron scattering.

Principles

Neutrons interact with atomic nuclei through the strong nuclear force and with unpaired electrons through their magnetic moment. Because neutron scattering lengths vary irregularly across the periodic table and between isotopes—most notably between hydrogen and deuterium—neutrons are especially sensitive to light elements and isotopic substitution, complementing X-ray scattering, which scales with atomic number. Thermal neutrons have wavelengths comparable to interatomic spacings and energies comparable to excitations in condensed matter, making them ideal probes of both structure and dynamics.

Facilities and applications

Neutron scattering experiments require large facilities: research reactors such as the Institut Laue–Langevin in Grenoble or spallation sources such as ISIS in the United Kingdom, the Spallation Neutron Source in the United States, and J-PARC in Japan. Applications range from determining crystal and magnetic structures to studying polymers, soft matter, superconductors, and protein dynamics.
