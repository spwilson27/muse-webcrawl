Cumberland is a historic county in North West England, bordered by Northumberland to the north-east, County Durham to the east, Westmorland to the south-east, Lancashire to the south, and the Scottish council areas of Dumfries and Galloway and the Scottish Borders to the north. It formed an administrative county from 1889 to 1974, when it was merged with Westmorland and parts of Lancashire and Yorkshire to form the new county of Cumbria.

Geography

Cumberland contains the northern and western parts of the Lake District, including Scafell Pike, the highest mountain in England, and lakes such as Wastwater, Derwentwater and Ullswater (shared with Westmorland). The county town is Carlisle, near the western end of Hadrian's Wall. Other towns include Workington, Whitehaven, Maryport, Keswick, Penrith and Cockermouth.

History

The name derives from the Brittonic kingdom of the Cumbri. The area formed part of the medieval border country between England and Scotland, long disputed and raided by reivers. Carlisle was granted to England by William II in 1092, and the county was formally organised in the 12th century. The west Cumberland coalfield and the ports of Whitehaven and Workington drove industrial growth from the 18th century.

Modern revival

In 2023 local government reorganisation in Cumbria created two unitary authorities, one of which revived the name as Cumberland Council, covering the area of the historic county minus the Penrith area. Cumberland also survives as a ceremonial and cultural identity, in the Cumberland flag, and in products such as Cumberland sausage.
