In the kidney, the loop of Henle (also Henle's loop) is the portion of a nephron that leads from the proximal convoluted tubule to the distal convoluted tubule. Named after its discoverer, the German anatomist Friedrich Gustav Jakob Henle, the loop's main function is to create a concentration gradient in the medulla of the kidney.

Structure

The loop consists of a descending limb and an ascending limb, each with thin and thick segments. It dips from the renal cortex down into the medulla and returns; loops of juxtamedullary nephrons extend deep into the inner medulla, while those of cortical nephrons are short.

Physiology: countercurrent multiplication

By means of a countercurrent multiplier system, which uses electrolyte pumps, the loop creates an osmotic gradient in the medullary interstitium, rising from about 300 mOsm/kg near the cortex to as much as 1200 mOsm/kg deep in the medulla:

  • The descending thin limb is permeable to water but not salt; water leaves, concentrating the filtrate.
  • The ascending limb is impermeable to water. In the thick ascending limb, Na+, K+ and 2Cl− are co-transported out of the tubule (via the NKCC2 cotransporter), diluting the filtrate and adding salt to the interstitium.
  • The vasa recta capillaries act as countercurrent exchangers, preserving the gradient.

This gradient allows antidiuretic hormone (ADH/vasopressin) to regulate water reabsorption in the collecting duct, so the kidney can produce concentrated urine and conserve water.

Clinical significance

Loop diuretics such as furosemide act by blocking the NKCC2 cotransporter in the thick ascending limb, producing powerful diuresis. The loop's efficiency at concentrating urine is especially developed in desert mammals, whose long loops allow them to survive with minimal water.
