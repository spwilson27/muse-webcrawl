The sarissa (Greek: σάρισα) was a long spear or pike, between about 4 and 6.5 metres (13–21 feet) in length, introduced into the army of Macedon by Philip II in the mid-4th century BC. Wielded with both hands by the infantrymen of the Macedonian phalanx, it replaced the shorter spear of the Greek hoplite and became the signature weapon of the armies of Philip, Alexander the Great and the Hellenistic kingdoms.

A phalanx presenting a hedge of sarissa points; the first five ranks could project their pikes beyond the front of the formation.

Description

The sarissa was made of tough cornel wood, joined in two sections by an iron sleeve, with a leaf-shaped iron head and a bronze butt-spike that served both as a counterweight and as a secondary weapon if the shaft broke. Its great length meant that the pikes of the first five ranks of the phalanx projected in front of the formation, presenting an enemy with a dense hedge of spearheads and keeping opposing infantry at a distance where their shorter weapons could not reach.

Use

Under Alexander the Great the sarissa phalanx formed the anvil of his battle tactics, pinning the enemy frontally while the Companion cavalry delivered the decisive blow. The weapon demanded extensive drill and was less effective on broken ground or if the formation lost cohesion, weaknesses exploited by the Roman legions in the 2nd century BC, after which the sarissa fell out of use.
