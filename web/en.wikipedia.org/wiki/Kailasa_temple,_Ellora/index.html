Kailasa temple, Ellora

This page reproduces the article on the Kailasa temple at Ellora — the great rock-cut Shiva temple of the Ellora Caves (Cave 16) in Maharashtra, India.

The Kailasa temple, commissioned under the Rashtrakuta dynasty in the 8th century CE and traditionally credited to King Krishna I, is a megalith carved in its entirety from a single basalt rock face. Craftsmen excavated a pit some 33 metres deep and sculpted the temple downwards from the living rock, producing a full-size structural temple — courtyard, gateway, subsidiary shrines, a pillared hall, and a towered sanctuary dedicated to Shiva as lord of Mount Kailasa — surrounded by carved galleries.

Notable features

The complex measures about 82 metres long and 46 metres wide. Two colossal elephants and two victory pillars flank the courtyard; the plinth of the main temple is carved with elephants and lions that appear to carry the structure on their backs. The vimana rises in receding storeys above the garbhagriha (sanctum), which enshrines a large lingam. Narrative friezes illustrate episodes from the Mahabharata and Ramayana, along with the famous sculpture of the demon king Ravana attempting to lift Mount Kailasa while Shiva calmly pins it down with his toe.

Later history

The temple remained an active place of worship and pilgrimage long after the Rashtrakutas. Mughal emperor Aurangzeb is said to have ordered its destruction in the 17th century; workmen reportedly laboured for years and achieved only minor damage before the effort was abandoned. Today the temple is protected by the Archaeological Survey of India and is the most visited monument in the Ellora World Heritage complex.
