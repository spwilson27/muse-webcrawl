Coronary stent

A coronary stent mounted on a balloon catheter before deployment.

A coronary stent is a small, expandable mesh tube placed in a narrowed or blocked coronary artery to keep it open and maintain blood flow to the heart muscle. Stents are deployed during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI, or coronary angioplasty): a balloon catheter is threaded to the blockage, inflated to expand the stent against the artery wall, and then withdrawn, leaving the stent permanently in place.

Types

  • Bare-metal stents (BMS): made of stainless steel, cobalt-chromium or similar alloys. They reduced the restenosis (re-narrowing) seen with balloon angioplasty alone, but tissue growth through the stent still causes restenosis in a significant minority of patients.
  • Drug-eluting stents (DES): coated with a polymer that slowly releases an antiproliferative drug such as sirolimus, everolimus or paclitaxel, greatly reducing restenosis. DES are now the standard in most PCI.
  • Bioresorbable scaffolds: temporary stents designed to dissolve over time; early devices were withdrawn after trials showed higher thrombosis rates, though research continues.

Risks and aftercare

The main risks include stent thrombosis — a clot forming inside the stent — and in-stent restenosis. To prevent thrombosis, patients typically receive dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor such as clopidogrel) for months after implantation, with duration depending on stent type and patient risk. Stent placement is a standard treatment for heart attacks (myocardial infarction) and for angina due to severe coronary artery disease, although trials such as COURAGE and ISCHEMIA have fuelled debate about its benefit over medication alone in stable disease.
