Volsci

The Volsci (also Volscians) were an Italic tribe of ancient Italy, well known in the history of the first century of the Roman Republic. They inhabited the partly hilly, partly marshy district between the Aequi and the coast of Latium, southeast of Rome, in the area of the modern Lepini Mountains and the upper Liri valley. Their language, Volscian, belonged to the Osco-Umbrian branch of Italic and is attested in a small number of inscriptions, most famously the bronze tablet from Velitrae.

Wars with Rome

From the early 5th century BC, the Volsci were among Rome's most persistent enemies, repeatedly invading Latium in alliance with the Aequi. Roman tradition associates their wars with the legend of Coriolanus, the exiled Roman who led a Volscian army against his own city and was turned back by his mother and wife — a story retold by Livy, Plutarch, and Shakespeare. Volscian towns such as Antium, Ecetra, and Corioli changed hands repeatedly; the Volsci captured the Roman colony of Velitrae and long contested the Pomptine plain.

Over the 4th century BC, Roman victories and colonies — at Satricum, Privernum, and elsewhere — steadily absorbed Volscian territory. After the revolt of Privernum and Fundi in 330 BC was suppressed, the Volsci disappear from history as an independent people, fully Romanized within a few generations.

Culture

Little is known of Volscian society beyond war. They lived in fortified hill towns, practiced agriculture and transhumant herding, and buried their dead with weapons; the imposing polygonal walls of towns like Norba and Segni are traditionally associated with the Volscian period, though dating is debated.
