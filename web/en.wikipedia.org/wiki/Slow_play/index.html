Slow play

Slow playing (also called sandbagging or trapping) is a deceptive play in poker where a player bets weakly or passively with a strong winning hand. It is the opposite of bluffing, and a strategy of flat calling a bet to build up the pot and induce betting from opponents who might fold to a raise. In essence, the strong player disguises the strength of their hand to extract more money later in the hand.

Conditions

Slow playing is generally advisable only when several conditions hold:

  • The player's hand is very strong, ideally the nuts or close to it.
  • The free card or cheap card the player allows opponents is unlikely to improve their hands to beat his.
  • The pot is small, so that building it up matters more than protecting it immediately.
  • Opponents are aggressive and likely to bet or bluff if shown weakness.
  • The board is not draw-heavy; on a coordinated board a free card is dangerous.

Examples

A classic slow play is flopping a full house and merely checking or calling, allowing opponents holding a flush draw, a straight draw or two pair to commit more chips on later streets before the trap is sprung with a raise on the turn or river. David Sklansky discusses the technique extensively in The Theory of Poker, under the heading of deception and the "fundamental theorem of poker".

Risks

The main risk of slow playing is giving free or cheap cards that let an opponent outdraw the strong hand. Another cost is the value foregone when opponents would have called a raise anyway. For these reasons many professionals advise slow playing rarely and with nearly unbeatable hands.

Relation to check-raising

A slow play often culminates in a check-raise: the player checks early in a betting round with a strong hand, allows an opponent to bet, and then raises. Check-raising was historically considered poor etiquette in some home games but is an accepted and essential part of modern casino poker.
