Alcohol intoxication

Alcohol intoxication
Other namesDrunkenness; alcohol poisoning (severe cases)
CauseConsumption of ethanol (alcoholic beverages)
SymptomsEuphoria, impaired coordination and judgement; at high levels vomiting, respiratory depression, coma
MeasurementBlood alcohol concentration (BAC)

Alcohol intoxication, commonly called drunkenness, is the physiological and behavioural state produced by consuming ethanol. Ethanol acts as a depressant of the central nervous system, enhancing the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and inhibiting excitatory glutamate, which produces dose-dependent effects ranging from mild euphoria to unconsciousness and death.

Effects by blood alcohol concentration

At low blood alcohol concentrations (around 0.02–0.05%) drinkers typically feel relaxation and lowered inhibition. Between roughly 0.06% and 0.15%, speech, balance, reaction time and judgement become measurably impaired — the basis for legal driving limits of 0.05% or 0.08% in many jurisdictions. Above 0.20%, vomiting, confusion and blackouts are common; concentrations above about 0.30–0.40% can cause coma, respiratory failure and death through alcohol poisoning.

Metabolism and factors

Alcohol is metabolised mainly in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase at a roughly constant rate. Body weight, sex, food intake, rate of drinking and tolerance all affect intoxication. The body eliminates roughly one standard drink per hour; coffee, cold air and other folk remedies do not speed the process.

Risks and treatment

Intoxication is a major risk factor for road traffic accidents, violence and accidental injury, and acute poisoning is a medical emergency treated supportively: maintaining the airway, monitoring breathing, and preventing aspiration of vomit (the recovery position). Long-term heavy intoxication is associated with alcohol use disorder, liver disease and the Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.
