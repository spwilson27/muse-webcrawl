CT scan

Computed tomography
Other namesComputed axial tomography, CAT scan
InventorsGodfrey Hounsfield, Allan Cormack

Measurement scaleHounsfield units (HU)

A computed tomography scan (CT scan), formerly called a computed axial tomography scan (CAT scan), is a medical imaging technique that uses computer-processed combinations of many X-ray measurements taken from different angles to produce cross-sectional (tomographic) images of the body, allowing users to see inside without cutting.

How it works

A motorised X-ray source rotates around the patient inside a ring-shaped gantry while detectors on the opposite side record the transmitted radiation. Computers reconstruct the data — using algorithms based on the Radon transform — into slices, which can be stacked into three-dimensional renderings. Tissue attenuation is expressed in Hounsfield units, with water at 0 HU and air at −1000 HU.

History

The first commercially viable scanner was invented by Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI Laboratories in England, building on the theoretical work of Allan Cormack; the first patient scan was performed in 1971. Hounsfield and Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Successive generations added faster rotation, helical (spiral) acquisition, and multi-detector rows.

Uses and risks

CT is widely used for diagnosing trauma, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and guiding biopsies and surgery. Its main drawback is ionising radiation exposure, which is substantially higher than plain radiography, motivating dose-reduction techniques and careful justification of scans, especially in children.
