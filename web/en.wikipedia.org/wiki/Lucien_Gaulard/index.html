Lucien Gaulard (1850–1888) was a French inventor and a pioneer of alternating current (AC) electrical power transmission. Together with the English engineer John Dixon Gibbs, he developed an early practical transformer system for distributing electric light over long distances.

The "secondary generator"

In 1882 Gaulard and Gibbs patented a device they called the "secondary generator" — in effect a transformer with an open iron core, in which a primary coil induced current in secondary coils whose connections could be arranged to step voltage up or down. In 1884 they demonstrated the system spectacularly in Turin, Italy, transmitting AC power over a 40-kilometre loop from Turin to Lanzo and back, lighting railway stations along the route with only modest losses.

Impact and later life

The demonstration, witnessed by engineers including Galileo Ferraris, showed that high-voltage AC transmission with transformers made large-scale electric distribution practical, in contrast to Thomas Edison's low-voltage direct current systems. Gaulard's patents were acquired by George Westinghouse, whose engineers — notably William Stanley — developed the closed-core transformer into the basis of modern AC distribution. Gaulard himself received little recognition or reward, lost disputes over his inventions, and died impoverished in a Paris asylum in 1888, aged 38.

Legacy

Historians of technology credit Gaulard as a genuine originator of the transformer-based AC system that ultimately prevailed in the "war of the currents."
