Ham

Ham is pork from a leg cut that has been preserved by wet or dry curing, with or without smoking. The word derives from the Old English ham or hamm, the hollow or bend of the knee. Because the word is defined by the cut rather than the process in some jurisdictions, it may be applied to products from other animals in some countries, but in most usage ham is specifically the cured hind leg of the pig.

Production

Fresh hams are cleaned and trimmed, then cured. In dry curing, the meat is rubbed with salt (traditionally with saltpetre or nitrites) and seasonings, then hung to age for months, losing moisture and developing concentrated flavours; weight loss of 18–25 percent is typical. In wet curing, the ham is immersed in or injected with brine, often with sugar and flavourings, and may then be smoked and cooked; this faster process yields the moist "city ham" common in North America. Smoking may be done over hardwood such as hickory, applewood or beech, sometimes after curing, mainly for flavour and colour rather than preservation.

Varieties

Many regions protect traditional hams with geographical indications. Famous dry-cured hams include Spanish jamón serrano and jamón ibérico (the finest from acorn-fed Iberian pigs), Italian prosciutto di Parma and prosciutto di San Daniele, French jambon de Bayonne, German Black Forest ham, and Chinese Jinhua ham, an ingredient in stocks and the dish "Buddha Jumps Over the Wall". In the United States, dry-cured, aged "country ham" is characteristic of Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee — Smithfield ham of Virginia being the best known — while spiral-sliced, honey-glazed baked ham is a standard festive dish at Christmas and Easter. In the United Kingdom, gammon is the name for the cured cut before cooking, ham after.

Use and storage

Ham is eaten cooked or, in the case of properly dry-cured products, raw in thin slices. It is a sandwich staple, a flavouring for soups, beans and greens, and a centrepiece of holiday meals. Processed meats including ham are classified by the World Health Organization's IARC as Group 1 carcinogens with respect to colorectal cancer when consumed in quantity, and health authorities commonly advise moderation. Whole cured hams keep for long periods; cooked ham must be refrigerated and used within days.
