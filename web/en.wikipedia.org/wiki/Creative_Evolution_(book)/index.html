Creative Evolution (French: L'Évolution créatrice) is a 1907 book by the French philosopher Henri Bergson. In it, Bergson proposed a version of orthogenesis in terms of a vital impetus — the élan vital — which he argued drives the evolution of life in creative, unpredictable directions. The book was enormously popular, secured Bergson's international fame, and contributed to his receiving the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1927.

Content

Bergson argued that neither mechanistic Darwinism nor finalism (the idea that evolution unfolds towards a predetermined goal) adequately explains life. Evolution, he held, proceeds by the continual splitting of an original vital impulse into divergent lines — as in the separate evolution of the eye in vertebrates and molluscs — through the resistance of inert matter. The intellect, evolved for acting on solid objects, spatialises and fragments reality; only intuition, a kind of sympathetic insight into duration, can grasp life's creative flow. Key chapters discuss the evolution of instincts and intelligence, the divergence of plant and animal lines, and a reinterpretation of the history of philosophy from Zeno to Kant.

Reception and influence

The book made Bergson a celebrity — his lectures at the Collège de France drew huge crowds, and the English translation (1911) was a bestseller. Critics included Bertrand Russell, who attacked Bergson's philosophy in a famous 1912 essay, and biologists who regarded the élan vital as untestable vitalism. The work influenced writers and thinkers from William James and Alfred North Whitehead to Gilles Deleuze, who led a revival of Bergson studies in the 1960s. Although vitalism lost scientific standing, the book remains a classic of the philosophy of biology and of process philosophy.
