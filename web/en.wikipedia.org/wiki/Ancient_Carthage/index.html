Ancient Carthage (Punic: 𐤒𐤓𐤕𐤇𐤃𐤔𐤕, Qart-ḥadašt, "New City") was an ancient Phoenician city-state and civilisation centred on the city of Carthage, on the eastern side of Lake Tunis in what is now Tunisia. Founded by settlers from Tyre, traditionally in 814 BC under the legendary queen Dido (Elissa), Carthage grew into the dominant commercial power of the western Mediterranean, commanding a maritime empire of colonies and trading posts across North Africa, Sardinia, Corsica, the Balearic Islands, Malta, western Sicily, and southern Iberia.

Society and government

Carthage was ruled first by kings and later as a republic headed by two annually elected magistrates called suffetes, advised by a senate of wealthy families and a popular assembly. Aristotle praised its mixed constitution in the Politics. Its wealth rested on trade — purple dye, metals, grain, wine, and manufactured goods — and on productive agriculture described in Mago's famous farming treatise. Carthaginian armies relied heavily on mercenaries and allied contingents (Numidian cavalry, Iberian infantry, war elephants), while its navy of quinqueremes guarded the trade routes.

Wars with Rome

Carthage's rivalry with the expanding Roman Republic produced the three Punic Wars (264–146 BC). In the First Punic War Rome took Sicily and then Sardinia and Corsica. In the Second, the general Hannibal Barca famously crossed the Alps with elephants in 218 BC and won crushing victories at Trebia, Lake Trasimene, and Cannae, but Rome refused terms, wore down Carthaginian strength in Spain and Italy, and Scipio Africanus finally defeated Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC. Fifty years later, after the Third Punic War, Rome destroyed the city in 146 BC and annexed its territory as the province of Africa.

Roman Carthage and legacy

A century later Julius Caesar and Augustus refounded Carthage as a Roman colony, and it became one of the greatest cities of the western empire — a centre of early Christianity associated with Tertullian and Cyprian — before passing to the Vandals, the Byzantines, and finally Arab conquerors in 698 CE. The Punic language survived for centuries (Augustine mentions Punic-speaking peasants), and the site of Carthage, now a suburb of Tunis, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
