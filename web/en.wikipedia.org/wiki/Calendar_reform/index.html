Calendar reform or calendrical reform is any significant revision of a calendar system. The term sometimes also refers to proposals to reform the modern Gregorian calendar, which is used worldwide, into something considered more rational, regular or equitable.

Historical reforms

The most consequential reforms in the Western tradition were the Julian reform of 45 BC, which introduced the leap-year solar calendar of Julius Caesar, and the Gregorian reform of 1582, which corrected the drift of the Julian calendar against the seasons by adjusting the leap rule and skipping ten days. Other states carried out their own reforms, including the French Republican Calendar during the Revolution, the Soviet revolutionary calendar, and various reforms of lunar and lunisolar calendars in Asia.

Modern proposals

Proposals for reforming the Gregorian calendar generally seek a perennial calendar, in which every year (or quarter) begins on the same weekday and dates always fall on the same weekday. The World Calendar (12 months, equal quarters of 91 days, plus intercalary "Worldsday") was promoted in the 20th century and considered by the League of Nations and the United Nations. The International Fixed Calendar has 13 months of 28 days. The Hanke–Henry Permanent Calendar keeps 12 months but inserts a leap week. Objections typically centre on religious observance of an unbroken seven-day week, disruption to established dates and anniversaries, and transition costs.
