Toleration

Toleration is the practice of allowing or permitting beliefs, practices, or groups that one disapproves of or disagrees with, without interference. In political philosophy it most often concerns religious toleration: the state's restraint from coercing religious belief and worship.

History

After the European wars of religion of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, arguments for toleration gained force. John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued that the magistrate's business is civil goods, not the salvation of souls, and that coercion cannot produce genuine belief. Earlier milestones include the Edict of Nantes (1598), granting limited rights to French Huguenots, and the English Toleration Act of 1689; Roger Williams and the colony of Rhode Island pioneered broader religious liberty in North America.

Concepts

Philosophers distinguish toleration from mere indifference or approval: one tolerates what one objects to but has overriding reasons not to suppress. Karl Popper's "paradox of tolerance" argues that unlimited tolerance may destroy itself if it extends to movements that reject rational debate and preach intolerance. Modern liberal democracies ground toleration in constitutional rights of free exercise, expression, and conscience, extended beyond religion to political opinion, sexuality, and cultural practice.
