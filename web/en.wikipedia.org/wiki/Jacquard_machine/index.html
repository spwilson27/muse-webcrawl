Jacquard machine

Jacquard machine (Jacquard loom)

Inventor: Joseph Marie Jacquard

Patented: 1804 (demonstrated 1801–1805)

Predecessors: Looms of Basile Bouchon (1725), Jean-Baptiste Falcon (1728), Jacques de Vaucanson (1745)

Significance: Programmable weaving via punched cards

The Jacquard machine is a device fitted to a loom that simplifies the manufacture of textiles with complex patterns, such as brocade, damask, and matelassé. Developed by Joseph Marie Jacquard and patented in 1804, it controls each warp thread individually through a chain of punched cards, allowing a single weaver to produce intricate figured cloth that previously required a skilled weaver and an assistant ("draw boy") to lift threads by hand.

How it works

Each punched card corresponds to one row (pick) of the design. Pressed against a spring-loaded array of needles and hooks, the card lets needles pass where holes are punched and blocks them elsewhere, so only the hooks controlling the required warp threads are raised for that pass of the shuttle. Cards are laced into a continuous loop, making the pattern a stored, repeatable program. A famous demonstration of its precision was a woven silk portrait of Jacquard himself, produced from some 24,000 cards.

Development and adoption

Jacquard combined earlier ideas — Bouchon's perforated paper tape, Falcon's individual cards, and Vaucanson's cylinder — into a practical system. Napoleon granted him a patent and a pension, and by 1812 there were some 11,000 Jacquard looms in France. Silk weavers in Lyon initially resisted, fearing unemployment, and destroyed machines, but the efficiency gains were irresistible; the system spread worldwide and powered the great patterned-textile industries of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Influence on computing

The punched-card principle passed directly into computing: Charles Babbage planned to use Jacquard-style cards to program his Analytical Engine, prompting Ada Lovelace's observation that "the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." Herman Hollerith's census tabulator and early IBM machines inherited the same technology, making the Jacquard machine a landmark ancestor of programmable machinery.
