Flamen Dialis

In ancient Roman religion, the Flamen Dialis was the high priest of Jupiter, the chief deity of the Roman pantheon. The term Dialis is related to Diespiter (Jupiter) and to dius, "god". The Flamen Dialis was the most senior of the fifteen flamines, priests devoted to individual deities, and ranked highest in the ordo sacerdotum, the formal hierarchy of Roman priests.

Appointment and privileges

The Flamen Dialis had to be a patrician married by the ancient ceremony of confarreatio to a wife, the Flaminica Dialis, who shared in his duties and restrictions. He wore distinctive dress: the apex, a conical cap with a spike of olive wood bound in wool, and the heavy woollen cloak called the laena. He was attended by a lictor, had a seat in the Senate, and was entitled to the curule chair and the toga praetexta. His house, the Flaminia, stood on the Velia near the Forum.

Restrictions

The priest lived under an extraordinary web of taboos, described by Aulus Gellius. He might not ride or touch a horse, look upon an army drawn up for battle, wear a closed ring, have a knot in any part of his clothing, touch or name a she-goat, a dog, raw meat, beans, or ivy, walk beneath a vine, or touch flour or leavened dough. His hair could be cut only by a free man, and the clippings of his hair and nails were buried beneath a lucky tree. He could not spend three nights away from his bed in Rome, nor be absent from the city in ways that would interrupt the daily sacrifices. If his wife died, he was obliged to resign the priesthood.

History

The office was traditionally said to have been instituted by Numa Pompilius, Rome's second king. Because of its restrictions the post was often unattractive: after the death of Cornelius Merula in 87 BC the priesthood stood vacant for more than seventy years until Augustus revived it in 11 BC. The office survived into late antiquity, lapsing with the suppression of pagan cults in the fourth century AD.
