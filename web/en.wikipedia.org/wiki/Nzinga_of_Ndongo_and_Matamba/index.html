Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Statue of Queen Nzinga in Luanda, Angola, holding her battle-axe]

Nzinga Mbande (c. 1583 – 17 December 1663), also spelled Njinga, was queen of the Mbundu kingdoms of Ndongo and Matamba in modern Angola, the most famous resister of Portuguese colonisation and the Atlantic slave trade in 17th-century Africa. Sent as her brother the ngola's envoy to Luanda in 1622 — where, in the famous story, she refused the humiliation of having no chair and sat upon a servant's back to negotiate as an equal — she was baptised as Ana de Sousa, seized her brother's throne (1624), and after the Portuguese drove her from Ndongo, conquered Matamba and waged forty years of war and diplomacy: allied with the Dutch (1641–1648) and with Imbangala bands, harbouring runaway slaves, and in her seventies negotiated the peace (1656) that secured her kingdom, dying in her bed at about eighty.

Queen and warlord

Nzinga's statecraft mixed every instrument: dynastic legitimacy and usurpation, baptism and traditional ritual, guerrilla war from the Matamba forests, control of trade routes to the interior fairs, and manipulation of the Portuguese–Dutch war for the coast. The Portuguese installed puppet ngolas in her place repeatedly; she outlasted governors, generals and two popes' peace efforts. Capuchin accounts (Cavazzi, her sometime guest) describe her court, her male dress and retinue of women soldiers — elements embellished by European fascination but grounded in Mbundu political practice of female rule.

Memory

Nzinga died in peace in 1663, reconciled with the Church; Matamba under her successors (her sister Barbara, then others) remained independent into the 19th century. Reclaimed by Angola's independence movement (MPLA iconography, the great statue in Luanda's Kinaxixi square, her face on the kwanza), she is the rare 17th-century African ruler documented in European archives, oral tradition and her own surviving letters — and a central figure in the modern reassessment of African resistance to the slave trade, complicated as her own participation in that trade, as a seller of war-captives, inescapably was.
