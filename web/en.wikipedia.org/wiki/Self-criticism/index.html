Self-criticism

Self-criticism is the act of pointing out one's own perceived faults. It may involve evaluating oneself, one's behaviour, or one's performance, and can range from constructive reflection to chronic, harsh negative self-evaluation. In psychology, habitual self-criticism is studied as a personality trait and as a feature of several mental disorders.

In psychology

Psychologists distinguish between adaptive self-reflection, which supports learning and self-correction, and maladaptive self-criticism, characterized by rumination, shame, and a punitive inner voice. Research by Sidney Blatt identified self-criticism as one of two major personality configurations associated with depression, alongside dependency. High levels of self-critical perfectionism are correlated with depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and poorer responses to psychotherapy.

Therapeutic approaches such as compassion-focused therapy, developed by Paul Gilbert, aim to counteract an overactive threat-and-self-criticism system by strengthening the capacity for self-compassion and self-reassurance. Two forms are commonly distinguished in clinical research: comparative self-criticism, judging oneself unfavourably against others, and internalized self-criticism, judging oneself against demanding internal standards.

In political movements

In Marxist–Leninist organizations, "criticism and self-criticism" (Russian: kritika i samokritika) was an institutionalized practice in which members publicly acknowledged errors before the collective. In Maoist China, written and public self-criticisms became a prominent feature of political campaigns, and during the Cultural Revolution struggle sessions compelled individuals to confess and denounce their own alleged faults.
