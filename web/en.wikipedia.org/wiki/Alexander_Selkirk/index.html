Alexander Selkirk (1676 – 13 December 1721) was a Scottish privateer and Royal Navy officer who spent four years and four months as a castaway (1704–1709) on an uninhabited island in the Juan Fernández Archipelago of the South Pacific. His survival story is widely regarded as a principal inspiration for Daniel Defoe's novel Robinson Crusoe (1719).

Marooning

Selkirk sailed on the privateering expedition of William Dampier as sailing master of the Cinque Ports. In October 1704, after quarrelling with the captain and judging the ship unseaworthy, Selkirk demanded to be put ashore at Más a Tierra island (now Robinson Crusoe Island, Chile); his judgement was vindicated when the Cinque Ports later foundered with heavy loss of life. Alone, Selkirk survived by hunting feral goats, building shelters, taming cats to protect him from rats, and reading the Bible.

Rescue and later life

On 2 February 1709 he was rescued by the privateers Duke and Duchess, commanded by Woodes Rogers — with Dampier aboard as pilot. Rogers's published account, A Cruising Voyage Round the World (1712), made Selkirk famous. He returned to sea, later serving as a lieutenant in the Royal Navy, and died of fever aboard HMS Weymouth off the coast of West Africa in 1721.
