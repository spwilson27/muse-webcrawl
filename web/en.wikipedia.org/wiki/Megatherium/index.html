Megatherium

Megatherium ("great beast") is an extinct genus of ground sloths endemic to South America that lived from the early Pliocene through the end of the Late Pleistocene. Its best-known species, Megatherium americanum, was among the largest land mammals known, comparable in size to a modern elephant.

Description

M. americanum could reach about 6 metres (20 ft) in length and weigh several tonnes. It had a robust skeleton, powerful hind limbs, and a massive pelvis and short tail that let it rear up into a tripodal stance to pull down high branches with its clawed forelimbs. Its teeth were simple, ever-growing pegs adapted for grinding tough vegetation; it walked on the sides of its hind feet (pedolateral locomotion) because of its large claws.

Discovery and significance

The first specimen was found in Argentina in 1788 and sent to Madrid, where it was mounted — one of the first fossil vertebrates ever assembled for display. Georges Cuvier described the genus in the 1790s, and Megatherium became famous through the work of Richard Owen and Charles Darwin, who collected its fossils in South America. It played a key role in establishing the then-controversial idea of extinction.

extinction

Megatherium survived the Great American Interchange and the ice ages but vanished around 10,000 years ago near the end of the Pleistocene, during the broader Quaternary extinction event. Both climate shifts at the end of the last glacial period and hunting by newly arrived humans have been proposed as causes; cut-marked bones at some sites suggest humans butchered giant ground sloths.
