Science communication

Science communication encompasses a wide range of activities that connect science and society. The field encompasses the communication of science — basic and applied — to audiences ranging from the general public and schoolchildren to policymakers, industry, and scientists in other disciplines. Science communication may be carried out by scientists themselves, by professional communicators and journalists, by museums and science centres, and increasingly through digital and social media.

Goals and models

Practitioners aim variously to inform, educate, entertain, persuade, raise awareness, and build trust, dialogue and public involvement in decisions about science. Scholars have historically described three overlapping models: the deficit model, which assumes public scepticism follows from a lack of knowledge to be filled by one-way transmission; the dialogue model, emphasising two-way engagement and listening; and the participation model, involving laypeople directly in framing questions and producing knowledge, as in citizen science.

History

Popular lectures, demonstrations and books made science accessible in the 19th century — figures such as Michael Faraday, whose Christmas Lectures at the Royal Institution began in 1825, are emblematic. The 20th century saw the rise of professional science journalism in newspapers, radio and television, and scientist-communicators such as Jacob Bronowski, Carl Sagan and David Attenborough reached mass audiences. A 1985 report of the Royal Society (the Bodmer Report) on the public understanding of science spurred institutional programmes and university courses, and in the 2000s funders increasingly required "broader impacts" and public engagement as part of research grants.

Fields and formats

Science communication includes science journalism, museum exhibitions, science festivals, public lectures, podcasts, popular science books and magazines, science cafés and social-media outreach. As academic discipline, it studies how audiences interpret scientific information, the effects of framing and misinformation, and the relationship between expertise, trust and democracy. Contemporary challenges include the rapid spread of misinformation online, politicisation of topics such as vaccination and climate change, and the pressures of the 24-hour news cycle on accuracy.
