The 1948 Palestinian expulsion and flight, known in Arabic as the Nakba (النكبة, "catastrophe"), was the displacement of more than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs — roughly half of Palestine's Arab population — who fled or were expelled from their homes during the 1948 Palestine war. The exodus took place during the civil war in Mandatory Palestine and the subsequent 1948 Arab–Israeli War, and it unfolded in several waves between December 1947 and mid-1949.

Causes and course

Historians continue to debate the relative weight of causes: direct expulsions by Jewish/Israeli forces (most notably of some 50,000–70,000 residents of Lydda and Ramle in July 1948), flight from fighting and fear of attack — intensified by news of the Deir Yassin massacre in April 1948 — military assaults on villages, psychological warfare, and collapse of Palestinian leadership and society. More than 400 Arab towns and villages were depopulated, and most were subsequently demolished or repopulated by Jewish immigrants; Israel's Absentees' Property Law transferred refugee property to state control, and Israel refused to allow the refugees to return after the war.

Aftermath

The refugees and their descendants settled mainly in the West Bank, the Gaza Strip, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon, where many remain in refugee camps administered by UNRWA, established in 1949. UN General Assembly Resolution 194 (December 1948) resolved that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace should be permitted to do so; the "right of return" remains a central and unresolved issue of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. Palestinians commemorate Nakba Day on May 15 each year.
