Fauvism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Woman with a Hat (1905) by Henri Matisse, portrait built from arbitrary patches of vivid unnatural colour

Fauvism (from the French les Fauves, 'the wild beasts') is a short-lived but influential movement of the early 20th century whose painters emphasised strong, arbitrary colour and painterly qualities over representational accuracy. It flourished roughly from 1904 to 1908, with its scandalous public debut at the 1905 Salon d'Automne in Paris, where the critic Louis Vauxcelles coined the name on seeing the violently coloured canvases.

The leading Fauves were Henri Matisse and André Derain, joined by Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy, Kees van Dongen and Georges Braque. Matisse's Woman with a Hat (1905) epitomises the style's liberation of colour from description.

Legacy

Though the group soon dispersed — Braque toward Cubism, Matisse toward his own decorative maturity — Fauvism's demonstration that colour could be an independent expressive language permanently changed modern painting.
