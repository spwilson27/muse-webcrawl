Ghazal

The ghazal (from Arabic ghazal, "spinning thread" or amatory talk) is a poetic form consisting of rhyming couplets and a refrain, with each line sharing the same metre. Originating in seventh-century Arabia, the ghazal spread with Islam through Persia, Turkey, Central Asia and the Indian subcontinent, becoming one of the principal lyric forms of Persian, Urdu, Turkish and many other literatures. Its themes are traditionally the pain of love and separation and the beauty of the beloved, though it ranges over mystical, philosophical and satirical subjects as well.

Form

A classical ghazal comprises between five and fifteen couplets (sher), each of which is semantically independent — a "pearl on a string" structure that lets topics shift abruptly between couplets. The opening couplet, the matla, sets the rhyme (qafia) and refrain (radif) that recur at the end of the second line of every subsequent couplet. In the closing couplet, the maqta, the poet traditionally inserts his or her pen name (takhallus). Ghazals are conventionally performed at gatherings called mushairas, where audiences applaud individual couplets.

History

The form grew out of the amatory prelude (nasib) of the Arabic qasida. Persian poets of the tenth and eleventh centuries made it their own, and it reached its greatest heights in the work of Saadi, Rumi and especially Hafez of Shiraz, whose Divan remains a household book in Iran. In Mughal India the ghazal flourished in Urdu: Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib are its acknowledged masters, and in the twentieth century Faiz Ahmed Faiz infused it with political longing. The Ottoman court poet Baki and the Azerbaijani poet Fuzuli brought it to perfection in Turkish.

The ghazal in the West

Goethe's West-Eastern Divan (1819), inspired by Hafez, introduced the form to European readers, and free adaptations followed in German, Spanish and English. In the late twentieth century the Kashmiri-American poet Agha Shahid Ali championed strict-form ghazals in English, insisting on the couplet independence, rhyme and refrain that free-verse imitations had abandoned.
