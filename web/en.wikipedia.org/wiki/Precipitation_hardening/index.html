Precipitation hardening

Precipitation hardening, also called age hardening or particle hardening, is a heat treatment technique used to increase the yield strength of malleable materials, including most structural alloys of aluminium, magnesium, nickel, titanium, and some steels and stainless steels. It relies on changes in solid solubility with temperature to produce fine particles of a second phase, which impede the movement of dislocations and thereby strengthen the material.

Process

The treatment generally involves three stages:

  • Solution treatment: the alloy is heated to a high temperature at which the alloying elements dissolve into a single-phase solid solution.
  • Quenching: the alloy is cooled rapidly, trapping the solute atoms in a supersaturated solid solution at room temperature.
  • Ageing: the alloy is held at an intermediate temperature (artificial ageing) or at room temperature (natural ageing), allowing fine precipitates to form and grow in a controlled way. Strength rises to a peak as precipitates reach an optimum size; prolonged ageing produces coarser particles and strength falls, a condition called over-ageing.

Mechanism

Strengthening occurs because precipitate particles obstruct dislocation motion, either by being cut by passing dislocations or by forcing dislocations to bow around them (the Orowan looping mechanism). Coherent precipitates, whose crystal lattice matches that of the matrix, additionally create strain fields that interact with dislocations. The phenomenon was discovered serendipitously by Alfred Wilm in 1906–1909, when he observed that a quenched aluminium-copper alloy grew harder over several days at room temperature; the theory of precipitation was developed in the 1920s and 1930s, including the identification of Guinier–Preston (GP) zones.

Applications

Precipitation hardening underpins much of aerospace engineering: high-strength aluminium alloys of the 2000 (Al–Cu), 6000 (Al–Mg–Si) and 7000 (Al–Zn–Mg) series; nickel-based superalloys such as Inconel 718 in jet engines, strengthened by gamma-prime precipitates; precipitation-hardening stainless steels such as 17-4 PH; and maraging steels and titanium alloys used in airframes, fasteners, turbines and surgical instruments.
