The Australasian gannet (Morus serrator), also known as the Australian gannet or tākapu in Māori, is a large seabird of the booby and gannet family, Sulidae. Adults are mostly white with black flight feathers and a golden-buff head. It breeds in dense colonies on islands and headlands around New Zealand and southeastern Australia and is famous for its spectacular plunge-diving for fish.

Description

The Australasian gannet has a wingspan of 170–200 cm, a long pointed bill with serrated edges, and a distinctive buff-yellow crown and nape. The sexes are similar; juveniles are mottled grey-brown and take up to five years to attain adult plumage. It is closely related to the northern gannet and Cape gannet, from which it differs in its darker tail and eye markings.

Breeding and behaviour

Major colonies include Muriwai (Ōtokamiro Point) near Auckland — unusual for being on the mainland — Cape Kidnappers, and islands in Bass Strait such as Lawrence Rocks and Pedra Branca. Pairs are monogamous and return to the same nest site, laying a single chalky blue egg incubated under the large webbed feet for about 44 days. Gannets feed on pilchards, anchovies, mackerel and squid, plunge-diving from heights of up to 30 metres and reaching speeds near 100 km/h. After breeding, many birds disperse across the Tasman Sea; young birds migrate to Australian waters for several years before returning to breed.

Population

Numbers have generally increased since protection; the world population is estimated in the low hundreds of thousands of birds, and the species is listed as Least Concern, though local colonies are sensitive to food availability, marine heatwaves and avian influenza.
