Cornish (Kernowek) is a Southwestern Brittonic Celtic language native to Cornwall in south-western England. It is a close relative of Welsh and Breton. Cornish declined steadily from the Middle Ages as English spread westward, and ceased to be a living community language by the end of the 18th century; Dolly Pentreath (died 1777) is often cited as its last fluent native speaker, though some knowledge persisted into the 19th century.

History

Old Cornish (c. 800–1200) is known mainly from glosses and place names. Middle Cornish (1200–1550) produced a rich religious literature, including the Ordinalia cycle of mystery plays and the poem Pascon agan Arluth. The Prayer Book Rebellion of 1549, partly a protest against the English-language prayer book, accelerated decline; Late Cornish (1550–1800) shows increasing English influence and loss of traditional mutations in writing.

Revival

The revival began with Henry Jenner's Handbook of the Cornish Language (1904). Twentieth-century revivalists developed competing orthographies — Unified Cornish, Kernewek Kemmyn, and Modern (Late) Cornish — a dispute sometimes called the "orthography wars." In 2008 the rival camps agreed on a Standard Written Form (SWF). Cornish was recognised under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in 2002, some Cornish schools teach it, and the 2011 census recorded 557 people declaring Cornish as their main language. UNESCO reclassified it from "extinct" to "critically endangered" in 2010.

Features

Like other Celtic languages, Cornish has initial consonant mutations, a verb-subject-object tendency in some constructions, and inflected prepositions. Example: My a gar Kernow — "I love Cornwall."
