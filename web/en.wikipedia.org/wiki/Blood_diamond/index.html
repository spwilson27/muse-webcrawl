Blood diamond

Blood diamonds (also called conflict diamonds, war diamonds or hot diamonds) are diamonds mined in a war zone and sold to finance an insurgency, an invading army's war efforts, or a warlord's activity. The term is used to highlight the negative consequences of the diamond trade in certain areas, or to label an individual diamond as having come from such an area. Diamonds mined during the civil wars in Angola, Ivory Coast, Sierra Leone and other nations have been given this label.

History

During the Sierra Leone Civil War (1991–2002), the Revolutionary United Front financed its insurgency through diamond mining, often using forced labour, and traded rough stones for weapons. Similar patterns occurred in Angola, where UNITA funded its war effort with diamonds, and in Liberia under Charles Taylor. Campaigning by organisations such as Global Witness and Partnership Africa Canada brought international attention to the issue in the late 1990s.

Kimberley Process

In response, diamond-producing states, the industry and civil society negotiated the Kimberley Process Certification Scheme, launched in 2003, which requires member states to certify shipments of rough diamonds as conflict-free. Critics argue that the scheme's narrow definition of conflict diamonds and weak enforcement limit its effectiveness, and abuses linked to state forces, as in Zimbabwe's Marange fields, exposed gaps in the system.

In popular culture

The issue reached a wide audience through the 2006 film Blood Diamond, set during the Sierra Leone civil war.
