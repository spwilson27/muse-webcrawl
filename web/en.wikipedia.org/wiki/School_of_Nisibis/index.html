School of Nisibis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

School of Nisibis

Location: Nisibis (modern Nusaybin, Turkey)

Founded: 4th century AD

Tradition: Church of the East, Syriac Christianity

Notable heads: Narsai, Abraham of Beth Rabban, Henana of Adiabene

The School of Nisibis was an institution of Christian theological learning in the city of Nisibis in Mesopotamia (modern Nusaybin in southeastern Turkey). For several centuries it was the leading centre of education of the Church of the East, and one of the most important schools of Syriac Christianity, sometimes described as one of the earliest universities in the world.

Origins

According to tradition the school was founded in the fourth century under Bishop Jacob of Nisibis, with Ephrem the Syrian among its early teachers. When Nisibis was ceded to the Sasanian Empire after the Roman defeat of 363, Ephrem and many Christian teachers withdrew westward to Edessa, where the School of Edessa (the "School of the Persians") developed. After the Emperor Zeno closed the Edessan school in 489 because of its association with the theology of Nestorius and Theodore of Mopsuestia, its scholars migrated east into Persian territory and re-established their teaching at Nisibis under the direction of Narsai, sometimes called the "Harp of the Holy Spirit."

Organisation

Under Narsai and his successor Abraham of Beth Rabban (director for some fifty years in the sixth century), the School of Nisibis acquired a formal organisation. Its statutes, preserved from 496 and revised in 590 and 602, regulate admission, discipline, the curriculum and the duties of officers. Students — who at times numbered in the hundreds and reportedly around a thousand — lived a quasi-monastic common life, studied for three years, and were supported in part by endowments and by work. The teaching staff included an interpreter (mephashqana) of Scripture, lectors, scribes and teachers of reading and writing.

Curriculum and theology

The curriculum centred on the close reading of the Bible in Syriac (the Peshitta), following the exegetical tradition of Theodore of Mopsuestia, whose works had been translated from Greek into Syriac. Students progressed from reading and calligraphy through grammar and exegesis to disputation; medicine and secular subjects were taught in a separate institution. The school's Antiochene, literal-historical method of interpretation shaped the theology of the Church of the East for generations.

In the late sixth century the director Henana of Adiabene attempted to move the school toward a more Alexandrian, allegorical exegesis and a theology closer to Byzantine orthodoxy, provoking a celebrated schism in which many masters and students seceded — an episode documented in the chronicles of the Church of the East.

Decline and legacy

The school survived the Muslim conquest of the seventh century and continued to train clergy and scholars, though its relative importance declined as Baghdad and other centres rose. Its graduates spread across Persia and Central Asia — among them missionaries, translators and administrators — and its statutes influenced later monastic and cathedral schools. The School of Nisibis is remembered as a principal conduit by which Greek learning, in Syriac translation, passed into the Persian and later the Islamic world.
