George Elwood Smith (born 10 May 1930) is an American physicist and co-inventor of the charge-coupled device (CCD). He shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics with Willard Boyle for the invention, which transformed photography, astronomy and imaging science.

Smith studied at the University of Pennsylvania and the University of Chicago (PhD 1959) and spent his career at Bell Labs (1959–86), heading the VLSI device department. The CCD emerged in 1969 from Boyle and Smith's search for a semiconductor analogue of magnetic bubble memory: within a week of the sketch, their colleagues had a working model, and within a few years Fairchild and others had imaging arrays — the first digital 'film'. Smith, a lifelong sailor, logged thousands of ocean miles after retirement.

The invention

A CCD pixel is a MOS capacitor on silicon: photons create electron–hole pairs, the electrons accumulate in a potential well proportional to the light received, and clock pulses shift the charge packets row by row to an amplifier. The device's extreme sensitivity and linearity — it counts photons almost perfectly — made it the scientific imager of choice for three decades, from Voyager's cameras to ground-based surveys, before CMOS sensors took over consumer devices.
