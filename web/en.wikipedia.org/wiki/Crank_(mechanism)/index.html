Crank (mechanism)

A crank is an arm attached at a right angle to a rotating shaft, by which circular motion is imparted to, or received from, the shaft. When combined with a connecting rod it forms one of the simplest and oldest mechanisms for converting reciprocating motion into rotary motion, or vice versa — the crank and slider mechanism at the heart of the piston engine.

Operation

The crank rotates about its axis; its offset pin (the crankpin) describes a circle of radius called the throw. A connecting rod joins the pin to a slider or piston, so that as the crank turns, the piston moves back and forth through a stroke equal to twice the throw. In a steam or internal combustion engine the expanding gases drive the piston and the crank converts that push into shaft rotation; run the other way, as in a crank-driven pump or a hand-cranked winch, rotary input produces reciprocating or lifting work.

History

Cranks appeared on rotary querns and grindstones in the ancient world, were depicted driving a mill in the third-century Hierapolis sawmill relief, and became common in medieval machinery — water-driven sawmills, forge bellows and the treadle lathe — long before their famous application in steam engines, where early patent disputes encouraged alternatives such as the sun-and-planet gear. Familiar examples include bicycle pedal cranks (see crankset), fishing reel handles and starting handles on early motor cars.
