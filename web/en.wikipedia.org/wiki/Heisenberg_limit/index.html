Heisenberg limit

The Heisenberg limit is a fundamental limit on the measurement precision that can be attained in quantum metrology using a given amount of resources. It is named after Werner Heisenberg, whose uncertainty principle underlies the quantum limits on measurement.

Statement

When estimating a parameter such as an optical phase using N independent particles (for example photons), classical strategies are limited by the standard quantum limit (or shot-noise limit), in which the measurement uncertainty decreases as 1/√N. Using quantum resources such as entanglement or squeezed states, the uncertainty can in principle be reduced to scale as 1/N — the Heisenberg limit — a quadratic improvement.

Origin of the limit

The 1/N scaling follows from the quantum Fisher information for states such as the N00N state, in which N particles are in a superposition of all being in one mode and all being in another; such states accumulate phase N times faster than a single particle. Because measurement disturbance and the Heisenberg uncertainty relations cap the available Fisher information for a fixed total resource count, the Heisenberg limit is regarded as the ultimate quantum bound for linear measurement strategies.

Applications and debates

The Heisenberg limit is a benchmark for interferometers, atomic clocks, magnetometers, and gravitational-wave detectors, where squeezed light and entanglement have been used to surpass the standard quantum limit over limited ranges of N. There has been considerable debate in the literature over how the resource should be counted — number of particles, number of passes through the sample, or total time and energy — and over how decoherence and loss degrade the attainable scaling in practical experiments.
