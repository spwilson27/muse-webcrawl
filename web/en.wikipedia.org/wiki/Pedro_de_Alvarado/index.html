Pedro de Alvarado

Born: c. 1485, Badajoz, Spain
Died: 4 July 1541, Guadalajara, New Spain
Occupation: Conquistador; Governor of Guatemala

Pedro de Alvarado y Contreras (c. 1485 – 4 July 1541) was a Spanish conquistador and governor of Guatemala. A principal lieutenant of Hernán Cortés in the conquest of the Aztec Empire, he led the Spanish conquest of much of Central America, including Guatemala and El Salvador. The indigenous peoples of Mexico called him Tonatiuh, "the Sun", for his blond hair and fair complexion.

Early career

Born in Badajoz, Extremadura, Alvarado sailed to Hispaniola around 1510 and took part in the conquest of Cuba. In 1518 he served under Juan de Grijalva's expedition to the Yucatán coast, and in 1519 he joined Cortés's expedition to Mexico, becoming one of its chief captains. During Cortés's absence from Tenochtitlan in May 1520, Alvarado ordered the Massacre in the Great Temple, in which many Mexica nobles were killed during a festival — an act that provoked the uprising in which the Spanish were driven from the city on the Noche Triste.

Conquest of Central America

In 1523–1524 Alvarado led an expedition south from Mexico that conquered the K'iche' and Kaqchikel Maya kingdoms of highland Guatemala, founding Santiago de los Caballeros (now Antigua Guatemala), and went on to subdue Cuscatlán, in modern El Salvador. He served as governor and captain general of Guatemala. Ambitious and restless, he also mounted expeditions to Honduras and Ecuador, where he clashed with the forces of Francisco Pizarro before selling his fleet and men to Diego de Almagro.

Death

Alvarado died in 1541 during the Mixtón War in New Galicia, crushed by a horse that fell on him during a retreat near Nochistlán. His widow, Beatriz de la Cueva, briefly succeeded him as governor of Guatemala before dying in the earthquake and flood that destroyed Santiago weeks later. Alvarado remains a controversial figure, remembered for both military daring and notorious cruelty, including massacres commemorated in indigenous accounts such as the Annals of the Kaqchikels.
