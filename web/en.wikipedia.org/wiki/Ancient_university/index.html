Ancient university

The term ancient university refers to the seven medieval universities of the British Isles that were founded before 1600 and have been in continuous operation since: the University of Oxford, the University of Cambridge, the University of St Andrews, the University of Glasgow, the University of Aberdeen, the University of Edinburgh, and the University of Dublin (Trinity College Dublin). The description is most commonly applied in the United Kingdom and Ireland, and the institutions share distinctive traditions and governance structures.

Oxford and Cambridge — collectively "Oxbridge" — are federations of autonomous colleges. The four ancient Scottish universities were founded by papal bull and share features such as the undergraduate Master of Arts degree. Dublin's Trinity College was established by Queen Elizabeth I on the model of Oxford and Cambridge.

Distinctive features

The ancient universities are characterized by collegiate organization (at Oxford, Cambridge, and Dublin), historic privileges, distinctive academic dress, and unusual degree structures. In Scotland, the rector — elected by students — and the general council of graduates remain part of governance. The undergraduate MA at the Scottish ancients and at Oxbridge differs from the postgraduate MA offered elsewhere.

Context

The ancient universities long predated the civic or "redbrick" universities of the nineteenth century, the plate-glass universities of the 1960s, and the post-1992 universities. Much older institutions existed elsewhere in the medieval world — Bologna (1088), Paris, and Salamanca in Europe, and al-Qarawiyyin in Fez and al-Azhar in Cairo in the Islamic world — but "ancient university" as a label is specific to the British and Irish seven.
