Surgical staple

Surgical staples are metal clips used in surgery to close wounds and join tissues, as an alternative or complement to sutures. They are applied with dedicated stapling devices and are widely used for skin closure, bowel resection and anastomosis, and lung and vascular surgery.

History

The first surgical stapling device was developed by Hungarian surgeon Hümér Hültl in 1908; later refinements by Aladár von Petz and, from the 1950s, Soviet and then American instrument makers (notably the work commercialised by Leon Hirsch and others) produced the modern linear, circular and endoscopic staplers. Early staples were stainless steel; titanium became common for its strength, low tissue reactivity and relative compatibility.

Types and use

Skin staples are removable clips applied with a handheld device for closing straight, low-tension wounds quickly; they are removed with a special extractor after healing. Internal staplers place rows of small staples that fold into a closed "B" shape, compressing tissue, stopping bleeding and sealing the join; circular staplers are used for end-to-end bowel anastomosis, and linear cutters staple and divide tissue in one motion in gastrointestinal and thoracic procedures. Absorbable staples also exist for some soft-tissue uses.

Comparison with sutures

Staples are faster to apply — a significant advantage in long operations and trauma — and can give consistent tension, but cost more per device and may be less precise cosmetically. Studies of skin closure generally find staples quicker with broadly similar infection and healing outcomes, though sutures can be preferred on the face and other cosmetically sensitive areas.
