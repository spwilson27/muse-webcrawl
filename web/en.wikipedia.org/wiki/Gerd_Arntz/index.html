Gerd Arntz

Born 11 December 1900, Remscheid, German Empire
Died 4 December 1988 (aged 87), The Hague, Netherlands
Occupation Graphic artist, designer
Known for Isotype pictograms

Gerd Arntz (11 December 1900 – 4 December 1988) was a German and Dutch graphic artist and designer, best known for developing, with Otto Neurath and Marie Neurath, the Isotype (International System of Typographic Picture Education) picture language — the standardized pictograms used to visualize statistical and social information.

Life and work

Arntz was born in Remscheid and grew up in Düsseldorf, where he worked in a factory and studied art. Politically radicalized by the First World War and the German revolution, he became associated with the Cologne Progressives (Kölner Progressive), a group of artists including Franz Wilhelm Seiwert and Heinrich Hoerle, and produced sharply satirical woodcuts and linocuts depicting class society, war, and capitalism for left-wing publications.

In 1929 the Austrian philosopher and sociologist Otto Neurath invited Arntz to Vienna to head the graphic department of the Gesellschafts- und Wirtschaftsmuseum (Social and Economic Museum). Together with Neurath and the "transformer" Marie Reidemeister (later Marie Neurath), Arntz designed the system of simple, repeatable pictorial symbols — men, women, workers, ships, sacks of grain, factories — by which charts could show quantities through repeated icons. He eventually drew more than 4,000 such pictograms.

In the early 1930s Arntz also worked in the Soviet Union, helping set up the Isostat institute in Moscow. After the Austrian Civil War of 1934 he moved to the Netherlands, co-founding the International Foundation for Visual Education in The Hague. Following Neurath's death in 1945, Arntz continued statistical graphic work for Dutch institutions, including UNESCO-related projects. His pictograms became a foundation of modern information design and an ancestor of today's ubiquitous public-information symbols and icons.
