Hiwi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hiwi (German: [ˈhiːviː]) is a German abbreviation of Hilfswilliger, "one willing to help," used during World War II for auxiliary volunteers recruited from the population of German-occupied Eastern Europe, particularly Soviet prisoners of war and civilians, who served the Wehrmacht and SS in non-combat and support roles.

Recruitment and roles

Following the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, German forces faced severe manpower shortages and appalling conditions in POW camps, where millions of Soviet prisoners starved. Many prisoners volunteered as Hiwis to survive, serving as drivers, cooks, stretcher-bearers, ammunition carriers, interpreters, and guards. By 1944 their numbers may have reached several hundred thousand to over a million. Despite Nazi racial ideology, which officially despised Slavs, the German army became increasingly dependent on them.

Atrocities and the Trawniki men

Some auxiliaries participated in war crimes. The "Trawniki men," Soviet POWs trained at the Trawniki camp, were deployed as guards in Operation Reinhard extermination camps including Belzec, Sobibor, and Treblinka, and in mass shootings and ghetto liquidations. Motivation ranged from survival and coercion to anti-communism, nationalism, and antisemitism, and historians continue to debate the balance of compulsion and voluntarism.

Aftermath

Under the Yalta agreements, most Hiwis repatriated to the Soviet Union faced imprisonment in the Gulag or execution as traitors. A few notorious individuals, such as John Demjanjuk, were prosecuted decades later in the West. The term Hiwi survives in modern German colloquially for a menial assistant.
