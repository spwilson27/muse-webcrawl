Gaia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gaia (Greek: Γαῖα), also spelled Gaea, is the personification of the Earth in Greek mythology and one of the primordial deities. Born after Chaos at the dawn of creation, she is the ancestral mother of all life: from her came the sky (Uranus), the mountains and the sea (Pontus), and with Uranus she bore the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hecatoncheires. Her Roman counterpart is Terra (Tellus).

Gaia, mother of gods and mortals, the primal Earth of Greek myth.

Mythology

In Hesiod's Theogony, Gaia is the second being to exist and the foundation of the cosmos. Oppressed by Uranus, who hid her monstrous children within her, Gaia fashioned a sickle and persuaded her son Cronus to castrate his father; from the blood that fell on her sprang the Furies, the Giants and the ash-tree nymphs. When Cronus in turn proved tyrannical, Gaia's prophecies aided Zeus in overthrowing him. Later she produced the monster Typhon against Zeus and sided with the Giants in the Gigantomachy. Gaia was also an oracular power — Delphi was said to have been hers before Apollo — and oaths sworn by her were among the most binding.

Worship and legacy

Gaia was worshipped throughout Greece, usually in association with fertility, agriculture and the dead, though she rarely had temples of her own. Her name and symbolism were revived in the twentieth century by the Gaia hypothesis of James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, which treats Earth's biosphere as a self-regulating system, and by environmental movements that adopted Gaia as an emblem of the living planet; the European Space Agency's star-mapping mission Gaia also bears her name.
