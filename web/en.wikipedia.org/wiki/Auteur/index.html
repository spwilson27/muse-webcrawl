Auteur

Auteur theory
OriginFrance, 1940s–1950s
Key textsTruffaut, "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema" (1954); Sarris, "Notes on the Auteur Theory" (1962)
JournalCahiers du Cinéma
ClaimThe director is the primary creative author of a film

An auteur (French for "author") is a filmmaker whose individual style and complete control over all elements of production give a film its personal and unique stamp. The concept rests on auteur theory, which holds that a film reflects its director's personal creative vision as if the director were the work's primary author — even within the industrial, collaborative studio system.

Origins

The idea grew out of French film culture: Alexandre Astruc's 1948 essay on the caméra-stylo ("camera-pen") proposed that directors should write with the camera as authors write with a pen. Critics at Cahiers du Cinéma — including François Truffaut, Jean-Luc Godard, and Éric Rohmer, many of whom became leading directors of the French New Wave — developed the politique des auteurs, revaluing Hollywood genre directors such as Alfred Hitchcock, Howard Hawks, and John Ford as artists with consistent worldviews and motifs across their bodies of work.

Reception and criticism

Andrew Sarris imported the theory to the United States in 1962 and ranked directors in The American Cinema (1968). Critics such as Pauline Kael objected that auteur theory over-credits directors at the expense of screenwriters, cinematographers, and editors, and that it can excuse weak films by admired directors. Subsequent scholarship (including Peter Wollen's structuralist version) refined the theory, which remains a default framework for film criticism, festival programming, and director-centred marketing.
