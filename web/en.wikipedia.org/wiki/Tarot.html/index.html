Tarot

Type: Playing card pack / divination tool
Origin: Northern Italy, mid-15th century
Cards: 78 (22 trumps, 56 suit cards)

Tarot is a pack of playing cards, first used in parts of Europe for games from the mid-15th century and, from the late 18th century onward, also widely used for divination. A traditional tarot pack has 78 cards: four suits of fourteen cards each (including four court cards), plus a special suit of twenty-one trump cards and a single card known as the Fool.

History

Tarot packs appeared in northern Italian city-states such as Milan, Ferrara and Bologna in the 1440s, where the cards were called carte da trionfi ("cards of the triumphs"). Sumptuous hand-painted packs, such as the Visconti-Sforza tarot, were commissioned by noble families. The game spread through Europe, and in France the pack became known as tarot; trick-taking tarot games are still played in France, Austria, Italy and elsewhere.

The suit signs of early Italian packs were swords, batons, cups and coins; French packs later adopted regional patterns. The allegorical trump sequence — including the Magician, the Popess (later the High Priestess), Death, the Tower and the World — appears to have originated as Renaissance allegory rather than as a repository of secret doctrine.

Occult tarot

In 1781 the French writer Court de Gébelin published an essay claiming, without evidence, that tarot originated in ancient Egypt. From that point the cards were taken up by occultists: Etteilla produced the first pack designed for divination, and Éliphas Lévi linked the trumps to the Hebrew alphabet and Kabbalah. In the English-speaking world the most influential pack is the Rider–Waite tarot (1909), designed by Arthur Edward Waite and illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith, whose fully illustrated suit cards established the model followed by most modern decks.

Structure

In occult usage the twenty-two trumps plus the Fool are called the Major Arcana, and the fifty-six suit cards the Minor Arcana. Divinatory practice ("tarot reading") interprets the cards drawn in a layout or "spread" as symbols bearing on a question, often incorporating elemental, astrological or numerological correspondences. Modern scholarship generally regards tarot's occult associations as an 18th-century invention layered onto a 15th-century card game.
