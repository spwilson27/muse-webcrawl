Violence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Definition (WHO)"The intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community, that either results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment or deprivation."

Violence is the use of physical force to cause harm. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines violence as "the intentional use of physical force or power, threatened or actual, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community, which either results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, maldevelopment, or deprivation"; it recognizes the inclusion of "power" in addition to the conventional use of "physical force" as broadening the definition to include acts arising from power relationships, including threats and intimidation.

Globally, violence resulted in the deaths of an estimated 1.28 million people in 2013, up from 1.13 million in 1990. However, relative to the world's population growth, the rate of violent death declined over that period. Of the deaths in 2013, roughly 842,000 were attributed to self-harm (suicide), 405,000 to interpersonal violence, and 31,000 to collective violence (war) and legal intervention.

Typology

The WHO divides violence into three broad categories according to the relationship between victim and perpetrator: self-directed violence (including suicidal behaviour and self-abuse), interpersonal violence (family and intimate partner violence, and community violence such as youth violence and assault by strangers), and collective violence (social, political, and economic violence committed by larger groups, including war and organized crime). Violence can also be classified by its nature: physical, sexual, psychological, and involving deprivation or neglect.

Causes

Research on violence draws on the ecological model, which considers interacting factors at the individual level (such as biological and personal history factors), the relationship level (family and peers), the community level (neighborhoods, schools, workplaces), and the societal level (norms, policies, and inequalities). No single factor explains why some people behave violently; rather, violence results from a combination of influences. Risk factors include exposure to violence in childhood, alcohol and drug abuse, access to firearms, and social norms condoning violence.

Prevention

Public-health approaches to violence prevention emphasize primary prevention—stopping violence before it occurs—through strategies such as early childhood development programs, life-skills and parenting training, reducing alcohol availability, restricting access to lethal means, changing gender norms, and poverty-reduction and urban-upgrading programs. The WHO and the United Nations promote evidence-based approaches summarized in frameworks such as INSPIRE and RESPECT. Criminal justice approaches focus on deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation.

History

Scholars debate whether violence has declined over the long term. Some, such as Steven Pinker in The Better Angels of Our Nature (2011), argue that rates of homicide and warfare have fallen markedly over centuries with the growth of states, commerce, and humanitarian norms. Others dispute aspects of this thesis, pointing to the unprecedented scale of 20th-century warfare and to violence that official statistics may miss. Historical homicide studies in Europe do show a long decline from medieval rates of tens per 100,000 to around one per 100,000 in many countries today.
