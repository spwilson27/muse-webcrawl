A microphone, colloquially called a mic, is a transducer that converts sound into an electrical signal. Microphones are used in telephones, hearing aids, public address systems, radio and television broadcasting, sound recording, voice-over-IP, and scientific instruments such as sound-level meters.

History

The first practical microphones were carbon-button devices developed in the 1870s for the telephone: Emile Berliner and Thomas Edison both claimed the invention, while David Edward Hughes demonstrated a loose-contact carbon device independently. The carbon microphone, standard in telephony for a century, was joined in the twentieth century by the condenser microphone (invented by E. C. Wente at Bell Labs in 1916), the dynamic (moving-coil) microphone and Harry F. Olson's ribbon microphone at RCA. Later advances included the electret microphone (1960s) and silicon MEMS microphones, now found in virtually every smartphone.

Types

Dynamic microphones move a coil of wire in a magnetic field and are rugged and tolerant of loud sounds, making them staples of live performance. Condenser microphones use a charged diaphragm forming one plate of a capacitor; they are more sensitive and extended in frequency response and dominate studio recording, requiring phantom power or a battery. Ribbon microphones suspend a thin corrugated metal ribbon in a magnetic field and are prized for a smooth, natural sound. Microphones also differ in directional pattern — omnidirectional, cardioid, supercardioid and figure-of-eight — determined by how sound reaches the diaphragm.

Applications

Beyond media and music, microphones serve in measurement (calibrated instrumentation mics), surveillance, ultrasound medical imaging and non-destructive testing, and as arrays in smart speakers and conferencing systems using beamforming.
