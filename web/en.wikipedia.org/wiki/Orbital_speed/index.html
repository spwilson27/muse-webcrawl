Orbital speed

In gravitationally bound systems, the orbital speed of an astronomical body or object — for example a planet, natural satellite, artificial satellite, or star — is the speed at which it orbits around either the barycentre of the system or another body. For a circular orbit, the speed is constant and is given by v = √(GM/r), where G is the gravitational constant, M the mass of the central body, and r the orbital radius.

Circular orbits

A body in a low Earth orbit travels at about 7.8 kilometres per second. The Moon orbits Earth at an average of about 1.02 km/s, and Earth orbits the Sun at about 29.8 km/s. Orbital speed decreases with distance from the central mass: the further away a body is, the slower it moves, in accordance with Kepler's third law.

Elliptical orbits

In an elliptical orbit the speed varies: it is greatest at periapsis (closest approach) and least at apoapsis. The instantaneous speed at any point is given by the vis-viva equation, v² = GM(2/r − 1/a), where a is the semi-major axis of the orbit.

Escape velocity

If a body's orbital speed at a given distance is increased by a factor of √2, it reaches escape velocity and leaves on a parabolic (or, with still more speed, hyperbolic) trajectory. Orbital speed is thus a central concept in astrodynamics and mission design.
