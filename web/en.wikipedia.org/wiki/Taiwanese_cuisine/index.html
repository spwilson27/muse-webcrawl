Taiwanese cuisine

Taiwanese cuisine (Chinese: 臺灣料理) is a popular style of food with several variations, including Chinese and that of the indigenous peoples of Taiwan. The cuisine has been shaped by the island's history: the cooking of Fujianese (Hokkien) settlers, Hakka communities, Japanese colonial rule from 1895 to 1945, and the arrival in 1949 of mainland Chinese from many provinces, who brought and adapted dishes such as beef noodle soup. Generous use is made of pork, seafood, chicken, rice and soy; beef was historically avoided by many farmers, whose water buffalo were working animals.

Characteristics

Taiwanese cooking emphasises fresh, seasonal ingredients and, in contrast to some mainland styles, relatively light seasoning for main ingredients, with flavour built from aromatics such as basil (notably "nine-story pagoda" basil), garlic, ginger, spring onions, cilantro and pickled mustard greens. Soy sauce, rice wine and sesame oil are common; sweet potato and taro are staple starches alongside rice.

Notable dishes

  • Beef noodle soup — braised or clear-broth beef with wheat noodles, often cited as Taiwan's national dish; an annual festival in Taipei judges the best bowls.
  • Gua bao — a steamed bun folded around braised pork belly, pickled mustard greens, coriander and ground peanuts.
  • Oyster omelette — small oysters in an egg and sweet-potato starch batter, served with a sweet-savoury sauce.
  • Lu rou fan — minced or diced braised pork over rice.
  • Three-cup chicken — chicken braised with equal measures of soy sauce, rice wine and sesame oil, finished with basil.
  • Danzai noodles, coffin bread (Tainan), iron eggs (Tamsui), a-gei, oyster vermicelli, pork pepper buns (hujiao bing), pineapple cakes and sun cakes.
  • Bubble tea (boba) — sweet milk tea with tapioca pearls, invented in Taiwan in the 1980s and now global.
  • Shaved ice desserts with mango, red beans, taro balls and grass jelly.

Night markets

Taiwan's night markets — such as Shilin in Taipei, Fengjia in Taichung, Ruifeng in Kaohsiung and the Miaokou market in Keelung — are central to its food culture, offering inexpensive small eats (xiao chi) from hundreds of stalls: fried chicken fillets, stinky tofu, pepper buns, grilled squid, papaya milk and much more. Snacking and grazing across many stalls is the characteristic way of eating.

Influences

Japanese rule left a legacy of tempura (adapted as tiánbùlà, from a misreading of the dish's name), oden-style simmered items, sushi, mochi and miso soup. Post-1949 mainland immigration added northern wheat-based foods and Sichuan-style spices. Indigenous Austronesian cuisines contribute mountain vegetables, millet and distinctive barbecue and preserved-meat traditions.
