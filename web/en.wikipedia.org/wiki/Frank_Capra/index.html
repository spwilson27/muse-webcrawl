Frank Capra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Frank Capra behind the camera in the 1930s, megaphone-era director in a soft cap, lining up a shot at Columbia]

Frank Russell Capra (18 May 1897 – 3 September 1991) was an Italian-American film director, producer and writer, the defining filmmaker of Depression-era America's faith in itself. His comedies and fables of the common man — It Happened One Night (1934), Mr. Deeds Goes to Town (1936), You Can't Take It with You (1938), Mr. Smith Goes to Washington (1939) — won him three Best Director Oscars in five years and made 'Capraesque' a word, before It's a Wonderful Life (1946) flopped on release and grew, by television repetition, into America's Christmas scripture.

Born in Sicily and raised in Los Angeles poverty, Capra trained as an engineer, drifted into films in San Francisco, and at Columbia Pictures under Harry Cohn became the studio's flagship — his name above the title, a director's prerogative he pioneered.

The Capra touch

Capra's method — fast pace, overlapping dialogue, a populist plot pitting a decent innocent against banks, press barons and political machines — crystallised in the films with writer Robert Riskin and stars Gary Cooper and James Stewart. It Happened One Night swept the five major Oscars in 1935, a feat unmatched for forty years. His wartime Why We Fight propaganda series (1942–45) earned him the Legion of Merit and taught documentary its modern rhetoric.

It's a Wonderful Life

Capra called it his favourite; 1946 audiences shrugged. Copyright lapse in the 1970s let television stations run it endlessly each December, and George Bailey's discovery — in summary, that his unremarkable life has held a whole town together — became the culture's own. The American Film Institute ranks it among the most inspiring American films; its despair (the Pottersville sequence, the suicidal bridge scene) is as much a part of the text as its redemption.
