Space physics

Space physics, also known as space plasma physics or solar-terrestrial physics, is the study of naturally occurring plasmas in the universe, principally within the Solar System. It includes the study of the solar wind, planetary magnetospheres, ionospheres, auroras, cosmic rays and synchrotron radiation. Space physics is both a pure science and an applied one, since the space environment directly affects spacecraft operations, astronaut safety, radio communications and power grids.

History

The field grew out of geomagnetism, the study of auroras and the discovery of the Van Allen radiation belts by Explorer 1 in 1958. Direct sampling of the solar wind by early spacecraft in the 1960s confirmed Eugene Parker's theory of a supersonic outflow from the solar corona. Later missions — ISEE, Ulysses, Cluster, THEMIS, the Van Allen Probes and the Parker Solar Probe — mapped magnetic reconnection, substorms and the acceleration of particles in near-Earth space.

Subject matter

Space physicists study how the Sun's plasma and magnetic field fill the heliosphere and couple to planets and other bodies. Key topics include the structure of the magnetosphere, magnetic reconnection at the magnetopause and in the magnetotail, geomagnetic storms and substorms, the ionosphere and thermosphere, radiation-belt dynamics, and the propagation of coronal mass ejections that drive space weather. Unlike astrophysics, which uses remote observations, space physics relies heavily on in situ measurements by spacecraft flying through the plasma itself.

Applications

Because geomagnetic storms can disrupt satellites, GPS navigation, HF radio and electrical grids, operational agencies such as NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center issue forecasts based on space-physics research. The discipline overlaps with plasma physics, atmospheric science and astrophysics.
