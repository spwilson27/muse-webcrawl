Supercontinent

A supercontinent is a landmass comprising more than one continental core, or craton. The assembly of cratons and accreted terranes that form Eurasia qualifies as a supercontinent today. The best-known past supercontinent is Pangaea, which incorporated almost all of Earth's landmasses during the late Paleozoic and early Mesozoic eras. The repeated assembly and breakup of supercontinents over geological time is known as the supercontinent cycle, driven by plate tectonics.

Pangaea, the most recent supercontinent, existed roughly 335 to 175 million years ago.

History

Evidence from paleomagnetism and geology indicates that supercontinents have formed and dispersed repeatedly. Rodinia assembled about 1.1 billion years ago and broke up during the Neoproterozoic, possibly triggering Snowball Earth episodes. Gondwana formed in the late Neoproterozoic and later merged with Laurasia's components to form Pangaea. Pangaea's breakup, beginning about 175 million years ago, created the Atlantic Ocean and shaped modern geography.

Effects

Supercontinent assembly reduces shallow marine habitat, alters ocean circulation and global climate (often producing arid interiors and ice ages), and affects the evolution and distribution of life. Breakup creates new ocean basins and rift systems, increases biodiversity through geographic isolation, and is associated with large igneous province eruptions that may cause mass extinctions.

Future supercontinents

Geologists have proposed several candidates for the next supercontinent, expected in 200–300 million years: Pangaea Ultima (or Proxima), formed by the closing of the Atlantic; Novopangaea, formed by closing of the Pacific; Amasia, formed by the merger of Asia and the Americas near the Arctic; and Aurica, combining both closures.
