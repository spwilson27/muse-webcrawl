Irish cuisine

Irish cuisine encompasses the cooking styles, traditions and recipes associated with the island of Ireland. It has evolved from centuries of social and political change and the mixing of different cultures, mainly the Gaelic, Norman, Viking and English influences. The cuisine is founded upon the crops and animals farmed in its temperate climate, most famously the potato, which arrived from the Americas in the late 16th century and became a staple.

History

There are many references to food and drink in early Irish literature. Honey was widely used, and mead was drunk. From the medieval period, the diet of the poor consisted largely of potatoes, oats, milk and butter, while the wealthy ate meat, including pork, beef and mutton. The Great Famine of 1845–1852, caused by potato blight, devastated the population and left a lasting mark on Ireland's food culture.

Representative dishes

  • Irish stew – a stew traditionally made with lamb or mutton, potatoes, onions and carrots.
  • Boxty – a traditional potato pancake.
  • Colcannon – mashed potatoes with kale or cabbage.
  • Soda bread – bread leavened with baking soda rather than yeast.
  • Full Irish breakfast – eggs, bacon, sausages, black and white pudding, beans and fried tomato.
  • Coddle – a Dublin dish of sausages, bacon, onion and potato stewed in stock.
  • Barmbrack – a yeasted bread with added sultanas and raisins.

Drinks

Ireland is famous for stout, most notably Guinness, and for Irish whiskey, produced by triple distillation. Tea is consumed in very large quantities and is a central feature of Irish hospitality.
