Nazi Germany undertook several research programmes relating to nuclear technology, including nuclear weapons and nuclear reactors, before and during World War II. These were variously called the Uranverein ('Uranium Club') or Uranprojekt ('Uranium Project'). Nuclear fission was discovered in Germany in December 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, with theoretical explanation by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch.

Programme history

The first Uranverein began in April 1939 under the Army Weapons Office; a second, larger effort followed the invasion of Poland. Leading physicists including Werner Heisenberg, Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker, and Paul Harteck worked on reactor theory, isotope separation, and heavy water moderation. The programme never received the industrial priority of the Allied Manhattan Project: it remained fragmented between competing groups, lost many Jewish and émigré scientists to Nazi persecution, and suffered setbacks such as Walther Bothe's erroneous measurement of graphite's neutron absorption, which pushed Germany toward scarce heavy water from the Vemork plant in Norway — repeatedly sabotaged by Norwegian commandos and Allied bombing.

End of the war

By 1945 the most advanced German experiment, the B-VIII reactor in a cave at Haigerloch, had assembled uranium cubes in heavy water but was not large enough to achieve criticality. The Allied Alsos Mission seized documents, materials, and scientists; ten leading figures were interned at Farm Hall in England, where secretly recorded conversations captured their surprise at the Hiroshima bomb — transcripts published decades later.

Assessment

Historians debate how close Germany came and whether scientists deliberately dragged their feet; the consensus is that the programme was years from a bomb owing to under-resourcing, miscalculations, and organisational fragmentation.
