Suffrage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Suffrage, political franchise, or simply franchise is the right to vote in public, political elections and referendums. It is sometimes combined with the right to stand for office (passive suffrage). Historically restricted by property, tax, literacy, race, religion and sex, suffrage expanded through struggle to near-universal adult citizenship in democracies — the central story of democratisation.

Expansion

Milestones include the British Reform Acts (1832, 1867, 1884) under Chartist and later pressure; New Zealand's world-first women's suffrage (1893); the US Fifteenth (1870, race — nullified by Jim Crow until the Voting Rights Act 1965), Nineteenth (1920, women), Twenty-fourth (1964, poll tax) and Twenty-sixth (1971, age 18) Amendments; post-1945 universal suffrage across Europe and the decolonising world; and South Africa's first universal election (1994). Remaining exclusions debated today include prisoners (blanket bans ruled incompatible with the ECHR in the UK's case, where a stand-off persists), non-citizen residents (who vote locally in some countries), citizens abroad, and 16–17-year-olds (admitted in Austria and in devolved Scottish and Welsh elections).
