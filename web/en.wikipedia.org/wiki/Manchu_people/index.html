The Manchu people (Manchu: ᠮᠠᠨᠵᡠ, Manju; Chinese: 满族, Mǎnzú) are a Tungusic ethnic group native to Manchuria in Northeast Asia. They are the largest of the Tungusic peoples and one of the 56 officially recognised ethnic groups of China. In the seventeenth century the Manchus, organised under Nurhaci and his successors, conquered Ming China and founded the Qing dynasty, which ruled China until 1912.

History

The Manchus descend in part from the Jurchen people, who had earlier founded the Jin dynasty (1115–1234). Nurhaci unified the Jurchen tribes from 1583, created the Eight Banners military-social system, and in 1635 his son Hong Taiji renamed the people "Manchu". In 1644 Manchu forces entered Beijing, beginning Qing rule over all China. Under the Qing, Manchus formed a privileged hereditary caste, garrisoned in banner cities across the empire, and imposed the queue hairstyle on Han Chinese men.

Over the Qing period the Manchus became heavily sinicised, adopting Chinese language and customs. After the dynasty's fall in 1912, the Manchu language declined sharply; today fluent native speakers number only in the dozens, though revival efforts exist. The 2010 Chinese census recorded about 10.4 million Manchus, making them one of China's largest ethnic minorities.

Culture

Traditional Manchu culture features shamanic religious practice, horseback archery, and distinctive dress — the women's qipao evolved from Manchu banner clothing. Manchu cuisine contributed dishes such as suancai (pickled cabbage) hot pot to northeastern Chinese cooking.
