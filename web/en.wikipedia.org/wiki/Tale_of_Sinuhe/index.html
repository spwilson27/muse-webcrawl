The Tale of Sinuhe is a work of ancient Egyptian literature, composed in the early 12th Dynasty (around the 20th–19th century BC) and universally regarded as one of the masterpieces of Middle Egyptian writing. Framed as the autobiography inscribed on the tomb of its narrator—a courtier named Sinuhe—it tells of his panicked flight from Egypt after the death of King Amenemhat I, his rise to wealth and honour among the Bedouin of Syria-Palestine, and his longing, in old age, to return home and be buried in Egypt. The tale was copied in schools for over a thousand years, and hundreds of fragments survive.

Plot

Sinuhe, "Son of the Sycamore", accompanies Crown Prince Senusret on campaign in Libya when news arrives that Amenemhat I has died (the text implies assassination). Overhearing the message, Sinuhe flees in terror—Egyptologists debate whether from guilt, fear of civil war, or mere panic. He crosses into Asia, is rescued half-dead by a Bedouin chief, and eventually settles in Upper Retjenu (Syria), where the ruler Ammunenshi gives him land and his daughter in marriage. Sinuhe becomes a tribal chieftain, defeats a challenger in single combat, and grows rich, yet yearns for Egypt. A letter from King Senusret I offers him pardon and an honoured burial; he returns, is received at court, cleansed and dressed as an Egyptian again, and is granted a stone tomb in the royal necropolis, where the narrative ends.

Manuscripts and significance

The two fullest copies are Papyrus Berlin 3022 and the more fragmentary Berlin 10499, supplemented by a large ostracon in the Ashmolean Museum and many smaller excerpts—a transmission unmatched for Egyptian fiction, reflecting its status as a school classic. The work mixes poetic hymns, letters, and narrative, and its themes—exile and homecoming, Egyptian identity versus foreign lands, royal mercy, and the terror of dying abroad—have generated extensive scholarly literature. Its motifs are often compared with the biblical story of Joseph and with later exile narratives, and it has been translated many times, including poetic renderings by R. B. Parkinson.
