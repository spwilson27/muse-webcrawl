The North-West Resistance was the 1885 uprising of Métis and First Nations peoples in the Saskatchewan district of Canada's North-West Territories. It is the preferred modern name for the conflict long called the North-West Rebellion; the shift in terminology reflects a reassessment of the Métis cause as the defence of established rights rather than a revolt against lawful authority.

Causes

The Métis of the South Saskatchewan River settlements farmed long river lots in the French seigneurial style, but Canadian surveyors imposed the square township system, threatening their tenure. The near-extinction of the bison destroyed the provisioning trade, and Ottawa ignored a December 1884 petition — the Bill of Rights drafted by Louis Riel and William Henry Jackson — requesting land titles, responsible government, and relief for both Métis and Indigenous peoples. Cree bands under Poundmaker and Big Bear, starving on reserves after the collapse of the buffalo hunt, shared many of the same grievances against the treaty process.

Provisional government and fighting

On 18–19 March 1885 Riel proclaimed the Provisional Government of Saskatchewan at Batoche, with Gabriel Dumont as military commander. Clashes followed at Duck Lake, Frog Lake, Fish Creek, Cut Knife, and Batoche, where the Métis capital fell on 12 May 1885 after a four-day battle. Riel surrendered three days later and insisted on a public trial to air Métis grievances.

Legacy and naming

Riel's hanging on 16 November 1885 made him a martyr in Quebec and among the Métis; he is now widely regarded as a founder of Manitoba and a defender of Métis and minority rights. In 1992 Parliament passed a resolution recognizing his role, and Manitoba observes Louis Riel Day. Many museums and official sources, including Parks Canada at the Batoche National Historic Site, now use "Resistance" in preference to "Rebellion," though debate over the term continues.
