Reaction wheel

A reaction wheel is a type of flywheel used primarily by spacecraft for three-axis attitude control, which does not require rockets or external applicators of torque. Reaction wheels provide a high pointing accuracy and are particularly useful when the spacecraft must be rotated by very small amounts, such as keeping a telescope pointed at a distant star.

Operation

A reaction wheel consists of an electric motor driving a massive wheel. When the wheel's rotational speed is changed, the spacecraft rotates in the opposite direction by conservation of angular momentum. Reaction wheels operate only around the spacecraft's center of mass and cannot move it from one place to another. Typically three or four wheels are mounted in a tetrahedral or orthogonal arrangement to provide control about all axes and redundancy.

Limitations and desaturation

Over time, external torques — from gravity gradients, solar radiation pressure, and atmospheric drag — cause the wheels to accumulate momentum. When a wheel approaches its maximum speed ("saturation"), it must be "desaturated" by applying external torque, usually with thrusters or magnetorquers, while other wheels hold the spacecraft's attitude.

Failures

Reaction wheel failures have affected several notable missions. The Kepler space telescope's original planet-hunting mission ended in 2013 after the failure of two of its four wheels, though an improvised "K2" mission followed. The Hubble Space Telescope uses reaction wheels alongside its gyroscopes for pointing.
