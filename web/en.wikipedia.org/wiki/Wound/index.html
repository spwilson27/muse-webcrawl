Wound

Wound
FieldSurgery, emergency medicine, dermatology
TypesOpen, closed; acute, chronic
CausesTrauma, surgery, burns, pressure, disease
TreatmentCleaning, closure, dressings, debridement

A wound is a break in the integrity of the skin or other tissues.

A wound is any disruption of the normal structure and function of the skin or other body tissues, usually caused by physical trauma such as a cut, blow, burn or puncture, but also arising from surgery, pressure or underlying disease. Wounds range from trivial abrasions to life-threatening injuries involving deep structures, and their care — cleaning, closing, dressing and preventing infection — is one of the oldest and most fundamental tasks of medicine.

Classification

Wounds are broadly divided into open wounds, in which the skin is broken, and closed wounds, in which damage occurs beneath intact skin, as in contusions (bruises) and internal crush injuries. Open wounds are further classified by mechanism:

  • Incisions — clean, straight cuts from sharp instruments, including surgical wounds;
  • Lacerations — irregular, torn wounds from blunt trauma;
  • Abrasions — superficial scrapes of the epidermis (grazes);
  • Puncture wounds — deep, narrow wounds from pointed objects, prone to infection;
  • Avulsions — tissue forcibly torn away;
  • Burns and gunshot wounds, each with specialised management.

Wounds are also classed as acute, healing in an expected timeframe, or chronic, failing to heal normally — a category including diabetic foot ulcers, venous leg ulcers and pressure ulcers (bedsores).

Healing

Wound healing proceeds through overlapping phases: haemostasis, in which clotting stops bleeding; inflammation, in which white blood cells clear debris and bacteria; proliferation, in which new blood vessels, granulation tissue and collagen rebuild the defect and the surface re-epithelialises; and remodelling, in which collagen is reorganised and the scar gains strength over months. Healing may occur by primary intention (edges closed directly, as with sutures), secondary intention (the wound left open to fill in), or delayed primary closure. Factors impairing healing include infection, poor blood supply, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking and certain drugs such as corticosteroids.

Treatment

Standard care involves controlling bleeding, thorough irrigation to remove contaminants, removal of dead tissue (debridement), appropriate closure with sutures, staples, strips or tissue adhesive, and protective dressings that maintain a moist environment. Tetanus prophylaxis is considered for contaminated wounds, and antibiotics are reserved mainly for infected or high-risk wounds. Complications include infection, excessive scarring (keloids), dehiscence (reopening) and, in severe trauma, haemorrhage and shock.
