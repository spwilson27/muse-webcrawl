Tom-Tit-Tot is an English fairy tale, best known in the version collected by Joseph Jacobs in English Fairy Tales (1890). It belongs to the tale type ATU 500, "The Name of the Supernatural Helper," whose best-known member is the German tale Rumpelstiltskin. Jacobs sourced his version from Suffolk folklore; the name appears in a 17th-century rhyme recorded by antiquarians.

Plot

A poor woman bakes five pies, which her daughter greedily eats. To save face, the mother tells a passing king that her daughter can spin five skeins of flax a day. The king marries the girl on condition that she spin five skeins daily for eleven months, after which she may do as she pleases; if she fails, she will lose her head. A small black imp-like creature, dancing and chanting, appears and offers to spin the flax for her; in return, every day she may guess his name three times, and if by the end of the month she has not guessed it, she must go with him.

On the last day, the king overhears the creature singing in a chalk pit: "Nimmy nimmy not, my name's Tom-Tit-Tot." At supper the girl guesses wrong twice before triumphantly naming "Tom-Tit-Tot," whereupon the creature flies away in fury through the window and is never seen again.

Analysis

The tale shares with Rumpelstiltskin the motif of the supernatural helper defeated by the discovery of his secret name, though it differs in details: the heroine is threatened by marriage conditions rather than by greed-driven gold spinning, and there is no child at stake. Folklorists place it among the "name of the helper" tales found across Europe and beyond, reflecting the widespread folk belief in the magical power of true names.
