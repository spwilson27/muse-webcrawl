The Lord Chancellor, formally the Lord High Chancellor of Great Britain, is the keeper of the Great Seal of the Realm and one of the most senior offices of state in the United Kingdom. The office is among the oldest in the British constitution, traceable to the chancellors of the Anglo-Saxon kings. Since the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 the role has been substantially reformed; the Lord Chancellor is now usually also the Secretary of State for Justice and head of the Ministry of Justice.

History

The chancellor was originally the monarch's chief secretary and chaplain, keeping the royal seal and heading the Chancery, which developed into a court of equity. Medieval and early modern chancellors included Thomas Becket, Cardinal Wolsey, and Thomas More. Francis Bacon held the office in the 17th century. Over time the Lord Chancellor accumulated a unique triple role: head of the judiciary of England and Wales, presiding officer (Speaker) of the House of Lords, and a cabinet minister — a combination long criticised as breaching the separation of powers.

Modern reforms

The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 removed the Lord Chancellor's judicial functions, transferring them to the Lord Chief Justice, and ended the role as Speaker of the Lords, which passed to the new Lord Speaker. The Lord Chancellor no longer needs to be a peer or a senior lawyer, though the Act imposes a duty to uphold the independence of the judiciary and the rule of law. In 2007 the office was combined with the new post of Secretary of State for Justice.

Functions

The Lord Chancellor remains custodian of the Great Seal, is responsible for the efficient functioning and independence of the courts, and oversees legal aid, prisons and probation through the Ministry of Justice. The office-holder also plays a ceremonial role at the State Opening of Parliament, presenting the sovereign's speech to the monarch.
