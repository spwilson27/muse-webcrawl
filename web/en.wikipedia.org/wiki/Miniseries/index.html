Miniseries

A miniseries (or mini-series) is a television series that tells a story in a predetermined, limited number of episodes, with a definite beginning, middle, and end. Unlike an ongoing series, a miniseries is not intended to continue indefinitely; its plot is resolved within the run, which typically spans between two and twelve episodes.

History

The form grew out of the television anthology and the filmed serial. In the 1970s American networks popularised the "novel for television" format with high-profile adaptations and original productions such as Rich Man, Poor Man (1976) and Roots (1977), which drew enormous audiences. British television has a long tradition of short self-contained serials, including classic literary adaptations. In the streaming era the term "limited series" has often been used for the same format, and award bodies such as the Emmys maintain separate categories for limited or anthology series.

Characteristics

Miniseries are frequently adaptations of novels or dramatisations of historical events, since a closed narrative suits a finite run. They tend to have larger per-episode budgets and prominent casts relative to regular series programming. If a miniseries proves unusually successful, producers occasionally follow it with a sequel miniseries or convert the concept into an ongoing series, though the original production remains self-contained.
