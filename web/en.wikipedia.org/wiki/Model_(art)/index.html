Model (art)

OccupationArtist's model
SettingsArt schools, studios, life drawing classes
ActivityPosing clothed or nude for artists

An art model is a person who poses for an artist as part of the creative process, providing a reference for the human body in a work of art. Art models are often paid professionals employed by art schools, universities and individual artists for life drawing, painting and sculpture classes, and for private studio work. The most common types of art model are nude models, who pose unclothed, and figure or portrait models, who may pose clothed or costumed.

History

The use of live models dates back to classical antiquity; the Greek sculptor Praxiteles is said to have used the courtesan Phryne as the model for his Aphrodite of Knidos. In the Renaissance, workshops relied on studio assistants and local people as models, and artists such as Leonardo da Vinci and Michelangelo studied anatomy from live subjects and cadavers. The academic system of the 17th to 19th centuries formalised life study: the nude model — the académie — became the centrepiece of training at institutions such as the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and the Royal Academy in London. Nineteenth-century Paris saw famous professional models, and women models increasingly common despite social stigma.

Life modelling today

Models work in poses ranging from a few seconds — for gesture drawing — to hours, days or weeks for a single sustained pose in painting and sculpture. Sustained poses are broken at regular intervals, typically every 20 to 45 minutes, to allow the model to rest. Poses may be standing, seated, reclining or in motion. Professional models are expected to hold still, return accurately to a pose after breaks, and invent expressive gestures when required. Rotating platforms, props, drapery and lighting are used to set the pose.

Conventions and etiquette

Life rooms operate under conventions designed to protect the model's dignity and privacy: models disrobe in private or wear a robe until the session begins, students do not touch the model or comment on their body, and photography is generally forbidden without explicit consent. Models are usually addressed by name only in professional terms, and art institutions maintain codes of conduct for both models and artists. Models' organisations and guilds, such as the Register of Artists' Models in the UK, promote fair pay and working conditions.

In art history

Some models became famous in their own right or as artists: Victorine Meurent modelled for Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe and Olympia and was herself a painter; Joanna Hiffernan modelled for Whistler and Courbet; Margherita Luti, "La Fornarina", was Raphael's model and lover according to tradition. The model–artist relationship has been a recurring subject of literature and film.
