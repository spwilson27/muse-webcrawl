Protocol (diplomacy)

In international politics, protocol (from the Greek protokollon, the first sheet glued to a papyrus roll) is the set of rules, etiquette and ceremonial conventions governing diplomacy and state affairs: how ambassadors are accredited and ranked, how treaties and credentials are exchanged, how heads of state and government are received and seated, the order of flags, anthems and speeches, and the forms of official correspondence. Protocol enables representatives of sovereign — and therefore formally equal — states to interact without disputes over status, and it signals respect, legitimacy and intentions.

History

Many rules derive from the courts of Renaissance Italy and the France of Louis XIV, where elaborate ceremonial (studied by writers such as Julius Bernhard von Rohr in 1728–29) fixed the behaviour of diplomats. Deadly rivalries over precedence — in 1661, a fight in London between the retinues of the French and Spanish ambassadors over carriage order left several dead — led European states to codify rules. The Congress of Vienna (1815) established the classification of diplomatic agents and the principle that ambassadors rank by date of accreditation, refined at Aix-la-Chapelle (1818) and finally codified globally in the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961).

Precedence and practice

The order of precedence determines seating, speaking order and ceremonial honour; in most diplomatic corps the longest-serving ambassador is the doyen (dean). Titles are regulated ("Excellency" for ambassadors and heads of state), letters of credence are presented to the head of state, and state visits, official visits and working visits form a descending hierarchy of ceremonial intensity. A breach of protocol — a wrongly displayed flag, a mis-seated minister — can cause genuine diplomatic offence, so foreign ministries maintain dedicated protocol departments, such as the Office of the Chief of Protocol in the United States and the Marshal of the Diplomatic Corps in London.
