Evil eye

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The evil eye is a supernatural belief in a curse brought about by a malevolent glare, usually inspired by envy. The belief is found in many cultures across the Mediterranean, the Middle East, South Asia, parts of Africa and Latin America, and is attested in ancient Greek and Roman texts as well as in the Bible and the Quran. The evil eye is thought to cause misfortune, illness or injury — especially to children, livestock and anything much admired or praised — and it may be cast deliberately or involuntarily, sometimes even by a well-meaning admirer.

Names and forms

The concept is known by many names: baskania in Greek tradition, malocchio in Italian, mal de ojo in Spanish, ayin ha-ra in Hebrew, nazar in Turkish and Arabic contexts, and buri nazar in South Asia. In classical antiquity, authors such as Plutarch attempted naturalistic explanations, suggesting that the eyes emit rays capable of carrying harm.

Protection

Defences against the evil eye are among the most widespread of folk practices. The best known is the nazar, a concentric blue-and-white glass amulet shaped like an eye, hung in homes, vehicles and shops and worn as jewellery throughout Turkey, Greece and the wider region. Other protections include the hamsa or Hand of Fatima, red ribbons and threads, horns and coral charms in Italy, spitting or making spitting sounds after praise, ritual washing, fumigation with incense or rue, and recited prayers and blessings. Compliments to children are traditionally accompanied by a formula such as mashallah to deflect envious attention.

Cultural significance

Anthropologists have interpreted the belief as a way of regulating envy and social inequality in close-knit communities, and of explaining sudden misfortune. The evil eye remains a living tradition: nazar imagery is ubiquitous in modern design, and the evil-eye emoji 🧿 was added to Unicode in 2018.
