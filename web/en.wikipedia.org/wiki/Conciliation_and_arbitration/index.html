Conciliation and arbitration

Conciliation and arbitration is a system of industrial relations in which disputes between employers and employees are settled by an independent tribunal rather than by strikes or lockouts. It is most famously associated with Australia and New Zealand, where compulsory arbitration systems were pioneered in the 1890s and 1900s.

Principles

Under the model, a court or commission first attempts conciliation: helping the parties reach agreement themselves. If that fails, it proceeds to arbitration, hearing evidence and imposing a binding determination — in Australia historically an industrial award setting wages and conditions for an industry or occupation.

Australian system

Australia's federal system was founded on the Commonwealth's constitutional power to make laws for conciliation and arbitration for the prevention and settlement of interstate industrial disputes. The Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904 created the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration. In the Harvester case of 1907, Justice H. B. Higgins established the concept of a "fair and reasonable" basic wage. Successor bodies include the Australian Industrial Relations Commission and today's Fair Work Commission.

New Zealand system

New Zealand's Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1894, championed by William Pember Reeves, was the first such national scheme, giving registered unions legal status in exchange for accepting arbitration in place of strikes.
