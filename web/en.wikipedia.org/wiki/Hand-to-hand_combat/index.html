Hand-to-hand combat

FocusStriking, grappling, weapons
HardnessFull contact
ParenthoodMartial arts, close-quarters combat

Hand-to-hand combat (sometimes abbreviated as HTH or H2H) is a physical confrontation between two or more persons at very short range, involving little or no use of ranged weapons. It encompasses fighting with the empty hand as well as with melee weapons such as knives, bayonets, clubs, and improvised objects, and is a fundamental component of military training and martial arts worldwide.

Techniques

Hand-to-hand fighting draws on the full range of unarmed technique: punches, kicks, knee and elbow strikes; throws, takedowns, joint locks and chokes from grappling; and, in military contexts, the use of the knife, bayonet, entrenching tool, or rifle butt. Combatants are typically taught to strike vulnerable points, to control an opponent's weapon, and to move from unarmed defence to weapon use as circumstances allow.

Military application

Modern armies teach hand-to-hand combat less as a primary battlefield tool than as a means of building aggression, confidence, and physical conditioning, and as a last resort when weapons are lost or engagements occur at arm's length — in trenches, buildings, or during sentry and boarding operations. The Second World War saw influential systems developed by instructors such as William E. Fairbairn and Eric A. Sykes for British commandos and the Shanghai Municipal Police, combining boxing, judo, and street fighting into short, brutally practical syllabuses.

Modern systems

Prominent modern military systems include the United States Marine Corps Martial Arts Program (MCMAP), the US Army's Modern Army Combatives Program based largely on Brazilian jiu-jitsu, the Israeli Krav Maga, and the Russian Systema and Sambo traditions. Civilian martial arts — boxing, wrestling, judo, karate, muay Thai, and mixed martial arts — all train aspects of hand-to-hand fighting, though usually within sporting or self-defence rules that exclude the lethal techniques taught to soldiers.

History

Unarmed combat is as old as organised warfare: Egyptian tomb paintings depict wrestling and stick fighting, Greek pankration combined boxing and wrestling in the Olympic Games, and codified empty-hand systems developed across Asia in traditions such as Chinese kung fu, Japanese jujutsu, and Korean and Okinawan arts. The spread of gunpowder reduced but never eliminated the role of close combat, and each world war prompted renewed systematic teaching.
