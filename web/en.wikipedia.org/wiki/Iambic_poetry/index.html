Iambic poetry

Iambic poetry is a form of poetry written in iambic metre, whose origins lie in ancient Greece. The term originally referred not only to the metre but to a genre of verse associated with invective, satire, blame, and ribald or obscene subject matter. The word "iambic" derives from the iamb, a metrical foot consisting of a short syllable followed by a long one in Greek and Latin verse, or an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one in English.

Greek iambus

In archaic Greece, the iambus was a genre performed at festivals, particularly those of Demeter and Dionysus, where ritual mockery and obscenity had a traditional place. Its earliest and most famous practitioner was Archilochus of Paros (7th century BC), whose bitter invectives against his former friend Lycambes and his daughters were legendary — ancient tradition held that the targets hanged themselves for shame. Other iambic poets included Semonides, known for a satirical catalogue of types of women, and Hipponax, associated with the "limping iamb" or choliamb, a trimeter with a dragging final foot.

Metres

The characteristic metres of Greek iambic poetry are the iambic trimeter and the trochaic tetrameter. The iambic trimeter was later adopted as the principal metre of dialogue in Greek tragedy and comedy, shedding its association with blame while the term "iambic" retained its generic sense in lyric contexts.

Later use

In English poetry, "iambic" refers purely to the metre, most famously iambic pentameter, the standard line of Shakespeare, Milton, and much of the English tradition. The classical genre of blame poetry itself faded, though its spirit survives in satirical and invective verse.
