Science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A scientist in a laboratory holding a flask of blue liquid up to the light]
Science is a systematic enterprise of inquiry.

Science is a systematic discipline that builds and organises knowledge in the form of testable hypotheses and predictions about the universe. Modern science is typically divided into natural sciences (such as physics, chemistry and biology), social sciences (such as economics, psychology and sociology) and formal sciences (such as mathematics, logic and theoretical computer science). It relies on empirical evidence, controlled methods and peer review.

The scientific method

Scientists observe phenomena, propose hypotheses, deduce predictions and test them by experiment or further observation — the cycle described in the article on the {W('Scientific_method')}. Karl Popper influentially argued that scientific claims must be falsifiable; Thomas Kuhn emphasised 'paradigm shifts' in how fields reorganise.

History

Science grew from the natural philosophy of ancient Greece, Babylonian astronomy, Indian and Chinese traditions, and medieval Islamic scholarship, through the Scientific Revolution of Copernicus, Galileo and Newton, to the professionalised, laboratory- and university-based enterprise of today. See {W('History_of_science')}.

Institutions and society

Modern research is funded by governments, universities and industry, communicated through journals and conferences, and applied in medicine, agriculture, engineering and technology. Public debates concern reproducibility, funding, open access and science's social responsibilities.
