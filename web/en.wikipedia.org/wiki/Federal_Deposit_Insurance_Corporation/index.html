The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) is an independent agency of the United States federal government that insures deposits in American banks and thrift institutions, supervises financial institutions for safety and soundness, and manages receiverships of failed banks. It was created by the Banking Act of 1933 (Glass–Steagall Act) in response to the thousands of bank failures during the Great Depression, and began insuring deposits on January 1, 1934.

Deposit insurance

The FDIC insures deposits — checking, savings, money market accounts, and certificates of deposit — up to the standard maximum of $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, for each account ownership category. It is funded by premiums assessed on member banks, not by congressional appropriations, and maintains the Deposit Insurance Fund. Since the FDIC's founding, no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds as a result of a bank failure.

Supervision and resolution

The FDIC is the primary federal regulator of insured state-chartered banks that are not members of the Federal Reserve System, and examines institutions for compliance with consumer-protection and safety-and-soundness rules. When a bank fails, the FDIC is appointed receiver, typically arranging the sale of the bank's deposits and assets to a healthy institution or paying out insured depositors — as in the large regional-bank failures of 2023, including Silicon Valley Bank and First Republic Bank.
