The saltwater crocodile (Crocodylus porosus) is a crocodilian native to saltwater habitats, brackish wetlands, and freshwater rivers of the Indo-Pacific region, from India's east coast and Sri Lanka across Southeast Asia to northern Australia and the western Pacific islands. It is the largest living reptile: males commonly reach 5 metres (16 ft) and exceptional individuals exceed 6 metres (20 ft). A hypercarnivorous apex predator, it is also the crocodilian most dangerous to humans.

Description

Saltwater crocodiles have a broad snout compared with most crocodiles, irregular and relatively few enlarged scales (osteoderms) on the neck, and a heavy, muscular build. Adults are dark olive to grey-brown with darker banding, juveniles paler with black spots and stripes. Lingual salt glands on the tongue excrete excess salt, an adaptation that genuinely fits the species for life at sea: individuals are regularly seen far offshore and have colonised islands across the western Pacific, explaining the species' vast range. The bite of a large individual is the strongest measured in any living animal, and the species performs the "death roll" to subdue and dismember large prey.

Behaviour and diet

Highly territorial, large males dominate stretches of river and tolerate females and smaller males on sufferance. Saltwater crocodiles are ambush predators that take almost any animal they can overpower — fish, crustaceans, birds, buffalo, wild pigs, monkeys, and occasionally sharks — typically seizing prey at the water's edge and dragging it under to drown. They can slow their metabolism to survive long periods without food. Females build mound nests of vegetation and mud in the wet season, lay 40–60 eggs, and guard the nest; temperature of incubation determines the sex of the hatchlings.

Conservation and human relations

Unregulated hunting for skins had reduced the species drastically by the 1960s. Protection — in Australia from 1971 in the Northern Territory — produced one of conservation's celebrated recoveries: the Northern Territory population rebounded to roughly 100,000 non-hatchling animals, and the species is now listed as Least Concern. Managed ranching and egg harvests support a valuable skin industry. The recovery has renewed conflict: saltwater crocodiles kill and injure people every year across their range, and attacks, such as those in Australia's north and in Borneo and the Philippines, drive continuing debate over culling and removal of problem animals. A captive Philippine specimen named Lolong, 6.17 m (20 ft 3 in) long, was certified in 2012 as the largest crocodile ever measured in captivity.
