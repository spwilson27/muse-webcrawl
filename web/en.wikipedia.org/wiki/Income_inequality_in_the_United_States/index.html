Income inequality in the United States

Income inequality in the United States has grown substantially since the 1970s. The United States has the highest level of income inequality among the G7 nations, whether measured by the Gini coefficient, by the share of income going to the top 1 percent, or by the ratio of executive to worker pay. The trend, its causes, and its consequences are major subjects of economic research and political debate.

The top 1 percent's share of US income roughly doubled between 1980 and the 2010s.

Trends

Income was distributed relatively equally during the post-war decades sometimes called the "Great Compression". From about 1979 onward, incomes at the top grew much faster than those in the middle and at the bottom. Work by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez using tax records showed the top 1 percent receiving roughly a tenth of national income in 1980 and around a fifth in recent years, levels last seen in the 1920s. The Congressional Budget Office finds that even after taxes and transfers, which reduce inequality, the pattern of rising concentration holds.

Causes

Proposed causes include skill-biased technological change and globalisation, which raised returns to education and exposed less-skilled workers to competition; the decline of trade unions and of the real minimum wage; changes in tax policy; the growth of the financial sector and of "superstar" and executive pay; and winner-take-all markets in entertainment, technology, and business. Economists differ on the relative weight of these factors.

Consequences and debate

Researchers link high inequality to slower mobility between generations, worse health and educational outcomes, and political polarisation, though the strength of each claim is contested. Defenders of higher inequality argue that it reflects rewards to innovation and risk-taking, and point to absolute gains in living standards. Policy debates centre on progressive taxation, minimum wages, education, antitrust, and transfers such as the earned income tax credit.
