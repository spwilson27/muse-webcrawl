Hull-down

Hull-down is a term used in armoured warfare for the position of a tank or other armoured fighting vehicle when its hull (the main body) is hidden behind cover — such as a hill crest, ridge line or prepared earthwork — so that only its turret, and therefore its gun and sights, is exposed to the enemy.

Advantages

A hull-down vehicle presents a much smaller target, and the cover in front protects the more vulnerable hull and running gear while the sloped ground may deflect incoming fire. From such a position the vehicle can observe and engage targets while exposing mainly its best-armoured component, the turret face. The related term turret-down describes a position entirely behind cover, where only observation devices or the commander's head are exposed while the vehicle waits or reconnoitres.

Tactical use

Crews are trained to select hull-down firing positions on reverse slopes and crests, to move between turret-down and hull-down as targets appear, and to withdraw to alternate positions after firing. Vehicle design supports the tactic through gun depression angles — the ability to point the main gun downward — which is why many tank designs emphasise a depression of around −10 degrees. The concept dates to the First World War use of tanks on reverse slopes and remains a standard element of armoured tactics.
