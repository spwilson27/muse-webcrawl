ʿUrabi revolt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ʿUrabi revolt, also known as the ʿUrabi revolution, was an Egyptian nationalist uprising of 1879–1882 against the rule of Khedive Tawfiq and European — especially British and French — dominance of Egypt. It was led by Colonel Ahmed ʿUrabi, an Egyptian officer of peasant origin.

Background

Egypt's enormous debts under Khedive Isma'il had brought the country under the Dual Control of Britain and France, whose officials dominated its finances. Egyptian army officers resented the privileges of Turco-Circassian officers; peasants resented taxation and foreign control. In 1879 officers including ʿUrabi protested dismissals and arrears of pay, beginning a movement that combined military grievance with proto-nationalist demands for constitutional government under the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians".

Course of events

In September 1881 ʿUrabi led troops to Abdeen Palace and demanded the dismissal of the government, a constitution, and an expanded army; Tawfiq yielded, and ʿUrabi became Minister of War. Tension with the European powers escalated, and anti-foreign riots in Alexandria in June 1882 killed dozens of Europeans. The British fleet bombarded Alexandria on 11 July 1882, and an expeditionary force under Sir Garnet Wolseley landed, defeating ʿUrabi's army decisively at the Battle of Tel el-Kebir on 13 September 1882.

Aftermath

ʿUrabi was tried for rebellion, sentenced to death, and exiled to Ceylon; he returned to Egypt in 1901 and died in 1911. The revolt's suppression began the British occupation of Egypt, which lasted in various forms until 1956. Later Egyptian nationalists rehabilitated ʿUrabi as a founding hero of the national movement.
