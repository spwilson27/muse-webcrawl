Dramatic monologue
Form: Poem in the voice of a single character
Key practitioners: Robert Browning, Alfred Tennyson, T. S. Eliot
Examples: 'My Last Duchess', 'The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock'

Dramatic monologue is a type of poetry written in the form of a speech of an individual character. The speaker addresses a silent listener whose presence shapes the speech, and in revealing themselves—often unintentionally—the speaker discloses character, motive, and situation.

Characteristics

Key features are a single persona distinct from the poet, a specific dramatic occasion, an implied auditor, and revelation of character through speech, often with irony between what the speaker intends and what the reader perceives.

History

The form was perfected by Robert Browning in poems such as 'My Last Duchess' and 'Fra Lippo Lippi', and by Tennyson in 'Ulysses'. Modernists including T. S. Eliot adapted it, and it influenced the soliloquy-like lyrics of later poetry and drama. It differs from a theatre soliloquy in addressing a listener within the poem.
