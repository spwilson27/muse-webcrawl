Arms Control and Disarmament Agency

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States Arms Control and Disarmament Agency (ACDA) was an independent agency of the US government (1961–1999) charged with formulating and implementing arms-control policy and advising the president and secretary of state. Created by the Arms Control and Disarmament Act of 26 September 1961 under President Kennedy — with William C. Foster as its first director — it institutionalised arms control as a permanent function of government at the height of the Cold War, staffing and analysing the negotiations that produced the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963), the NPT (1968), SALT and the ABM Treaty (1972).

ACDA's independent status waxed and waned with the politics of its mission: it was the bureaucratic home of arms-control advocates against sceptics in the Pentagon and Congress, directed in the 1970s–80s by figures from both camps (Fred Iklé; Eugene Rostow; Kenneth Adelman). In 1997–99, under a congressional-presidential bargain, the agency was abolished and its functions folded into the Department of State (the Under Secretary for Arms Control and International Security), ending the experiment of a statutory, semi-independent disarmament voice inside the US government — an institutional history often revisited when arms-control architecture is debated after treaty collapses.
