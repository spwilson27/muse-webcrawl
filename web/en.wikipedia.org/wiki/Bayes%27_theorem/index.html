Bayes' theorem (also Bayes' law or Bayes' rule) describes the probability of an event based on prior knowledge of conditions related to the event. It is named after the Reverend Thomas Bayes, whose work was published posthumously in 1763, and was later developed independently by Pierre-Simon Laplace.

Statement

For events A and B with P(B) ≠ 0, the theorem states: P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B), where P(A) is the prior probability of A, P(B|A) is the likelihood of B given A, P(B) is the marginal probability of the evidence, and P(A|B) is the posterior probability of A given B.

Applications

Bayes' theorem is the foundation of Bayesian inference, in which probabilities are updated as new evidence arrives. Applications include medical diagnosis (interpreting test results in light of disease prevalence), spam filtering, machine learning classifiers such as naive Bayes, forensic science, and search and rescue planning. Neglecting the prior — treating P(B|A) as if it were P(A|B) — is a common reasoning error known as the base rate fallacy or prosecutor's fallacy.
