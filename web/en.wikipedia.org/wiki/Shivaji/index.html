Shivaji Bhonsle I (c. 1630 – April 3, 1680), better known as Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, was an Indian warrior-king and the founder of the Maratha Empire. From a small patrimony in the western Deccan, Shivaji carved out an independent kingdom in defiance of the Adil Shahi sultanate of Bijapur and the Mughal Empire, pioneering guerrilla warfare in the hill country of the Western Ghats and establishing an administration noted for religious tolerance, disciplined military organisation and the protection of civilians. He is revered in Maharashtra and across India as a hero of resistance and self-rule (swarajya).

Early life

Shivaji was born at the hill fort of Shivneri, near Junnar, to Shahaji Bhonsle, a Maratha general serving Deccan sultanates, and Jijabai, whose devout religious upbringing tradition credits with shaping his character. Raised at Pune under the guardianship of Dadoji Kondadeo, Shivaji gathered a band of local Maval youths and, from the mid-1640s, began seizing hill forts from Bijapur, beginning with Torna.

Expansion and conflicts

In 1659 Shivaji killed the Bijapur general Afzal Khan at a parley beneath Pratapgad Fort, a famous episode in which he is said to have disembowelled the general with concealed tiger-claw weapons (wagh nakh). In 1663 he wounded the Mughal governor Shaista Khan in a daring night raid into Pune. Captured forts and plundered Mughal Surat (1664) funded his growing state, but the Mughal general Jai Singh compelled him to accept the Treaty of Purandar (1665) and visit Aurangzeb's court at Agra, from which Shivaji escaped in 1666 — legend says concealed in a basket of sweets. He recovered his forts by 1670 and sacked Surat a second time.

Coronation and administration

On June 6, 1674, Shivaji was crowned Chhatrapati ("paramount sovereign") at Raigad in an elaborate Vedic ceremony, asserting his status as an independent Hindu king. His council of eight ministers, the Ashta Pradhan, organised revenue, military and judicial affairs. Shivaji built a navy on the Konkan coast — earning him the description "father of the Indian Navy" — maintained a network of sea and hill forts, paid troops in cash, forbade plunder of civilians, and treated Muslim subjects and holy places with notable respect, employing Muslims in senior posts.

Death and legacy

Shivaji died at Raigad on April 3, 1680, probably of fever, and was succeeded by his son Sambhaji. Under his successors the Maratha polity grew into the dominant power in eighteenth-century India. Shivaji's memory was revived powerfully in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by reformers and nationalists such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who launched the public Shivaji festival in the 1890s; statues, the Mumbai international airport and Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus railway station bear his name.
