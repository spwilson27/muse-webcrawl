Intermittent explosive disorder

Intermittent explosive disorder (IED) is a mental disorder characterized by explosive outbursts of anger and/or violence, often to the point of rage, that are disproportionate to the situation at hand — for example, impulsive screaming or aggression triggered by relatively inconsequential events. It is classified in the DSM-5 among the disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders.

Diagnosis

DSM-5 criteria require recurrent behavioral outbursts representing a failure to control aggressive impulses: either verbal aggression or non-damaging physical aggression occurring on average twice weekly for three months, or more severe outbursts involving destruction of property or assault causing injury. The outbursts are not premeditated and cause marked distress or impairment.

Epidemiology and causes

Lifetime prevalence estimates in the United States range from about 2.7% to over 7%, with onset typically in adolescence. The disorder is associated with serotonergic dysfunction, abnormalities in the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, genetic factors, and early exposure to violence or trauma.

Treatment

Treatment combines psychotherapy, especially cognitive behavioral therapy and anger-management techniques, with pharmacotherapy. SSRIs, mood stabilizers, and anticonvulsants have shown benefit in reducing aggression, though no medication is specifically approved for IED.
