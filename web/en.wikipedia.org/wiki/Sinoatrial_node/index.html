The sinoatrial node (SA node, also called the sinus node) is a group of specialised cells located in the wall of the right atrium of the heart, near the entrance of the superior vena cava. It is the heart's natural pacemaker: its cells spontaneously generate electrical impulses that spread through the atria and, via the atrioventricular node, to the ventricles, setting the normal sinus rhythm.

Structure and function

The node is a crescent-shaped structure of small pacemaker cells embedded in connective tissue, described by Arthur Keith and Martin Flack in 1906. Its cells lack a stable resting potential and instead drift upward during diastole (the "pacemaker potential", driven partly by the funny current If through HCN channels) until threshold triggers an action potential. In an adult at rest the node typically fires 60–100 times per minute, with the rate modulated by the autonomic nervous system: sympathetic input accelerates it, vagal (parasympathetic) input slows it.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the SA node causes sick sinus syndrome, with bradycardia, pauses, and sometimes alternating fast and slow rhythms; it is a common indication for an artificial pacemaker. The nodal artery arises from the right coronary artery in about 60% of people and from the left circumflex in most of the rest, so nodal ischaemia can accompany myocardial infarction.
