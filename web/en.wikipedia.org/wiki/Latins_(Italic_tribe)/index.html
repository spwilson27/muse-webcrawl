The Latins (Latin: Latini) were an ancient Italic people who inhabited Latium Vetus ("Old Latium") in west-central Italy from about the 1st millennium BC onward. They spoke Latin, shared a common religious centre in the cult of Jupiter Latiaris on Monte Cavo, and organised themselves politically through the Latin League. One of their cities, Rome, eventually absorbed all the others and spread the Latin language across the Mediterranean world.

Origins

Archaeology associates the early Latins with the Latial culture of the early Iron Age, characterised by hut-urn cremation burials in Latium from roughly the tenth century BC. Linguistically, Latin belongs with Faliscan in the Latino-Faliscan branch of the Italic languages, related more distantly to Oscan and Umbrian. Roman tradition traced Latin origins to Aeneas and the foundation of Lavinium and Alba Longa, stories modern historians treat as legend layered over real population movements into the Tiber plain.

Society and politics

Latin communities were small city-states and villages whose citizens shared rights of intermarriage and commerce with one another. Their federal sanctuary and the Latin Festival gave the ethnos a durable common identity even as individual cities competed — and as Rome, advantageously placed at a Tiber crossing, grew at their expense. After the Latin War, Rome dissolved the League in 338 BC, but "Latin status" survived for centuries as a legal category exported to colonies across Italy and later the provinces.

Legacy

Through Rome, the Latins' language became the ancestor of the Romance languages and a lasting medium of law, science, and the Church in Europe. The word "Latin" has since been extended — often confusingly — to the Romance-speaking peoples of Europe and Latin America, senses quite distinct from the ancient tribe.
