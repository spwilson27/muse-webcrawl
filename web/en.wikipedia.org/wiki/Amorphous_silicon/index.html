Amorphous silicon (a-Si) is the non-crystalline form of silicon used for solar cells and thin-film transistors in liquid-crystal displays. Unlike crystalline silicon, its atoms form a continuous random network without long-range order. Because the structure contains many dangling bonds that act as defects, practical material is almost always hydrogenated (a-Si:H), with hydrogen passivating those bonds and dramatically improving electronic quality.

Properties

Hydrogenated amorphous silicon has a band gap of about 1.7 eV, absorbs visible light much more strongly than crystalline silicon, so films only a few hundred nanometres thick suffice for solar cells, and it can be deposited at low temperatures (about 200 °C) over large areas on glass, metal, or flexible plastic by plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition. A drawback is the Staebler–Wronski effect: light-induced degradation that reduces solar-cell efficiency during the first months of exposure before stabilizing.

Applications

Amorphous silicon was first developed for electronics in the 1970s, with the first solar cells reported in 1976 by David Carlson and Christopher Wronski at RCA. It is used in thin-film photovoltaic modules, pocket calculators, amorphous-silicon thin-film transistors that drive LCD pixels, image sensors, and photoreceptors in photocopiers, though crystalline silicon has displaced it from most large-scale solar installations on efficiency grounds.
