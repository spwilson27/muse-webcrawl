The South Downs are a range of chalk hills in the south-eastern coastal counties of England, extending for about 110 km (68 mi) from the Itchen Valley near Winchester in Hampshire eastward to Beachy Head near Eastbourne in East Sussex. Together with the North Downs across the Weald, they are the eroded remnants of a great dome of chalk laid down in a shallow sea during the Late Cretaceous. Much of the range is now protected as the South Downs National Park, designated in 2010, the most populous national park in England.

Geology and landscape

The downs form a steep north-facing escarpment (scarp slope) and a gentler southward dip slope running down to the coastal plain and the English Channel, where the chalk meets the sea in dramatic cliffs, including the Seven Sisters and Beachy Head (162 m), the highest chalk sea cliff in Britain. Dry valleys, coombes, and dew ponds dot the surface, since porous chalk absorbs rainfall and surface streams are rare except for the winterbournes and the rivers — the Itchen, Meon, Arun, Adur, Ouse, and Cuckmere — that cut gaps through the ridge. The highest point is Butser Hill in Hampshire at 271 m; the best-known summit is Devil's Dyke near Brighton.

Ecology and history

Chalk grassland, maintained for centuries by sheep grazing, is one of Britain's richest habitats for wildflowers and butterflies, noted for orchids and species such as the Adonis blue and chalkhill blue butterflies. The downs are crossed by ancient trackways, including the Ridgeway-like South Downs Way, a 160 km National Trail from Winchester to Eastbourne popular with walkers, cyclists, and riders. Prehistoric remains include Neolithic long barrows and causewayed enclosures such as Windmill Hill's southern counterparts, Iron Age hill forts like Cissbury Ring and Chanctonbury Ring, and the Long Man of Wilmington hill figure.
