Palermo Stone

Palermo Stone
MaterialBlack basalt (diorite)
Created5th Dynasty, c. 2400 BC
DiscoveredAcquired 1877; donated to Palermo museum
LocationRegional Archaeological Museum, Palermo, Sicily

The Palermo Stone is one of seven surviving fragments of a stele known as the Royal Annals of the Old Kingdom of Ancient Egypt. The stele contained a list of the kings of Egypt from the first dynasty (c. 3150–2890 BC) through to the middle of the fifth dynasty (c. 2392–2283 BC), and recorded notable events during each king's reign. It was probably made during the fifth dynasty. The Palermo Stone is held in the Regional Archaeological Museum Antonio Salinas in the city of Palermo, Italy, from which it derives its name.

Description

The fragment is a slab of black basalt roughly 43.5 cm high and 25 cm wide, inscribed on both sides with registers divided into compartments, each representing one regnal year. Entries record events such as censuses of cattle and gold, festivals, donations to temples, expeditions, and—crucially—the annual height of the Nile flood in cubits, palms and fingers, an invaluable record for reconstructing early Egyptian chronology and climate.

The other fragments

Six smaller pieces, known as the Cairo fragments, are held in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo; one is in the Petrie Museum, London. Reconstruction of the original annals slab—probably over two metres wide—suggests it stood in a temple and recorded history from mythical predynastic kings onward.

Significance

Together with the Turin King List and Manetho's history, the Royal Annals are a primary source for the sequence and reign lengths of Egypt's earliest pharaohs. Debates continue over the reading of damaged entries and the lengths of reigns, especially for the First and Second Dynasties.
