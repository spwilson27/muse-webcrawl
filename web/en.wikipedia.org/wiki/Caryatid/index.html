A caryatid (Greek: Καρυᾶτις, plural Καρυάτιδες) is a sculpted female figure serving as an architectural support, taking the place of a column or pillar and supporting an entablature on her head. The Greek term karyatides literally means "maidens of Karyai", an ancient town in Laconia (in the Peloponnese), where women danced in honour of the goddess Artemis Karyatis.

History

The earliest known examples date from the 6th century BC, including the Siphnian Treasury at Delphi. The most celebrated caryatids are the six draped female figures of the south porch of the Erechtheion on the Acropolis of Athens, built between 421 and 406 BC during the Classical period of ancient Greek architecture.

The Roman architect Vitruvius offered a moralising explanation: the women of Caryae were enslaved after their city sided with the Persians, and architects depicted them as burden-bearers to commemorate their punishment. Modern scholars generally doubt this account and connect the figures instead with ritual dancers for Artemis.

Later use

Caryatids were revived in Renaissance, Baroque and Neoclassical architecture across Europe, appearing on buildings in Paris, Vienna and elsewhere. In the nineteenth century they were widely used on furniture, decorative arts and academic painting. One of the original Erechtheion maidens was removed by Lord Elgin in the early 19th century and is now in the British Museum; the remaining figures were moved to the Acropolis Museum in 1979 to protect them from air pollution, with replicas in their place.
