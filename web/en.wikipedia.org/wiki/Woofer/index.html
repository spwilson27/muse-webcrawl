A woofer or bass speaker is a technical term for a loudspeaker driver designed to produce low frequency sounds, typically from 20 Hz up to a few hundred Hz. The name comes from the onomatopoeic English word for a dog's bark, "woof" (in contrast to the name used for loudspeakers designed to reproduce high-frequency sounds, tweeter, from the chirp of a bird, "tweet").

The most common design for a woofer is the electrodynamic driver, which uses a cone connected to a rigid basket via a flexible suspension (surround and spider) and a coil used to drive the cone in a magnetic field. In most modern systems, crossovers filter the audio signal so that a flexible signal is sent to the woofer.

Design and operation

Woofers are made larger than tweeters and mid-range drivers because reproducing a given sound pressure at low frequencies requires moving a large volume of air, which is achieved by a large cone area, long excursion, or both. Cone materials include treated paper, polypropylene, aluminium, magnesium, and composites such as Kevlar. A surround of rubber or foam and a centring spider hold the voice coil aligned in the magnet gap while allowing linear piston-like motion.

Key parameters describing woofer behaviour, codified by A. Neville Thiele and Richard H. Small, include the free-air resonance frequency (Fs), total Q (Qts), and equivalent air volume (Vas). These Thiele/Small parameters predict the low-frequency response of the driver in sealed, ported (bass reflex), or other enclosures.

Active and passive woofers

In a passive loudspeaker system, the woofer is driven by an external amplifier through a passive crossover network. Active (powered) designs place the amplifier and electronic crossover in the enclosure, common in studio monitors and most subwoofers. A subwoofer is a woofer dedicated to the very lowest frequencies, generally below 100–200 Hz.

Applications

Woofers are used in home hi-fi and home-theatre speakers, car audio, public-address systems, musical instrument amplifiers (especially bass guitar), and cinema sound systems. Large-format woofers of 15 or 18 inches are typical of sound-reinforcement systems for concerts.
