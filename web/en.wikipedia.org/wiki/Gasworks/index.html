Gasworks

A gasworks (or gas house) is an industrial plant for the manufacture of gaseous fuel from coal. In a gasworks, coal was heated in airtight retorts — a process called carbonization — to produce coal gas (also called town gas), with coke, coal tar, and ammonia as valuable by-products. Town gas was used for street lighting, domestic lighting, cooking, and heating throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Process

Coal was distilled in horizontal or vertical retorts at over 1,000 °C. The crude gas was cooled, scrubbed of tar and ammonia, and purified of hydrogen sulfide before being stored in large telescopic water-sealed holders — the gasholders that became landmarks of industrial cities — and distributed through cast-iron mains. Residual coke was sold as smokeless fuel, and tar and ammonia fed the chemical industry, yielding dyes, fertilizers, and explosives.

History

William Murdoch demonstrated coal-gas lighting in the 1790s, and the Gas Light and Coke Company, chartered in London in 1812, built the first public gasworks. Gaslight spread rapidly through European and American cities. With the arrival of natural gas in the mid-20th century, manufactured-gas plants were progressively closed; Britain converted to North Sea gas between 1967 and 1977. Former gasworks sites are often contaminated with tar and require remediation, though some gasholder frames have been preserved and incorporated into housing and parks, as at King's Cross in London and the Gasometer complex in Vienna.
