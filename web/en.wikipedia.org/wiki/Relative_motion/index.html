Relative motion

In physics, relative motion is the calculation of the motion of an object with regard to some other moving or stationary object. In classical (Newtonian) mechanics, the velocity of an object measured in one reference frame can be transformed to another frame by vector addition: the velocity of object A relative to frame C equals the velocity of A relative to frame B plus the velocity of B relative to frame C.

The concept rests on the principle that motion is only meaningful when specified with respect to a frame of reference. There is no absolute state of rest in Newtonian mechanics: an object described as stationary by one observer may be described as moving at constant velocity by another, and the laws of mechanics take the same form in all inertial frames (Galilean relativity).

Classical mechanics

For two objects A and B moving in one dimension, the velocity of A relative to B is vA/B = vA − vB. A familiar example is a passenger walking forward inside a moving train: the passenger's speed relative to the ground is the sum of the walking speed and the train's speed. Conversely, two vehicles approaching each other on a road each measure the other approaching at the sum of their speeds.

Special relativity

In Einstein's special relativity, the simple addition rule breaks down at speeds comparable to the speed of light. Velocities combine according to the relativistic velocity-addition formula, which guarantees that no relative speed exceeds the speed of light in vacuum. This framework also introduces effects such as time dilation and length contraction, which depend on the relative motion between observer and observed.
