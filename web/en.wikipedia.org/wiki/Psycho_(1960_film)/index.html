Psycho (1960 film)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Bates house looming on its hill above the motel, in the stark black-and-white of the film's California gothic]

Psycho is a 1960 American psychological horror film produced and directed by Alfred Hitchcock, written by Joseph Stefano from Robert Bloch's 1959 novel (itself inspired by the murderer Ed Gein). Marion Crane (Janet Leigh), on the run with stolen money, stops at the Bates Motel and meets its shy young proprietor Norman Bates (Anthony Perkins) — and Hitchcock kills his star a third of the way in, in the shower scene that changed cinema forever.

Made cheaply in black-and-white with his television crew, against studio resistance, Psycho became the highest-grossing film of Hitchcock's career and the founding text of the modern horror and slasher genres. Bernard Herrmann's shrieking all-strings score and Saul Bass's sequence design (the shower scene's 77 camera setups, chocolate syrup for blood) are film-school scripture.

The shower scene and marketing

Hitchcock bought up copies of Bloch's novel to keep the twist secret, shot the ending's explanation while despising its patness, and enforced a no-late-admission policy that invented modern event-movie marketing. The shower murder — implied, never explicit, assembled entirely by montage and music — rewired what audiences believed film could do, and what it could do to them.

Legacy

Perkins, typecast for life, returned for sequels; Gus Van Sant's shot-for-shot colour remake (1998) proved by experiment that the original's magic is not in its shots alone. Psycho anchors the American Film Institute lists, the National Film Registry, and every argument about suspense, censorship and the director as brand.
