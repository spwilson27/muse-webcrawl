Epistemic logic

Epistemic logic is a subfield of epistemology and modal logic concerned with reasoning about knowledge. It formalises statements such as "agent a knows that φ" using a modal operator typically written Kaφ, and studies the logical properties of knowledge, belief, and related notions like common knowledge.

History

Although discussions of the logic of knowledge date back to medieval philosophers, epistemic logic emerged as a distinct field with Georg Henrik von Wright's An Essay in Modal Logic (1951) and Jaakko Hintikka's Knowledge and Belief (1962), which introduced the possible-worlds semantics for epistemic statements. The field was further developed in computer science and economics for reasoning about multi-agent systems and distributed protocols.

Semantics and axioms

Epistemic logic uses Kripke models in which each agent has an accessibility relation between possible worlds, representing the worlds the agent considers possible. Common axioms include the truth axiom (Kφ → φ, distinguishing knowledge from mere belief), positive introspection (Kφ → KKφ), and negative introspection (¬Kφ → K¬Kφ). The system S5 is often taken as the standard logic of knowledge, though its idealisations — such as logical omniscience — are widely debated.

Common knowledge

Common knowledge arises when everyone knows φ, everyone knows that everyone knows φ, and so on ad infinitum. It is central to game theory and coordination problems, such as the muddy children puzzle.
