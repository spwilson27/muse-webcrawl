Ehud

Ehud ben-Gera (Hebrew: אֵהוּד בֶּן־גֵּרָא) is described in the Book of Judges (chapter 3) as a judge of ancient Israel and the assassin of Eglon, king of Moab. He is famous for his left-handedness, which enabled him to conceal a double-edged sword on his right thigh and kill the king during a private audience.

Biblical narrative

According to Judges, the Israelites had been subject to Eglon for eighteen years and sent Ehud, of the tribe of Benjamin, to deliver tribute. Ehud made himself a double-edged dagger about a cubit long and strapped it to his right thigh under his clothing. After presenting the tribute, he asked for a private audience, claiming to have a secret message — "a message from God" — for the king. When Eglon rose, Ehud drew the dagger with his left hand and plunged it into the king's belly, then locked the doors of the chamber and escaped. By the time Eglon's servants discovered the murder, Ehud had rallied the Israelites at Mount Ephraim; they seized the fords of the Jordan and defeated the Moabites, winning Israel eighty years of rest.

Interpretation

Ehud's story is often noted for its irony and humour, and for the play on his tribal identity: Benjamin means "son of the right hand," yet its hero is left-handed (or, in the Hebrew phrase, "restricted in the right hand"). The account is part of the cycle of oppression and deliverance that structures the Book of Judges.
