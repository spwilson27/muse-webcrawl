Theories of humour

Many theories of humour attempt to explain what humour is, what social functions it serves, and what would be considered humorous. Although there is no universal agreement, most accounts draw on a handful of major families of theory, often tracing their discussion back to Plato and Aristotle.

Major theories

  • Superiority theory — associated with Plato, Aristotle and Hobbes, it holds that laughter expresses a feeling of superiority over others' misfortunes or over one's former self.
  • Relief theory — developed by Herbert Spencer and Sigmund Freud, it treats laughter as the release of pent-up nervous or psychic energy.
  • Incongruity theory — favoured by Kant, Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard, it locates humour in the perception of something that violates our mental patterns and expectations, and its sudden resolution.
  • Benign violation theory — a modern synthesis (Peter McGraw) proposing that humour arises when something is simultaneously perceived as a violation and as benign.

Other approaches

Linguistic accounts, such as Victor Raskin's Semantic Script Theory of Humor and the General Theory of Verbal Humor, analyse jokes in terms of competing scripts. Philosophers including Henri Bergson (Laughter, 1900) treated the comic as the mechanical encrusted upon the living. Misattribution and play theories emphasise humour's role in social bonding and cognitive play.
