Incandescent light bulb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An incandescent bulb produces light by heating a filament until it glows.

An incandescent light bulb, incandescent lamp, or incandescent light globe is an electric light with a filament that is heated until it glows. The filament is enclosed in a glass bulb that is either filled with an inert gas or evacuated to protect the filament from oxidation. Current is supplied by terminals or wires embedded in the glass; most bulbs are used with an electric lamp socket.

History

Many inventors experimented with glowing filaments in the early 19th century, including Humphry Davy and Warren de la Rue. Joseph Swan demonstrated a working carbon-filament lamp in England in 1878, while Thomas Edison developed a high-resistance carbon filament lamp and a complete distribution system in 1879–80, leading to the first commercial electric lighting. The ductile tungsten filament, developed around 1910, displaced carbon, and Irving Langmuir's gas-filled bulb (1913) greatly improved efficiency and life.

Construction

A bulb consists of a glass envelope, a coiled tungsten filament supported by molybdenum wires, a filling of argon or nitrogen at low pressure (or a vacuum in small lamps), and a screw or bayonet base. When current passes through the filament, it heats to roughly 2,700 °C and radiates mostly in the infrared, with about 10% of the energy emerging as visible light.

Efficiency and phase-out

Because most of an incandescent lamp's output is heat, it is far less efficient than fluorescent lamps and LED lamps. Many jurisdictions have restricted or banned general-purpose incandescent bulbs since the 2000s — the European Union phased them out between 2009 and 2012, and U.S. rules effectively ended sales of common household types in 2023 — though specialty bulbs (oven, rough-service, decorative) often remain exempt.
