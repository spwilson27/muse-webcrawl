Planetarium

A planetarium is a theatre built primarily for presenting educational and entertaining shows about astronomy and the night sky. Its defining feature is a large domed projection screen onto which scenes of stars, planets and other celestial objects are projected, usually by a central star projector or modern digital fulldome system, while the audience sits in reclined seats beneath the artificial sky.

A planetarium dome during a presentation

History

Mechanical precursors include the orrery and the eighteenth-century Gottorf globe, which visitors entered. The modern planetarium began in 1923, when the first Zeiss projector (the Mark I, developed under Walther Bauersfeld) was installed at the Deutsches Museum in Munich; the first permanent public planetarium opened there in 1925. The first major American planetariums followed in the 1930s — Chicago's Adler Planetarium (1930) and New York's Hayden Planetarium (1935). The Japanese firm Goto and the American Spitz company later made smaller projectors affordable for schools and regional museums.

Technology

Classic opto-mechanical projectors — the iconic "dumbbell" with star balls at each end — project thousands of fixed stars and move the Sun, Moon and planets by separate mechanisms. Since the 1990s, digital fulldome video systems have largely superseded them, allowing flight through real astronomical datasets, full-motion video and interactive shows. Domes range from small portable inflatable models to the Nagoya City Science Museum's 35-metre dome, among the world's largest.

Role

Planetariums combine science education with spectacle; many now function as general "digital theatres" presenting programmes on Earth science, biology and art, while live star talks remain the core of the traditional format. There are roughly several thousand planetariums worldwide.
