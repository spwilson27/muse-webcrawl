The national parks of England and Wales are a group of thirteen areas of relatively undeveloped and scenic landscape designated under the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949. Unlike the national parks of many other countries, they are not state-owned wildernesses: most land remains in private hands, the parks contain towns and villages, and each is administered by a national park authority that acts as the local planning authority and works to conserve the landscape, wildlife, and cultural heritage while promoting public enjoyment.

Purposes and administration

Under the Environment Act 1995, national park authorities have two statutory purposes: to conserve and enhance the natural beauty, wildlife, and cultural heritage of the park, and to promote opportunities for the understanding and enjoyment of its special qualities by the public. Where the two conflict, the Sandford principle gives conservation priority. Authorities must also seek to foster the economic and social well-being of local communities. The Broads has a status similar to a national park but is governed by its own act, with an additional purpose of protecting the interests of navigation.

History

The movement for national parks grew from nineteenth-century campaigns for access to the countryside, energised by mass trespasses such as the Kinder Scout trespass of 1932. The 1949 Act created the framework, and ten parks were designated during the 1950s. The Lake District, the largest and most visited, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017 as a cultural landscape. Scotland has its own separate system of national parks (Loch Lomond and The Trossachs, and the Cairngorms), established under Scottish legislation in 2000.
