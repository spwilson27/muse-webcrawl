Tweed

Tweed is a rough, woollen fabric of a soft, open, flexible texture, resembling cheviot or homespun but more closely woven. It is usually woven with a plain weave, twill, or herringbone structure, often in mixtures of colours obtained by twisting together differently coloured woollen strands. Tweed was traditionally worn for outdoor country clothing — shooting jackets, plus fours, and deerstalkers — because it is durable, warm, and moisture-resistant.

History

The fabric originated in Scotland and Ireland for farmers' and crofters' work clothing. The name is popularly (if probably apocryphally) said to derive from a misreading of the Scots word tweel (twill) as "Tweed", after the River Tweed — a story repeated since the 1830s. In the 19th century, British estates commissioned distinctive "estate tweeds" as camouflage-like liveries for gamekeepers and ghillies.

Types

The most famous tweed is Harris Tweed, handwoven by islanders in the Outer Hebrides and protected by the Harris Tweed Act 1993 and its Orb certification mark. Other types include Donegal tweed from Ireland, with its characteristic flecks, and Cheviot tweed from the border hills. Coco Chanel's adoption of tweed for women's suits in the 20th century carried the fabric into high fashion.
