Hall–Héroult process

The Hall–Héroult process is the major industrial process for smelting aluminium. It involves dissolving aluminium oxide (alumina), obtained from bauxite through the Bayer process, in molten cryolite and electrolysing the mixture to produce pure aluminium metal.

Process

Alumina is dissolved in a bath of molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) with calcium fluoride at about 940–980 °C. Carbon electrodes carry a direct current of several hundred thousand amperes: carbon anodes are consumed as oxygen combines with them to form carbon dioxide, while molten aluminium collects at the cathode on the cell floor and is siphoned off.

History

The process was invented independently and almost simultaneously in 1886 by American chemist Charles Martin Hall and French scientist Paul Héroult — both aged 23, and both born and died in the same years. Before it, aluminium was a precious metal; Hall–Héroult, together with the Bayer process, made aluminium cheap and widely available, though the process remains very electricity-intensive and a significant source of CO₂ and perfluorocarbon emissions.
