Impeachment (evidence)
FieldLaw of evidence
Governed byFederal Rules of Evidence 607–610, 613 (U.S.)
PurposeAttack a witness's credibility
SettingCross-examination at trial

Impeachment, in the law of evidence, is the process of questioning the credibility of a witness. A party impeaches a witness by presenting evidence or eliciting testimony that casts doubt on the witness's truthfulness, perception, memory, or impartiality—for example, by showing bias, prior inconsistent statements, a prior criminal conviction, or a defect in the witness's ability to observe or recall events.

Methods

  • Prior inconsistent statements: confronting the witness with earlier statements that conflict with trial testimony (Federal Rule of Evidence 613).
  • Bias or interest: showing the witness has a motive to lie, such as a relationship with a party or a financial stake.
  • Character for untruthfulness: opinion or reputation testimony that the witness is untruthful, and cross-examination about specific bad acts (Rules 608 and 405).
  • Prior convictions: certain criminal convictions are admissible to attack credibility (Rule 609), subject to balancing tests and time limits.
  • Capacity: evidence that the witness's perception or memory was impaired at the relevant time.

Rules and limits

Under Rule 607, any party—including the party that called the witness—may attack the witness's credibility. Impeachment evidence is generally admitted only for the limited purpose of evaluating credibility, not as substantive proof, though prior statements made under oath at a prior proceeding may serve both purposes. The right to impeach interacts with the Confrontation Clause, which guarantees criminal defendants a meaningful opportunity for cross-examination, and with restrictions on certain topics, such as a witness's religious beliefs (Rule 610).

Rehabilitation

After a witness has been impeached, the calling party may attempt rehabilitation—restoring credibility through redirect examination, explaining an apparent inconsistency, or offering evidence of prior consistent statements where the rules allow.
