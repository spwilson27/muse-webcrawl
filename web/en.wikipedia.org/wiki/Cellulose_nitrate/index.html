Cellulose nitrate (also nitrocellulose) is a highly flammable compound formed by nitrating cellulose through exposure to a mixture of nitric acid and sulfuric acid. Depending on its degree of nitration, it has been used as a low-order explosive (guncotton), a propellant, a lacquer, and — plasticised with camphor — as celluloid, one of the first synthetic plastics and the standard base for photographic and cinema film until the 1950s.

History

Christian Friedrich Schönbein discovered guncotton in 1846 (independently found by Rudolf Christian Böttger), after earlier work by Henri Braconnot and Théophile-Jules Pelouze. Early factories suffered dangerous explosions until Frederick Augustus Abel developed a pulping and washing process that made manufacture safer. John Wesley Hyatt patented celluloid in the United States in 1869 while seeking a substitute for ivory in billiard balls, and Alexander Parkes had earlier exhibited a similar material, Parkesine. Hilaire de Chardonnet used nitrocellulose to make artificial silk in the 1880s.

Uses

Highly nitrated material (about 12.5–13.5% nitrogen) serves as guncotton and in smokeless powder propellants. Less nitrated forms dissolve in solvents to make collodion (once used in photography and medicine), lacquers and nail polish, and flash paper used by magicians. Nitrate film stock dominated cinema until it was replaced by acetate "safety film" because old nitrate prints burn fiercely and decompose, sometimes spontaneously; archives take special precautions when storing and projecting it.

Hazards

Cellulose nitrate burns rapidly even without atmospheric oxygen and deteriorating stocks pose a fire risk; it is regulated as an explosive in transport and storage.
