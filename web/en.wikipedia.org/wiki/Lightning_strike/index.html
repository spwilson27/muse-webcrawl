Lightning strike

A lightning strike or lightning bolt is a lightning event in which an electric discharge takes place between the atmosphere and the ground. Most originate in a cumulonimbus cloud and terminate on the ground, although a small percentage terminate on elevated structures. Lightning strikes cause thunder, a phenomenon from the initial shock waves created as air along the bolt's path rapidly heats and expands.

Formation

Charge separation inside a thundercloud leaves the cloud base typically negatively charged. When the electric field becomes strong enough, a stepped leader descends from the cloud in branching steps; as it nears the ground, upward streamers rise from tall objects, and the connection completes a conductive path. The bright return stroke then carries a large current — typically tens of kiloamperes — up the channel at a substantial fraction of the speed of light, heating the air to temperatures around 30,000 K.

Types and effects

Negative cloud-to-ground flashes are the most common, while positive flashes, though rarer, tend to carry greater charge and are more damaging. Lightning strikes kill or injure people outdoors (about a few dozen deaths per year in the United States in recent decades), ignite wildfires and structure fires, damage electrical and electronic systems, and strike aircraft routinely, usually without serious harm because the current flows over the airframe. Survivors of strikes may suffer burns, cardiac and neurological injuries; branching skin patterns called Lichtenberg figures can appear.

Protection and safety

Lightning rods and building protection systems intercept strikes and conduct the current safely to earth. Safety guidance is commonly summarized as "when thunder roars, go indoors": substantial buildings and enclosed metal vehicles provide protection, while open fields, isolated trees, water, and ridgelines are hazardous.
