Manslaughter in English law

In English law, manslaughter is a less serious form of homicide than murder, covering unlawful killings committed without the intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm that defines murder ("malice aforethought"). It is divided broadly into voluntary manslaughter — where the defendant had the intent for murder but a partial defence applies — and involuntary manslaughter — where the defendant lacked that intent.

Voluntary manslaughter

Voluntary manslaughter arises where the partial defences in the Homicide Act 1957 (as amended by the Coroners and Justice Act 2009) succeed:

  • Diminished responsibility — the defendant's ability to understand their conduct, form rational judgment, or exercise self-control was substantially impaired by a recognised medical condition.
  • Loss of control — the defendant lost self-control due to a qualifying trigger (fear of serious violence, or things said/done of an extremely grave character causing a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged).
  • Suicide pact — killing in pursuance of a genuine suicide pact.

Involuntary manslaughter

  • Gross negligence manslaughter — death caused by a gross breach of a duty of care owed to the victim (R v Adomako [1995]).
  • Unlawful act (constructive) manslaughter — death caused by an unlawful act that a reasonable person would realise risks at least some harm.
  • Corporate manslaughter — under the Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act 2007, organisations whose senior management failures grossly breach a duty of care may be convicted and fined.

Sentencing

Manslaughter carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment, but the offence covers an enormous range of culpability and sentences range from suspended terms to very long determinate or life sentences. The mandatory life sentence for murder does not apply.
