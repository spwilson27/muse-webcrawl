Hulagu Khan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hulagu's siege of Baghdad as depicted in Rashid al-Din's world history]

Hulagu Khan (c. 1217 – 8 February 1265) was a Mongol ruler, grandson of Genghis Khan and brother of Möngke and Kublai, who conquered south-western Asia and founded the Ilkhanate of Persia. Commissioned by Möngke in 1253 to subdue the region, his armies destroyed the Nizari Ismaili state of Alamut (1256), then in 1258 stormed Baghdad, executed the last Abbasid caliph of Baghdad, and — in the traditional accounts — massacred a vast share of the city's population, an event seared into Islamic memory as one of history's great catastrophes. Checked at Ain Jalut by the Mamluks (1260) after withdrawing the bulk of his army on Möngke's death, Hulagu spent his last years fighting his cousin Berke of the Golden Horde in the first intra-Mongol civil war.

The Baghdad campaign

Hulagu's expedition — perhaps 150,000 men, with Chinese siege engineers, a vassal host from Armenia and Georgia, and meticulous logistics — first extinguished the Assassins of Alamut (whose strongholds surrendered in 1256; the great library was burned, though the astronomer al-Tusi saved instruments and books for the observatory Hulagu later built him at Maragha). Baghdad, warned and defiant under Caliph al-Musta'sim, was invested in January 1258, its walls breached by dikes diverted from the Tigris and massed artillery; the caliph surrendered on 10 February and was executed (rolled in a carpet and trampled, by the famous account, to avoid shedding royal blood on the earth). The sack's death toll — 200,000 to a million in the sources — cannot be verified; the destruction of libraries, mosques and irrigation was real and long-remembered, though modern scholars debate how much of Iraq's agricultural decline to lay on 1258 versus longer trends.

Ilkhanate

From his capitals at Maragha and Tabriz, Hulagu ruled Persia, Iraq and Anatolia's fringe; his successors (Abaqa, then Ghazan, who embraced Islam in 1295) made the Ilkhanate a Persianate state and patron of Rashid al-Din's world history. His war with Berke — a Muslim convert enraged by Baghdad — over the Caucasus pastures opened the permanent fracture of the Mongol world into rival khanates. Hulagu was buried, with human sacrifices by some accounts, on Shahi Island in Lake Urmia.
