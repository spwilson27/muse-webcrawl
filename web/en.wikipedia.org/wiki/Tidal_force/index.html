Tidal force

The tidal field stretches a body along the line to the perturbing mass.

The tidal force is the difference between the gravitational force exerted on different parts of an extended body by another mass. Because gravity weakens with distance, the side of a body nearer to the attracting mass is pulled more strongly than its centre, and the centre more strongly than the far side. The result is a stretching effect along the line joining the two bodies, responsible for the ocean tides on Earth, and — over time — for tidal locking, tidal heating of moons, and the disruption of bodies that approach within the Roche limit.

Formulation

For a small displacement Δr along the axis toward a mass M at distance r, the tidal acceleration is approximately

atidal ≈ 2GMΔr / r³

The crucial feature is the inverse-cube dependence on distance, in contrast to the inverse-square law of gravity itself: tidal effects therefore matter most for close encounters and for compact, massive bodies. In general relativity, the same phenomenon is expressed by the geodesic deviation equation, in which the Riemann curvature tensor measures how neighbouring freely falling paths converge or diverge — tidal effects are, in that sense, the observable signature of curved spacetime.

Effects

On Earth

The Moon produces the dominant terrestrial tidal force, raising two ocean bulges — on the side facing the Moon and, counter-intuitively, on the opposite side, where the water is pulled least. The Sun contributes a smaller tide; when the two align (spring tides) the effects add, and when opposed (neap tides) they partly cancel. Tidal friction is gradually transferring Earth's rotational angular momentum to the Moon's orbit, lengthening the day and pushing the Moon away by a few centimetres a year.

Elsewhere

Jupiter's tidal forces flex its moon Io, driving the most intense volcanism known; they also heat a subsurface ocean in Europa. Saturn's rings lie inside its Roche limit, where tides prevent material gathering into a moon. A star wandering too near a black hole is torn apart by tides — "spaghettification" in the extreme vertical stretching near a small black hole, or a tidal disruption event observable by telescopes when a star approaches a supermassive black hole.
