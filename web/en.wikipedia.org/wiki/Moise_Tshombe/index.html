Moïse Tshombe

Moïse Kapenda Tshombe (sometimes written Tshombé; 10 November 1919 – 29 June 1969) was a Congolese businessman and politician. He led the secession of the mineral-rich Katanga province from the newly independent Republic of the Congo in 1960, and later served as Prime Minister of the Congo from 1964 to 1965.

Early life

Tshombe was born in Musumba, in the Lunda region of the Belgian Congo, into a wealthy trading family. Educated at American Methodist mission schools, he went into commerce and later politics, founding the CONAKAT party, which drew support from the peoples of Katanga and advocated a federal Congo with strong provincial autonomy.

Katanga secession

Days after Congolese independence on 30 June 1960, amid army mutinies, Tshombe declared Katanga's secession on 11 July 1960, backed by Belgian mining interests, in particular Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, and Belgian officers and mercenaries. The breakaway State of Katanga was never internationally recognised, and United Nations forces (ONUC) fought to end the secession, which collapsed in January 1963; Tshombe went into exile in Spain.

Prime Minister and exile

Recalled in 1964 to head a government of national reconciliation, Tshombe served as Prime Minister during the Simba rebellion, relying again on mercenaries and Western support, and the rescue of hostages at Stanleyville (Operation Dragon Rouge). President Joseph Kasa-Vubu dismissed him in October 1965. After Mobutu Sese Seko's coup, Tshombe was sentenced to death in absentia for treason. In 1967 his plane was hijacked to Algeria, where he remained under house arrest until his death in Algiers in 1969, officially of heart failure.

Legacy

Tshombe remains a controversial figure: a symbol of neo-colonial interference and of Katangese separatism to his critics, and to supporters a pragmatic, pro-Western statesman in a turbulent era.
