Portland cement

Portland cement is the most common type of cement in general use around the world as a basic ingredient of concrete, mortar, stucco and non-speciality grout. It was developed from other types of hydraulic lime in England in the early 19th century by Joseph Aspdin, who patented it in 1824, and usually originates from limestone. It is a fine powder, produced by heating limestone and clay minerals in a kiln to form clinker, grinding the clinker with about 2–3% gypsum.

History

Aspdin named the product "Portland cement" because the set mortar resembled Portland stone, a prized building stone from the Isle of Portland. His son William Aspdin is credited with developing the modern, higher-temperature clinker process in the 1840s. Production spread rapidly; by the late 19th century Portland cement had displaced natural cements worldwide, and rotary kilns allowed output on an industrial scale.

Manufacture

Raw materials — chiefly limestone with smaller amounts of clay, shale, sand and iron ore — are ground, blended and fed into a rotary kiln heated to about 1,450 °C by a flame of roughly 2,000 °C. In the kiln the mix is decarbonated and partially fused into marble-sized nodules of clinker, whose principal mineral phases are alite (impure tricalcium silicate), belite, tricalcium aluminate and ferrite. The clinker is cooled and ground with gypsum, which controls setting time.

Setting and hardening

Mixed with water, cement hydrates: alite and belite react to form calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and calcium hydroxide, giving concrete its strength over days and weeks. Cement production is a major source of industrial carbon dioxide — roughly 8% of global emissions — both from fuel combustion and from the calcination of limestone itself, which has driven interest in blended cements and supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and slag.

Types

Standards such as ASTM C150 define several types: Type I (general purpose), Type II (moderate sulfate resistance), Type III (high early strength), Type IV (low heat) and Type V (high sulfate resistance). European standard EN 197 classifies cements from CEM I (pure Portland) to CEM V (composite).
