Meningococcal disease

Meningococcal disease describes infections caused by the bacterium Neisseria meningitidis (also termed meningococcus). It has a high mortality rate if untreated but is vaccine-preventable. While best known as a cause of meningitis, it can also result in sepsis (meningococcemia), both of which can be severely disabling or fatal. The two most common presentations are meningococcal meningitis and meningococcal septicaemia.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms of meningococcal meningitis include sudden fever, headache, stiff neck, nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and altered mental status. Meningococcal sepsis is characterized by a hemorrhagic (purpuric or petechial) rash, rapid circulatory collapse, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. In severe cases, Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome—bleeding into the adrenal glands—may occur.

Cause and transmission

Neisseria meningitidis is a Gram-negative diplococcus carried asymptomatically in the nasopharynx of roughly 5–10% of adults. It spreads through respiratory droplets and close contact. Thirteen serogroups are recognized, of which A, B, C, W, X, and Y cause most invasive disease.

Treatment and prevention

Meningococcal disease is a medical emergency. Treatment consists of prompt intravenous antibiotics such as ceftriaxone, together with supportive care. Close contacts are offered antibiotic chemoprophylaxis. Vaccines exist against serogroups A, C, W, and Y (conjugate vaccines) and against serogroup B (protein-based vaccines). Large epidemics have historically occurred in the "meningitis belt" of sub-Saharan Africa.

Epidemiology

Infants, adolescents, and young adults are at highest risk. Crowded living conditions such as dormitories and military barracks increase transmission. Even with treatment, case fatality rates are around 10–15%, and survivors may suffer amputations, hearing loss, or neurological damage.
