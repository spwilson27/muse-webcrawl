Tikanga Māori

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tikanga is a Māori term meaning "customary system of values and practices" — the set of cultural habits, protocols and rules that govern behaviour in te ao Māori (the Māori world). Sometimes loosely translated as "the Māori way of doing things", tikanga encompasses everything from the formal rituals of encounter on a marae (such as the pōwhiri, or welcome ceremony) to everyday practices around food, death, names and the environment. It is derived from the word tika, meaning "right" or "correct".

Key concepts

Tikanga rests on interlocking values. Mana is prestige, authority and spiritual power; tapu denotes sacred restriction, balanced by noa, the ordinary state free from restriction. Whanaungatanga is kinship and relationship-building, manaakitanga is hospitality and care for others, and kaitiakitanga is guardianship of the natural world. Protocols vary between iwi (tribes) — a practice often summarised as tikanga tātau tātau differing from place to place — but the underlying values are widely shared.

Tikanga and New Zealand law

Tikanga Māori has been increasingly recognised within New Zealand's legal system. The Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Takamore v Clarke and the recognition of tikanga in cases concerning burial disputes and environmental rights, together with statutes such as the Resource Management Act 1991 and the settlement legislation for the Whanganui River (Te Awa Tupua), have led commentators to describe tikanga as a first law of Aotearoa and an emerging source of the common law.
