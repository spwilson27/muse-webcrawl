Keratin

Keratin filaments within a cell

Keratin is one of a family of structural fibrous proteins, known as scleroproteins, and is the key structural material making up scales, hair, nails, feathers, horns, claws, hooves, and the outer layer of skin among vertebrates. Keratin also protects epithelial cells from damage or stress. It is insoluble in water and organic solvents and is unusually rich in the amino acid cysteine, whose disulfide bonds give keratin its strength and stiffness.

Types

Keratins are divided into the "hard" keratins of hair, nails, and horns, and the "soft" keratins of skin. In humans there are 54 functional keratin genes, classified as type I (acidic) and type II (basic-to-neutral), which pair to form intermediate filaments in epithelial cells.

Structure

Alpha-keratins, found in mammals, are built from alpha-helical strands twisted into coiled coils. Beta-keratins, found in reptiles and birds, consist of beta-pleated sheets and are harder than alpha forms. The silk proteins of spiders and insects are also sometimes grouped with keratins.

Disease

Mutations in keratin genes cause a range of skin and hair disorders, including epidermolysis bullosa simplex and pachyonychia congenita. Certain fungi, such as those causing athlete's foot and ringworm, feed on keratin.

Uses

Keratin derived from wool, feathers, and horns is used in cosmetics, hair treatments, and as a biomaterial in wound dressings and tissue engineering.
