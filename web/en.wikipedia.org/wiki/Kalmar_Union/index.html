Kalmar Union

The Kalmar Union (Danish, Norwegian and Swedish: Kalmarunionen) was a personal union of the kingdoms of Denmark, Norway (including Iceland, Greenland and the Faroe Islands), and Sweden (including much of Finland) under a single monarch that existed, with interruptions, from 1397 to 1523. It was founded by Queen Margaret I of Denmark to counterbalance the influence of the Hanseatic League.

Foundation

Margaret, regent of Denmark and Norway, secured the election of her great-nephew Eric of Pomerania and had him crowned king of all three realms at Kalmar Castle in Sweden in 1397. The accompanying union letter envisaged a permanent union, though its constitutional terms were never fully ratified in all three kingdoms; each realm retained its own laws and council of the realm.

History

Margaret ruled effectively until her death in 1412. Eric's tax policies and appointments of Danish and German officials provoked rebellion in Sweden — most notably the Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson rising of 1434 — and he was deposed in all three kingdoms by 1439. Thereafter the union alternated between restoration and Swedish secession: Christopher of Bavaria, Christian I, and Hans each briefly reunited the crowns, while Sweden was often ruled by regents (riksföreståndare) of the Sture family.

King Christian II attempted to secure Sweden by force after his victory in 1520, culminating in the Stockholm Bloodbath, in which scores of Swedish nobles and clergy were executed. The atrocity ignited Gustav Vasa's war of liberation; his election as king of Sweden on 6 June 1523 is conventionally taken as the end of the union, though Denmark and Norway remained united until 1814.

Legacy

The Kalmar Union remains the largest political union ever achieved in Scandinavia and a recurring reference point in later Nordic cooperation. Its history is often told through competing Danish and Swedish national narratives.
