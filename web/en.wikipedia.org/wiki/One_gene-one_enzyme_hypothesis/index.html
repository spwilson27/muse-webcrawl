One gene–one enzyme hypothesis

The one gene–one enzyme hypothesis is the idea that genes act through the production of enzymes, with each gene responsible for producing a single enzyme that in turn affects a single step in a metabolic pathway. It was proposed by George Beadle and Edward Tatum in 1941.

The experiments

Working with the bread mould Neurospora crassa, Beadle and Tatum exposed spores to X-rays to create mutations, then tested the mutants' ability to grow on minimal medium. Mutants that failed to grow could often be rescued by adding a specific nutrient, showing that a single mutation had disabled a single step in the synthesis of that nutrient — implying one gene controls one enzymatic step. Their paper, "Genetic Control of Biochemical Reactions in Neurospora", is a landmark of molecular genetics, and they shared the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Joshua Lederberg.

Refinement

Later research showed the hypothesis was an oversimplification: some genes encode proteins that are not enzymes, and many proteins consist of multiple polypeptide chains encoded by different genes. The hypothesis was therefore restated as "one gene–one polypeptide". Discoveries such as alternative splicing, in which one gene can produce several proteins, complicated the picture further, but the core insight — genes specify the structure of proteins — remains foundational.
