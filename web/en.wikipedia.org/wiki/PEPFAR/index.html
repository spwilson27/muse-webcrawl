PEPFAR

The U.S. President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) is a United States government initiative to address the global HIV/AIDS epidemic, principally in Africa. Announced by President George W. Bush in his 2003 State of the Union address and authorised that year by the United States Leadership Against HIV/AIDS, Tuberculosis, and Malaria Act, it is the largest commitment by any nation to combat a single disease in history, and is widely credited with saving tens of millions of lives.

History

PEPFAR's first phase (2004–2008) set goals of supporting treatment for two million people, preventing seven million new infections and supporting care for ten million people in fifteen focus countries. The programme was reauthorised in 2008 (the Tom Lantos and Henry J. Hyde Act) with authorisation rising to $48 billion, and again in modified form in 2013 and 2018. Across its first two decades the United States invested well over $100 billion through PEPFAR and related global-health accounts.

Programmes and impact

PEPFAR funds antiretroviral therapy, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, HIV testing, voluntary medical male circumcision, pre-exposure prophylaxis, and care for orphans and vulnerable children. By the early 2020s the programme reported supporting antiretroviral treatment for more than 20 million people and enabling millions of babies to be born HIV-free; U.S. government estimates credit it with saving more than 25 million lives.

The programme works through partner governments, non-governmental organisations, faith-based groups and the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria, to which the United States is the largest contributor.

Criticism and controversies

Early PEPFAR legislation earmarked a share of prevention funding for abstinence-until-marriage programmes and included an anti-prostitution pledge requirement, both criticised by public-health researchers; the earmark was later softened and the pledge requirement was struck down in part by the U.S. Supreme Court as applied to American organisations. The programme's continuation and funding levels became subjects of renewed political debate in the mid-2020s.
