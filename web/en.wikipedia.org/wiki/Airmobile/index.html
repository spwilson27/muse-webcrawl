Airmobile (or air mobility) describes a military doctrine and form of maneuver in which ground combat units are transported by helicopter — and supported by helicopter gunships and aerial artillery — to seize terrain, raid, reinforce, or encircle an enemy. Airmobile operations differ from airborne operations in that troops are lifted over relatively short distances within or near the battlefield, often repeatedly, rather than parachuted deep behind enemy lines from fixed-wing aircraft.

Development

Helicopter troop lift was first used in the Korean War mainly for evacuation and logistics, but the concept matured in the early 1960s when the U.S. Army's Howze Board (1962) recommended a radical expansion of organic aviation. The result included the 11th Air Assault Division (Test) and, on its activation in 1965, the 1st Cavalry Division (Airmobile), which deployed to Vietnam with over 400 helicopters. In Vietnam, turbine-powered types such as the UH-1 Iroquois ("Huey") troop carrier, CH-47 Chinook heavy lifter, and AH-1 Cobra gunship enabled battalions to be inserted into landing zones, supplied in the field, and extracted at will; the 1965 Battle of Ia Drang was the first large-scale test of the concept, and operations such as the 1970 Cambodian incursion and Lam Son 719 depended on it. The 101st Airborne Division was likewise converted to an airmobile (later "air assault") formation.

Characteristics and modern use

Airmobile forces trade armor and heavy equipment for speed and reach: they can bypass terrain and strongpoints, conduct vertical envelopment, raid, and rapidly concentrate at unexpected points, but are vulnerable to air defenses, bad weather, and the lift capacity and range of their helicopters. The Soviet Union and later Russia developed parallel "heliborne" assault forces, and most modern armies maintain dedicated air assault brigades. Today the term overlaps with air assault, while "air mobility" in U.S. usage also denotes the Air Force's global airlift and tanker enterprise under Air Mobility Command.
