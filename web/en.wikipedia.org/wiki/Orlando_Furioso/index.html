Orlando Furioso ("The Frenzy of Orlando") is an Italian epic poem by Ludovico Ariosto. It has been described as the definitive text of the Italian Renaissance and a work of enduring European influence. The poem, which first appeared in 1516 and reached its definitive 46-canto form in 1532, is a continuation of Matteo Maria Boiardo's unfinished Orlando Innamorato and draws on the Carolingian cycle of legends about Charlemagne and his paladins.

Plot

Set against the war between Charlemagne's Christian knights and the Saracen armies invading Europe, the poem interlaces dozens of storylines in the technique of entrelacement. The titular thread follows the paladin Orlando (Roland), whose unrequited love for the pagan princess Angelica drives him mad (furioso) when he learns she has married the foot-soldier Medoro; his wits, lost on Earth, are recovered from the Moon by the English knight Astolfo, who rides the hippogriff. Parallel plots include the love of the Saracen warrior Ruggiero for the Christian warrior-maiden Bradamante — destined founders of Ariosto's patrons, the Este family of Ferrara — the exploits of the sorceress Alcina and the enchantress Logistilla, and the wars of the African king Agramante and the Spanish king Marsilio.

Form and influence

The poem is written in ottava rima, eight-line stanzas (abababcc) handled with celebrated fluency and irony. Ariosto's narrator frequently interrupts the action with asides and mock-apologies. Orlando Furioso profoundly influenced Spenser's Faerie Queene, Shakespeare, Cervantes, and the whole tradition of European romance; its episodes supplied subjects to painters, and to composers including Handel (Orlando, Ariodante, Alcina), Vivaldi, and Haydn. Sir John Harington's English translation appeared in 1591.
