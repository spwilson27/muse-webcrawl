Planchette

A planchette (French: little plank) is a small, usually heart-shaped flat piece of wood or other material, traditionally supported by casters and fitted with a pencil or pointer, used in séances and automatic writing to supposedly receive messages from spirits. In the later nineteenth century the planchette was adapted into the talking board, most famously commercialised as the Ouija board, in which the planchette serves as a movable pointer that indicates letters and numbers printed on the board.

History

Planchettes came to prominence in France in the 1850s, at the height of the Spiritualist movement that had spread from the United States to Europe following the fame of the Fox sisters. Early planchettes were small wheeled baskets or boards into which a pencil was fixed point-downward; participants rested their fingers lightly on the device and waited for it to move across a sheet of paper, tracing out words and messages believed to come from the dead.

The device was a commercial success: Parisian bookshops and novelty sellers reported strong demand, and planchettes were exported widely. By the 1860s the fashion had reached Britain and the United States, where planchettes were marketed as parlour amusements as much as instruments of spirit communication.

Talking boards

In the 1880s and 1890s, American entrepreneurs combined the planchette with a board printed with the alphabet, numbers, and the words "yes" and "no". The resulting talking board removed the need for paper and pencil; the planchette, now often a simple heart- or teardrop-shaped pointer with a viewing window or felt-tipped legs, glided over the board to spell out answers. The Kennard Novelty Company's "Ouija" board, patented in 1891 and later produced by the Fuld company and Parker Brothers, became the best-known example.

Explanation

Scientists explain the movement of the planchette by the ideomotor effect: small, unconscious muscular movements of the participants, guided by suggestion and expectation, steer the pointer. Controlled experiments in which participants are blindfolded show that the messages degenerate into gibberish, suggesting that the operators themselves — without conscious intent — produce the movement. Psychologists class the planchette with dowsing rods, pendulums, and facilitated communication as devices whose outputs arise from ideomotor action rather than any external agency.
