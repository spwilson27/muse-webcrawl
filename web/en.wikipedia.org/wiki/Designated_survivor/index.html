In the United States, a designated survivor (or designated successor) is a named individual in the presidential line of succession, chosen to stay at a secure and undisclosed location when the President, Vice President and other officials in the line of succession are gathered at a single place, such as during State of the Union addresses and presidential inaugurations. The practice is intended to guarantee continuity of government in the event of a catastrophic attack that kills the assembled leadership.

History

The practice dates to the Cold War era and the fear of a nuclear strike on Washington. The identity of the designated survivor is generally not revealed until after the event, though it sometimes leaks or is later disclosed. Typically a Cabinet secretary is chosen; the person must be constitutionally eligible to serve as president (including being a natural-born citizen and at least 35 years old), so officials in the line of succession who do not meet those requirements are skipped.

Procedure

During the event, the designated survivor is taken to a secure location with a military aide carrying the "nuclear football" (the briefcase containing nuclear launch authentication materials), receives briefings, and is accompanied by Secret Service protection. If disaster struck, the designated survivor would become Acting President under the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

In popular culture

The concept was popularised by the television series Designated Survivor (2016–2019), in which the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development becomes president after an explosion destroys the Capitol during the State of the Union address.
