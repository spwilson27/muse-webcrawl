The Broads is a network of mostly navigable rivers and lakes (known locally as broads) in the English counties of Norfolk and Suffolk. The lakes, though often broad and shallow, are not natural in origin: they are the flooded remains of medieval peat excavations, a discovery made in the 1960s by botanist Joyce Lambert and colleagues.

The Broads Authority, established in 1989, manages the area, which has a status similar to that of a national park. The Broads form Britain's largest protected wetland and one of Europe's finest river landscapes, renowned for boating holidays, sailing, wildlife, and characteristic drainage windmills (windpumps).

Geography

The Broads largely follow the line of three rivers: the Bure, the Yare, and the Waveney, together with their tributaries including the Ant, the Thurne, the Chet, and the Wensum. More than 40 individual broads survive, of which around 25 are navigable or partly navigable, including Barton Broad, Hickling Broad, Horsey Mere, Ranworth Broad, and Wroxham Broad, often called the "capital of the Broads".

History

For centuries the broads were thought to be natural features, possibly the remains of a great estuary. Research in the 1960s showed that their steep sides and flat bottoms resulted from peat digging in the Middle Ages, when Norwich Cathedral Priory and other landowners excavated vast quantities of peat for fuel. Rising sea levels in the fourteenth century flooded the pits, creating the lakes. The waterways were used for transporting fuel and goods, and the flat-bottomed Norfolk wherry became the region's characteristic cargo vessel. From the nineteenth century the Broads became a popular destination for boating holidays and recreation.

Ecology

The area supports a rich variety of wildlife, including the swallowtail butterfly, found in Britain only in the Broads, the Norfolk hawker dragonfly, bitterns, marsh harriers, and the rare fen orchid. Much of the area is designated under national and international conservation schemes, including Ramsar sites and Special Areas of Conservation.
