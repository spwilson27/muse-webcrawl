Folk psychology
The ordinary, commonsense framework by which people explain and predict behaviour in terms of beliefs, desires and other mental states.

Folk psychology, or commonsense psychology, is the understanding of human behaviour and the mind based not on academic research or formal theorising, but on the accumulated observations and inherited presuppositions of everyday social life. It is the framework of concepts — including belief, desire, intention, perception, emotion, and consciousness — and the rough generalizations connecting them, which people use to explain and predict one another's actions. The psychologist and philosopher Jerry Fodor remarked on its remarkable predictive power: if folk psychology did not work tolerably well, human social life as we know it would be impossible.

Belief–desire explanation

The characteristic pattern of folk psychological explanation cites a person's beliefs and desires to rationalize their behaviour: someone went to the kitchen because they wanted a drink and believed there was water in the fridge. Such "belief–desire psychology" treats agents as broadly rational — acting on what they take the world to be like in light of what they want. Attributions work even when the beliefs are false, which is central to developmental tests such as the false-belief task, generally passed by children around age four.

Folk psychology in philosophy

In the philosophy of mind, folk psychology is contested:

  • Theory theory holds that our mental-state attributions deploy an implicit theory of mind, acquired and revised like a scientific theory; children progressively refine it.
  • Simulation theory argues that we understand others by simulating their mental processes with our own minds, putting ourselves in their place rather than applying a theory.
  • Eliminative materialism, defended by Paul and Patricia Churchland, holds that folk psychology is a false and stagnant theory whose categories (belief, desire) will ultimately be eliminated by a mature neuroscience, as "phlogiston" was eliminated from chemistry.
  • Functionalists and interpretivists such as Daniel Dennett defend folk psychology (the "intentional stance") as an indispensable and largely reliable level of description, whatever neuroscience discovers.

Related ideas

Folk psychology belongs to a family of "folk" domains — folk physics, folk biology — studied in cognitive psychology as intuitive theories. Developmental psychologists such as Henry Wellman have mapped how children's theory of mind matures, while researchers on autism (notably Simon Baron-Cohen's "mindblindness" hypothesis) have studied its impairments. Cross-cultural studies investigate how far the belief–desire framework is universal and how far it is culturally shaped.
