Colorimetry is "the science and technology used to quantify and describe physically the human color perception". It is similar to spectrophotometry, but is distinguished by its interest in reducing spectra to the physical correlates of color perception, most often the CIE 1931 XYZ color space tristimulus values and related quantities.

Principles

Human color vision is trichromatic: three types of cone cells in the retina respond differently across the visible spectrum, so most colors can be matched by mixing three primaries in suitable proportions. Color-matching experiments, notably those of Wright and Guild in the 1920s, measured the amounts of three primaries needed by an average observer to match monochromatic test lights, yielding the color matching functions. Because some matches required "negative" amounts of a primary, the International Commission on Illumination (CIE) in 1931 defined the XYZ color space with imaginary primaries that make all matching functions non-negative.

Instruments

A colorimeter measures color through three (or more) filtered photodetectors whose spectral responses approximate the color matching functions, reporting tristimulus values directly. A spectrophotometer instead measures the spectral reflectance or transmittance of a sample at many wavelengths, from which tristimulus values are computed. Colorimeters are faster and cheaper and are widely used for display calibration; spectrophotometers are more accurate and general.

Applications

Colorimetry underpins color management in printing, photography and displays; quality control of paints, textiles and food; and the specification of light sources through quantities such as correlated color temperature and the color rendering index. Derived spaces such as CIELAB (1976) were designed so that Euclidean distances approximate perceived color differences, enabling practical color-difference formulas.
