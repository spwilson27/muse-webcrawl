Steppe bison

The steppe bison or steppe wisent (Bison priscus) is an extinct species of bison that ranged across the mammoth steppe of Europe, Central Asia and Siberia during the Pleistocene, and crossed the Bering land bridge into North America. It is considered the ancestor of both the modern American bison and the European bison (wisent).

Description

The steppe bison was larger than most modern bison, standing about 2 metres (6.6 feet) at the shoulder, with massive horns that spread up to a metre across in the biggest bulls. Its appearance is recorded in remarkable detail by Palaeolithic artists: steppe bison are among the animals depicted in cave paintings at sites such as Altamira and Lascaux, and a nearly complete figure was sculpted in clay in the Tuc d'Audoubert cave.

Mummified specimens

Permafrost in Siberia and Alaska has yielded frozen carcasses. The most famous is "Blue Babe", a 36,000-year-old bull found in Alaska in 1979, named for the blue mineral vivianite coating its hide; it is displayed at the University of Alaska Museum of the North. A remarkably complete mummy found in Yakutia in 2014 has enabled detailed studies of anatomy and ancient DNA.

Extinction

The steppe bison survived in Siberia until roughly 8,000–9,000 years ago, disappearing as the steppe gave way to forest and tundra at the end of the Ice Age and as human hunting pressure grew. In North America it evolved into later forms, including Bison antiquus, from which the modern plains bison descends.
