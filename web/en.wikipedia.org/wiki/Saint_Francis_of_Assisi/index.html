Francis of Assisi (Italian: Francesco d'Assisi; born Giovanni di Pietro di Bernardone, 1181 or 1182 – 3 October 1226) was an Italian Catholic friar, deacon and mystic who founded the Franciscan orders. Renowned for his vow of poverty, his love of nature and animals, and his missionary zeal, he is one of the most venerated figures in Christianity and the patron saint of Italy, animals, and ecology.

Early life and conversion

The son of a wealthy silk merchant, Francis led a carefree youth and aspired to knighthood. After imprisonment in a war with Perugia and a serious illness, he underwent a gradual conversion: he embraced a leper on the road, heard the crucifix at San Damiano command "rebuild my church", renounced his inheritance before the bishop of Assisi, and began living as a penitent, begging and repairing ruined chapels, including the little Porziuncola.

The Franciscan movement

In 1209 Francis and his first companions received oral approval from Pope Innocent III for a rule of life based on poverty and preaching. Clare of Assisi founded the female branch (Poor Clares) in 1212, and a Third Order served lay penitents. Francis travelled to Egypt during the Fifth Crusade and met Sultan al-Kamil in a celebrated encounter. In 1223 he arranged the first Christmas crèche at Greccio, and in 1224, on Mount La Verna, he is said to have received the stigmata — the wounds of Christ — the first recorded case in Christian history.

Death and legacy

Nearly blind and in pain, Francis composed the Canticle of the Sun, praising God through "Brother Sun" and "Sister Moon", and died at the Porziuncola in 1226. He was canonised within two years. The Basilica of Saint Francis in Assisi, decorated with frescoes attributed to Giotto, is a UNESCO site and major pilgrimage destination. Pope Francis took his name in 2013, the first pope to do so.
