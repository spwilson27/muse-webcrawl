William Joseph Seymour (May 2, 1870 – September 28, 1922) was an American holiness preacher who initiated the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles in 1906, the event most often credited with launching the worldwide Pentecostal movement.

Early life

Seymour was born in Centerville, Louisiana, to formerly enslaved parents. He was blinded in one eye by smallpox as a young man. While working as a waiter in Indianapolis and Cincinnati he was drawn to holiness teaching, and in Houston, Texas, he sat outside the segregated classroom of Charles Fox Parham, listening through the doorway to Parham's teaching that speaking in tongues was the Bible evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit.

Azusa Street Revival

Invited to preach in Los Angeles in early 1906, Seymour was locked out of the church that had called him after preaching on tongues. Prayer meetings in a home on Bonnie Brae Street — and the outbreak of tongues there on April 9, 1906 — led to the move to a former stable and church building at 312 Azusa Street. For roughly three years the Azusa Street Mission held services day and night, drawing interracial crowds remarkable for the Jim Crow era and sending missionaries around the world. Its newspaper, The Apostolic Faith, reached tens of thousands of readers.

Later years

The revival waned after 1909 amid disputes, defections and the segregation of American religious life. Seymour continued to pastor the Azusa Street Mission on a smaller scale until his death from a heart attack in 1922. Modern Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity — now numbering hundreds of millions of adherents — traces much of its origin to his ministry.
