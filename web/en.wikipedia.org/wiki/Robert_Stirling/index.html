Robert Stirling

Robert Stirling
Born: 25 October 1790, Cloag, Perthshire, Scotland
Died: 6 June 1878, Galston, Ayrshire, Scotland
Occupation: Church of Scotland minister, inventor
Known for: Inventing the Stirling engine (hot air engine) and the regenerator ("economiser")

Robert Stirling (25 October 1790 – 6 June 1878) was a Scottish clergyman and inventor of the Stirling engine, a closed-cycle external combustion engine. He patented his "hot air engine" together with its key component, the regenerator, in 1816.

Life

Stirling was born at Cloag Farm near Methven in Perthshire and studied at the Universities of Edinburgh and Glasgow before being licensed as a minister of the Church of Scotland. He served as minister at Laigh Kirk, Kilmarnock, and from 1824 at Galston, where he remained for the rest of his life. Alongside his ministry he pursued engineering, encouraged by concern for the safety of workers exposed to dangerous steam boiler explosions of the era.

The engine

Stirling's 1816 patent described an engine in which air is heated and cooled in a closed cycle, passing through a "regenerator" (he called it an economiser) that stores heat from one part of the cycle and returns it in another, greatly improving efficiency. Working with his brother James, an engineer, he developed engines used industrially — including a 45-horsepower engine that drove machinery at the Dundee Foundry in the 1840s. The ideal thermodynamic cycle the engine approximates is called the Stirling cycle. Though hot air engines could not then compete with steam, the Stirling engine was revived in the 20th century for applications such as submarine propulsion, solar power, and cryocoolers.
