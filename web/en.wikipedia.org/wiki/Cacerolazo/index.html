Cacerolazo

TypeForm of protest
OriginChile, 1971
Named forCacerola (saucepan)

A cacerolazo (in Spanish: cacerolazo; also cacerolada) is a form of popular protest in which participants bang pots, pans, and other kitchen utensils to make noise and demonstrate discontent. It is particularly associated with Latin America and has been adopted in Spain, Iceland, Canada, and elsewhere.

The name derives from 'cacerola', a Spanish word for a saucepan or stew pot.

History

The first recorded cacerolazos took place in Chile in 1971 against food shortages under Salvador Allende, including the 'March of the Empty Pots'. The tactic was later used against the Pinochet dictatorship in the 1980s, sometimes from within homes to avoid repression.

Notable uses

Cacerolazos erupted across Argentina during the December 2001 crisis, forcing President Fernando de la Rúa's resignation. They featured in Iceland's 2009 'Kitchenware Revolution', Quebec's 2012 student protests against tuition hikes, Venezuelan protests, and Chile's 2019 social outburst.

Character

The cacerolazo is notable for allowing participation from balconies and kitchens, making it accessible to people who may not join street marches, and for turning a domestic object into a symbol of economic grievance.
