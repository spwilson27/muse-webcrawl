In building science, thermal mass is the property of a mass of material that enables it to store heat, providing "inertia" against temperature fluctuations. Materials with high thermal mass — such as concrete, brick, stone, tile, rammed earth, and water — absorb heat slowly when surroundings are hotter and release it when surroundings cool, flattening daily temperature swings inside a building. Thermal mass is sometimes called the thermal flywheel effect.

Properties

The effectiveness of a material as thermal mass depends on its specific heat capacity, density, and thickness; the product of the first two is the volumetric heat capacity. Water has an exceptionally high volumetric heat capacity (about 4.18 MJ/m³·K), roughly double that of concrete. Thermal mass is distinct from insulation: insulation resists heat flow, whereas thermal mass stores and re-radiates heat, and the two are combined in good design — insulation outside, mass inside.

Use in design

In passive solar design, sun-facing glazing admits winter sunlight onto interior mass floors and walls (as in a Trombe wall), which store the heat and release it at night; in hot-arid climates such as the desert Southwest, massive adobe walls absorb daytime heat and radiate it away to the cool night sky, a strategy used for millennia in vernacular architecture. Thermal mass is most effective where diurnal temperature swings are large; poorly placed mass in cold, cloudy climates can instead increase heating loads.
