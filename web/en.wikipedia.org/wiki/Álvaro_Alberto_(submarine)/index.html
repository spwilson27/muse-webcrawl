Álvaro Alberto (SN-10) is a nuclear-powered attack submarine under development for the Brazilian Navy under the PROSUB programme. When completed, Brazil will become the seventh country in the world to operate nuclear-powered submarines, and the first country without nuclear weapons to build one. The submarine is named after Admiral Álvaro Alberto da Motta e Silva (1889–1976), a pioneer of Brazil's nuclear science programme.

Development

Brazil's ambition to field a nuclear submarine dates to the 1970s, when the Navy began a long-term programme combining mastery of the nuclear fuel cycle with indigenous reactor development. The Navy's Technological Center in São Paulo (CTMSP) developed uranium enrichment centrifuges and built the land-based prototype reactor, LABGENE (Laboratório de Geração Nucleoelétrica), at Aramar. The broader PROSUB programme, launched in 2008 in partnership with France, produced four enlarged Scorpène-class conventional submarines (the Riachuelo class) and the shipyard and naval base complex at Itaguaí, along with the design of the nuclear boat. The conventional submarines serve partly as stepping stones in skills and design practice for the SN-10.

Design

The design takes the conventional Riachuelo-class boats as its point of departure but is considerably larger — reported at roughly 6,000 tonnes submerged displacement and about 100 metres in length — driven by a pressurised water reactor of domestic design (developed from the LABGENE prototype, approximately 48 MW thermal). Nuclear propulsion gives essentially unlimited submerged endurance, limited mainly by crew provisions. The submarine is intended for the defence of Brazil's offshore resources and the "Blue Amazon" (Amazônia Azul) maritime zone, and is armed with torpedoes and, reportedly, anti-ship missiles in place of the strategic weapons carried by other nations' SSNs; Brazil is a party to the Treaty of Tlatelolco and the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), so the boat is not nuclear-armed.

Challenges and schedule

The programme has experienced long delays from funding constraints, and its timetable has repeatedly slipped; the critical design phase was authorised in the early 2020s, with construction of the hull sections beginning at Itaguaí in the mid-2020s, and completion expected in the 2030s. The project has also attracted international attention regarding safeguards: arrangements for applying IAEA safeguards to naval nuclear fuel — the first such case for a non-nuclear-weapon state — have been the subject of negotiation between Brazil, the IAEA and ABACC.
