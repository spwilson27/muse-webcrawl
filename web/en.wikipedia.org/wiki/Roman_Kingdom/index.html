Roman Kingdom

The Roman Kingdom (Latin: Regnum Romanum) was the earliest period of Roman history, when the government of Rome and its territory was a monarchy. According to tradition, it began with the founding of Rome by Romulus in 753 BC and ended with the overthrow of the last king, Tarquinius Superbus, in 509 BC, after which the Roman Republic was established.

Kings of Rome

Roman tradition lists seven kings: Romulus, Numa Pompilius, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius and Tarquinius Superbus. The first four were of Latin and Sabine origin, while the last three were Etruscan, reflecting the growing influence of Etruria over the young city.

Government

The king (rex) held supreme military, religious and judicial authority, but was advised by the Senate, a council of elders drawn from the leading families (patres). The king was not strictly hereditary: he was nominated by an interrex and confirmed by the comitia curiata, an assembly of the people organized by curiae. The Senate's role grew over time, and its resentment of royal power contributed to the monarchy's fall.

Fall of the monarchy

According to Livy, the rape of Lucretia by Sextus Tarquinius, son of the king, provoked a revolt led by Lucius Junius Brutus. The Tarquins were expelled, and Rome replaced the kingship with two annually elected consuls. The office of rex sacrorum preserved the king's former religious duties in attenuated form.

Historiography

Modern scholars regard the regal period as semi-legendary: the seven kings may include historical figures, but the chronology and narrative details were shaped by later Roman storytelling. Archaeology confirms that Rome in the eighth to sixth centuries BC was a growing settlement of huts that gradually acquired monumental temples, drainage works such as the Cloaca Maxima, and the Forum.
