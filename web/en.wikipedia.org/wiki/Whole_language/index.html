Whole language

Whole language is a philosophy and instructional approach to teaching reading and language that emphasises learning to read through immersion in meaningful, authentic text, rather than through the explicit, systematic teaching of phonics and decoding rules. Popularised in the 1970s and 1980s by educators such as Ken Goodman and Frank Smith, it treats reading as a natural process analogous to learning to speak, in which children construct meaning from whole texts using semantic, syntactic, and graphophonic "cueing systems".

Whole language emphasises immersion in real books

Principles and practice

Classroom practice associated with whole language includes shared reading of "real" literature rather than basal readers, writing workshops, invented spelling in early writing, and literacy integrated across the curriculum. Goodman famously described reading as a "psycholinguistic guessing game", and adherents argued that breaking language into drills destroys motivation and meaning.

The reading wars

Whole language became one side of the prolonged "reading wars" against phonics-based instruction. A large body of cognitive-science research, summarised by the U.S. National Reading Panel in 2000, concluded that systematic phonics instruction benefits beginning readers, and that the three-cueing approach encouraged guessing rather than decoding. By the 2010s–2020s many jurisdictions had legislated against cueing-based methods in favour of "science of reading" curricula centred on phonics, though elements of whole language — rich literature, writing for real purposes — persist in "balanced literacy" programmes, themselves increasingly contested.
