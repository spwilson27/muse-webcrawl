Color constancy

Color constancy is an example of subjective constancy and a feature of the human color perception system which ensures that the perceived color of objects remains relatively constant under varying illumination conditions. A green apple, for instance, looks green in daylight and also at sunset when the main illumination is reddish orange. Color constancy helps us identify objects consistently.

Mechanism

The visual system estimates the colour of the illuminant and discounts it, so that perception reflects the reflectance of surfaces rather than the raw light reaching the eye. This involves adaptation of the cone photoreceptors in the retina (von Kries adaptation) and computations in the visual cortex, where neurons responsive to colour regardless of illumination have been found in area V4.

Algorithms

Edwin Land's retinex theory proposed that the visual system computes lightness by comparing responses across the visual scene. Computational algorithms that imitate constancy include white-point algorithms such as "gray world", which assumes the average colour of a scene is neutral, and "white patch" methods, which take the brightest patch as a reference. Such algorithms are used in digital cameras for white balance.

Failures and illusions

Color constancy is imperfect, and its failures produce notable phenomena. The 2015 viral image known as "the dress" divided viewers between those who saw it as blue and black and those who saw white and gold, reflecting individual differences in assumptions about the illumination. Checker-shadow illusions similarly exploit the visual system's constancy mechanisms.
