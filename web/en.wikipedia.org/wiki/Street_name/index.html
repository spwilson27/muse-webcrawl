Street name

A street name sign (nameplate) at an intersection.

A street name is an identifying label given to a street or road. In toponymic terminology, a street name is an odonym (from Greek hodos, "way"). A complete street name typically consists of a specific element (the name proper, such as "Oxford" or "Martin Luther King Jr.") and a generic element indicating the type of thoroughfare — Street, Avenue, Boulevard, Road, Lane, Drive, Court, and so on. In addressing, the street name combines with a house number, locality, and postal code.

Naming practices

Street names arise in many ways:

  • Descriptive — after the destination (London Road), terrain (Hill Street), or features along it (Mill Lane, Church Street).
  • Commemorative — after people (Washington Avenue), events, or battles.
  • Thematic — developers naming a subdivision's streets after trees, birds, poets, states, or other themes.
  • Numbered and lettered — grid systems such as Manhattan's numbered avenues and streets, or Washington, D.C.'s lettered and numbered grid.
  • Self-descriptive grids — sequences that encode location, as in Salt Lake City's coordinate-style addresses.

In many jurisdictions, municipal authorities approve new street names and regulate renaming, sometimes requiring that names not duplicate others nearby to avoid emergency-service confusion. Renamings can be politically charged, as when streets named for colonial or Confederate figures are renamed, or when cities add honorary secondary names (such as ceremonial brown signs) without changing the legal name.

Generic elements around the world

English-speaking countries use Street, Road, Avenue, Boulevard, Lane, Drive, Court, Place, Terrace, and Way, with conventions varying by country — in North America an "avenue" often runs perpendicular to "streets" in a grid. Other languages place the generic first (French rue, avenue; Spanish calle, avenida) or last (German Straße, compounded as in Hauptstraße; Dutch straat, laan).

History

Named streets date to antiquity — Roman roads bore names such as the Via Appia. Medieval street names usually described trades practiced there (Bread Street, Tenterden's "The Mint") or landmarks. Systematic house numbering and standardized naming arrived in the 18th and 19th centuries with postal services and city planning, including Haussmann's Paris and the Commissioners' Plan of 1811 for Manhattan.
