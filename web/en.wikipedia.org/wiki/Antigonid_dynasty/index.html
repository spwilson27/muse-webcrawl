The Antigonid dynasty was a Hellenistic royal house that ruled the kingdom of Macedon from 276 BC until the Roman conquest in 168 BC. It descended from Antigonus I Monophthalmus ("the One-Eyed"), one of the Diadochi — the generals who fought over the empire of Alexander the Great after his death in 323 BC.

Origins

Antigonus seized control of much of Alexander's Asian territory and in 306 BC was the first of the Successors to assume the royal title, alongside his son Demetrius Poliorcetes ("the Besieger"). Antigonus was killed at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC fighting a coalition of rivals, and Demetrius briefly held Macedon (294–288 BC) before losing it. The dynasty's durable hold on Macedon began with Demetrius's son Antigonus II Gonatas, who consolidated power after defeating a Galatian invasion and was recognised as king from 276 BC.

Rule over Macedon

From their capitals at Pella and Demetrias, the Antigonids maintained hegemony over much of Greece through garrisons at strategic points — the so-called "Fetters of Greece": Corinth, Chalcis and Demetrias. Notable kings include Antigonus II Gonatas, Demetrius II, Antigonus III Doson, and Philip V, who expanded Macedonian power but clashed with Rome.

Fall

Philip V's defeat by Rome at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) ended Macedonian dominance in Greece. His son Perseus fought Rome in the Third Macedonian War and was crushed at the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC, ending the dynasty and, after a brief puppet arrangement, leading to Macedon's annexation as a Roman province in 146 BC.
