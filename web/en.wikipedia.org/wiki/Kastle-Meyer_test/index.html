Kastle–Meyer test

The Kastle–Meyer test, also called the phenolphthalein test, is a presumptive forensic test for the presence of blood, first described in the early 1900s by the American chemists Joseph Hoeing Kastle and Erich Meyer. It is a standard first-line screening test at crime scenes: a positive result suggests blood but must be confirmed by further analysis, since the test can react with other substances.

Principle

Hemoglobin in blood has peroxidase-like activity. The Kastle–Meyer reagent contains phenolphthalin, the colourless reduced form of phenolphthalein, prepared by refluxing phenolphthalein with zinc in alkaline solution. A suspect stain is swabbed and treated first with the reagent (or ethanol), then with hydrogen peroxide. If blood is present, the iron in hemoglobin catalyses the peroxide's oxidation of phenolphthalin back to phenolphthalein, which turns bright pink in the alkaline medium almost instantly.

Limitations

The test is extremely sensitive — detecting blood diluted to about one part in ten million — but it is not specific: plant peroxidases (e.g. in horseradish and some vegetables), certain metal salts and chemical oxidants can give false positives. Conversely it cannot distinguish human from animal blood, so positives require confirmatory tests such as immunological assays or DNA profiling. The procedure consumes very little material and does not prevent subsequent DNA analysis.
