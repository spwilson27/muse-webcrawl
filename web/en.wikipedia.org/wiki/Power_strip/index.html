Power strip

A power strip (also known as a multi-socket extension, extension block, power board, or multiplug) is a block of electrical sockets that attaches to the end of a flexible cable, allowing multiple electrical devices to be powered from a single socket outlet. Power strips are widely used where wall outlets are insufficient — for example around desks, home entertainment systems, and workshops.

Features

Basic power strips simply split one outlet into several. More elaborate models add features such as:

  • Surge protection — components that clamp voltage spikes, protecting connected electronics.
  • Master switches — a single switch controlling all sockets, or individual switches per socket.
  • Overload protection — a fuse or circuit breaker that trips if the total load exceeds the strip's rating.
  • USB charging ports — for charging phones and tablets.
  • Master–slave ("smart") strips — slave sockets switch on/off in response to a device plugged into the master socket, reducing standby power.
  • EMI/RFI filtering — suppressing electrical noise for audio and computing equipment.

Safety considerations

Power strips have a maximum current rating, and exceeding it — for instance by plugging in space heaters, kettles or other high-wattage appliances — creates a fire risk. Safety authorities warn against "daisy-chaining" strips together or connecting them in ways that circumvent fuse ratings, and advise against using indoor strips in damp locations. Many power strips lack the safety features of permanently installed outlets such as ground-fault protection.

History and regulation

Extension multi-socket devices appeared in the early 20th century as domestic electrification spread; the modern form, a plastic bar with standardised sockets, became commonplace after the Second World War. Standards for power strips are set by bodies such as UL in the United States (UL 1363 for relocatable power taps) and national standards elsewhere, specifying construction, spacing, cord ratings and marking.
