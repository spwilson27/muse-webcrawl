Classical economics

[Image: title page of the 1776 first edition of The Wealth of Nations]

Classical economics is the school of economic thought that dominated from the late 18th to the late 19th century, founded by Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) and developed by David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, Jean-Baptiste Say and John Stuart Mill. The term was coined by Karl Marx and later popularised by John Maynard Keynes, who used it loosely for his predecessors.

Doctrine

Classical economists explained value chiefly by labour embodied in production, analysed distribution among the classes of landlords (rent), workers (wages) and capitalists (profit), and championed free markets, Free trade and limited government, against the mercantilist system of protection and monopoly. Say's law — that supply creates its own demand — anchored a belief that economies tend toward full employment, with gluts temporary. Malthus's population principle and Ricardo's diminishing returns gave the school a pessimistic streak about long-run living standards. The marginal revolution of the 1870s (Jevons, Menger, Walras) replaced classical value theory with marginal Utility and produced neoclassical economics, though classical themes survive in growth theory and in modern critiques of Macroeconomics orthodoxy.
