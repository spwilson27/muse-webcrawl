Defamation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Defamation is the communication of a false statement about another person that injures their reputation. In common-law systems it divides into libel (in permanent form, chiefly writing) and slander (spoken). A claimant generally must show a defamatory statement of and concerning them, published to a third party, causing or likely to cause serious harm; truth (justification) is a complete defence, and statements of honest opinion and matters of public interest are protected in varying degrees.

The law balances reputation against freedom of expression. In the United States, New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) requires public officials (later extended to public figures) to prove “actual malice” — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth — giving the press wide protection. English law, historically more claimant-friendly, was rebalanced by the Defamation Act 2013 (a serious-harm threshold and a public-interest defence); “libel tourism” and high costs remain debated. Online publication has complicated jurisdiction, repetition and the position of platforms as publishers.
