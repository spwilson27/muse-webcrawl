Phase velocity

The phase velocity of a wave is the rate at which a point of constant phase of the wave propagates through space. For a monochromatic wave with angular frequency ω and wavenumber k, the phase velocity is

vp = ω / k,

equivalently the wavelength λ divided by the period T. An observer surfing along with a particular crest or trough of the wave moves at the phase velocity.

Relation to group velocity

Phase velocity contrasts with group velocity, the speed at which the overall envelope — and with it information and energy in most cases — travels. In a non-dispersive medium the two are equal; in a dispersive medium, where ω is a nonlinear function of k, they differ, with group velocity given by dω/dk. For example, deep-water ocean waves are dispersive: the phase velocity of each component is twice its group velocity.

Faster than light

Phase velocity is not bounded by the speed of light: in plasmas and in waveguides above cutoff, and for de Broglie matter waves, the phase velocity can exceed c (for matter waves it equals c2/v). This does not violate special relativity, because a pure monochromatic wave carries no information; signals travel at the group (or front) velocity. In media with anomalous dispersion near an absorption line, the group velocity itself can exceed c or become negative, but the wave front still propagates at most at c.

Refractive index

For electromagnetic waves, phase velocity in a medium is c/n, where n is the refractive index; where n < 1, as for X-rays in matter, the phase velocity exceeds c while the group velocity remains below it.
