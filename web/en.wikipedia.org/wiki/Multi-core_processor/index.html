Multi-core processor

A multi-core processor is a computer processor on a single integrated circuit (or in a single package) with two or more separate processing units, called cores, each of which reads and executes program instructions. The cores share interfaces to the rest of the system — typically cache memory and the memory controller — and can run multiple threads of execution simultaneously, so that a suitably written program, or several programs at once, can work in true parallel.

History and motivation

Through the 1990s, processor performance grew mainly by raising clock speeds. Around 2004–2005 that strategy hit a "power wall": higher frequencies produced unsustainable heat and power consumption, and Dennard scaling broke down. Manufacturers therefore turned to parallelism on a chip. IBM's POWER4 (2001) was the first commercial dual-core microprocessor; AMD and Intel brought dual-core x86 chips to market in 2005, and core counts climbed through quad-core and hexa-core consumer parts to the dozens of cores found in modern server processors and the heterogeneous performance/efficiency core clusters of contemporary laptops and phones.

Architecture and software

Cores may share some or all cache levels, and hardware protocols (such as MESI) keep their caches coherent. Many cores also support simultaneous multithreading, presenting two logical processors per physical core. Exploiting multi-core hardware requires parallel software: operating systems schedule threads across cores, but sequential programs gain little, and Amdahl's law limits the speedup available from parallelising only part of a workload. Many-core designs — with tens or hundreds of simpler cores — extend the same idea in GPUs and specialised accelerators.
