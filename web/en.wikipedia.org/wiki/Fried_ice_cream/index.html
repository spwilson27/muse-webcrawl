Fried ice cream

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fried ice cream is a dessert made of a scoop of ice cream that is frozen hard, coated in batter or crumbs, and quickly deep-fried, creating a warm, crispy shell around the still-cold ice cream. The dish is associated in North America with Mexican restaurants — where it is often served in a fried tortilla bowl topped with cinnamon, whipped cream and honey — and versions of it also appear in Asian cuisines and at fairs and carnivals.

Fried ice cream: a crisp, hot crust around a still-frozen centre.

Preparation

The key to the dish is temperature contrast. Scoops of ice cream are frozen as hard as possible, then rolled in a coating — cornflake crumbs, cookie crumbs, coconut, or a tempura-style batter — and returned to the freezer. The coated scoops are then plunged into hot oil for only a few seconds, just long enough to crisp the exterior, and served immediately. Because the frying is so brief, the ice cream inside remains solid while the shell becomes golden and crunchy.

Origins

The origins of fried ice cream are disputed. References to ice cream encased in pastry and briefly cooked appear in the late 19th century, and a "fried ice cream" was reported at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair era; similar desserts were served in Philadelphia and in Chinese restaurants in the United States. In Japan, tempura ice cream is a long-standing restaurant dessert. Its modern American fame dates chiefly from the 1980s, when the Chi-Chi's restaurant chain popularised "Mexican fried ice cream", typically coated in cinnamon-sugar crumbs.

Variations

Variations include a batter of egg and crushed cornflakes, a shell of pound cake or sponge wrapped around the scoop before frying, and flambéed presentations. Fairground versions use deep-fried batter coatings similar to those on other novelty fried desserts.
