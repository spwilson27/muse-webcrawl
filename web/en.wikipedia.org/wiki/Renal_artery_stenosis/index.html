Renal artery stenosis

Renal artery stenosis (RAS) is the narrowing of one or both of the renal arteries, most often caused by atherosclerosis (90% of cases) or fibromuscular dysplasia. When the narrowing impairs blood flow to the kidney, it can cause renovascular hypertension and ischaemic nephropathy.

Causes

Atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis typically affects older patients and occurs at the ostium or proximal segment of the artery, in association with widespread vascular disease. Fibromuscular dysplasia is a non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory disease of the arterial wall, most often affecting younger women and typically the mid and distal renal artery, where it may produce a "string of beads" appearance on angiography. Rarer causes include vasculitis, dissection, neurofibromatosis and extrinsic compression.

Pathophysiology

Reduced perfusion pressure in the affected kidney activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, causing vasoconstriction and sodium retention and hence hypertension. With bilateral disease (or stenosis of a solitary kidney), volume retention predominates and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers can precipitate acute kidney injury.

Signs, diagnosis and treatment

Clues include hypertension resistant to three or more drugs, onset before 30 or after 55 years of age, an abdominal bruit, flash pulmonary oedema, and deterioration of renal function after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB. Diagnosis is by duplex ultrasound, CT angiography, MR angiography or catheter angiography. Treatment centres on medical therapy — antihypertensives, statins and antiplatelet agents — with percutaneous angioplasty and stenting reserved for selected cases such as fibromuscular dysplasia, recurrent flash pulmonary oedema or refractory hypertension; large trials (ASTRAL, CORAL) found no routine benefit of stenting over medical therapy in atherosclerotic disease.
