Roald Hoffmann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Roald Hoffmann (born Roald Safran; 18 July 1937) is a Polish-American theoretical chemist who won the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, shared with Kenichi Fukui, for theories concerning the course of chemical reactions. A longtime professor at Cornell University, he is also known as a poet, playwright, and popular writer on the philosophy and aesthetics of science.

Life

Hoffmann was born in Złoczów, Poland, to a Jewish family. During the Second World War he and his mother survived the Holocaust hidden by a Ukrainian schoolteacher, while his father was murdered by the Nazis. The family emigrated to the United States in 1949. He studied at Columbia University and earned his PhD at Harvard University in 1962 under William Lipscomb.

Scientific work

At Harvard, Hoffmann developed the extended Hückel method for molecular orbital calculations. Working with Robert Burns Woodward, he formulated the Woodward–Hoffmann rules (1965), which use orbital symmetry to predict whether pericyclic reactions are thermally allowed — work central to the 1981 Nobel Prize. His later research applied molecular orbital ideas to organometallic and solid-state chemistry, including the isolobal analogy.

Writing

Hoffmann has written extensively for general audiences, including The Same and Not the Same and Oxygen, a play co-written with Carl Djerassi about the discovery of oxygen. His poetry has appeared in literary journals, and he hosted the television series The World of Chemistry.
