The Oostvaardersplassen is a nature reserve in the Netherlands, managed by the Staatsbosbeheer (State Forestry Service). Covering about 56 square kilometres (22 sq mi) in the province of Flevoland, it is an experiment in rewilding: a polder wetland left to develop with minimal human intervention, populated by large herbivores introduced to approximate the grazing ecology of prehistoric Europe.

History

The reserve lies on land reclaimed from the IJsselmeer when the Southern Flevoland polder was drained in 1968. The area was originally earmarked for industry, but during construction a wet marsh developed spontaneously and attracted large numbers of birds, including greylag geese that used it as a moulting site. Recognising its ecological value, conservationists persuaded the government to designate it as a nature reserve. Rail and road routes were moved to accommodate the protected area.

Rewilding experiment

Under the influence of ecologist Frans Vera, large herbivores were introduced to maintain an open landscape through grazing: Heck cattle (introduced 1983) as proxies for the extinct aurochs, Konik horses (1984) as proxies for the extinct tarpan, and red deer (1992). With no natural predators, populations were regulated by food availability, and the reserve became the flagship — and later the controversy — of the Dutch rewilding movement. Vera's grazing-ecology theory proposed that large herbivores historically maintained a shifting mosaic of grassland, scrub, and woodland rather than closed-canopy forest.

Controversy and management changes

The hands-off policy became publicly controversial during harsh winters, notably in early 2018, when thousands of large herbivores starved and images of emaciated animals provoked protests and political debate. Following an advisory report, the provincial government adopted active population management, reducing red deer numbers to a target of about 490 animals and relocating or culling surplus horses and cattle, while supplementary feeding in severe weather also became policy. In 2018 the reserve was incorporated into the new Nationaal Park Nieuw Land.

Wildlife

The wetlands are of international importance for birds, hosting breeding white-tailed eagles (from 2006, the first in the Netherlands in centuries), great egrets, spoonbills, bitterns, marsh harriers, and large moulting flocks of greylag geese. Foxes, beavers (introduced), and a rich invertebrate fauna also inhabit the reserve.
