Kolmogorov microscales

The turbulent energy cascade ends at the Kolmogorov scale, where viscosity dissipates the motion as heat

The Kolmogorov microscales are the smallest scales of motion in a turbulent flow, introduced by Andrey Kolmogorov in his 1941 theory of turbulence. At these scales viscosity dominates inertia: the kinetic energy of the flow, cascaded down from large eddies through ever smaller ones, is finally converted to heat by viscous dissipation. The microscales are determined entirely by two quantities — the kinematic viscosity ν of the fluid and the mean rate of energy dissipation per unit mass ε.

η = (ν³/ε)1/4,   τη = (ν/ε)1/2,   vη = (νε)1/4

The three scales are constructed so that the Reynolds number formed from them is exactly one: vηη/ν = 1, expressing the balance of inertial and viscous forces at the smallest eddies. Kolmogorov's hypotheses hold that, for sufficiently high Reynolds number turbulence, the statistics of these smallest scales are universal — independent of the geometry that produced the flow — depending only on ν and ε.

Typical values

In the atmospheric boundary layer, η is typically around a millimetre; in laboratory wind tunnels it may be a few tenths of a millimetre, and in the ocean of the order of millimetres to centimetres. Because direct numerical simulation of turbulence must resolve motions down to η in all three dimensions, the required number of grid points grows roughly as Re9/4, which is why high-Reynolds-number flows remain computationally out of reach for exact simulation.

Context

The microscales close Kolmogorov's picture of the cascade: energy injected at the large integral scale L passes through an inertial range — where the famous −5/3 energy spectrum law applies — and is destroyed at η. The ratio of largest to smallest scale grows as Re3/4, so the breadth of the cascade increases rapidly with Reynolds number. A related, slightly larger scale, the Taylor microscale, sits between the integral scale and η but is not itself a physical eddy size.
