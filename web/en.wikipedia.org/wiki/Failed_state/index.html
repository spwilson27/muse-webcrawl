A failed state is a state that has lost its ability to fulfill fundamental security and development functions, lacking effective control over its territory and population. Common characteristics include a central government so weak or ineffective that it has little practical control over much of its territory, widespread corruption and criminality, involuntary movement of populations, and sharp economic decline. The level of government control required to avoid being considered "failed" varies considerably.

The term was first used extensively in the early 1990s. A state can be described as "failing" when it can no longer enforce its laws uniformly or provide basic services to its citizens. State failure is closely related to the concepts of state fragility and state collapse, and has been a central concern of international relations, development policy, and security studies since the end of the Cold War.

Definition

There is no single agreed definition of a failed state. Scholars such as Robert Rotberg have described failed states as polities where violence has become endemic, where the government has lost legitimacy among large segments of the population, and where public goods are directed at only a narrow clientele. Charles T. Call has criticised the concept itself, arguing that the label lumps together very different situations and encourages one-size-fits-all policy responses.

Max Weber's classic definition of the state requires a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a territory; when that monopoly is lost to warlords, paramilitary groups, or organised crime, the state may be said to have failed. Analysts also look at a state's capacity for governance: the ability to collect taxes, maintain infrastructure, run a court system, and deliver education and health care.

Measurement

The best-known quantitative measure is the Fragile States Index, produced annually since 2005 by the Fund for Peace and published in Foreign Policy magazine. It ranks 179 countries on twelve social, economic, and political indicators, including demographic pressures, refugees and internally displaced persons, group grievance, human flight, uneven economic development, poverty and economic decline, state legitimacy, public services, human rights and rule of law, the security apparatus, factionalised elites, and external intervention. Countries such as Somalia, Yemen, Syria, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo have consistently appeared at the most fragile end of the ranking.

The World Bank maintains a related list of "fragile and conflict-affected situations" used to guide concessional lending, and the OECD publishes a States of Fragility report.

Causes

Causes of state failure identified in the literature include colonial legacies that left artificial borders and weak institutions, corruption and predatory elites, ethnic or religious polarisation, economic shocks and dependence on a single commodity, protracted civil war, and foreign intervention. Weak states may enter a "conflict trap" in which low income and slow growth raise the risk of further conflict. Historical institutionalist accounts emphasise the difference between states that developed accountable institutions over centuries and states that were rapidly decolonised without them.

Consequences

State failure has consequences that spill across borders: refugee flows, regional instability, safe havens for terrorist and criminal networks, piracy, and the spread of infectious disease. These spillovers have made failed states a focus of international security policy, particularly after the September 11 attacks, when U.S. strategy documents began describing weak states as a greater danger than strong ones. Responses have included peacekeeping operations, state-building missions, and conditional development aid, with mixed records in places such as Afghanistan, Haiti, Liberia, and Sierra Leone.

Criticism of the concept

Critics argue the "failed state" label is vague, Eurocentric, and often used to legitimise intervention. Others note that many so-called failed states continue to function at local and regional levels through informal institutions, and that the binary of success and failure conceals wide variation in how states actually govern.
