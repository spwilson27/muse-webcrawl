The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme (KPCS) is an international certification scheme established in 2003 to prevent "conflict diamonds" (also known as blood diamonds) from entering the mainstream rough diamond market. It was set up under United Nations mandates to sever the link between the diamond trade and armed conflicts, notably in Sierra Leone, Angola, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Background

In the 1990s, rebel movements in several African states financed civil wars by selling rough diamonds mined in territory they controlled. Reports by the United Nations and the advocacy group Global Witness (whose 1998 report A Rough Trade drew wide attention) exposed the trade. UN Security Council sanctions targeted rebel diamond exports from Angola (1998) and Sierra Leone (2000), and the Fowler Report of 2000 documented sanctions-busting networks. In May 2000, diamond-producing states, the industry (through the newly formed World Diamond Council), and civil-society groups met in Kimberley, South Africa — the historic diamond-mining town — to design a certification system.

How it works

Under the scheme, each shipment of rough diamonds crossing an international border must be sealed in a tamper-resistant container and accompanied by a forgery-resistant Kimberley Process certificate stating that the diamonds are conflict-free. Participating countries may trade rough diamonds only with other participants and must enact national legislation, maintain internal controls, and submit production and trade statistics. The scheme is chaired on a rotating basis by a participating country and operates by consensus.

Criticism

The scheme has been criticised for its narrow definition of a conflict diamond — rough diamonds used by rebel movements, which excludes abuses by governments — for weak monitoring and enforcement, and for decisions such as certifying exports from Zimbabwe's Marange fields despite documented violence there. In December 2011 Global Witness, one of its founding NGOs, withdrew from the process, calling it inadequate. Supporters credit the scheme with sharply reducing the share of conflict diamonds in world trade from several percent in the 1990s to a small fraction, though such figures are contested.
