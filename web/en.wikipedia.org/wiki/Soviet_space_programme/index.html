Soviet space programme

The Soviet space programme (Russian: Космическая программа СССР) was the national space programme of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, active from the 1930s rocketry experiments until the dissolution of the USSR in 1991. Directed for its first two decades by chief designer Sergei Korolev, it achieved a series of historic firsts that opened the Space Race: the first intercontinental ballistic missile (R-7, 1957), the first artificial satellite (Sputnik 1, 1957), the first animal in orbit (Laika, 1957), the first human in space (Yuri Gagarin aboard Vostok 1, 1961), the first woman in space (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963), the first spacewalk (Alexei Leonov, 1965) and the first space stations (Salyut and Mir).

Interplanetary and lunar efforts

Soviet probes achieved the first impact on the Moon (Luna 2, 1959), the first photographs of the lunar far side (Luna 3), the first soft landings on the Moon, Venus and Mars, the first lunar rovers (Lunokhod) and the first robotic sample returns. The crewed lunar programme, built around the giant N1 rocket, was cancelled after four launch failures, and the United States won the race to land humans on the Moon in 1969.

Later years

From the 1970s the programme emphasised long-duration space stations, culminating in Mir (1986), and developed the Energia–Buran system, whose shuttle flew once uncrewed in 1988. The programme was famously secretive: failures were concealed, cosmonaut deaths (Komarov on Soyuz 1, the Soyuz 11 crew) acknowledged only partly, and Korolev's identity was a state secret until his death in 1966. After 1991 its infrastructure passed mainly to Russia.
