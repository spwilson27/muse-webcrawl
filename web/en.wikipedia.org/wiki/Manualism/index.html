Manualism is a method of education of deaf students using sign language and other manual modes of communication — fingerspelling, cued speech, and gestures — as the primary medium of instruction. It stands in contrast to oralism, which insists that deaf children be taught exclusively through speech and lip-reading and historically discouraged or banned signing in schools.

History

Manual education traces its origins to eighteenth-century France, where the Abbé Charles-Michel de l'Épée established a public school for the deaf in Paris and systematised instruction through signed French. His successor tradition influenced Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet, who, together with the deaf teacher Laurent Clerc, founded the first permanent American school for the deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1817, teaching through sign language. For much of the nineteenth century, manualism predominated in the United States, and deaf teachers were common in deaf schools.

Decline under oralism

The balance shifted after the Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf in Milan in 1880, which passed resolutions declaring the superiority of speech over signs. Oral methods spread worldwide; signing was suppressed in many classrooms, deaf teachers were displaced, and the academic achievement of deaf students often suffered, a pattern documented by later researchers.

Modern position

From the 1960s, linguistic research by William Stokoe and others established that sign languages are full natural languages, undermining the theoretical basis of pure oralism. Manual communication returned in new forms — Total Communication, and later bilingual-bicultural (Bi-Bi) education, in which a sign language is the language of instruction and the written (and, where feasible, spoken) majority language is taught as a second language. The manualism–oralism controversy continues in debates over cochlear implants and mainstreaming.
