Factoring (finance)

Factoring is a financial transaction and a type of debtor finance in which a business sells its accounts receivable (invoices) to a third party called a factor at a discount, in exchange for immediate cash.

Factoring
TypeDebtor finance, trade finance
PartiesSeller, factor, debtor
RelatedInvoice discounting, supply chain finance

Overview

Factoring differs from a bank loan: it is the sale of an asset rather than borrowing against it, and emphasis is on the value of the receivables and the debtor's creditworthiness rather than the seller's. It is widely used by small and growing firms to improve cash flow while waiting 30 to 90 days for customers to pay.

Types

In recourse factoring the seller retains the risk if the debtor does not pay; in non-recourse factoring the factor assumes that risk. Maturity factoring involves collection and credit protection without an advance. Export factoring supports international trade.

History

Precursors existed in ancient Mesopotamia and in medieval trade, and factoring grew in the textile and garment industries in England and the United States, becoming a large global industry in the twentieth century.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include faster access to working capital and outsourced credit control; disadvantages include cost, loss of direct customer contact, and possible signalling of financial weakness.
