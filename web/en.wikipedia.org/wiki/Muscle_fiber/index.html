Muscle fiber

A muscle fiber is a single muscle cell. In skeletal muscle, fibers are long, cylindrical, multinucleated cells formed by the fusion of myoblasts during development; each contains bundles of myofibrils built from repeating contractile units called sarcomeres, whose actin and myosin filaments slide past one another to produce contraction. Muscle fibers are bundled into fascicles, wrapped in connective tissue layers (endomysium around each fiber, perimysium around each fascicle).

Fiber types

Skeletal muscle fibers are traditionally classified by contraction speed and metabolic profile:

  • Type I (slow-twitch, oxidative) fibers contract slowly, are rich in mitochondria and myoglobin, resist fatigue, and predominate in endurance activities and postural muscles.
  • Type II (fast-twitch) fibers contract rapidly and powerfully but fatigue more quickly; subtypes IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic) and IIx (fast glycolytic) differ in oxidative capacity.

The proportions of fiber types vary between muscles and individuals and can shift modestly with training, aging, and disease.

Other muscle tissues

Cardiac muscle fibers are branched, joined by intercalated discs, and usually single-nucleated; smooth muscle fibers are spindle-shaped and non-striated, found in the walls of organs and blood vessels.
