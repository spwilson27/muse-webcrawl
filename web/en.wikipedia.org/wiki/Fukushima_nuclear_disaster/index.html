Fukushima nuclear accident

The Fukushima nuclear accident began on 11 March 2011 at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant in Ōkuma, Fukushima, Japan. It was the most severe nuclear accident since Chernobyl in 1986, and the only other accident rated Level 7 on the International Nuclear Event Scale.


Damaged reactors at Fukushima Daiichi

Cause

The accident was triggered by the Tōhoku earthquake and the tsunami that followed it. The earthquake caused the operating reactors to shut down automatically, but the tsunami, with waves of roughly 14–15 metres at the plant, flooded the site and disabled the emergency diesel generators and electrical switchgear. The loss of all AC power led to a station blackout: cooling to Units 1–3 failed, fuel overheated and melted, and hydrogen explosions destroyed the outer reactor buildings of Units 1, 3 and 4. Radioactive material was released to the atmosphere and the Pacific Ocean.

Consequences

Around 150,000 people were evacuated from the surrounding area; no deaths from radiation exposure among the public were identified, though more than 2,000 disaster-related deaths were recognised among evacuees, chiefly elderly people. Decommissioning of the plant is expected to take decades. In 2023, Japan began releasing treated water (processed by the ALPS system to remove most radionuclides except tritium) into the Pacific, a plan endorsed by the IAEA but opposed by China and some fishing communities.

Investigations

Investigations by the Japanese Diet and the operator TEPCO concluded the accident was foreseeable and preventable, citing failures to anticipate a tsunami of the size that occurred and regulatory capture. The accident prompted stress tests and shutdowns of reactors worldwide and accelerated Germany's nuclear phase-out.
