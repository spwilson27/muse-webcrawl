Venice (Italian: Venezia) is a city in northeastern Italy built on a group of 118 small islands in the Venetian lagoon, separated by canals and linked by over 400 bridges. For a thousand years it was the capital of the Republic of Venice — La Serenissima — a maritime empire and the richest trading city of medieval and Renaissance Europe.

History

Founded by refugees from the mainland invasions of the fifth century, Venice grew rich between East and West: its merchants traded Byzantine silk and Levantine spices, its Arsenal mass-produced war galleys, and its doges ruled an empire of Adriatic and Aegean ports (see Balkan Peninsula). St Mark's Basilica, the Doge's Palace, and the art of Bellini, Giorgione, Titian, Tintoretto and Veronese made it a capital of beauty as of trade. Decline came with the Atlantic trade shift and Ottoman pressure; Napoleon ended the republic in 1797.

Modern Venice

Venice is now both a marvel and a warning: some 20–30 million visitors a year overwhelm a historic-centre population that has fallen below 50,000, while acqua alta flooding — worsened by subsidence and sea-level rise (see climate) — is held back since 2020 by the MOSE barrier system. The Biennale (art since 1895, architecture, film — the world's oldest film festival) keeps it a living cultural capital, twinned in lagoon-fragility with Geneva's very different shore and in fame with every city that trade once made.
