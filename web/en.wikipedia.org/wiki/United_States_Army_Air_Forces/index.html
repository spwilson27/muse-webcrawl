The United States Army Air Forces (USAAF or AAF) was the major land-based aerial warfare service component of the United States Army and the direct predecessor of the United States Air Force. Created on 20 June 1941 from the Army Air Corps, the USAAF grew during the Second World War into the largest air force in history, reaching a peak of about 2.4 million personnel in March 1944.

Second World War

Under its commanding general, Henry H. "Hap" Arnold, the USAAF enjoyed substantial autonomy within the Army and ran its own logistics, training, and procurement. Its numbered air forces fought in every theatre: the Eighth and Fifteenth Air Forces conducted the daylight strategic bombing campaign against Germany from England and Italy; the Fifth, Seventh, and Thirteenth Air Forces fought across the Pacific; the Twentieth Air Force's B-29 Superfortresses firebombed Japanese cities and dropped the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. Air Transport Command operated a global airlift network, and the Women Airforce Service Pilots (WASP) ferried aircraft domestically.

Dissolution

The war record of the USAAF made the case for an independent air service irresistible. Under the National Security Act of 1947, most USAAF personnel and assets were transferred to the newly created United States Air Force on 18 September 1947, and the Army Air Forces were formally abolished. The USAAF's wartime model of centralized control of airpower remains foundational to American air doctrine.
