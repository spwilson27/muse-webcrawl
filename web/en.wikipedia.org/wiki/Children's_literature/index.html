Children's literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A shelf of beloved children's classics: a bear in a blue coat, a very hungry caterpillar, a boy wizard and a wardrobe door ajar with lamplight beyond]

Children's literature or juvenile literature includes stories, books, magazines and poems that are created for children. It emerged as a distinct category in the 18th century, with books such as John Newbery's A Little Pretty Pocket-Book (1744), and flowered in the 19th-century 'golden age' of the Brothers Grimm's and Andersen's fairy tales, Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Treasure Island.

The 20th century added picture books (The Tale of Peter Rabbit, Where the Wild Things Are), the fantasy of C. S. Lewis and Tolkien, Roald Dahl's subversive comedies and, from 1997, J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter books — the best-selling book series in history — which reshaped global publishing for young readers. Major honours include the Newbery and Caldecott Medals and the Carnegie Medal.
