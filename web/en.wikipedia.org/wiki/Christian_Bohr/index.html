Christian Harald Lauritz Peter Emil Bohr (14 February 1855 – 3 February 1911) was a Danish physician and physiologist, professor at the University of Copenhagen. He is best known for describing the Bohr effect — the influence of carbon dioxide on haemoglobin's oxygen-binding — and for pioneering the quantitative study of respiration. He was the father of the physicist Niels Bohr and the mathematician Harald Bohr.

Life and career

Bohr studied medicine at the University of Copenhagen, taking his degree in 1880 and a doctorate in 1881 with a thesis on milk fat globules. He worked in Leipzig under the physiologist Carl Ludwig, a leader in experimental physiology, and became professor of physiology at Copenhagen in 1890. His Copenhagen home was a gathering place for scientists and intellectuals, and his sons grew up in that milieu.

Research

The Bohr effect

In 1904 Bohr, together with Karl Hasselbalch and August Krogh, showed that increasing carbon dioxide concentration in blood reduces haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, shifting the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve to the right. This Bohr effect means that in metabolically active tissues, where CO₂ is abundant, haemoglobin releases oxygen more readily, while in the lungs the loss of CO₂ promotes oxygen uptake. It remains a cornerstone of respiratory physiology.

Lung function and dead space

Bohr developed an equation for computing physiological dead space — the portion of each breath that does not participate in gas exchange — which is still used in clinical and exercise physiology as the Bohr equation. He also investigated lung volumes and gas exchange and debated with John Scott Haldane about whether the lungs actively secrete oxygen, arguing (with Krogh) that diffusion alone suffices to explain pulmonary gas exchange.

Family and legacy

Bohr married Ellen Adler in 1881. Their sons Niels and Harald became, respectively, a Nobel Prize-winning founder of quantum theory and a noted mathematician and Olympic footballer. Christian Bohr's careful, measurement-driven approach to physiology influenced the environment in which his sons' scientific careers took shape. He died suddenly in 1911, at the height of his academic career.
