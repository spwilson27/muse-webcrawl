Pincer movement

The pincer movement, or double envelopment, is a military manoeuvre in which forces simultaneously attack both flanks of an enemy formation. The attacking forces close behind the enemy like the arms of a pincer, encircling them and cutting off their retreat and supply lines, with the aim of destroying the trapped force.

History

The classic example is the Battle of Cannae (216 BC), where Hannibal's Carthaginian army enveloped and annihilated a much larger Roman force in one of the most studied battles in military history. Double envelopments featured in the campaigns of Alexander the Great, and in the German Vernichtungsgedanke (concept of annihilation) that produced the Schlieffen Plan. In the Second World War, large-scale encirclements (Kesselschlachten, "cauldron battles") occurred on the Eastern Front, including the Soviet encirclement of the German Sixth Army at Stalingrad in Operation Uranus (1942).

Conditions and risks

A successful pincer requires mobility, coordination, and an enemy whose centre holds or advances into the trap. The manoeuvre is risky: the enveloping arms may themselves be cut off or defeated in detail, and the attacker must supply forces operating around the enemy's rear. A force threatened by envelopment may respond by withdrawing, attacking one arm, or forming a defensive perimeter.
