Arachidonic acid (ARA) is a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid with 20 carbon atoms and four cis double bonds (20:4 ω-6). It is present in the phospholipids of cell membranes throughout the body — and is especially abundant in the brain, muscles, and liver — and is the principal precursor of the eicosanoids, a family of potent local signalling molecules that includes prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes. Despite its name (from the Latin arachis, peanut), arachidonic acid is not found in significant amounts in peanut oil.

Biochemistry

Arachidonic acid is stored esterified in membrane phospholipids and is not normally free in the cytosol. When cells are activated — by hormones, cytokines, tissue injury, or other stimuli — the enzyme phospholipase A2 cleaves it from the membrane, launching the "arachidonic acid cascade". The free fatty acid is then metabolised along three main enzymatic pathways:

  • Cyclooxygenases (COX-1 and COX-2) convert it to prostaglandins and thromboxanes, which mediate pain, fever, inflammation, blood clotting, and protection of the stomach lining. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen work by inhibiting these enzymes; aspirin does so irreversibly.
  • Lipoxygenases produce leukotrienes and lipoxins, important in asthma and allergic inflammation and in the resolution of inflammation, respectively.
  • Cytochrome P450 enzymes produce epoxyeicosatrienoic acids and hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (such as 20-HETE), which influence blood pressure and renal function.

In the brain, arachidonic acid is also a precursor of the endocannabinoid anandamide (via N-arachidonoylethanolamine), linking it to neuromodulation, appetite, and mood.

Diet and synthesis

Humans obtain arachidonic acid directly from animal foods (meat, eggs, dairy) and can synthesise it from the essential fatty acid linoleic acid through desaturation and elongation steps (linoleic acid → γ-linolenic acid → dihomo-γ-linolenic acid → arachidonic acid), though conversion is inefficient. Because ARA is important for infant brain and visual development, it is added (together with DHA) to many infant formulas, typically sourced from fungal oil of Mortierella alpina. It is also sold as a bodybuilding supplement, where it is claimed — with limited and contested evidence — to amplify muscle protein synthesis following resistance training.
