Price index

A price index is a normalised average — typically a weighted average — of prices for a basket of goods and services, expressed relative to a base period conventionally set to 100. Price indices measure how the general level of prices changes over time and are the standard basis for calculating inflation. The best-known examples are the consumer price index (CPI), the producer price index (PPI) and the GDP deflator.

Index formulas

Different formulas weight the basket differently. A Laspeyres index uses base-period quantities, asking what the old basket costs at new prices; it tends to overstate inflation because it ignores consumers substituting away from items that become dearer. A Paasche index uses current-period quantities and tends to understate inflation. The Fisher index, the geometric mean of the two, is theoretically preferred and is used in chained form by some statistical agencies — the US personal consumption expenditures price index, for example, is a chained Fisher-type index. Chained indices update their weights every period, limiting substitution bias but complicating comparisons across long spans.

Major indices

Consumer price indices track the cost of a representative household basket and are used to index wages, pensions, tax brackets and inflation-linked bonds, and by central banks as inflation targets. Producer price indices measure prices received by domestic producers and can foreshadow consumer inflation. The GDP deflator covers all domestically produced goods and services and is the broadest measure, though published less frequently. Asset prices and wages have their own indices, from house price indices to employment cost indices.

Problems of measurement

Constructing an index raises persistent difficulties: quality changes (a faster computer at the same price represents a price fall, handled by hedonic adjustment), new products entering the basket late, outlet substitution as shoppers move to discounters, and unrepresentative baskets for particular groups — pensioners and students face different inflation than the average household. The 1996 Boskin Commission estimated that the US CPI overstated inflation by about 1.1 percentage points a year, prompting methodological reforms; statistical offices periodically rebase and revise their baskets to keep indices representative.
