Occident

The Occident is a term for the West, traditionally comprising the countries of Western Europe and, by extension, the Americas, Australia and New Zealand. It is contrasted with the Orient, the East. The word derives from the Latin occidens, "setting" or "falling", referring to the direction in which the sun sets.

Etymology and usage

Latin occidens is the present participle of occidere, "to fall down, set (of the sun)". The paired term Orient comes from oriens, "rising". In Roman usage the words could denote the western and eastern parts of the empire, and later the Western (Latin) and Eastern (Greek) churches, a division formalised in the East–West Schism of 1054.

Historical development

During the Cold War, "the West" acquired a political meaning, denoting the capitalist democracies aligned with the United States against the communist East. In modern usage, Occident is less common than "the West" in English, though related words remain current in other languages, such as French Occident and German Abendland ("evening land").

In scholarship

The contrast between Occident and Orient has been examined critically, most famously in Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), which argued that Western scholarship constructed an essentialised image of the East.
