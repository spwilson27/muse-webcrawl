Close-in weapon system

A close-in weapon system (CIWS) is a point-defense weapon system for detecting and destroying short-range incoming threats that have penetrated a ship's outer defenses, principally anti-ship missiles and aircraft. Typically mounted on naval vessels, a CIWS combines radar, a high-rate-of-fire automatic cannon or short-range missiles, and autonomous fire control in a single mounting, able to search for, track, and engage targets with little or no human intervention.

Gun systems

The best-known gun CIWS is the American Phalanx, recognizable by its white cylindrical radome, which houses search and tracking radars above a 20 mm M61 Vulcan rotary cannon firing up to 4,500 rounds per minute at targets within roughly 1.5–2 km. Other gun systems include the Dutch Goalkeeper, which pairs a 30 mm seven-barrel cannon with separate surveillance and tracking radars; the Russian AK-630 and the twin-mount Kashtan, which combines guns and missiles; and China's Type 730 and Type 1130 systems.

Missile systems

Missile-based CIWS trade the gun's very short engagement window for longer reach. Examples include the American RIM-116 Rolling Airframe Missile, developed jointly with Germany, and the SeaRAM variant, which fits RAM missiles into a Phalanx mount; Russia's Pantsir-M and various short-range naval surface-to-air missiles fill a similar role. Laser-based CIWS, such as the U.S. Navy's LaWS demonstrator, have been tested as a low-cost-per-shot alternative.

Limitations

Gun CIWS have a very limited effective range, meaning a fast sea-skimming or supersonic missile can be engaged only in the last seconds of flight, and even a successful intercept may shower the ship with debris. Consequently CIWS is regarded as a last line of defense within a layered air-defense system, and saturation attacks remain a serious threat.
