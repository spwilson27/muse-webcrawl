Louisiana Voodoo

Louisiana Voodoo (French: Vaudou louisianais), also known as New Orleans Voodoo, is an African diasporic religion that originated in Louisiana, primarily in the city of New Orleans. It developed from the traditions of the West and Central African peoples enslaved and brought to French and Spanish colonial Louisiana, blending African spiritual practices with Roman Catholicism and, later, influences from Haitian Vodou. Its best-known historical practitioner is the 19th-century "Voodoo Queen" Marie Laveau.

History

Enslaved Africans arrived in Louisiana under French rule in the early 18th century, many of them Senegambians and, under Spanish rule, people from the Kongo region. Their religious practices were sustained partly through Sunday gatherings in New Orleans's Congo Square, where drumming and dancing were permitted. After the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), thousands of refugees from Saint-Domingue — white colonists, free people of colour and enslaved people — settled in New Orleans in 1809–1810, strengthening Caribbean Vodou influences and roughly doubling the city's population.

During the 19th century, prominent "Voodoo Queens" and "Doctors," most famously Marie Laveau (1801–1881) and her daughter of the same name, led public ceremonies, notably the St. John's Eve rituals on Bayou St. John, and served clients across racial lines with charms, healing and divination. In the 20th century, sensational press coverage and tourism reshaped the public image of Voodoo, while a revival movement from the 1970s onward sought to practise it as an organised religion with temples and ordained priesthoods.

Beliefs and practices

Louisiana Voodoo is monotheistic in orientation, centred on a remote creator God, with devotion directed to spirits (loa) who mediate between God and humanity, often identified with Catholic saints — for instance, the serpent spirit Li Grand Zombi is associated with Saint Patrick. Devotees maintain altars with candles, offerings of food, drink and flowers, and seek the spirits' help for healing, protection and success. Other characteristic practices include the making of gris-gris (amulet bags), spiritual baths, drumming and dance, and, historically, rituals involving snakes. Practices vary widely, and the tradition has no central authority or fixed creed.

Relationship to other traditions

Louisiana Voodoo is closely related to Haitian Vodou but developed its own local character, with heavier emphasis on gris-gris, rootwork and individual practitioners; it is distinct from, but often intertwined with, the African American folk-magic tradition of hoodoo. Since the late 20th century, revivalist communities in New Orleans have reconnected with Haitian and West African sources.
