Georges Seurat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–86): Parisians at leisure on the Seine, rendered in tiny distinct touches of colour]

Georges Pierre Seurat (2 December 1859 – 29 March 1891) was a French Post-Impressionist painter, the founder of Neo-Impressionism. Drawing on contemporary colour theory, he developed the technique of Pointillism — painting in small, distinct dots of pure colour intended to fuse in the viewer's eye — and applied it to monumental modern-life canvases built up over dozens of preparatory studies.

His masterpiece, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–86), now in the Art Institute of Chicago, was the sensation of the last Impressionist exhibition of 1886. Other major works include Bathers at Asnières (1884), Le Bec du Hoc, Grandcamp, A Sunday on La Grande Jatte's pendant studies, and the late, unfinished Le Cirque (1891).

The scientific method

Trained at the École des Beaux-Arts, Seurat steeped himself in the writings of Chevreul and Ogden Rood on simultaneous contrast and optical mixture, believing painting could be placed on a rational, almost scientific footing. With Paul Signac he founded the Société des Artistes Indépendants and its jury-free Salon in 1884. Working slowly and methodically, he produced only seven large paintings before his sudden death at 31, probably from meningitis or pneumonia.

Influence

Though his career lasted barely a decade, Seurat's example transformed younger painters: the Fauves, the young Picasso and the Italian Futurists all passed through Neo-Impressionism. La Grande Jatte later inspired Sondheim's musical Sunday in the Park with George (1984).
