Equation of exchange

In monetary economics, the equation of exchange is the relation:

M · V = P · Q

where, for a given period:

  • M is the total nominal amount of money supply in circulation on average in an economy during the period;
  • V is the velocity of money, that is, the average frequency with which a unit of money is spent;
  • P is the price level;
  • Q is an index of the real value of final expenditures, often represented by real output or real GDP.

Thus P · Q is the level of nominal expenditures over the period. The equation is an identity: it holds by definition because both sides measure the same total spending, once in terms of money changing hands and once in terms of goods and services purchased.

History

The equation was stated in various forms by early economists, but its best-known formulation appears in Irving Fisher's The Purchasing Power of Money (1911), which used the form MV + M′V′ = PT, including bank deposits (M′) and the volume of trade (T). Fisher's version underpins the quantity theory of money.

Quantity theory of money

The equation of exchange becomes a theory rather than an identity when assumptions are added. Monetarists, notably Milton Friedman, argued that velocity is stable or predictable in the long run and that output is determined by real factors, so sustained changes in the money supply translate mainly into changes in the price level — that is, inflation.

Criticism

Critics, including Keynesian economists, note that velocity is not constant in the short run: it can fall when money demand rises, so increases in M need not produce proportional increases in nominal spending. The sharp fall in measured velocity during and after the 2008 financial crisis is frequently cited in this debate.
