The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) is a volcano observatory on the island of Hawaii operated by the United States Geological Survey. It monitors six Hawaiian volcanoes — Kīlauea, Mauna Loa, Hualālai, Mauna Kea, Haleakalā, and Lōʻihi — issuing forecasts and warnings of eruptions and related hazards such as lava flows, ashfall, and volcanic gas emissions.

History

HVO was founded in 1912 by geologist Thomas A. Jaggar of MIT, inspired by the catastrophic eruptions of Mount Pelée and La Soufrière in 1902 to pursue the systematic study of volcanoes so that disasters could be foreseen. It is one of the oldest volcano observatories in the world and pioneered techniques of volcano monitoring, including seismometry, ground-deformation measurement, and gas geochemistry. Administration passed to the USGS in 1935 (via earlier federal arrangements).

Work

The observatory maintains dense networks of seismometers, GPS stations, tiltmeters, webcams, and gas sensors on Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, publishing daily status reports and alert levels under the USGS volcano alert-level system. HVO scientists monitored the long 1983–2018 eruption of Kīlauea, the 2018 summit collapse and rift eruption, and the November 2022 eruption of Mauna Loa, the volcano's first in 38 years.

Facilities

HVO's historic headquarters stood on the rim of Kīlauea caldera at Uēkahuna Bluff beside the Jaggar Museum. The buildings were severely damaged by earthquakes during the 2018 eruption and abandoned; the observatory relocated to Hilo while a replacement facility within Hawaii Volcanoes National Park is planned.
