Grand design spiral galaxy

A grand design spiral galaxy is a type of spiral galaxy with prominent and well-defined spiral arms, as opposed to multi-arm and flocculent spirals which have a patchier, less organised structure. Grand design spirals typically have two symmetric spiral arms that extend across much of the galactic disc and can be traced clearly from the central region outward.

Characteristics

Only about ten percent of spiral galaxies are of the grand design type. Their arms are sites of intense star formation, traced by bright blue young stars, glowing H II regions, and dark dust lanes along the inner edges of the arms. Classic examples include the Whirlpool Galaxy (M51) and the Southern Pinwheel Galaxy (M83).

Origin of the structure

The leading explanation for grand design structure is the density wave theory, proposed by C. C. Lin and Frank Shu in the 1960s, in which the spiral pattern is a quasi-stationary wave of compression rotating through the disc more slowly than the stars themselves. In many grand design galaxies, however, the pattern is driven by tidal interaction with a companion galaxy — as in M51, whose arms were likely sharpened by the passage of NGC 5195 — or by a central bar that organises the disc's response.
