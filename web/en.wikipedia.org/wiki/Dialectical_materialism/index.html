Dialectical materialism

Dialectical materialism is a philosophical approach to reality derived from the writings of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. Marx defined dialectical method as the opposite of the idealist philosophy of Hegel: where Hegel saw change as driven by ideas, Marx held that material conditions — above all the economic base of society — drive historical development. Dialectical thought views change and development as resulting from internal contradictions within phenomena.

Concepts

The classical codification, associated with Georgi Plekhanov and later Soviet textbooks, presents three laws of dialectics drawn from Engels's Dialectics of Nature: the unity and struggle of opposites; the transformation of quantity into quality (and vice versa); and the negation of the negation. Matter, in this view, is primary; consciousness is a product of highly organised matter.

Dialectical materialism is distinguished from historical materialism, which applies the materialist outlook to the study of society and history — though in Soviet usage the two were treated as parts of a single doctrine ("diamat"), which served as the official philosophy of the USSR and other Marxist–Leninist states.

Criticism

Critics argue that the "laws" of dialectics are too vague to be falsifiable, and that dialectical materialism as an official state doctrine stifled philosophy and science, most notoriously in Lysenkoism.
