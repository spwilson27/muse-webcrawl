Culture of Japan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

The culture of Japan has evolved over millennia, from the country's prehistoric Jōmon period to its contemporary form, combining influences from Asia and the West. Long periods of isolation under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868) preserved distinctive traditions, while the Meiji Restoration and post-1945 development modernized the country and exported Japanese popular culture worldwide.

Traditional arts

Japan is known for the tea ceremony (chanoyu), calligraphy (shodō), flower arranging (ikebana), woodblock prints (ukiyo-e), Noh and Kabuki theatre, bunraku puppetry, haiku poetry and ink painting. Aesthetics such as wabi-sabi — the beauty of imperfection and impermanence — and mono no aware pervade the arts. Martial arts including judo, karate, kendo and aikido developed from samurai traditions.

Religion and customs

Shinto and Buddhism have coexisted for over a thousand years and shape festivals (matsuri), shrine and temple visits, and life-cycle rituals. Social customs emphasize etiquette, hierarchy, honorific language (keigo), bowing, gift-giving and group harmony.

Contemporary culture

Post-war Japan created globally influential popular culture: manga and anime, video games, J-pop, karaoke, and the "Cool Japan" phenomenon of fashion, cuisine (sushi, ramen) and design. Japanese cinema, from Kurosawa to Studio Ghibli, enjoys worldwide acclaim.
