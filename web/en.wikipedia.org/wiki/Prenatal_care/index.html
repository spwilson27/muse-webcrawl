Prenatal care

Prenatal care, also known as antenatal care, is a type of preventive healthcare provided during pregnancy. Its goal is to provide regular check-ups that allow doctors or midwives to treat and prevent potential health problems throughout the course of the pregnancy and to promote healthy lifestyles that benefit both mother and child. Prenatal care began in its modern form in the early 20th century and is associated with sharp declines in maternal and infant mortality.

Components

Typical prenatal care includes monthly visits during the first two trimesters, fortnightly visits from around 28 to 36 weeks, and weekly visits until delivery. Visits commonly involve measuring blood pressure and weight, urine testing, blood tests for anaemia, infections and blood group, fundal height measurement, and fetal heartbeat checks.

Ultrasound scans are used to confirm gestational age, screen for anomalies (around 18–20 weeks), and monitor growth. Screening is also offered for conditions such as Down syndrome, gestational diabetes, and pre-eclampsia. Folic acid supplementation before and during early pregnancy greatly reduces the risk of neural tube defects.

Global variation

The World Health Organization recommends a minimum of eight antenatal contacts. Access and quality vary widely; in low-income countries fewer visits and shortages of skilled providers contribute to higher maternal mortality. Group prenatal care models, in which expectant parents meet together, have been adopted in some settings.
