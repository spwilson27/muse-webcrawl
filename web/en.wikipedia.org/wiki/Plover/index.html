Plovers are a group of wading birds belonging to the subfamily Charadriinae of the family Charadriidae. There are about 40 species, most in the genus Charadrius (now often split into several genera) and the closely related genera of dotterels. They are found worldwide on coasts, lakeshores, grasslands, tundra, and steppe, except for the deep polar regions and dense forests.

Description

Plovers are small to medium-sized birds, compact and short-necked, with short, straight bills, large eyes, and relatively long wings. Their plumage typically combines brown, grey, or black upperparts with white underparts and bold black facial or breast markings in the breeding season; many species are strongly patterned, while others are plain sandy-coloured for camouflage. Unlike sandpipers, which probe continuously, plovers hunt visually using a characteristic run-and-pause technique: they sprint a few steps, stop, and then peck at invertebrates—worms, molluscs, crustaceans, and insects—on the surface.

Breeding

Most plovers nest on the ground in a simple scrape, often with little or no lining, where their speckled eggs are well camouflaged. Chicks are precocial and can run and feed themselves soon after hatching; parents may perform distraction displays, such as the "broken-wing" act, to lure predators away. Several species are long-distance migrants, such as the American and Pacific golden-plovers, which fly thousands of kilometres between Arctic breeding grounds and southern wintering areas.

Conservation

A number of plovers have declined owing to habitat loss and human disturbance of nesting beaches. The piping plover of North America is listed as threatened or endangered under species-protection laws, and conservation measures include nest exclosures, beach closures during breeding, and predator management. The critically endangered spoon-billed sandpiper, though a sandpiper rather than a true plover, is often discussed alongside them.
