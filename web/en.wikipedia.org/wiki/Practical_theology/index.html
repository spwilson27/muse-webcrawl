Practical theology

Practical theology is an academic discipline that examines and reflects on religious practices in order to understand the theology that is enacted in those practices and to consider how theological theory and theological practices can be more fully aligned, changed, or improved. It has traditionally been one of the four branches of theological study in the university curriculum, alongside biblical studies, historical theology, and systematic theology.

History

The modern discipline is often traced to Friedrich Schleiermacher, who in his Brief Outline of Theology as a Field of Study (1811) described practical theology as the "crown" of theological study, concerned with the theory of church leadership and practice. In the twentieth century the field broadened well beyond clergy training to encompass the practices of all believers, and it developed empirical and ethnographic methods borrowed from the social sciences.

Method

Practical theologians commonly work with some version of a practice–theory–practice cycle: a concrete situation or practice is described thickly, analysed theologically and with tools from other disciplines, and the results are brought back to inform more faithful or effective practice. Richard Osmer's widely used model organises the work into four tasks: the descriptive-empirical ("What is going on?"), the interpretive ("Why is this going on?"), the normative ("What ought to be going on?"), and the pragmatic ("How might we respond?").

Subdisciplines

The field includes pastoral theology and pastoral care, homiletics (preaching), liturgics (worship), Christian education, missiology, congregational studies, spiritual formation, and public theology, among others.

Associations and journals

Scholars in the field are organised in bodies such as the International Academy of Practical Theology and publish in journals including the International Journal of Practical Theology and Practical Theology.
