Semitic languages

The Semitic languages are a branch of the Afroasiatic language family. They include Arabic, Hebrew, Aramaic, Amharic, Tigrinya, Maltese and many extinct languages such as Akkadian, Ugaritic, Phoenician and Ge'ez. As of the 2020s the Semitic languages are spoken by more than 450 million people. Many are written in abjad scripts in which vowels are secondary or omitted, and the languages are characterised by nonconcatenative (root-and-pattern) morphology, in which words are formed around consonantal roots, typically of three consonants.

Classification

The earliest attested Semitic language is Akkadian of Mesopotamia (from c. 2500 BCE), which with Eblaite forms the extinct East Semitic branch. West Semitic divides into Central Semitic — including Arabic and the Northwest Semitic languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, Ugaritic, Phoenician) — and South Semitic, comprising the modern South Arabian languages of Yemen and Oman and the Ethiosemitic languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea (Ge'ez, Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre, Harari).

Features

Semitic languages typically build words from triconsonantal roots: the root k-t-b ("write") yields Arabic kataba "he wrote", kitāb "book", and Hebrew kāṯaḇ "he wrote". Nouns and verbs are formed by interleaving vowel patterns and affixes with the root. Most Semitic languages have emphatic (pharyngealised or ejective) consonants and pharyngeal sounds.

History

Semitic languages originated in the Levant or Arabia and spread across the Near East and North Africa with the expansion of Islam after the 7th century CE, which carried Arabic from Spain to Persia. Hebrew, long preserved as a liturgical and literary language, was revived as a spoken vernacular in the late 19th and early 20th centuries and is now the language of the State of Israel.
