Solomon Asch

Solomon Eliot Asch (1907–1996) was a Polish-American Gestalt psychologist and a pioneer in social psychology. He is best known for his experiments on conformity in the 1950s, which demonstrated the power of group pressure on individual judgement.

Life

Asch was born in Warsaw, then part of the Russian Empire, and emigrated with his family to the United States in 1920, growing up on New York's Lower East Side. He studied at the College of the City of New York and Columbia University, where he earned his doctorate in 1932 and came under the influence of Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer. He taught at Brooklyn College and the New School for Social Research, spent two decades at Swarthmore College (1947–1966), and later worked at Rutgers University and the University of Pennsylvania.

Conformity experiments

In Asch's line-judgement experiments, a participant sat with a group of confederates and was asked which of three comparison lines matched a standard line in length. On critical trials the confederates unanimously gave a wrong answer. About 75% of participants conformed at least once, and on average roughly a third of responses on critical trials followed the incorrect majority — even though the correct answer was perceptually obvious. When even one confederate dissented, conformity fell sharply. The studies showed that people conform both to gain accurate information and, especially, to avoid standing apart from the group.

Other work

Asch also conducted influential research on impression formation, showing that certain "central" traits (such as warm versus cold) disproportionately shape how a person is perceived, and on prestige suggestion in attitude formation. His student Stanley Milgram carried out the famous obedience experiments.
