Cravat (early)

TypeNeckband / neckcloth
OriginCroatian mercenaries in French service, 17th century
MaterialsLinen, muslin, lace
DescendantsNecktie, ascot tie, bow tie, stock

The early cravat was a neckband worn by men in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the direct ancestor of the modern necktie. The word derives from the French cravate, a corruption of Croate ("Croat"), after the Croatian mercenary regiments in French service during the Thirty Years' War, whose distinctive linen neckcloths attracted attention at the court of Louis XIV.

History

Croatian soldiers wore knotted neckerchiefs of varying quality—plain linen for common soldiers, silk and lace for officers. The fashion spread through the French court in the 1660s, displacing the falling collar. Early cravats were long strips of fine white linen or muslin, sometimes edged with lace, tied loosely at the throat with the ends hanging down the chest. The king was said to employ a cravatier, an officer charged with caring for the royal cravats.

Styles

By the late seventeenth century, variants such as the Steinkirk (or Steinkerque), popularised after the Battle of Steenkerque in 1692, arranged the ends twisted and pulled through a buttonhole. In the eighteenth century the cravat evolved into the stock—a stiffened neckband buckled at the back—and later into the elaborate starched white cravats of the Regency period associated with Beau Brummell, before giving way to the modern tie in the later nineteenth century.

Legacy

The cravat survives today in formal dress as the ascot and in the name cravate still used in French for a necktie. Croatia marks Cravat Day on 18 October, celebrating the garment as a Croatian contribution to world fashion.
