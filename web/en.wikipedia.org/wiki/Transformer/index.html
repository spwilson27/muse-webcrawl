A transformer is a passive component that transfers electrical energy from one electrical circuit to another circuit, or multiple circuits, through electromagnetic induction. A varying current in any coil of the transformer produces a varying magnetic flux in the transformer's core, which induces a varying electromotive force (voltage) across any other coils wound around the same core.

Basic principle

In an ideal transformer, the ratio of primary to secondary voltage equals the ratio of the numbers of turns in the windings: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np. A transformer with more secondary turns than primary turns is a step-up transformer; the reverse is step-down. Power is conserved (minus small losses), so stepping voltage up steps current down. Transformers work only with alternating (or otherwise changing) current.

Construction

Windings are wound on a core of laminated soft iron or ferrite, the laminations suppressing eddy currents that would waste energy as heat. Large power transformers are immersed in insulating oil that cools the windings and insulates them; smaller ones may be dry-type or potted. Losses arise from winding resistance (copper losses), hysteresis and eddy currents in the core (iron losses), and stray flux.

Applications

Transformers made practical the transmission of electric power over long distances: generators step voltage up for efficient transmission at hundreds of kilovolts, and successive stages step it down for distribution and final use. They also appear in power supplies, audio equipment, impedance matching, isolation and instrumentation throughout electronics.

History

Induction coils date to Faraday's experiments of 1831. Practical closed-core transformers were developed in the 1880s by the Zipernowsky–Bláthy–Déri team in Hungary, by Lucien Gaulard and John Gibbs, and by William Stanley in the United States, forming a cornerstone of the AC power systems championed by Westinghouse and Tesla.
