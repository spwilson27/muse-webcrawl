Acousmatic

Acousmatic (from Greek akousmatikoi, the disciples who listened to Pythagoras lecturing from behind a curtain) describes sound that is heard without its source being seen. In music, acousmatic music is a form of electroacoustic music composed for presentation over loudspeakers, in which the sounds — recorded, synthesised or transformed — are deliberately detached from any visible cause.

Origins

The term was adopted in the 1950s by Pierre Schaeffer, founder of musique concrète, and elaborated by the writer Jérôme Peignot. Schaeffer argued that recording technology created a new listening situation: sound severed from its source invites "reduced listening" (écoute réduite), attention to the sound's intrinsic qualities rather than to what produced it. François Bayle, who succeeded Schaeffer's milieu at the Groupe de Recherches Musicales, developed acousmatic music as a concert art diffused over an "acousmonium" — an orchestra of loudspeakers played by an interpreter.

Practice

Acousmatic works are fixed media, usually stereo or multichannel, performed in darkened halls. The aesthetic encourages ambiguity between recognisable sound sources and abstract sonic material, and has strongly influenced sound design, radiophonic art and contemporary electronic music.
