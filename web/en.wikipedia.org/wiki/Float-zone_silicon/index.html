Float-zone silicon

Float-zone silicon is very pure silicon obtained by vertical zone melting. The process was developed at Bell Labs by Henry Theuerer in 1955 as a modification of a method developed by William Pfann for germanium. In the vertical configuration, the molten silicon is held in place by surface tension.

Process

A polycrystalline silicon rod is mounted vertically and a radio-frequency induction coil melts a narrow zone that travels slowly along the rod. Impurities preferentially remain in the liquid phase and are swept to one end, while a seed crystal at the top determines the crystal orientation of the resolidified material. Because the melt never contacts a crucible, contamination — particularly oxygen — is far lower than in the Czochralski process.

Properties and uses

Float-zone material can reach resistivities far higher than Czochralski silicon, making it preferred for power electronics such as thyristors and IGBTs, and for radiation detectors. The process is limited to smaller ingot diameters and is more expensive, so it accounts for a minority of silicon production.
