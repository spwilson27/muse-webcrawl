Tropical ecology

Tropical ecology is the study of the relationships between organisms and their environment in the tropics, the region of Earth between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. This region, characterized by high temperatures and small seasonal variation in day length, contains the greatest biodiversity on the planet, including tropical rainforests, savannas, mangroves and coral reefs.

Patterns and hypotheses

The latitudinal diversity gradient — the increase in species richness toward the equator — is ecology's most pervasive pattern, and explaining it has generated dozens of hypotheses. Proposed mechanisms include greater solar energy and productivity, larger area, faster evolutionary rates, lower extinction rates in climatically stable refuges, and stronger biotic interactions such as predation and competition that force finer niche partitioning. The Janzen–Connell hypothesis proposes that host-specific natural enemies maintain tree diversity by preventing any one seedling species from dominating near its parent.

Community processes

Tropical systems feature especially intricate webs of mutualism: frugivores disperse the seeds of a large fraction of rainforest trees, figs sustain keystone frugivore communities, and ants defend many plants in exchange for food and housing. Nutrient cycling is rapid, with most nutrients held in biomass rather than in the frequently poor, deeply weathered soils.

Research and conservation

Long-term research stations such as Barro Colorado Island in Panama and La Selva in Costa Rica have anchored the field. Tropical ecology is deeply entwined with conservation biology, as deforestation, fragmentation, hunting and climate change threaten the most species-rich ecosystems on Earth; forest plots coordinated by networks such as ForestGEO monitor trees across the tropics to detect change.
