Petrifaction

In geology, petrifaction or petrification (from Ancient Greek πέτρα, pétra, "rock, stone") is the process by which organic material becomes a fossil through the replacement of the original material and the filling of the original pore spaces with minerals. Petrified wood, formed by permineralization of trees buried under sediment, is the best-known example. Petrifaction is also the title experience in mythology and fiction, in which creatures are turned to stone.

Geological process

Petrifaction occurs when organisms are buried in sediment rich in mineral-laden groundwater. In permineralization, minerals precipitate in the pores and cell lumens of the tissue, preserving internal structure; in replacement, the original material dissolves molecule by molecule and is substituted by silica, calcite, dolomite, or pyrite. Because silica-bearing waters are common around volcanic ash deposits, petrified forests such as Arizona's Petrified Forest National Park preserve cellular detail in wood buried by ash falls.[1] The process can preserve soft anatomy in exceptional deposits, and microbial activity often accelerates mineral precipitation on cell walls, a fact exploited in laboratory experiments that have artificially petrified wood in days by soaking it in silica solutions.

In mythology and fiction

Turning to stone is a classic mythological fate: Medusa's gaze petrified onlookers in Greek myth, and the basilisk and cockatrice share the power in European folklore. In fantasy literature, petrifaction appears as a spell or curse — for example in the works of J. R. R. Tolkien (trolls turned to stone by sunlight) and in role-playing games, where "petrification" is a standard status effect countered by restorative magic or potions.
