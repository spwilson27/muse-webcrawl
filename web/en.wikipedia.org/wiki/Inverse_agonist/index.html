Inverse agonist


An inverse agonist reduces activity below the basal level.

An inverse agonist is a drug that binds to the same receptor as an agonist but induces a pharmacological response opposite to that of the agonist. Whereas a neutral antagonist merely blocks agonist binding without affecting basal receptor activity, an inverse agonist suppresses the constitutive (spontaneous) activity of a receptor that is active even in the absence of any ligand.

Mechanism

Many G protein-coupled receptors display some level of constitutive activity. Inverse agonists stabilise the inactive conformation of the receptor, shifting the equilibrium away from active states and thereby reducing baseline signalling. The concept became workable once constitutively active receptors were characterised, notably in work on benzodiazepine binding sites and beta-adrenergic and cannabinoid receptors in the 1980s and 1990s.

Examples

Rimonabant, a former anti-obesity drug, is an inverse agonist at the CB1 cannabinoid receptor. Several drugs long classified as antihistamines, such as cetirizine, are in fact inverse agonists at the H1 histamine receptor, and compounds at the 5-HT2C serotonin receptor and opioid receptors show similar behaviour. Pimavanserin, used in Parkinson's disease psychosis, acts as an inverse agonist at 5-HT2A receptors.

Clinical significance

Distinguishing inverse agonists from neutral antagonists matters where constitutive activity contributes to disease, and where long-term treatment may cause receptor up-regulation in response to suppressed basal signalling.
