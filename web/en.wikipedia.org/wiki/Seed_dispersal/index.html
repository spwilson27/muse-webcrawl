Seed dispersal

Seed dispersal is the movement, spread or transport of seeds away from the parent plant. Plants have limited mobility and rely on a variety of dispersal vectors to transport their propagules, including both abiotic vectors such as wind and water, and biotic vectors such as birds and other animals. Seeds can be dispersed individually or collectively, as well as dispersed in both space and time.

Benefits

Dispersal reduces competition between parent and offspring and among siblings, helps offspring escape seed predators and pathogens concentrated near the parent (the Janzen–Connell effect), and enables colonisation of new habitats — crucial for range shifts under climate change and for the founding of populations on islands and disturbed ground.

Modes of dispersal

In anemochory, seeds are carried by wind, often aided by wings, plumes, or hairs, as in dandelions, maples, and ashes. In hydrochory, buoyant seeds or fruits such as coconuts travel on water. Zoochory covers dispersal by animals: seeds may be eaten and pass through the gut (endozoochory), as in fleshy fruits dispersed by birds and mammals, or attach to fur and feathers with hooks and barbs (epizoochory). Ants disperse the seeds of many plants, attracted by fatty appendages called elaiosomes (myrmecochory). Ballochory is explosive dispersal, in which dehiscing fruits fling seeds mechanically, as in touch-me-not balsam. Plants lacking specialised structures rely on gravity (barochory).

Dispersal syndromes and distance

Seed traits often form recognisable "dispersal syndromes" matched to particular vectors. Most seeds land close to the parent, producing a leptokurtic dispersal kernel, but rare long-distance events disproportionately shape colonisation and gene flow. Loss of large frugivores can impair dispersal of large-seeded trees, an ecological concern known as defaunation.
