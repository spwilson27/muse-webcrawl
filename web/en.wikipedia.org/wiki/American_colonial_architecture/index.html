American colonial architecture includes several building design styles associated with the colonial period of the United States, including First Period English (late-medieval), Spanish Colonial, French Colonial, Dutch Colonial, and Georgian. These styles are associated with the houses, churches and government buildings of the period from about the 1600s through the 19th century. Several relatively distinct regional styles of architecture developed during the colonial period, reflecting the traditions of the settlers from different parts of Europe as well as the local climate and available building materials.

Subtypes

First Period English

First Period English was the first of the styles, built by English colonists in New England during the late 1620s through the 1720s. Post-and-beam framed houses were typically clad in wooden clapboards, had steeply pitched roofs, and large central chimneys. The saltbox and Cape Cod house are characteristic forms.

Spanish Colonial

Spanish Colonial developed from the earliest settlements in Florida, the American Southwest, Texas, and California. It featured thick adobe or masonry walls, flat or low-pitched roofs, arcaded courtyards, and tile or thatch roofing suited to warm climates.

Dutch Colonial

Dutch Colonial is recognised by its gambrel roof, which maximised attic space. It was built by Dutch settlers in the Hudson Valley and Long Island beginning in the 1620s.

French Colonial

French Colonial developed in the Illinois Country and Louisiana, characterised by wide hipped roofs, wrap-around galleries (porches) to shade walls from subtropical sun, and raised basements.

Georgian Colonial

Georgian architecture appeared during the 1700s and was the most popular style of the colonial era for wealthier colonists. Georgian homes are characterised by strict symmetry, five-ranked windows, panelled doors with decorative crowns, and dentil mouldings along the cornice.

Materials and climate

Wood was abundant in the forested eastern colonies and was the most common building material in New England and the Middle Colonies. Brick became common in the South, while stone was used in parts of Pennsylvania and New York. Steep roofs in the north shed snow; deep porches and high ceilings in the south promoted cooling.

Legacy

The Colonial Revival movement of the late 19th and 20th centuries renewed interest in these forms, and colonial-derived houses remain common in American suburbs.
