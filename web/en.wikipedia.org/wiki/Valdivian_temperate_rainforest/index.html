Valdivian temperate rainforest
Location: Southern Chile and southwestern Argentina
Area: approx. 248,100 km²
Biome: Temperate broadleaf and mixed forests

The Valdivian temperate rainforest is an ecoregion on the west coast of southern South America, in Chile and extending into Argentina. It is part of the Neotropical realm and one of the world's few temperate rainforests, characterised by high rainfall, evergreen broadleaf trees and a rich assemblage of endemic species.

Geography and climate

The ecoregion stretches along the Pacific coast and the western slopes of the Andes roughly between latitudes 37°S and 48°S. Westerly winds bring heavy precipitation, commonly between 2,000 and 5,000 mm per year, distributed fairly evenly across the seasons, with cool summers and mild winters near the coast.

Flora

Characteristic trees include alerce (Fitzroya cupressoides), one of the longest-lived tree species on Earth, the evergreen coihue (Nothofagus dombeyi), ulmo, tineo and dense understories of bamboo (Chusquea), tree ferns and the Chilean rhubarb (Gunnera tinctoria). Many plant lineages are Gondwanan relicts with relatives in New Zealand and Australia.

Fauna

Native fauna includes the pudú, the world's smallest deer, the kodkod cat, Darwin's fox, the monito del monte (a marsupial considered a living link to Australian marsupials) and the Magellanic woodpecker.

Conservation

Large areas have been converted to forestry plantations and agriculture, but significant tracts survive in national parks such as Pumalín, Alerce Andino and Nahuel Huapi. See also Magellanic subpolar forests.
