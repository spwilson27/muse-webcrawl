Pratt & Whitney PW4000

The Pratt & Whitney PW4000 is a family of high-bypass turbofan aircraft engines produced by Pratt & Whitney, developed as the successor to the JT9D. Certified in 1987, the family spans thrust ratings from about 52,000 to 99,040 pounds-force (lbf) and powers wide-body airliners including the Boeing 747-400, 767, 777 and KC-46, the Airbus A300, A310 and A330, and the McDonnell Douglas MD-11.

Design and variants

The PW4000 uses a two-spool architecture with full-authority digital engine control (FADEC) — a first for a large commercial engine of its generation — hollow wide-chord fan blades and single-crystal turbine airfoils. The 94-inch-fan models power the 747-400, 767, A300/A310 and MD-11; the 100-inch models were developed for the Airbus A330; and the 112-inch-fan PW4090, certificated at up to about 91,000 lbf (with the growth PW4098 rated near 99,000 lbf), was a launch engine on the Boeing 777. A derivative, the PW9000 concept, drew on the core for military applications, and the Engine Alliance GP7000 for the Airbus A380 was developed jointly with GE using PW4000-derived technology.

Notable incidents

In February 2021, a PW4077-powered Boeing 777-200 operating as United Airlines Flight 328 suffered an uncontained fan-blade failure shortly after departure from Denver, shedding debris and its inlet cowl; the aircraft landed safely. Similar blade failures in 2018 and 2020 led the FAA to mandate enhanced inspections of PW4000-112 fan blades, and the type's 777 fleet was temporarily grounded worldwide.
