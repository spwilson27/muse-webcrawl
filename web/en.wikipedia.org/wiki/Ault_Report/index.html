Ault Report

The Ault Report, formally the Report of the Air-to-Air Missile System Capability Review, was a 1968–69 United States Navy study led by Captain Frank W. Ault into the disappointing performance of American air-to-air missiles and fighter tactics in the Vietnam War. Its most famous outcome was the recommendation that led to the creation of the Navy's Fighter Weapons School — better known as TOPGUN — in March 1969.

Background

During the first years of the Vietnam War, US Navy and Air Force fighters achieved a far lower kill ratio against North Vietnamese MiGs than expected, and early air-to-air missiles such as the AIM-7 Sparrow and AIM-9 Sidewinder performed well below their design expectations — the Sparrow achieved a kill probability of under ten percent. Contributing factors included restrictive rules of engagement requiring visual identification (negating the missiles' beyond-visual-range design), poor missile reliability in tropical storage and handling, and fighter crews who had received little training in air combat manoeuvring, since doctrine had assumed missiles made dogfighting obsolete.

The study and recommendations

Ault's team reviewed every aspect of the missile system — design, manufacture, testing, handling, maintenance, aircraft integration and aircrew training — and produced hundreds of detailed recommendations. The best known called for a dedicated school to teach air combat manoeuvring against dissimilar aircraft. The Navy Fighter Weapons School was established at Naval Air Station Miramar on 3 March 1969.

Results

After TOPGUN's introduction, and the changes flowing from the report, the Navy's exchange ratio against North Vietnamese fighters improved dramatically in the later phase of the war (often cited as rising to around 13:1 in 1972), while the Air Force — which had not made equivalent training changes — saw less improvement.
