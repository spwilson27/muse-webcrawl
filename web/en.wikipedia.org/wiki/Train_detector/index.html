Train detector

A train detector is a device or system used on a railway to detect the presence, passage, or absence of a train on a section of track. Train detection is fundamental to railway signalling: it proves that a block section is occupied or clear, operates signals and points safely through interlocking, and activates level crossing warnings. A train detector should be fail-safe, so that a failure of the device is interpreted as the presence of a train.

A rail-mounted wheel sensor, a form of point train detector.

Types

  • Track circuit — the most common form of continuous detection: an electrical circuit through the rails of a section is short-circuited by the wheels and axles of a train, proving occupancy of the whole section. It can also detect many broken rails.
  • Axle counter — counts axles entering and leaving a section at its boundaries; when the counts agree, the section is clear. Axle counters are immune to many track-circuit problems (such as poor shunting on contaminated rails and electrical interference) but do not detect broken rails.
  • Treadle — a mechanical or hydraulic arm depressed by a passing wheel flange, used historically and still for some local functions such as initiating crossing sequences.
  • Wheel sensors / pedal detectors — inductive sensors clamped to the rail that register each passing wheel, used singly or as part of axle counters and hot-box detectors.
  • Radar, optical, and transponder-based systems — used in some metros and in moving-block systems, where the train itself reports its position by radio (as in CBTC and ETCS Level 3 concepts), reducing dependence on trackside detection.

Applications

Beyond signalling, train detectors data triggers level crossing barriers and flashing lights, records train movements for traffic management, activates hot axle box and dragging equipment detectors, and operates passenger information systems announcing approaching trains.
