Paleoanthropology is a subfield of anthropology and paleontology concerned with the study of human evolution through the fossil and archaeological records of early humans and their ancestors, the hominins. It combines primatology, skeletal biology, archaeology, geology and, increasingly, genetics to reconstruct the behaviour, biology and chronology of species from the last common ancestor with chimpanzees to the emergence of Homo sapiens.

History

The field's origins lie in 19th-century discoveries, including the Neanderthal type specimen (1856) and Eugène Dubois's "Java Man" (Homo erectus, 1891). The 1924 Taung Child (Australopithecus africanus), described by Raymond Dart, shifted attention to Africa, a case strengthened by Robert Broom and by Louis and Mary Leakey's finds at Olduvai Gorge. The Piltdown hoax, exposed in 1953 with methods refined by Mortimer Wheeler's generation of archaeologists and by fluorine testing, stands as a cautionary episode. Donald Johanson's "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis, 1974) and the Laetoli footprints (1978) demonstrated early bipedalism.

Methods

Paleoanthropologists date fossils using stratigraphy and radiometric techniques such as potassium–argon and argon–argon dating, together with luminescence and electron spin resonance for younger or carbonate-poor contexts. Comparative anatomy, computed tomography, stable-isotope analysis of diet and, since 2010, ancient genomes — which revealed the Denisovans and interbreeding between archaic and modern humans — have transformed the discipline.

Major themes

Central questions include the origins of bipedal locomotion, the enlargement of the brain, the development of stone-tool technologies (Oldowan, Acheulean, Mousterian), the control of fire, the dispersal of Homo erectus and later Homo sapiens out of Africa, and the extinction of the Neanderthals and other archaic humans around 40,000 years ago.
