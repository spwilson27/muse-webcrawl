Dryburgh Abbey

Dryburgh Abbey, near Dryburgh on the banks of the River Tweed in the Scottish Borders, is a ruined Premonstratensian abbey founded in 1150. Set in a secluded loop of the Tweed amid woodland, it is often considered the most romantic of the four great Border abbeys, the others being Melrose, Jedburgh, and Kelso.

History

The abbey was founded by Hugh de Morville, Constable of Scotland and Lord of Lauderdale, for canons of the Premonstratensian (White Canon) order from Alnwick Abbey. Although endowed by David I and de Morville, Dryburgh was never as wealthy as its neighbours and its history was repeatedly interrupted by war: English armies burned the abbey in 1322 under Edward II (retreating after a failed campaign, troops reportedly set fire to it after hearing its bells ring in triumph), again in 1385, and it was sacked in the 1540s during the Rough Wooing. After the Scottish Reformation of 1560 the abbey declined, its lands passing through various hands.

The ruins

Substantial fragments survive in a pointed (Early Gothic) style: the transepts of the abbey church with their wheel window, the chapter house — noted for its intact acoustics and painted plaster — parts of the cloister ranges, and the precinct wall and gatehouse. The site is in the care of Historic Environment Scotland.

Burials

Dryburgh is famous as the burial place of Sir Walter Scott, interred in 1832 in the north transept (the Haig aisle, formerly St Mary's aisle) alongside his wife and family. Field Marshal Earl Haig, commander of British forces in the First World War, was also buried there in 1928.
