Valence band

In solid-state physics, the valence band is the highest range of electron energies in which electrons are normally present at absolute zero temperature. In semiconductors and insulators, the valence band is separated from the empty (or partly filled) conduction band above it by a band gap; in metals, the valence and conduction bands overlap or the valence band itself is only partly filled.

Band theory

In a crystal, the discrete energy levels of isolated atoms broaden into bands of allowed energies. The valence band derives largely from the outer (valence) atomic orbitals and is fully occupied in a semiconductor at zero temperature, so — being full — it carries no current by itself. Electrical conduction begins when electrons are excited across the band gap into the conduction band, for example thermally, optically, or by doping.

Holes

Each electron promoted to the conduction band leaves behind a vacancy in the valence band that behaves as a mobile positive charge carrier called a hole. In p-type semiconductors, deliberately introduced acceptor impurities create many such holes, and current is carried mainly by their motion through the valence band. The top of the valence band, and the size of the gap to the conduction band (1.12 eV in silicon, 0.67 eV in germanium), determine a material's electronic and optical properties.
