Algebraic K-theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algebraic K-theory is a branch of algebra that studies rings and modules by associating to them a sequence of abelian groups K0, K1, K2, …, called K-groups. First developed in the late 1950s by Alexander Grothendieck in his work on the Riemann–Roch theorem, the subject grew into a deep theory connecting algebraic geometry, number theory, and topology. While topological K-theory concerns vector bundles on spaces, algebraic K-theory is built from projective modules and invertible matrices over a ring.

The low K-groups

  • K0 of a ring R is the Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective modules over R; for a field it is simply the integers, and for the ring of integers of a number field it detects the ideal class group.
  • K1 is related to the group of units and to determinants: for a field F, K1(F) = F×; in general it involves the abelianization of the general linear group, and the special Whitehead group SK1.
  • K2 was characterized by John Milnor using symbols and Steinberg relations; for number fields it ties into quadratic reciprocity, and Matsumoto's theorem describes K2 of a field entirely in terms of symbols.

Higher K-theory

The higher groups Kn for n ≥ 3 were defined by Daniel Quillen in 1972 using the "+ construction" and the Q-construction, work for which he received the Fields Medal. Quillen proved fundamental theorems including localization and dévissage, and computed the K-groups of finite fields. The K-theory of the integers connects to deep conjectures: the Lichtenbaum–Quillen conjectures related K-groups to étale cohomology, and the groups K4n+1(ℤ) involve Bernoulli numbers and values of the Riemann zeta function.

Applications

Algebraic K-theory has applications in algebraic number theory (class groups, Iwasawa theory), geometry (intersection theory and motivic cohomology, where Voevodsky's work resolved the Milnor and Bloch–Kato conjectures), and topology, where Waldhausen's variant for spaces underlies work on manifold classification. The related subject of hermitian K-theory studies forms and orthogonal groups.
