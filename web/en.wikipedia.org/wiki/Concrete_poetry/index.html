Concrete poetry

Concrete poetry is an arrangement of linguistic elements in which the typographical effect is more important in conveying meaning than verbal significance. It is sometimes referred to as visual poetry, a term that has now developed a distinct meaning of its own. Concrete poetry relates more to the visual arts than to literature as traditionally understood.

In concrete poetry, words are arranged spatially so that the poem's visual form is integral to its meaning.

History

Shaped verse has ancient precedents, including the pattern poems of Simmias of Rhodes and medieval carmina figurata, and the shaped poems in George Herbert's The Temple (1633). The modern movement crystallised in the 1950s: Eugen Gomringer in Switzerland published his "constellations" and a manifesto of concrete poetry, while in Brazil the Noigandres group (Augusto and Haroldo de Campos and Décio Pignatari) issued the "Pilot Plan for Concrete Poetry" in 1958, treating the poem as a visual object governed by the "verbivocovisual" interplay of word, sound and image. The movement spread internationally in the 1960s through figures such as Ian Hamilton Finlay in Scotland.

Characteristics

Concrete poems exploit typography, spacing, repetition and the disposition of words on the page. Meaning is conveyed through structure as much as through semantics; reading order may be non-linear, and the poem is often designed to be apprehended at a glance as an image.
