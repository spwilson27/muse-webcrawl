Etrog

The etrog (Hebrew: אֶתְרוֹג; plural etrogim) is the yellow citron (Citrus medica) used by Jews during the week-long holiday of Sukkot as one of the Four Species. Together with the lulav (date palm frond), hadass (myrtle), and aravah (willow), it is held and waved in a ritual described in Leviticus 23:40, which commands taking "the fruit of a beautiful tree" — understood by rabbinic tradition to be the citron.

Requirements

For the ritual, an etrog must be unblemished, of good colour and shape, and grown on an ungrafted tree. Particular value is placed on an etrog whose pitom — the remnant of the flower's style at the fruit's tip — remains intact, though an etrog that naturally never had one is also acceptable to most authorities. Because a perfect specimen is prized, fine etrogim can sell for hundreds of dollars, and they are kept protected, traditionally in a padded silver or wooden box, sometimes wrapped in flax.

Cultivation

Citrons have been cultivated around the Mediterranean since antiquity; tradition associates the "Yanover" etrog with Calabria in southern Italy, where the variety known as the Diamante citron has long been grown for the Sukkot market. Today etrogim are also produced in Israel, Morocco, and elsewhere, and inspectors check orchards to certify that trees are ungrafted. After the holiday, the fruit is sometimes preserved as jam or candied peel, and a custom holds that a woman who eats from the etrog of Sukkot will be blessed with children.
