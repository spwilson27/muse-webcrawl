Amorphous solid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In condensed matter physics and materials science, an amorphous solid (or non-crystalline solid) is a solid that lacks the long-range order that is characteristic of a crystal. The terms "glass" and "glassy solid" are sometimes used synonymously with amorphous solid; however, these terms refer specifically to solids that undergo a glass transition. Examples of amorphous solids include glass, many polymers, gels, thin films, and nanostructured materials.

Structure

Unlike crystalline solids, whose atoms are arranged in a regular repeating lattice, amorphous solids possess only short-range order — order over distances of a few atomic spacings. This absence of long-range periodicity means amorphous solids do not produce sharp diffraction patterns; X-ray diffraction instead shows broad diffuse halos.

Glass transition

Many amorphous solids are formed by cooling a liquid fast enough to avoid crystallisation. As temperature falls through the glass transition temperature (Tg), the supercooled liquid's viscosity rises enormously and the material behaves as a solid while retaining the disordered structure of the liquid. Below Tg the material is called a glass.

Examples and applications

  • Silicate glasses — window glass is primarily amorphous silica with additives.
  • Polymers — polystyrene and many plastics are amorphous or partly amorphous.
  • Amorphous metals (metallic glasses) — alloys cooled extremely rapidly, with high strength and corrosion resistance.
  • Amorphous silicon — used in thin-film solar cells and display backplanes.
  • Amorphous pharmaceuticals — drugs prepared in amorphous form can dissolve faster than crystals.
