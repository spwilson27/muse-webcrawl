Harmful algal bloom

A harmful algal bloom (HAB) is an algal bloom that harms people, animals or ecosystems, either because the algae produce toxins or because the bloom's sheer biomass depletes oxygen, clogs gills and shuts out light. Harmful blooms occur in oceans, estuaries and fresh water worldwide; marine events caused by dinoflagellates are popularly called red tides, while many freshwater events are caused by toxin-producing cyanobacteria.

Causes

Blooms develop when temperature, light, salinity and nutrient conditions align. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff, wastewater and aquaculture — eutrophication — are major human drivers, and warming waters have lengthened bloom seasons and expanded the ranges of several harmful species. Resting cysts seeded in sediments can re-inoculate the water column year after year.

Effects

Some species produce potent neurotoxins and hepatotoxins: saxitoxin causes paralytic shellfish poisoning, domoic acid causes amnesic shellfish poisoning and mass strandings of marine mammals, and brevetoxins are responsible for fish kills and respiratory irritation along the Florida Gulf coast. In fresh water, microcystins from cyanobacteria threaten drinking-water supplies — in 2014 a bloom in Lake Erie forced Toledo, Ohio to shut off tap water to half a million people. Blooms also devastate aquaculture, close beaches and shellfisheries, and create hypoxic "dead zones" as they decay.

Monitoring and management

Agencies monitor blooms using satellite ocean-colour imagery, automated buoys and shellfish toxin testing, issuing closures and forecasts. Long-term control rests mainly on reducing nutrient pollution; clay dispersal and other interventions are used locally in some countries.
