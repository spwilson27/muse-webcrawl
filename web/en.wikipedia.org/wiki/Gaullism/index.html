Gaullism (French: Gaullisme) is a French political stance based on the thought and action of Second World War French Resistance leader Charles de Gaulle, who would become the founding president of the Fifth French Republic. Gaullism holds that France should assert itself as an independent great power — "a certain idea of France", in de Gaulle's famous phrase from his war memoirs.

Principles

Core Gaullist principles include the independence of France in foreign policy (including an independent nuclear deterrent, the force de frappe), national sovereignty above party interest, a strong executive presidency, direct appeals to the people through referendums, state-led economic development (dirigisme) combined with market mechanisms, and a "Europe of nations" rather than a supranational federation. De Gaulle twice vetoed British entry into the European Economic Community and withdrew France from NATO's integrated military command in 1966.

History

Gaullism began with de Gaulle's Appeal of 18 June 1940 and the Free French movement. After the war he founded the Rally of the French People (RPF). Returning to power in 1958, he founded the Fifth Republic with a strengthened presidency. Successor parties — UNR, UDR, Jacques Chirac's RPR (1976), and its successors the UMP and Les Républicains — carried the Gaullist tradition, though later leaders moderated its economic statism and European scepticism.
