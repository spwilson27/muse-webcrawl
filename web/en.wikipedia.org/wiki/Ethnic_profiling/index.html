Ethnic profiling

Ethnic profiling, often used interchangeably with racial profiling, is the practice by law-enforcement or security officials of targeting individuals for suspicion of crime or security checks on the basis of their perceived race, ethnicity, religion or national origin, rather than on individual behaviour or objective evidence. It encompasses practices such as disproportionate stop-and-search, identity checks, border screening and surveillance of particular communities.

Definitions and evidence

Human-rights bodies such as the European Commission against Racism and Intolerance (ECRI) define ethnic profiling as the use by police, without objective and reasonable justification, of grounds such as race, colour, language, religion, nationality or ethnic origin in control, surveillance or investigation activities. Studies in numerous countries have documented persistent ethnic disproportionality in stops and searches, with "hit rates" for contraband or offences often no higher — sometimes lower — for the groups stopped most frequently.

Contexts and debates

Ethnic profiling is documented in ordinary street policing, drug enforcement, immigration control and counter-terrorism. Defenders of appearance-based selection argue it can be an efficient use of limited resources when offence patterns correlate with group membership. Criminologists and rights organisations respond that the practice is discriminatory, empirically inefficient because it diverts attention from behaviour-based indicators, and corrosive of the community trust and cooperation on which policing depends. After terrorist attacks, measures targeting Muslim or Arab-appearing travellers have drawn particular controversy.

Legal position

Ethnic profiling is inconsistent with prohibitions of discrimination in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the Convention on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination and, in Europe, the European Convention on Human Rights. Courts have restricted some practices: in the United States, race may not be the sole factor for a stop; in the United Kingdom, the Equality Act 2010 prohibits direct race discrimination in public functions. Several jurisdictions now require recording the ethnicity of those stopped to monitor disproportionality.
