Tulsa race massacre

The Tulsa race massacre, also known as the Tulsa race riot or the Greenwood massacre, took place on May 31 and June 1, 1921, when mobs of white residents, some deputised by city officials, attacked the Black residents and businesses of the Greenwood District in Tulsa, Oklahoma. It is considered one of the worst incidents of racial violence in American history.

Background

Greenwood was one of the most prosperous Black communities in the United States, nicknamed "Black Wall Street." Oklahoma had become a state in 1907 with Jim Crow laws, and Tulsa was a segregated, oil-boom city with a growing Ku Klux Klan presence and a recent history of lynching.

Events

The violence was sparked by the arrest of Dick Rowland, a young Black shoeshiner accused of assaulting Sarah Page, a white elevator operator. Fearing a lynching, armed Black men went to the courthouse; a confrontation with a white crowd turned violent. Over the following hours, thousands of white rioters invaded Greenwood, looting and burning homes and businesses across some 35 city blocks. Some survivors reported attacks from private aircraft. Estimates of the death toll range from 75 to 300; thousands of Black residents were interned, and roughly 10,000 were left homeless.

Aftermath and legacy

No one was prosecuted for the violence, and the event was largely omitted from Oklahoma's history curricula for decades. A state commission established in 1996 documented the massacre and recommended reparations. In 2021, the centennial brought national attention, renewed searches for mass graves, and a lawsuit by survivors. The massacre is commemorated at the Greenwood Cultural Center and the Greenwood Rising museum.
