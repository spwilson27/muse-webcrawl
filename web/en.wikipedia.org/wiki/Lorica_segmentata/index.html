Lorica segmentata

Lorica segmentata

Type: Body armour

Used by: Roman legionaries

In use: c. 9 BC – late 3rd century AD

Material: Iron (sometimes bronze fittings)

The lorica segmentata (Latin pronunciation: [loːˈriːka s̺eɡmenˈtaːta]), also called lorica lamminata, or banded armour is a type of personal armour that was used by soldiers of the Roman army, consisting of metal strips ("girth hoops") fastened to internal leather straps. It was in use from the time of Augustus, around 9 BC, until the late 3rd century AD, when it fell out of favour and was replaced by other forms of armour.

History

Although the lorica segmentata was favoured only by soldiers who formed the heavy infantry of the Roman legion, auxiliaries wore the lorica hamata or lorica squamata. The earliest evidence for the lorica segmentata comes from finds at Dangstetten in Germany, dated to around 9 BC. It remained in service for over 250 years, a remarkable lifespan for a piece of military equipment.

Construction

The plates were soft iron inside and (some) mild steel on the outside, to make them resistant to penetration. The strips were arranged horizontally on the body, overlapping downwards, and fastened at the front and back. The upper body and shoulders were protected by additional shoulder guards, breast- and back-plates. The form of the armour allowed it to be stored very compactly, since it was possible to separate it into four sections. The weight of a typical lorica segmentata was about 9 kilograms (20 lb).

Use

The lorica segmentata provided better protection than other types of armour, such as the lorica hamata (chain mail), and was lighter. It was also cheaper and faster to produce than chain mail, which required thousands of individually riveted rings. However, it required more maintenance, since the leather straps and brass buckles and hinges tended to break with use, and the iron could rust if not cared for.

Reconstructions

No complete example of the lorica segmentata survives, but important finds at Corbridge in England and Newstead in Scotland, together with depictions on Trajan's Column in Rome, have allowed modern reconstructions. Modern reenactors and experimental archaeologists have built copies to test the armour's flexibility and protective qualities.
