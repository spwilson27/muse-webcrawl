Liquidity premium theory

The liquidity premium theory of the term structure of interest rates holds that the interest rate on a long-term bond equals an average of the short-term interest rates expected to occur over the life of the bond, plus a positive liquidity (term) premium that compensates investors for the greater risk of holding longer maturities.

Explanation

Because long-term bonds are more sensitive to interest-rate changes and less liquid, investors demand extra yield beyond expected future short rates. The premium is generally assumed to grow with maturity. The theory therefore explains why yield curves are usually upward-sloping even when short rates are expected to stay flat, and why a steeply inverted curve is a strong signal of expected rate declines: expectations must fall enough to overcome the added premium at longer maturities.

Relation to other theories

It combines the expectations theory with the market segmentation insight that maturities are not perfect substitutes, and contrasts with the preferred habitat theory, in which the premium can vary sign depending on investors' preferred maturities.
