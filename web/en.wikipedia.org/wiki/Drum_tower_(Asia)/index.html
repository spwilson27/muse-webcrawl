Drum tower (Asia)

A drum tower (Chinese: 鼓樓; pinyin: gǔlóu) is a tower in traditional Chinese architecture that houses large drums, historically used to signal the time of day, mark ceremonies, and sound alarms. Drum towers were commonly paired with bell towers in Chinese cities: the bell was struck in the morning and the drum beaten at dusk, a rhythm summarized in the phrase "morning bell, evening drum" (晨鐘暮鼓).

Function

In imperial China, drum towers played a central role in urban timekeeping. Drummers struck the drum at intervals through the night to announce the watches (更, gēng), and also beat the drum for official ceremonies, warnings of fire or attack, and public announcements. The largest drums, such as those in Beijing and Xi'an, were several metres across and covered in cowhide.

Notable examples

The Beijing Drum Tower (Gulou), originally built in 1272 during the Yuan dynasty and rebuilt under the Ming and Qing dynasties, stands on the central axis of the old city, north of the Forbidden City; its drums marked the time for the capital until 1924. The Xi'an Drum Tower, built in 1380 early in the Ming dynasty, is among the largest surviving drum towers in China and stands near the city's Bell Tower. Drum towers also survive in Nanjing, and in many smaller cities and in Dong (Kam) ethnic villages in Guizhou and Guangxi, where the drum tower serves as the social and ceremonial centre of the village.

Dong drum towers

Among the Dong people of southwestern China, drum towers (Dong: lao) are wooden pagoda-like structures built without nails, serving as meeting places, festival venues, and symbols of the village. They are among the most distinctive buildings of Dong architecture.
