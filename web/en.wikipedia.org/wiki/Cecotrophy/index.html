Cecotrophy

Cecotrophy (also spelled caecotrophy) is the consumption of soft faecal pellets, called cecotropes or cecals, produced by hindgut-fermenting animals such as rabbits, hares and pikas, and many rodents. It is a form of coprophagy specialised to recover nutrients generated by microbial fermentation in the cecum.

Process

In lagomorphs, the colon separates digesta into indigestible fibre, which is rapidly expelled as hard dry pellets, and fluid and fine particles, which are moved back into the cecum for fermentation. The fermented material is periodically expelled as clusters of soft, mucus-coated pellets. The animal normally takes the cecotropes directly from the anus and swallows them whole, so they are rarely seen by observers. Passage through the stomach and small intestine a second time allows absorption of microbial protein, B vitamins and vitamin K produced by the cecal bacteria.

Significance

Cecotrophy can supply a substantial fraction of a rabbit's protein and vitamin requirements. Prevention — for example by an ill-fitting collar or obesity that stops the animal reaching its anus — leads to malnutrition and to soiling of the hindquarters, while uneaten cecotropes are a common sign of an excessively rich diet in pet rabbits. Related behaviour occurs in rodents such as rats, mice, guinea pigs and chinchillas, and in some other hindgut fermenters.
