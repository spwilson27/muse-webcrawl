Colours, standards and guidons

Colours, standards and guidons are the distinctive ceremonial flags carried by military units. In the Commonwealth realms, colours are the large flags carried by infantry regiments, standards are the smaller square flags of heavy cavalry and armoured units, and guidons are the swallow-tailed flags of light cavalry and certain other arms.

Historically, such flags served as rallying points on the battlefield; their loss was a disgrace, and their capture a prized trophy. Today they are primarily ceremonial, but they remain the symbolic embodiment of a regiment's spirit, embroidered with its battle honours.

History

The practice descends from Roman standards such as the legionary eagle (aquila) and from medieval banners. By the 18th century, British infantry regiments carried two colours — the King's (or Queen's) Colour, based on the Union Flag, and the Regimental Colour, in the regiment's facing colour — while cavalry regiments carried standards or guidons.

Battle honours

Selected names of battles and campaigns in which a regiment distinguished itself are emblazoned on its colours. Because silk flags cannot hold unlimited honours, regiments select a limited number for display, though all awarded honours are recorded.

Ceremonial use

Colours are consecrated when presented, treated with the utmost reverence, and saluted by soldiers. Trooping the Colour in London is the best-known annual ceremony. When colours wear out, they are "laid up" in a church or cathedral rather than destroyed. On parade they are carried by ensigns or lieutenants and escorted by a colour party of senior non-commissioned officers.

Other nations

Many other armed forces maintain analogous traditions: the United States Army carries the national color alongside organizational colors with campaign streamers; French regiments guard their drapeaux and étendards; German units historically carried Fahnen and Standarten.
