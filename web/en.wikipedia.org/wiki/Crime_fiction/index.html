Crime fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A shadowy bookstore shelf of crime novels with stark mid-century paperback covers]
A shadowy bookstore shelf of crime novels with stark mid-century paperback covers

Crime fiction is a literary genre centred on crimes, their detection and their perpetrators, encompassing the detective story, the police procedural, the thriller, true crime and noir. From Edgar Allan Poe's Dupin tales (1841) and Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, through the 'Golden Age' puzzle mysteries of Agatha Christie and the hardboiled school of Raymond Chandler, it is the world's most popular fiction category.

Forms and history

Poe invented the detective and the locked-room mystery; Wilkie Collins's The Moonstone (1868) founded the English detective novel. Between the wars, Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham and Ngaio Marsh codified the fair-play whodunit. America's pulps answered with hardboiled fiction — Dashiell Hammett, Chandler, James M. Cain — whose moral murk became Film noir. Post-war came the police procedural (Ed McBain's 87th Precinct; Sweden's Sjöwall and Wahlöö), the spy thriller and psychological suspense (Patricia Highsmith).

Contemporary landscape

Crime dominates global fiction markets, renewed by Nordic noir (Henning Mankell, Stieg Larsson), domestic noir (Gone Girl, 2012), and regional schools from Japanese honkaku to Mediterranean noir (Andrea Camilleri's Montalbano). True crime — from In Cold Blood (1966) to the podcast boom (Serial, 2014) — blurs the genre into journalism, raising ethical questions about victims as entertainment.

Themes

Scholars read crime fiction as society's negotiation with order: classical detection restores a rational world; hardboiled and noir traditions doubt it can be restored; feminist and postcolonial crime writing relocates guilt from the individual criminal to the system itself.
