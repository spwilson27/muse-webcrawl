Niacin, also known as nicotinic acid, is an organic compound and a vitamer of vitamin B3, an essential human nutrient. It can be manufactured by plants and animals from the amino acid tryptophan. Niacin is obtained in the diet from a variety of whole and processed foods, with highest contents in fortified packaged foods, meat, poultry, red fish such as tuna and salmon, and lesser amounts in nuts, legumes and seeds. As a dietary supplement, it is used to treat pellagra, a disease caused by niacin deficiency.

Chemistry

Niacin is a derivative of pyridine, with a carboxyl group at the 3-position. It is a precursor to the coenzymes nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP), which are involved in DNA repair, cellular signaling and a wide range of redox reactions central to energy metabolism.

Deficiency

Severe niacin deficiency causes pellagra, classically described by the "four Ds": diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia and death. Pellagra was once epidemic in the American South and parts of Europe where diets were dominated by untreated corn. Milder deficiency can cause fatigue, depression, headache and poor memory.

Dietary recommendations

The recommended dietary allowance for adults is about 14 mg per day for women and 16 mg per day for men, expressed as niacin equivalents (NE), which take into account dietary tryptophan conversion.

Pharmacology

In large doses (1–3 g/day), niacin lowers LDL cholesterol and triglycerides and raises HDL cholesterol, though outcome trials have not shown a clear mortality benefit when added to statins. A common side effect is the "niacin flush", a prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation causing warmth and redness of the skin.

History

Nicotinic acid was first synthesized in 1867 by oxidizing nicotine, and its vitamin role was established in the 1930s when Conrad Elvehjem showed that it cured black tongue in dogs, the analogue of human pellagra. The name "niacin" was coined from nicotinic acid vitamin, partly to avoid association with nicotine.
