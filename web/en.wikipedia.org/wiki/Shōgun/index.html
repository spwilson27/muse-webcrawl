Shōgun

Shōgun (将軍, lit. "army commander"), officially sei-i taishōgun (征夷大将軍, "great general who subdues the barbarians"), was the title of the military rulers of Japan during most of the period spanning 1185 to 1868. Nominally appointed by the Emperor of Japan, shōguns were the de facto rulers of the country, governing through a military administration called a shogunate or bakufu ("tent government"), while the imperial court continued in Kyoto with ceremonial and religious functions.

Origins

The title was originally granted in the Heian period to generals commanding imperial armies against the Emishi peoples of northern Honshu; Ōtomo no Otomaro and Sakanoue no Tamuramaro were among the earliest holders. The office became permanent when Minamoto no Yoritomo, having defeated the Taira clan in the Genpei War, received the title in 1192 and established the Kamakura shogunate, the first warrior government.

The three shogunates

Kamakura shogunate (1185/1192–1333). Governed from Kamakura, effectively ruled by Hōjō regents after Yoritomo's line died out; it repelled the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281 but collapsed amid imperial restoration attempts.

Ashikaga shogunate (1336–1573). Founded by Ashikaga Takauji and based in Kyoto's Muromachi district, it presided over a flowering of Zen-influenced culture but weakened into the provincial warfare of the Sengoku period.

Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868). Founded by Tokugawa Ieyasu after the Battle of Sekigahara, it governed from Edo (Tokyo) for over 260 years of relative peace, maintaining the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance, a rigid status order, and restricted foreign contact (sakoku).

End of the office

Under pressure from Western powers after Commodore Perry's arrival in 1853, the last shōgun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, returned governing authority to the Emperor in 1867 (taisei hōkan), and the office was abolished in the Meiji Restoration of 1868 after the Boshin War.
