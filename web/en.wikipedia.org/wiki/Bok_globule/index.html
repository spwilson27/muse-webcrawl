Bok globule

In astronomy, Bok globules are isolated and relatively small dark nebulae containing dense cosmic dust and gas from which star formation may take place. Bok globules are found within H II regions, and typically have a mass of about 2 to 50 solar masses within a region about a light year across. They contain molecular hydrogen (H2), carbon oxides and helium, and around 1% (by mass) silicate dust.

History

Bok globules were first observed by the Dutch-American astronomer Bart Bok in the 1940s. In a paper published with Edith Reilly in 1947, Bok hypothesised that these globules were undergoing gravitational collapse and would eventually form new stars. This hypothesis was difficult to verify at the time because the dark clouds obscure visible light from anything inside them. Infrared observations from the 1990s onward confirmed that stars are indeed forming within Bok globules: the warmed dust glows at infrared wavelengths, and many globules contain embedded protostars and Herbig–Haro objects.

Characteristics

Bok globules are among the coldest objects in the natural universe, with internal temperatures as low as about 10 kelvin. Their cores are dense enough — typically hundreds of thousands of molecules per cubic centimetre — to shield their interiors from external radiation. Many globules show evidence of multiple collapsing cores, suggesting that they form small clusters of stars or multiple-star systems rather than single stars.

Famous examples include the globules in the Eagle Nebula region and Barnard 68, one of the nearest and most studied Bok globules, about 400 light-years away in the constellation Ophiuchus.

Formation

Globules are believed to form in the turbulent interstellar medium, where compressions in the gas create dense pockets that become gravitationally bound. Molecular-line observations show complex internal motions, including rotation, infall and outflows from young stars within.
