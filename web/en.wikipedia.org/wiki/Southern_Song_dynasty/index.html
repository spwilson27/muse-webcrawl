Southern Song dynasty

The Southern Song dynasty (Chinese: 南宋; 1127–1279) was the second period of the Song dynasty, following the loss of northern China to the Jurchen-led Jin dynasty. After the Jingkang Incident of 1127, in which the Jin captured the capital Kaifeng and imprisoned Emperor Qinzong, the Song prince Zhao Gou re-established the dynasty in the south as Emperor Gaozong, eventually settling the capital at Lin'an (modern Hangzhou). The Southern Song ruled south of the Huai River until its conquest by the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty in 1279.

History

Gaozong's court survived repeated Jin invasions, aided by generals such as Yue Fei — later executed in 1142 as the court pursued peace — and by naval victories on the Yangtze. The Treaty of Shaoxing (1141) fixed the border along the Huai River and obliged the Song to pay annual tribute. Despite military weakness, the Southern Song was an age of dazzling commercial, technological and cultural achievement: maritime trade flourished from ports such as Guangzhou and Quanzhou, paper money (huizi) circulated widely, gunpowder weapons and paddle-wheel warships were developed, and Neo-Confucianism was systematised by Zhu Xi.

Fall

The Song initially allied with the Mongols to destroy the Jin in 1234, but conflict soon followed. Mongol invasions under Möngke and Kublai ground down Song resistance over decades; the fortress cities of Xiangyang fell in 1273, Lin'an surrendered in 1276, and the dynasty ended at the naval Battle of Yamen in 1279, where the child emperor Zhao Bing died.
