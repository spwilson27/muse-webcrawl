Scansion

Scansion (from Latin scandere, "to climb, scan") is the analysis of a line of verse into its metrical components — dividing it into feet, marking stressed and unstressed syllables, and identifying the metre, such as iambic pentameter. Derived from classical Latin prosody, scansion in English-language criticism is used to describe how metre shapes rhythm, emphasis, and meaning in a poem.

Notation

Conventional marks include the breve (˘) for an unstressed syllable and the macron or acute accent (– or ´) for a stressed syllable; feet are separated by vertical bars. A line of iambic pentameter, for example, scans as five repetitions of an unstressed–stressed pair (da-DUM). Common feet include the iamb, trochee (DUM-da), anapaest (da-da-DUM), dactyl (DUM-da-da), and spondee (DUM-DUM).

Systems

Different schools scan differently. Traditional scansion stresses relative prominence within a four-level hierarchy of stress, while generative metrics, following linguists such as Morris Halle and Samuel Jay Keyser, treats metre as an abstract pattern against which the natural stresses of language are matched, allowing systematic description of permissible variations such as trochaic substitutions and feminine endings. Classical Greek and Latin poetry used quantitative scansion based on syllable length rather than stress.
