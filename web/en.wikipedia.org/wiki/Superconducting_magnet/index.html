Superconducting magnet

A superconducting magnet can sustain very strong magnetic fields with almost no power consumption.

A superconducting magnet is an electromagnet made from coils of superconducting wire. They must be cooled to cryogenic temperatures during operation. In their superconducting state the wires have no electrical resistance and therefore can conduct much larger electric currents than ordinary wire, creating intense magnetic fields. They can also be cheaper to operate than conventional electromagnets because, once energized, the current persists with almost no energy input.

Construction

Most superconducting magnets are wound from niobium–titanium (NbTi) alloy filaments embedded in a copper matrix, cooled by liquid helium to around 4.2 K. Higher-field magnets use niobium–tin (Nb3Sn) or high-temperature superconductors such as REBCO tape. The copper matrix provides a safety path for the current if a section of the superconductor warms up and "quenches", transitioning suddenly to the resistive state.

Applications

Superconducting magnets are essential in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrometers, particle accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider, fusion experiments such as tokamaks, maglev trains, and laboratory high-field research magnets, which can exceed 20 tesla.

Quench

A quench is the sudden loss of superconductivity, in which the stored magnetic energy is dumped as heat. Protection systems detect quenches and spread or extract the energy to prevent damage to the coil.
