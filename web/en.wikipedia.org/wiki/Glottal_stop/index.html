Glottal stop

IPA symbolʔ
TypeVoiceless glottal plosive
PlaceGlottal
ExamplesHawaiian ʻokina; Cockney English

The glottal stop (or glottal plosive) is a type of consonantal sound used in many spoken languages, produced by obstructing airflow in the vocal tract by closing the glottis, the space between the vocal folds. In the International Phonetic Alphabet it is represented by the symbol ʔ. Although English has no letter for the sound, it occurs in the exclamation "uh-oh" and, in many accents, as a replacement for /t/ in words such as butter or bottle.

Occurrence

The glottal stop is a phoneme in many languages, including Arabic, Hawaiian, Malay and numerous indigenous languages of the Americas and Oceania. In Hawaiian it is written with the ʻokina, a letter resembling an opening single quotation mark, and distinguishes words such as koʻu ("my") from kou ("your"). In German, a glottal stop is typically inserted before vowel-initial syllables within words, though it is not written. In Danish, the related prosodic feature called stød involves glottal constriction.

In English

In English the glottal stop is usually allophonic rather than phonemic. It appears as t-glottalisation, in which /t/ is replaced by a glottal stop before a consonant or pause, a feature long associated with Cockney speech in London but now widespread in British and other varieties of English. It also reinforces voiceless stops at the ends of phrases and appears between vowels in emphatic speech.
