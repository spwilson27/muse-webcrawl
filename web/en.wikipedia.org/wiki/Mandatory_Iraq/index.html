Mandatory Iraq, formally the British Mandate for Mesopotamia, was a League of Nations Class A mandate administered by the United Kingdom from 1920 to 1932. It was created from the former Ottoman vilayets of Baghdad, Basra, and (after a 1925 League decision) Mosul, following the Ottoman Empire's defeat in the First World War.

History

Britain's assumption of control provoked the Iraqi revolt of 1920, a widespread uprising suppressed at considerable cost. The Cairo Conference of 1921 settled on indirect rule: Faisal, son of Sharif Hussein of Mecca, was installed as King Faisal I in August 1921, and a High Commissioner supervised British interests. The Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1922, and subsequent treaties in 1926 and 1930, formalized the relationship; the 1930 treaty promised independence and Iraqi admission to the League of Nations, which took place on 3 October 1932, ending the mandate while preserving British air bases and oil interests.

Administration and society

The mandate government built the structures of a modern Iraqi state — ministries, a parliament, an army, and a legal system — while Britain retained control of foreign affairs and key military assets, including RAF bases at Habbaniya and Shaibah. The mandate period also saw the development of Iraq's oil industry under the Turkish Petroleum Company (later the Iraq Petroleum Company) and continuing tensions among the country's Sunni, Shia, Kurdish, and other communities.
