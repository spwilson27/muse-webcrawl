Spinning frame

The spinning frame is an industrial machine for spinning fibres such as cotton into thread or yarn, and one of the key inventions of the early Industrial Revolution. The term most often refers to the water frame, patented by Richard Arkwright in 1769, which used a series of rollers turning at different speeds to draw out the roving and twist the fibres, producing a strong cotton thread suitable for use as warp.

Development

The roller-spinning principle behind the frame derived from the work of Lewis Paul and John Wyatt, who patented a roller spinning machine in 1738. Thomas Highs and his clockmaker John Kay developed related designs, and Arkwright — employing Kay — patented his version in 1769. The first machines were driven by horses, but Arkwright's mill at Cromford, Derbyshire (1771) was powered by a waterwheel, hence "water frame". The frame worked continuously, spun many threads at once (initially dozens of spindles, later over a hundred per frame), and produced yarn much stronger than that of James Hargreaves's spinning jenny, whose finer, weaker yarn suited only weft.

Impact

Because water frames were too large and power-hungry for domestic workshops, they demanded the factory system: concentrated workers, powered machinery, and shift labour. Arkwright's Cromford and later mills became models of the factory organisation that transformed textile manufacture and, eventually, industry in general. The spinning frame's descendants include Samuel Crompton's hybrid spinning mule (1779) and modern ring spinning frames, still the dominant spinning technology today.
