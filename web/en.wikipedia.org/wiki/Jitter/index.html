Jitter

Jitter is the deviation of a signal's timing from its ideal periodicity.

In electronics and telecommunications, jitter is the deviation from true periodicity of a presumably periodic signal, often in relation to a reference clock signal. In clock recovery applications it is called timing jitter. Jitter is a significant and usually undesired factor in the design of almost all communications links.

Metrics

Jitter can be quantified as phase jitter (the time difference from the expected position of an edge), period jitter, or cycle-to-cycle jitter, and is commonly expressed in unit intervals (UI), picoseconds, or as a root-mean-square or peak-to-peak value. In packet-switched networks, jitter commonly refers to packet delay variation — the variation in the latency of packets within a flow — which affects real-time applications such as voice over IP and streaming media; jitter buffers are used to smooth it.

Causes and effects

Sources of jitter include electromagnetic interference, crosstalk, power-supply noise, thermal effects, and the accumulated characteristics of clock-distribution networks. Excess jitter raises the bit error rate in digital links and degrades the signal-to-noise ratio of analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters in sampled systems, including digital audio. Mitigation techniques include phase-locked loops, careful clock design, and shielding.
