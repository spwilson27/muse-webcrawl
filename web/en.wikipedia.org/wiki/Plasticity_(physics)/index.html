Plasticity (physics)

Stress–strain curve: plastic deformation begins at the yield point

In physics and materials science, plasticity is the ability of a solid material to undergo permanent, non-reversible deformation in response to applied forces. A paperclip bent out of shape, for example, has undergone plastic deformation. Plasticity contrasts with elasticity, in which deformation disappears when the load is removed.

Description

For most materials there is a range of stress over which strain is proportional to stress (Hooke's law). Beyond the yield point, the material deforms plastically: in crystalline metals this occurs chiefly through the motion of dislocations, while in amorphous materials such as polymers it involves sliding and reorientation of molecular chains. Continued plastic deformation may harden the material (work hardening) until it eventually fractures.

Theory

Plasticity theory, a branch of continuum mechanics, describes this behaviour mathematically using yield criteria such as the von Mises and Tresca conditions, flow rules, and hardening laws. It is widely used in structural engineering, metal forming, and geomechanics, where it models phenomena from the bending of beams to the deformation of soils and rock. At small scales, the size of a specimen can strongly affect plastic behaviour, a topic studied in strain-gradient plasticity and dislocation theory.
