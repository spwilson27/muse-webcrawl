Ekman spiral

The Ekman spiral is an arrangement of ocean currents: the directions of horizontal current shear rotate as one moves up or down in the ocean, so that the steady-state current profile forms a spiral when plotted. The phenomenon is a consequence of the Coriolis effect. It is named after the Swedish oceanographer Vagn Walfrid Ekman, who published the theory in 1905, building on observations made during Fridtjof Nansen's Fram expedition (1893–1896), when icebergs were seen to drift 20–40° to the right of the prevailing wind in the Arctic.

The Ekman spiral: with depth, the current weakens and veers (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere).

Theory

When wind blows over the ocean surface, friction drags the surface layer in the wind direction, but the Coriolis effect deflects the flow (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern). Each successive deeper layer is dragged by the layer above and deflected further, while speed decreases with depth. In the idealized Ekman model — steady state, homogeneous water, constant eddy viscosity, deep water away from boundaries — the surface current flows at 45° to the wind direction, the velocity vector rotates clockwise (Northern Hemisphere) with increasing depth tracing a spiral, and the speed decays exponentially. At the "depth of frictional influence" (the Ekman depth, typically on the order of 10–100 m), the current flows opposite to the surface current at a small fraction of its speed.

Ekman transport

The net transport of the whole Ekman layer, integrated over depth, is directed at 90° to the wind direction — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere. This Ekman transport is fundamental to ocean circulation: it drives coastal upwelling where winds blow parallel to a coast (bringing nutrient-rich deep water to the surface, sustaining fisheries such as the Humboldt system), produces convergence and downwelling in subtropical gyres that helps drive wind-driven gyre circulation, and underlies Ekman pumping and suction in the ocean interior. An analogous spiral exists in the atmosphere above the friction layer near the ground.

Observations

The spiral has been observed in field measurements, though real-world profiles are modified by stratification, waves (Stokes drift), and time-varying winds. The theory remains a cornerstone of physical oceanography and its simple predictions of net transport are widely used in coupled ocean–atmosphere models.
