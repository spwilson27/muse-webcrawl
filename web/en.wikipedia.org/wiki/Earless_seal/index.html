The earless seals, phocids or true seals are one of the three main groups of mammals within the seal lineage, Pinnipedia. All true seals are members of the family Phocidae. They are sometimes called "crawling seals" to distinguish them from the fur seals and sea lions of the family Otariidae.

Characteristics

Earless seals have no external ear flaps (pinnae) — only a small ear opening — and, unlike eared seals, they cannot rotate their hind flippers forward under the body. On land they therefore move by wriggling or caterpillar-like undulation, while in the water the hind flippers, held out behind, provide the main propulsion, the foreflippers being used chiefly for steering. Their claws are generally smaller than those of otariids, and they are typically less vocal and less social ashore than sea lions.

Distribution and species

Phocids are widely distributed along coastlines of the northern hemisphere and around Antarctica. The family includes the small harbour and freshwater seals of the north (such as the Baikal seal and ringed seal), the grey seal, the crab-eating and leopard seals of the Southern Ocean, and the enormous elephant seals — southern elephant seal bulls may exceed 4,000 kilograms, making them the largest pinnipeds and the largest living members of the order Carnivora.

Evolution

The earliest known phocid fossils date to the middle Miocene, about 15 million years ago, in the North Atlantic. Pinnipeds as a whole descend from terrestrial carnivorans, with early forms such as Puijila illustrating the transition to an aquatic life.
