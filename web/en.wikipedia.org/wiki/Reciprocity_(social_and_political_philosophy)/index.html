Reciprocity (social and political philosophy)

Reciprocity in social and political philosophy is the concept that social and political relations ought to be arranged so that benefits and burdens are shared in a mutually responsive way: people who contribute to a cooperative scheme, or who comply with its rules, are entitled to fair treatment in return from those who benefit. It is a foundational idea in theories of justice, gift exchange, and democratic citizenship.

Anthropological foundations

The modern discussion draws on Marcel Mauss's The Gift (1925), which described the obligations to give, to receive and to reciprocate in gift economies, and on Marshall Sahlins's typology of generalised, balanced and negative reciprocity, distinguished by how directly and immediately a return is expected.

Political philosophy

John Rawls treated reciprocity as central to justice as fairness: citizens who abide by fair terms of cooperation are owed institutions that work to everyone's reasonable benefit, an idea lying "between impartiality and mutual advantage". Reciprocity also underlies defences of the welfare state (benefits are fair returns on social contribution), theories of punishment and restorative justice, and international relations concepts of tit-for-tat cooperation.

Criticism

Critics note that reciprocity can exclude those unable to reciprocate, such as people with severe disabilities, and that it may entrench transactional thinking where unconditional obligations are appropriate.
