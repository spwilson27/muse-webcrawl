Neuron doctrine

The neuron doctrine is a central principle of neuroscience holding that the nervous system is made up of discrete individual cells, the neurons, which are the structural and functional units of the brain, rather than a continuous reticulum of fused tissue. The doctrine was articulated principally by the Spanish histologist Santiago Ramón y Cajal in the late 19th century, building on the cell theory and on the work of Wilhelm His, Auguste Forel, and Fridtjof Nansen.

One of Ramón y Cajal's drawings of individual neurons.

Principles

In its classical formulation, the doctrine states that neurons are discrete cells, distinct from one another; that each neuron consists of a cell body, dendrites, and an axon; that neurons communicate across specialized contacts (later termed synapses by Charles Sherrington) rather than by cytoplasmic continuity; and that information flow within a neuron is polarized, from dendrites to axon — Cajal's law of dynamic polarization.

The debate with reticular theory

The doctrine opposed the reticular theory championed by Camillo Golgi, who held that the nervous system was a continuous network. Ironically, Cajal used Golgi's own silver-chromate staining method to produce the decisive evidence for discrete neurons. Golgi and Cajal shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine despite their opposing views. The discovery of the synaptic cleft by electron microscopy in the 1950s settled the question in favor of the neuron doctrine, though later findings — electrical synapses (gap junctions) and volume transmission — qualify its simplest form.
