Abdul Ghaffar Khan (6 February 1890 – 20 January 1988), known as Bacha Khan ("King of Chiefs"), was a Pashtun independence activist and spiritual leader who campaigned against British rule in India through nonviolent resistance. A close associate of Mahatma Gandhi, he was called the "Frontier Gandhi".

Khudai Khidmatgar

In 1929 Khan founded the Khudai Khidmatgar ("Servants of God"), known as the Red Shirts, a movement of Pashtuns pledged to nonviolence, social reform, and Hindu–Muslim unity. It grew to some 100,000 members and allied with the Indian National Congress. In April 1930, British troops fired on unarmed demonstrators in Peshawar's Qissa Khwani Bazaar, killing hundreds; the massacre became a landmark of the independence struggle.

Partition and Pakistan

Khan opposed the partition of India and demanded an autonomous Pashtunistan; he felt betrayed by Congress's acceptance of partition, remarking "you have thrown us to the wolves". After 1947 he was repeatedly imprisoned by the Pakistani state, spending roughly half of his remaining life in jail or exile — in all, about 37 years of his life in prison.

Later life and legacy

In 1987 India awarded him the Bharat Ratna, its highest civilian honour; he was the first non-Indian citizen to receive it. He died under house arrest in Peshawar in 1988 and was buried, as he wished, in Jalalabad, Afghanistan, where a ceasefire in the Soviet–Afghan war was observed for his funeral.
