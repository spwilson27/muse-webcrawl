Viceroy (butterfly)

Viceroy
Scientific nameLimenitis archippus
FamilyNymphalidae
RangeNorth America: Canada to Mexico
Wingspan53–81 mm
Host plantsWillows, poplars, aspens

The viceroy (Limenitis archippus) is a North American butterfly in the family Nymphalidae, long famous as a textbook example of mimicry: its orange wings crossed by black veins and marginal white spots closely resemble those of the monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus), differing most visibly in a thin black line that runs across the hindwings.

Mimicry debate

For much of the 20th century the viceroy was presented in textbooks as a harmless Batesian mimic of the supposedly toxic monarch, which predators avoid after tasting its milkweed-derived poisons. Experiments published in 1991 by David Ritland and Lincoln Brower, however, showed that viceroys are themselves unpalatable to birds — the viceroy's caterpillars feed on willows and accumulate bitter salicylic acid — leading to the relationship being reclassified as Müllerian mimicry between two defended species, or "co-mimicry". The finding is now a standard cautionary tale about assuming rather than testing textbook examples.

Life cycle and habitat

Viceroy caterpillars feed on trees in the willow family, including willows, poplars and aspens. Young larvae build a hibernaculum from a rolled leaf for winter shelter — unique among North American butterflies — and the caterpillars themselves are mottled olive-green and white, resembling bird droppings. Adults are found near wetlands, meadows and roadsides across most of the United States and southern Canada, flying from spring into autumn in two or three broods. In the southwestern United States and Mexico the viceroy mimics the queen butterfly instead of the monarch.
