History of circumcision

The history of circumcision — the surgical removal of the foreskin of the penis — spans prehistoric times to the present. Circumcision is one of the oldest surgical procedures, practised by ancient Egyptians, Semitic peoples, and various cultures in Africa, Australia and the Americas. It acquired central religious significance in Judaism, where it marks the covenant with Abraham, and in Islam. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, routine infant circumcision spread in English-speaking countries for purported medical and hygienic reasons, and its prevalence and medical justification remain debated.

A relief in the tomb of Ankhmahor at Saqqara (c. 2345–2182 BC) is among the earliest depictions of circumcision.

Ancient world

Cave paintings and Egyptian tomb reliefs dating to before 2300 BC depict the rite. The Greek historian Herodotus attributed the practice among Egyptians, Colchians, Ethiopians and Syrians to great antiquity. In the Hebrew Bible, Genesis 17 records circumcision on the eighth day of life as the sign of God's covenant with Abraham; the rite (brit milah) remains obligatory in Judaism. Philo of Alexandria and later rabbinic sources discuss its meaning and technique, including the later addition of periah (full exposure of the glans).

Greco-Roman opposition

Greeks and Romans regarded circumcision as a mutilation; Hellenised Jews sometimes sought surgical foreskin restoration (epispasm). Under Antiochus IV and later the emperor Hadrian, bans on circumcision helped provoke the Maccabean revolt and the Bar Kokhba revolt respectively. Early Christianity, following Paul's teaching, held circumcision unnecessary for Gentile converts, a position affirmed at the Council of Jerusalem (c. AD 50).

Islam and other traditions

Circumcision (khitan) is widely practised in Islam, though not commanded in the Qur'an; it is performed at ages varying from infancy to puberty. Ritual circumcision as an initiation rite is also traditional among many sub-Saharan African peoples, Aboriginal Australians, and Pacific Islanders.

Medicalisation in the modern era

In Victorian Britain and America, physicians such as Lewis Sayre promoted circumcision as a cure or preventive for masturbation, paralysis and other conditions. Routine neonatal circumcision became standard in the United States during the twentieth century, peaking around the 1960s–1980s, while rates remained low in most of Europe. From the 2000s, randomised trials in Africa showed circumcision reduces female-to-male HIV transmission risk, leading the WHO to recommend voluntary medical male circumcision in high-prevalence settings. Debates over infant rights, pain, and medical benefit continue.
