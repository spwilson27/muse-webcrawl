Battle of Falkirk

The Battle of Falkirk, fought on 22 July 1298, was a major engagement of the First War of Scottish Independence. The army of the Kingdom of Scotland, led by William Wallace, was defeated by the English army of King Edward I. The battle is noted for Edward's effective use of longbowmen against the Scottish schiltrons.

Falkirk, 1298

Background

After Wallace's victory at Stirling Bridge in 1297, Edward I returned from Flanders and invaded Scotland with a large army in the summer of 1298. Wallace pursued a scorched-earth policy, hoping to starve the English into retreat, but Edward's scouts located the Scottish army near Falkirk, where Wallace took up a defensive position.

The battle

Wallace deployed his spearmen in four great schiltrons — dense circular (or rectangular) hedgehog formations — with archers between them and a small body of cavalry in reserve. The Scottish cavalry fled early in the battle, and the Scottish archers were ridden down. English cavalry charges failed to break the schiltrons, but Edward brought up his longbowmen and Welsh and English archers, whose concentrated volleys, supported by crossbowmen and slingers, tore gaps in the static formations. The English cavalry and infantry then charged through the breaches and destroyed the schiltrons.

Aftermath

Wallace escaped the field but resigned as Guardian of Scotland shortly afterwards. Falkirk demonstrated the vulnerability of static spear formations to massed archery, a lesson Edward's grandson would apply in the Hundred Years' War; Scottish tactics later adapted, notably under Robert the Bruce at Bannockburn.
