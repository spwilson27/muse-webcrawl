Interest rate

[Image: a bank's illuminated sign displaying current interest rates for savers and borrowers]

An interest rate is the proportion of a loan charged as interest to the borrower, expressed as an annual percentage of the principal; it is the Price of borrowing Money and the reward for deferring consumption and bearing risk. Rates differ by borrower, maturity and security — from central-bank policy rates through government bond yields to credit-card rates above 20%.

Determination and effects

Rates equilibrate saving and Investment and are steered, at the short end, by central banks implementing Monetary policy: the Federal Reserve's federal funds rate, the Bank of England's Bank Rate, the ECB's deposit rate. The Fisher effect (Irving Fisher) decomposes nominal rates into a real rate plus expected Inflation. Changing rates ripple through mortgages, business investment, exchange rates and asset prices, with long and variable lags; at the effective lower bound near zero, central banks turned after 2008 to quantitative easing. Persistently negative policy rates in Europe and Japan in the 2010s, and the rapid hikes of 2022–23, mark the recent experimental range of the instrument.
