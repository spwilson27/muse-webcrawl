Endemism

Endemism is the state of a species being found only in a single defined geographic location, such as an island, habitat type, nation or other zone. Organisms indigenous to a place are not endemic to it if they also occur elsewhere. Endemic species are often of special conservation concern because their restricted range makes them vulnerable to habitat loss and introduced species.

Origins

Endemics arise through isolation and speciation. Paleoendemics are ancient lineages that were once widespread but now survive in a small refuge — the ginkgo tree and the tuatara are examples. Neoendemics have recently evolved in place and never spread, as with many island radiations: the lemurs of Madagascar, the finches and tortoises of the Galápagos, and the marsupials of Australia arose after long geographic isolation.

Hotspots

Islands and isolated mountain ranges have the highest rates of endemism. Madagascar, separated from mainland Africa for over 80 million years, has around 90 percent endemic plant and animal species. Biodiversity hotspots such as Madagascar, the California Floristic Province and the Tropical Andes are identified partly by their exceptional concentrations of endemic species.
