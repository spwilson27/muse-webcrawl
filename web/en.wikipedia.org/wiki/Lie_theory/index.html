Lie theory

Lie theory (pronounced "lee") is the branch of mathematics concerned with Lie groups, Lie algebras, and their representations. It was founded in the late 19th century by the Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie, who introduced continuous transformation groups in order to apply symmetry methods — analogous to Galois theory for algebraic equations — to differential equations.

History

Sophus Lie developed his theory between the 1870s and his death in 1899, working at times with Felix Klein and Friedrich Engel. His student Wilhelm Killing, and later Élie Cartan, achieved the classification of simple Lie algebras, one of the great accomplishments of early 20th-century mathematics. Hermann Weyl and others then developed the representation theory of semisimple Lie groups and algebras, showing in particular that finite-dimensional representations of semisimple Lie algebras are completely reducible.

Basic objects

A Lie group is a group that is also a smooth manifold, whose group operations are smooth. To each Lie group one associates its Lie algebra: the tangent space at the identity element, equipped with a bilinear operation called the Lie bracket. For matrix groups the bracket is the commutator of matrices. The exponential map connects the two structures, and much of the local structure of a Lie group is captured by its algebra, while representation theory studies linear actions of these objects on vector spaces.

Applications

Lie theory pervades modern mathematics and physics. It underlies the theory of special functions and differential equations, the classification programs in geometry, and the gauge theories of particle physics, where groups such as SU(2) and SU(3) describe fundamental interactions. It also plays central roles in number theory (through automorphic forms and the Langlands program), robotics, and control theory.
