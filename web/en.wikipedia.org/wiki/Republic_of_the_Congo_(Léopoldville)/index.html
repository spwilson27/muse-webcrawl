The Republic of the Congo (French: République du Congo) was a sovereign state in Central Africa, created upon independence from Belgium on 30 June 1960. It is commonly distinguished from its neighbour of the same name — the former French Congo centred on Brazzaville — by the parenthetical suffix of its capital, (Léopoldville). The country was renamed the Democratic Republic of the Congo in 1964 and, under Mobutu Sese Seko's authenticité campaign, became Zaire in 1971.

Independence and crisis

On Independence Day, 30 June 1960, before the Belgian King Baudouin, Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba delivered an unscheduled speech denouncing colonialism, while President Joseph Kasa-Vubu struck a conciliatory tone. Within days the Force Publique mutinied, Belgian troops intervened, and on 11 July Moise Tshombe declared the mineral-rich province of Katanga independent, backed by Belgian mining interests. The resulting Congo Crisis drew in the United Nations (ONUC), whose Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash while mediating in 1961.

Lumumba's fall and the Mobutu era begins

Dismissed by Kasa-Vubu in a constitutional crisis, Lumumba was arrested, transferred to Katanga, and murdered in January 1961 with Belgian complicity (established by a 2001 Belgian parliamentary inquiry). A rival Lumumbist government under Antoine Gizenga operated from Stanleyville until suppressed. In 1964–65 Kwilu and Simba rebellions swept the east, culminating in the hostage crisis at Stanleyville and rescue by Belgian paratroops (Operation Dragon Rouge). After years of parliamentary deadlock, Colonel Joseph-Désiré Mobutu seized power in a coup on 25 November 1965, beginning a 32-year rule. In 1966 the capital Léopoldville was renamed Kinshasa.
