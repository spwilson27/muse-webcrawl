Sperm

Sperm (from the Greek sperma, "seed") is the male reproductive cell, or gamete, in anisogamous forms of sexual reproduction. In animals, motile sperm cells with a whip-like tail (flagellum) are called spermatozoa, while some red algae and fungi produce non-motile sperm cells known as spermatia. A sperm cell contributes a haploid set of chromosomes to the egg to form a diploid zygote.

Structure

A typical animal spermatozoon consists of a head containing the nucleus and the acrosome (a cap of enzymes used to penetrate the egg), a midpiece packed with mitochondria that supply energy, and a long flagellum that propels the cell. Human sperm measure about 50 micrometres in length and are produced continuously in the testes by spermatogenesis.

Function

The primary function of sperm is to reach and fuse with the oocyte during fertilisation. In mammals, sperm must undergo capacitation in the female reproductive tract before they can penetrate the zona pellucida. The acrosome reaction releases enzymes that digest a path through the egg's outer layers.

Discovery

Spermatozoa were first observed in 1677 by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, who described the "animalcules" in semen under his microscope. For a time they were thought to contain a fully formed miniature human (the homunculus theory of preformationism).
