The random walk hypothesis is a financial theory stating that stock market prices evolve according to a random walk (so price changes are random) and thus cannot be predicted. It is consistent with the efficient-market hypothesis.

The concept can be traced to French broker Jules Regnault, who published a book in 1863, and to the French mathematician Louis Bachelier, whose 1900 PhD thesis "The Theory of Speculation" presented the first mathematical treatment of the idea. In 1953 Maurice Kendall presented the surprising result, in the paper "The Analysis of Economic Time-Series — Part 1: Prices", that changes in stock and commodity prices appeared to be random: each change was essentially independent of the last and uninfluenced by the pattern of past changes.

Core claim

In its strong popular form, the hypothesis holds that because prices already reflect all past price information — and, in the efficient-market version, all publicly available information — future price changes are determined only by genuinely new information, which arrives unpredictably. It follows that neither technical analysis (reading charts of past prices) nor fundamental analysis can systematically earn above-average risk-adjusted returns, and that a blindfolded monkey throwing darts at a stock page would do about as well as professional managers — an image made famous by Burton Malkiel's A Random Walk Down Wall Street, first published in 1973 and repeatedly reissued.

Evidence and criticism

Statistical tests generally find daily and weekly price changes close to serially independent, and most actively managed funds underperform index funds after fees over long horizons — evidence supporters cite for the hypothesis. Critics, including behavioural economists, point to phenomena such as momentum, mean reversion at longer horizons, the small-cap and value effects, bubbles and crashes, and the apparent excess volatility of prices relative to dividends, arguing that markets are predictable to a degree and that deviations from a pure random walk are systematic rather than accidental. Some empirical work argues that stock prices do not follow random walks at all, and that past returns carry usable information about future returns.

Even so, the hypothesis remains a benchmark in finance: strategies claiming to beat the market must demonstrate returns that cannot be explained by risk, chance, or the costs of trading.
