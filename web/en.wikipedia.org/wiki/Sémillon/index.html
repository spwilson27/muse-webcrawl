Sémillon (pronounced [semijɔ̃]) is a golden-skinned grape used to make dry and sweet white Bordeaux wines. Destined for Sauternes — where it makes up the majority of most blends — it was also, in the mid-20th century, one of the most planted white grape varieties in the world, before its area declined sharply; important plantings remain in France, Australia and South Africa.

Wine styles

Sémillon's thin skin makes it prone to Botrytis cinerea; under the right conditions this becomes "noble rot", concentrating sugars and flavours to produce the great sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac, where Sémillon is blended with Sauvignon blanc and Muscadelle. As a dry wine it is full-bodied, with low acidity and flavours of lemon, fig, lanolin and honey with age. In Australia's Hunter Valley, picked early at low alcohol, it produces austere wines that develop celebrated toast-and-honey complexity after long bottle ageing; South Africa (where it was once dominant, and known locally just as "the wine grape") also produces notable old-vine examples.

Viticulture

The variety is vigorous and productive, ripens relatively early, and keeps well, but its susceptibility to rot requires careful site selection. DNA profiling has shown few close relatives; plantings have declined in Bordeaux in favour of Sauvignon blanc, though Sémillon remains essential to the region's sweet wines and to dry Graves blends.
