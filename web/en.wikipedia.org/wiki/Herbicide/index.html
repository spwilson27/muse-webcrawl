Herbicide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Herbicides (US: /ˈɜːrbɪsaɪdz/, UK: /ˈhɜːr-/), also commonly known as weed killers, are substances used to control undesired plants, also known as weeds. Selective herbicides control specific weed species while leaving the desired crop relatively unharmed, while non-selective herbicides kill most plant material they contact and are used commercially to clear industrial sites, railways, and waste ground.

History

Before synthetic herbicides, weed control relied on hand labour, tillage, and inorganic salts. The modern era began with 2,4-D, introduced in the 1940s as the first widely successful selective herbicide. Later major classes include the triazines (atrazine), the bipyridiniums (paraquat), and glyphosate (marketed as Roundup from 1974), which became the world's most-used herbicide, especially after the introduction of glyphosate-resistant crops in 1996.

Mode of action

Herbicides are classified by their biochemical target, including:

  • Photosynthesis inhibitors (e.g., atrazine, diuron)
  • EPSP synthase inhibitors (glyphosate)
  • Synthetic auxins (2,4-D, dicamba)
  • ACCase and ALS inhibitors, widely used in cereal and oilseed crops
  • Pigment and membrane disruptors (e.g., paraquat)

Use and concerns

Herbicides transformed agriculture by enabling reduced tillage and large increases in labour productivity. Concerns include the evolution of herbicide-resistant weeds, water contamination (notably atrazine), and health and ecological effects debated for glyphosate and paraquat. They are regulated as pesticides in most countries. Military use as defoliants — most notoriously Agent Orange in Vietnam — remains controversial.
