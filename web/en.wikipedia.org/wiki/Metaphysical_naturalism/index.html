Metaphysical naturalism

Metaphysical naturalism (also called ontological naturalism, philosophical naturalism, and scientific materialism) is a philosophical worldview which holds that there is nothing but natural elements, principles, and relations of the kind studied by the natural sciences. It holds that supernatural entities and phenomena — such as gods, souls, and miracles — do not exist, and that all phenomena, including mind and consciousness, can in principle be explained in natural terms.

Definition

Metaphysical naturalism is distinguished from methodological naturalism, which is the working assumption of science that phenomena should be investigated as if they had natural causes, without committing to the claim that supernatural causes are impossible. The metaphysical naturalist goes further, asserting that the natural world is all that exists. The position is associated with physicalism, the view that everything that exists is physical or supervenes on the physical.

History

Ideas resembling metaphysical naturalism appear in ancient Greek atomism (Democritus, Epicurus) and in Lucretius's poem De rerum natura. In the modern era, figures such as Paul-Henri Thiry, Baron d'Holbach defended explicit materialism in the 18th century. The position gained prominence in the 20th century through philosophers such as W. V. O. Quine, and through scientific advances in cosmology, evolution, and neuroscience that were taken to reduce the explanatory space for supernatural hypotheses.

Arguments and criticisms

Supporters typically appeal to the track record of naturalistic explanation, parsimony (Occam's razor), and the causal closure of the physical world. Critics — including religious philosophers and proponents of dualism — raise objections concerning consciousness (the "hard problem"), intentionality, mathematical and moral truths, and the origin of the universe, arguing that naturalism cannot account for these without remainder. Other critics question whether a precise definition of "natural" can be given that neither excludes parts of physics nor becomes vacuous.
