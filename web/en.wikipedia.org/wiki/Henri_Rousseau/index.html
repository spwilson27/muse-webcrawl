Henri Julien Félix Rousseau (21 May 1844 – 2 September 1910) was a French post-impressionist painter in the Naïve or Primitive manner. He was also known as Le Douanier ("the customs officer"), a humorous description of his occupation as a toll and tax collector. Largely self-taught, he began painting seriously in his early forties and took early retirement at 49 to work on his art full time.

Work

Ridiculed by critics during his lifetime, Rousseau came to be recognised as a self-taught genius whose work exerted a wide influence on modern art. His best-known paintings depict lush jungle scenes — although he never left France or saw a jungle; his vision was fed by the botanical gardens and taxidermy displays of Paris, illustrated books, and stories told by soldiers who had served in Mexico. Works such as The Sleeping Gypsy (1897), The Snake Charmer (1907), The Hungry Lion Throws Itself on the Antelope (1905), and his final painting The Dream (1910) combine meticulous detail with a dreamlike, flattened space.

Legacy

Rousseau exhibited at the Salon des Indépendants from 1886 and was championed by younger avant-garde artists; Picasso held a famous banquet in his honour in 1908. His art influenced the Surrealists and generations of "outsider" and naïve painters. He died in Paris in 1910 and was buried in the Cimetière de Bagneux.
