Old quantum theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of Sommerfeld's elliptical electron orbits extending the Bohr model]
Sommerfeld's relativistic elliptical orbits (1916).

The old quantum theory is a collection of results obtained between about 1900 and 1925 which predate modern quantum mechanics. Never complete or self-consistent, it was an amalgam of classical mechanics with ad hoc quantisation rules for periodic motions — most famously Bohr's quantised orbits in the Bohr model (1913) and the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantisation conditions (1915–16).

The old quantum theory explained the hydrogen spectrum, the Stark and Zeeman effects (partly) and, via the Einstein and Debye models, specific heats of solids. But it failed on helium, on molecular bonding, on transition intensities, and on the anomalous Zeeman effect — accumulating contradictions that drove Werner Heisenberg, Max Born, Erwin Schrödinger and Paul Dirac to construct full quantum mechanics in 1925–26, in which the old results re-emerged as limiting cases. Einstein's 1917 radiation theory (A and B coefficients) and the Bohr–Kramers–Slater episode (1924) mark the period's creative ferment.

Key milestones

1900: Planck's quanta (E = hν) for Black-body radiation. 1905: Einstein's light quanta and the Photoelectric effect. 1913: Bohr's atomic model and Balmer spectrum. 1916: Sommerfeld's relativistic fine structure — where α, the Fine-structure constant, first appeared. 1923: Compton's photon scattering. 1924: de Broglie's matter waves and Bose's statistics. The semiclassical (WKB) descendants of the old theory remain in use today.
