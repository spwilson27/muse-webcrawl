Public debt

Public debt, also called government debt or sovereign debt, is the total stock of financial liabilities owed by a government, accumulated through past budget deficits. It is usually expressed as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP) so that countries and periods can be compared. Public debt differs from a deficit, which is a flow over one year; debt is the accumulated stock.

Measurement and instruments

Debt may be measured gross or net of government financial assets, and by sector — central government only or general government including local authorities and social security funds. Governments borrow by issuing securities: short-term treasury bills, medium- and long-term bonds (such as UK gilts and US Treasury notes), inflation-indexed bonds, and through loans. Debt held in a country's own currency by domestic residents is generally considered less risky than foreign-currency or externally held debt, because the state can tax, refinance, or in extremis inflate — though each carries costs.

Sustainability

The debt ratio evolves according to the difference between the interest rate on debt (r) and the growth rate of the economy (g), together with the primary budget balance. When r is persistently below g, governments can run modest primary deficits while the debt ratio falls; when r exceeds g, primary surpluses are needed to stabilise the ratio. Concerns include crowding out of private investment, intergenerational transfers, rollover risk, and sovereign default, as in the euro-area debt crisis of 2010–12.

History

Modern public debt developed with funded national debts in the Dutch Republic and, after 1694, England, where the Bank of England and a market in long-term annuities lowered borrowing costs and financed war. Debt ratios peaked after the Second World War — above 250% of GDP in the United Kingdom — and declined through growth and inflation before rising again after the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.
