Ludwig Heinrich Edler von Mises (29 September 1881 – 10 October 1973) was an Austrian-American economist, historian, and philosopher, and a leading figure of the Austrian School of economics. He developed praxeology, a deductive science of human action, and is known for the economic calculation argument against socialist central planning, his theory of money and credit, and his magnum opus Human Action (1949).

Life

Born in Lemberg (now Lviv) into a Jewish family ennobled in the Habsburg monarchy, Mises studied law and economics at the University of Vienna, where he attended the seminars of Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk. His Theory of Money and Credit (1912) integrated marginal utility theory with monetary economics. After the First World War he became an economic adviser to the Austrian Chamber of Commerce and ran an influential private seminar in Vienna attended by economists including Friedrich Hayek and Fritz Machlup. In 1934 he moved to Geneva; in 1940 he emigrated to the United States, where he taught at New York University from 1945 to 1969. The Ludwig von Mises Institute, founded in 1982 in Auburn, Alabama, promotes his work.

Ideas

In Socialism (1922) Mises argued that without private ownership of the means of production there can be no genuine market prices for capital goods, and hence no rational economic calculation — a thesis debated with market socialists such as Oskar Lange. His business-cycle theory attributed booms and busts to credit expansion by central banks. Human Action presented economics as a branch of praxeology, derived from the axiom that humans act purposefully.
