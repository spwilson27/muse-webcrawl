The Trilateral Commission is a non-governmental, non-partisan discussion group founded in 1973 by banker David Rockefeller and political scientist Zbigniew Brzezinski. It brings together senior political, business, academic, and media figures from three regions — North America, Europe, and the Asia-Pacific — to promote cooperation among the world's leading industrialized democracies. The Commission publishes reports (the "Triangle Papers"), holds annual plenary meetings, and organizes regional conferences; its members serve in a private capacity and its deliberations are generally held off the record.

History

The Commission grew out of discussions at the Bilderberg meetings and within the Council on Foreign Relations, where Rockefeller and others argued that Japan should be included in elite transatlantic dialogue and that closer coordination among the United States, Western Europe, and Japan was needed in an era of economic interdependence, strained by the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the 1973 oil crisis. Brzezinski, then a professor at Columbia University, became its first director. The founding meeting took place in Tokyo in October 1973.

The Commission attracted wider attention when many of its American members joined the administration of President Jimmy Carter, himself a member, with Brzezinski serving as National Security Advisor. Its 1975 report The Crisis of Democracy (by Michel Crozier, Samuel P. Huntington, and Joji Watanuki) argued that Western democracies faced a crisis of governability amid an "excess of democracy" — a thesis that drew lasting criticism from the left.

Organization and membership

Membership is divided among three regional groups: the North American group (United States, Canada, and later Mexico), the European group, and the Asia-Pacific group (centred on Japan, later joined by South Korea, Australia, China, India, and Southeast Asian countries). Members are drawn from government, business, finance, academia, labour, and the media; serving senior government officials generally do not hold membership while in office. The Commission is funded by foundations, corporations, and individual contributions.

Criticism and conspiracy theories

Critics from across the political spectrum have portrayed the Commission as an undemocratic elite steering global policy toward corporate and free-market ends; it also features prominently in conspiracy theories about a "new world order" and world government. The Commission itself describes its role as fostering dialogue and understanding, noting that it takes no policy positions as an organization and that its reports reflect their authors' views.
