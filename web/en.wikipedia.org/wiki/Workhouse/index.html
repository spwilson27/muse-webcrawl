Workhouse

A workhouse was a publicly supported institution in Britain and Ireland where those unable to support themselves were offered accommodation and employment in return for labour. Workhouses were part of the Old Poor Law system from the 17th century and were systematised by the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, which aimed to deter dependency by making conditions inside the workhouse deliberately worse than those of the lowest-paid independent labourer — the principle of "less eligibility."

History

The Workhouse Test Act of 1723 allowed parishes to deny relief to anyone refusing to enter a workhouse. After the 1834 Act, parishes were grouped into Poor Law Unions, each building a union workhouse administered by a board of guardians. Inmates were classified (aged and infirm, able-bodied, children) and segregated; families were split by sex and age. Work included stone-breaking, oakum picking, corn milling, and laundry.

Conditions

Life was deliberately austere: uniforms, plain diets of bread, gruel, and cheese, silence at meals, and strict discipline. Scandals such as the Andover workhouse scandal of 1845 — where starving inmates gnawed the bones they were crushing for fertiliser — led to reforms and national inspection. Workhouses nevertheless provided most institutional care of the era: hospitals, infirmaries, schools, and orphanages often grew up inside them.

Decline and legacy

The Local Government Act 1929 abolished boards of guardians and converted workhouses into municipal hospitals and public assistance institutions. The system formally ended with the National Assistance Act 1948, though many buildings continued as hospitals or care homes. Workhouses loom large in social history and literature, most famously in Charles Dickens's Oliver Twist (1838).
