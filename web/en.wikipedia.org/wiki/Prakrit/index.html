Prakrit

Prakrit (Sanskrit: प्राकृत, prākṛta, "natural, ordinary") is a group of vernacular Middle Indo-Aryan languages that were spoken and written in the Indian subcontinent from roughly the 3rd century BCE to the 8th century CE. The term contrasts with Sanskrit (saṃskṛta, "perfected, refined"), the learned classical language: where Sanskrit was codified by grammarians, the Prakrits were the spoken languages of everyday life, later themselves formalised for literary and religious use.

Varieties

Major literary Prakrits include Maharashtri, the language of lyric poetry; Shauraseni, used in classical Sanskrit drama for prose dialogue; Magadhi, associated with the east; and Ardhamagadhi and Jain Prakrit, used in the scriptures of Jainism. Pali, the language of the Theravada Buddhist canon, is closely related and often grouped with the Prakrits. The edicts of the emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) were inscribed in regional Prakrits across his empire, providing some of the earliest written records of any Indo-Aryan language.

Features

Compared with Sanskrit, the Prakrits show extensive phonological simplification: consonant clusters were assimilated or reduced, intervocalic stops were voiced, weakened or lost, and the complex Sanskrit inflectional system was eroded. Grammar was progressively simplified, anticipating the transition to the later Apabhramsha stage and eventually to the modern Indo-Aryan languages such as Hindi, Bengali, and Marathi.

Literature

Prakrit literature includes the Gatha Sattasai, an anthology of Maharashtri love lyrics attributed to the Satavahana king Hala; Jain canonical and narrative texts; and the speeches of women and lower-status characters in Sanskrit plays, a convention reflecting the languages' vernacular character.
