Meteorite fall

A meteorite fall, also called an observed fall, is a meteorite collected after its arrival on Earth was observed by people or automated devices. Any other meteorite is called a "meteorite find". There are more than 1,100 documented falls listed in widely used databases, most from the modern era — the Meteoritical Bulletin Database contains over a thousand observed falls among tens of thousands of catalogued meteorites.

Importance

Observed falls are prized by science because they are collected soon after landing and are minimally contaminated by terrestrial weathering. A witnessed fall also allows the meteorite's orbit to be reconstructed when the fireball is recorded by cameras, as with the Příbram (1959), Lost City (1970), Innisfree (1977), and Peekskill (1992) meteorites.

Notable falls

  • Ensisheim (1492, Alsace) — one of the oldest documented falls in the Western world.
  • L'Aigle (1803, France) — investigated by Jean-Baptiste Biot, it helped establish the extraterrestrial origin of meteorites.
  • Hraschina (1751, Croatia) and Chassigny (1815, France) — early scientifically studied falls; Chassigny gives its name to a class of Martian meteorites.
  • Allende (1969, Mexico) — a vast carbonaceous chondrite fall, extensively studied.
  • Murchison (1969, Australia) — famous for its rich inventory of organic compounds, including amino acids.
  • Chelyabinsk (2013, Russia) — a superbolide that injured over 1,000 people, mostly from broken glass, with many recovered fragments.
  • Peekskill (1992, United States) — a fall widely filmed by camcorders; a fragment struck a parked car.
