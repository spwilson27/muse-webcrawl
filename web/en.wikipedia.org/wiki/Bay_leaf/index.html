Bay leaf

Source plant: Laurus nobilis
Form: Whole dried or fresh leaves
Use: Culinary herb and flavouring

Bay leaf is an aromatic leaf commonly used as a herb in cooking. It can be used whole, either dried or fresh, in which case it is removed from the dish before consumption, or less commonly used in ground form. The flavour that a bay leaf adds to a dish has not been universally agreed upon, but many agree it is a subtle addition.

Bay leaves come primarily from the bay laurel tree, Laurus nobilis. Other plants with leaves known as "bay leaves" include the California bay laurel (Umbellularia californica), the Indian bay leaf (Cinnamomum tamala), the Indonesian bay leaf (Syzygium polyanthum), the West Indian bay leaf (Pimenta racemosa), and the Mexican bay leaf (Litsea glaucescens).

Taste and aroma

The leaves contain the essential oil eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), along with eugenol and methyl eugenol, which give the leaf its distinctive aroma. If eaten whole, bay leaves are pungent and sharp; when dried, the fragrance is herbal and slightly floral, somewhat similar to oregano and thyme. They are commonly used to flavour soups, stews, braises, rice dishes, and sauces, and are a component of the classic French bouquet garni together with parsley and thyme.

History and folklore

In ancient Greece and Rome, the bay laurel was sacred to the god Apollo, and wreaths of bay leaves were awarded to victors in athletic contests and used to crown poets and scholars — the origin of terms such as "poet laureate" and "baccalaureate". In folklore, bay leaves were placed in food stores to repel insects and were believed to ward off lightning and evil.

Culinary use

Bay leaves are used in cuisines around the Mediterranean, in Indian cooking (particularly in biryani and garam masala blends), in Filipino adobo, and in many European soups and stews. Because the stiff leaves do not soften during cooking, they are removed before serving. Ground bay leaf must be used sparingly, as the flavour can be overpowering.
