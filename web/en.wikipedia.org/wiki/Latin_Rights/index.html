Latin Rights

Latin Rights (Latin: ius Latii or ius latinum) were a civic status granted by ancient Rome to certain communities, originally the Latins of Latium and later to peoples across Italy and the provinces. Latin status stood between full Roman citizenship and the status of a foreigner (peregrinus).

Content of the rights

Holders typically enjoyed commercium (legal capacity to own property and make contracts under Roman law), conubium (legal intermarriage with Romans) in some grants, and—crucially—the right for local magistrates to acquire full Roman citizenship by holding office (ius adipiscendae civitatis per magistratum). Latins could also migrate to Rome and under early rules gain citizenship by residence, a provision later restricted.

History

After the Latin War (340–338 BC), Rome settled allies in Latin colonies with this status. Vespasian famously granted Latin rights to all communities of Spain around AD 74, accelerating municipalisation and Romanisation, a process documented by the Flavian municipal laws such as the Lex Irnitana. After the Constitutio Antoniniana of AD 212 granted citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants, Latin status became largely obsolete, though a residual form (Junian Latins) applied to informally freed slaves.
