Peacekeeping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Peacekeeping is a technique designed to preserve peace where fighting has been halted, and to assist in implementing agreements achieved by peacemakers. In its dominant modern form it is conducted by the United Nations: uniformed personnel ("Blue Helmets") and civilian staff deployed with the consent of the parties, impartiality, and non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate — the three classic principles articulated under Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld during the first emergency force deployment (UNEF, Suez 1956).

History and evolution

Early missions observed ceasefires (Kashmir, Palestine); the Congo operation (ONUC, 1960–64) previewed enforcement entanglements, and Cyprus (UNFICYP, 1964–) exemplified long-duration interposition. The post-Cold War surge produced triumphs (Namibia, El Salvador, Mozambique, East Timor) and catastrophic failures — Rwanda (UNAMIR, 1994) and Srebrenica (UNPROFOR, 1995) — prompting the Brahimi Report (2000) and a turn to multidimensional missions with civilian protection, policing, elections, disarmament and state-building mandates, and more robust rules of engagement. Regional organisations (AU, EU, NATO coalitions) also mount peace operations, usually with UN authorisation.

Assessment

Quantitative studies find UN peacekeeping substantially reduces the recurrence and civilian toll of civil wars when adequately resourced; persistent problems include funding (assessed contributions with chronic arrears), troop quality and misconduct (including sexual exploitation scandals addressed by zero-tolerance policies), host-state consent withdrawals (Mali's MINUSMA ended in 2023), and Security Council politics shaping mandates.
