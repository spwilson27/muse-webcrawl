Céide Fields

Locationnear Ballycastle, County Mayo, Ireland
PeriodNeolithic, c. 3700–3200 BC
Areaabout 1,500 hectares of preserved field system
Discovered1930s; excavated from the 1970s (Seamas Caulfield)

The Céide Fields (Irish: Achaidh Chéide) is an archaeological site in County Mayo, Ireland, where the remains of a Neolithic farming landscape — stone field walls, houses, and megalithic tombs — have been preserved beneath a blanket of peat bog. Dating to roughly 5,700 years ago, it is considered the oldest known enclosed field system in the world and one of the most extensive Stone Age monuments anywhere, extending over some 1,500 hectares along the north Mayo coast near Ballycastle.

Discovery

The walls were first noticed in the 1930s by Patrick Caulfield, a local schoolteacher cutting turf, who observed regular courses of stones deep below the bog surface. His son, archaeologist Seamas Caulfield, investigated the site from the 1970s, using iron probes to trace the buried walls over many kilometres and demonstrating that the bog concealed a complete prehistoric landscape of small rectangular fields — evidence that Neolithic Ireland supported an organised farming community far larger and more sophisticated than previously believed.

The landscape

The Céide Fields comprise miles of parallel stone walls forming a coaxial field system of long rectangular grazing enclosures, together with the foundations of houses and several megalithic tombs. Pollen analysis of the bog shows that the farmers cleared a pine forest to create pasture for cattle around 3700 BC; within several centuries the climate grew wetter, blanket bog spread over the fields, and the farmland was abandoned and preserved under metres of peat. The visitor centre, opened in 1993 and distinguished by its glass pyramid, presents the archaeology and preserves in place a section of excavated wall and a pine tree grown from the ancient bog pine preserved on the site.

Significance

The site transformed understanding of Neolithic society in Ireland and Britain, demonstrating large-scale cooperative land division at a very early date. The Céide Fields are managed by the Office of Public Works and are on Ireland's tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage status.
