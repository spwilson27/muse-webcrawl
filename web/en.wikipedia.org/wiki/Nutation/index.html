Nutation

Nutation (from Latin nūtātiō, "a nodding") is a small, irregular wobbling in the axis of rotation of a spinning object, superimposed on its precession. The term is used in two main contexts: the nodding motion of the Earth's axis, and the analogous motion of gyroscopes and other rigid rotors in classical mechanics. (In botany, "nutation" also names the bending movements of growing plant organs.)

Astronomical nutation

The Earth's axis precesses with a period of about 25,800 years, mainly under the gravitational pull of the Sun and Moon on the Earth's equatorial bulge. Nutation is the smaller, faster ripple on that motion, discovered by English astronomer James Bradley in 1728 (and published in 1748 after a full cycle had been observed). Its largest component has a period of 18.6 years — the regression period of the Moon's orbital nodes — and an amplitude of about 9.2 arcseconds in obliquity and 17 arcseconds in longitude. Smaller terms arise from the varying distances and positions of the Sun and Moon; modern models, computed by the International Astronomical Union, sum hundreds of periodic terms. Nutation must be taken into account to convert between the true and mean positions of celestial objects and in precise pointing of telescopes and spacecraft antennas.

Mechanical nutation

For a symmetric spinning top or gyroscope, nutation is the oscillation in the tilt angle that occurs when the rotation is disturbed: the axis nods up and down as it precesses, settling into steady (sleeping-top) precession as friction damps the nutation. It appears in the Euler equations of rigid-body dynamics and in devices such as swashplates and nutating (wobble-plate) engines and pumps.
