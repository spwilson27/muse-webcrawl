Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio, or Liaozhai Zhiyi (聊齋誌異, "Strange Tales from the Studio of Idle Talk"), is a collection of nearly five hundred marvel tales in the zhiguai and chuanqi traditions, written in Classical Chinese by Pu Songling (1640–1715) during the early Qing dynasty.

Contents and themes

The tales—often very short—feature fox spirits, ghosts, scholars, corrupt officials, Taoist adepts and magical transformations. Famous stories include "The Painted Skin" (畫皮), in which a demon disguises itself in a painted human skin; "Nie Xiaoqian" (聶小倩), a romance between a scholar and a female ghost; and "The Cricket" (促織). Beneath the supernatural surface, Pu satirised the corruption of the imperial examination system and the bureaucracy that repeatedly failed him—he never rose beyond the lowest degree despite a lifetime of trying—and celebrated love, friendship and moral integrity.

Reception and adaptations

Circulating in manuscript during Pu's lifetime, the collection was first printed in 1766 and has remained a cornerstone of Chinese literature, frequently adapted for stage, film and television; "Nie Xiaoqian" inspired the Hong Kong film A Chinese Ghost Story (1987) and "Painted Skin" a 2008 film. Major English translations include Herbert Giles's Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio (1880) and John Minford's Penguin Classics selection (2006).
