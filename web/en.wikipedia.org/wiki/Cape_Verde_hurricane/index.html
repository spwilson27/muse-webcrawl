Cape Verde hurricane

Image placeholder: A Cape Verde-type hurricane over the eastern Atlantic.

A Cape Verde hurricane is an Atlantic tropical cyclone that originates in the deep tropics of the eastern Atlantic Ocean, typically from a tropical wave that emerges off the west coast of Africa near the Cape Verde islands. Such storms are among the most intense and long-lived Atlantic hurricanes.

Formation

During the peak of the Atlantic hurricane season — typically from mid-August through September — easterly waves moving off Africa encounter warm ocean waters and favourable atmospheric conditions in the Main Development Region. Because these systems have thousands of kilometres of open ocean over which to travel, they often strengthen steadily into major hurricanes (Category 3 or higher on the Saffir–Simpson scale) before approaching the Caribbean or North America, or recurving out to sea.

Notable examples

Many of the most destructive Atlantic hurricanes were Cape Verde storms, including the 1900 Galveston hurricane, Hurricane Hugo (1989), Hurricane Andrew (1992), Hurricane Ivan (2004), Hurricane Irma (2017), and Hurricane Dorian (2019). On average, a few Cape Verde-type storms form each year.

Climatology

Cape Verde hurricanes account for a disproportionate share of major hurricanes in the Atlantic basin. Their long tracks make them the archetypal "long-track" hurricanes of late summer, and their development is closely linked to the West African monsoon and the absence of disruptive Saharan air outbreaks.
