Barbarossa decree

The Barbarossa decree (formally the "Decree on the Jurisdiction of Martial Law and on Special Measures of the Troops," German: Erlass über die Ausübung der Kriegsgerichtsbarkeit im Gebiet "Barbarossa" und über besondere Maßnahmen der Truppe) was a criminal order issued by the German Wehrmacht High Command on 13 May 1941, shortly before Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union. It removed the prosecution of crimes committed by German soldiers against Soviet civilians from military courts and authorized collective punitive measures.

Contents

The decree, signed by Wilhelm Keitel on Hitler's authority, provided that offenses committed by enemy civilians in the operational area were to be dealt with summarily—suspects could be shot on the spot by an officer—rather than through courts-martial. It explicitly removed any obligation to prosecute German personnel for offenses against Soviet civilians, even where the act constituted a military crime, leaving punishment to the discretion of commanders. It also ordered that entire villages could be subjected to collective measures where the individual perpetrator of an attack could not be identified.

Context and consequences

Together with the Commissar Order of June 1941 and the guidelines for the conduct of troops in Russia, the decree formed part of the planned "war of annihilation" (Vernichtungskrieg) in the East, deliberately suspending the laws and customs of war. Historians regard it as giving advance legal cover for mass violence against Soviet civilians, contributing to the deaths of millions. At the Nuremberg High Command Trial, the decree was presented as evidence of the criminal orders passed down the Wehrmacht chain of command.
