Compact Disc Digital Audio

CD-DA discs store stereo audio as 16-bit PCM sampled at 44.1 kHz.

Compact Disc Digital Audio (CD-DA, also called Red Book) is the standard format for audio compact discs. The standard is defined in a document originally published in 1980 by the format's creators, Philips and Sony, and has been maintained by the IEC as standard IEC 60908. It was the first standard in the Rainbow Books series of optical disc specifications.

Format details

Audio is stored as two-channel linear PCM sampled at 44,100 Hz with 16 bits per sample, giving a bit rate of 1,411.2 kbit/s. This sample rate was chosen to satisfy the Nyquist criterion for the 20 kHz upper limit of human hearing, with allowance for anti-aliasing filters. A standard disc holds up to about 74–80 minutes of audio; the oft-told story holds that 74 minutes was chosen so that Beethoven's Ninth Symphony would fit on a single disc. Data is protected by cross-interleaved Reed–Solomon coding (CIRC), enabling correction of scratches and manufacturing defects.

History

The first commercial CD players and discs were released in Japan in October 1982, reaching Europe and North America in 1983. The format rapidly displaced the vinyl LP and cassette as the dominant medium for recorded music in the late 1980s, and remained so until downloads and streaming took over in the 2000s and 2010s.

Extensions and related formats

Extensions to Red Book include CD-Text, which embeds album and track titles, and multi-session formats such as CD-Extra. Copy-protection schemes introduced in the early 2000s deliberately violated the standard, causing compatibility problems, and such discs were not permitted to carry the Compact Disc logo.
