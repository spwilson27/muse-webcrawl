Hungarian invasions of Europe

The Hungarian invasions of Europe (Hungarian: kalandozások, German: Ungarneinfälle) took place in the 9th and 10th centuries, during the transition that followed the Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin. Between roughly 899 and 970 the Magyars launched a long series of plundering raids — traditionally counted at some fifty campaigns — against East and West Francia, Italy, the Balkans, and even Iberia and Anatolia. The period is sometimes grouped with the Viking and Saracen raids as part of the last wave of barbarian invasions of Europe.

Hungarian campaigns reached from Constantinople to the Atlantic coast of Iberia.

Background

The Magyars, a steppe people of mounted archers, settled in the Carpathian Basin around 895–896 under Árpád. Their raids combined plunder, tribute extraction, and mercenary service for rival rulers: in 899 they devastated northern Italy as allies of Emperor Arnulf against King Berengar I, crushing the Italian army at the Battle of the Brenta. Their victory over an East Frankish army at the Battle of Pressburg in 907 secured the new homeland.

Course of the raids

Hungarian armies penetrated deep into Germany (to Saxony and Thuringia), France (to Burgundy, Lorraine, and as far as Nîmes), Italy (to Apulia), and the Byzantine Balkans, where they repeatedly extracted tribute from Constantinople. Their tactics relied on speed, feigned retreat, and devastating archery from composite bows. A turning point came in 933, when Henry I of Germany defeated them at the Battle of Riade after years of tribute payments and military reform. Raids continued, however, until the decisive Battle of Lechfeld on 10 August 955, where Otto I destroyed a Hungarian army near Augsburg; the surviving commanders were executed, and large-scale western raiding ended. Raiding against Byzantium continued until a heavy defeat in 970 at the Battle of Arcadiopolis.

Aftermath

In the later 10th century the Hungarians settled into an agricultural, Christianising kingdom under Géza and Stephen I, transforming from a raiding confederation into a European monarchy integrated into Latin Christendom.
