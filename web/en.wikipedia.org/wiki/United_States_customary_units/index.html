United States customary units

United States customary units form a system of measurement units commonly used in the United States since its formalization in 1832, derived from English units used in the British Empire before American independence.

U.S. customary units
Length: inch, foot, yard, mile
Volume: fluid ounce, pint, quart, gallon
Mass: ounce, pound, ton

Units

Length is measured in inches, feet (12 inches), yards (3 feet), and miles (5,280 feet). Liquid volume uses fluid ounces, cups, pints, quarts, and the gallon (231 cubic inches). Weight commonly uses ounces and pounds (16 ounces), with the short ton of 2,000 pounds. Temperature is usually Fahrenheit.

Relation to SI

Since the Mendenhall Order of 1893 and the international yard and pound agreement of 1959, customary units are defined exactly in terms of the metric system: 1 inch = 25.4 mm exactly, 1 pound = 0.45359237 kg. The United States is one of few countries where metric units are not the primary everyday system, though they dominate science, medicine, and much industry.

History

Attempts at metrication, including the Metric Conversion Act of 1975, made conversion voluntary. Road signs, weather, and cooking remain largely customary.
