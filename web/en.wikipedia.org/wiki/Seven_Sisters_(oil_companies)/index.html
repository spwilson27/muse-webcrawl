Seven Sisters (oil companies)

The Seven Sisters were seven transnational oil companies that dominated the global petroleum industry from the mid-1940s to the late 1970s. The term was popularised by the Italian state oil chief Enrico Mattei, who jokingly called the Anglo-Saxon majors le sette sorelle ("the seven sisters") after his own company, Eni, was excluded from their consortium arrangements.

The seven companies

  • Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (now BP)
  • Gulf Oil (later merged into Chevron)
  • Royal Dutch Shell
  • Standard Oil of California (SoCal, now Chevron)
  • Standard Oil of New Jersey (Esso, now ExxonMobil)
  • Standard Oil of New York (Socony, now ExxonMobil)
  • Texaco (later merged into Chevron)

Dominance and decline

Through interlocking joint ventures, concession agreements and the Achnacarry "As-Is" Agreement of 1928, the sisters controlled the great majority of oil reserves, refining capacity and marketing networks outside North America and the Soviet bloc. Their power began to wane with the founding of OPEC in 1960 and the wave of nationalisations in the 1970s, when producing countries seized majority control of their concessions, as in Saudi Arabia, Libya and Iran.

Through mergers, the seven companies eventually consolidated into four supermajors: BP, Chevron, ExxonMobil and Shell. Commentators have since applied the phrase "new Seven Sisters" to the most influential national oil companies outside the OECD, such as Saudi Aramco, Gazprom, CNPC, the National Iranian Oil Company, PDVSA, Petrobras and Petronas.
