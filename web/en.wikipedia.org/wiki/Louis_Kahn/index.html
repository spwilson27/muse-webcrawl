Louis Isadore Kahn (born Itze-Leib Schmuilowsky; February 20, 1901 – March 17, 1974) was an Estonian-born American architect based in Philadelphia. After working in various capacities for several firms in Philadelphia, he founded his own atelier in 1935. Kahn was a professor of architecture at Yale University from 1947 to 1957 and later at the University of Pennsylvania.

Although attaining a measure of international fame late in life, Kahn is recognized as one of the most influential architects of the 20th century. His works are considered monumental and monolithic, beyond the modernist and International Style movements; heavy, often largely unadorned buildings constructed of brick and poured concrete, with powerful geometric forms and carefully modulated natural light.

Major works

Kahn's first celebrated building was the Yale University Art Gallery (1951–1953). His mature style emerged with the Alfred Newton Richards Medical Research Building at the University of Pennsylvania (1957–1965). The Salk Institute for Biological Studies in La Jolla, California (1959–1965), arranged as two mirror-image laboratory blocks flanking a travertine plaza cut by a narrow channel of water, is widely considered his masterpiece. Other major works include the Phillips Exeter Academy Library (1965–1972), the Kimbell Art Museum in Fort Worth (1966–1972), with its cycloid barrel vaults and reflected light, and the vast National Assembly Building (Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban) in Dhaka, Bangladesh, completed after his death in 1982.

Death and legacy

Kahn died of a heart attack in the men's room of Pennsylvania Station in New York City in March 1974, returning from a work trip to India; identification was delayed because he had crossed out the address on his passport. His complex personal life — he maintained three families — and his poetic approach to architecture were explored in the 2003 documentary My Architect, directed by his son Nathaniel Kahn. He was awarded the AIA Gold Medal in 1971 and the RIBA Gold Medal in 1972.
