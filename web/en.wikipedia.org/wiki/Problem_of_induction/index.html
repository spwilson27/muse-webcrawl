Problem of induction

The problem of induction is the philosophical question of whether inductive reasoning — inferring general laws or future events from observed regularities — can ever be justified. David Hume argued in 1739 that such inference assumes nature's uniformity, which cannot itself be proved without circularity: the sun's past risings do not logically guarantee tomorrow's. All observed swans being white never entailed that no black swans exist — as Australia's proved.

Significance

The problem reshaped the philosophy of science. Karl Popper concluded science never confirms but only falsifies, replacing induction with conjecture and refutation; Nelson Goodman's 'new riddle' (grue) showed the problem is deeper than counting instances. Hume himself treated inductive habit as natural and unavoidable — custom, not reason, is 'the great guide of human life'.
