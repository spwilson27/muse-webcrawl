Elective share

An elective share is a term used in the law of property in some common-law jurisdictions — most U.S. states — for a proportion of a deceased person's estate that the surviving spouse may claim in preference to, and regardless of, the provisions of the deceased's will. The purpose of the elective share is to prevent one spouse from disinheriting the other, reflecting the idea that marriage is an economic partnership and that a surviving spouse contributed to the accumulation of the couple's wealth.

History

The elective share developed as a statutory replacement for the common-law doctrines of dower and curtesy, which gave widows and widowers life interests in a fraction of the deceased spouse's land. Modern statutes, many modelled in part on the Uniform Probate Code, grant the surviving spouse an absolute interest in a percentage of the estate instead. The Uniform Probate Code's revised scheme scales the percentage with the length of the marriage, rising from a small supplemental amount for short marriages to 50 per cent after fifteen years.

Mechanics

To take an elective share, the surviving spouse must file an election within a statutory period after the will is admitted to probate. The spouse then receives the statutory fraction — commonly one-third of the estate where the deceased left children, and one-half otherwise, though the fraction varies by state — in place of any bequest under the will. Many states compute the share against an "augmented estate" that includes certain non-probate transfers, such as revocable trusts and jointly held property, to prevent evasion by will substitutes.

Community property comparison

Nine U.S. states follow community property regimes, in which each spouse already owns an undivided half of marital property; those states generally have no elective share, because the surviving spouse's own half is protected automatically. Georgia is notable as a state with neither community property nor a traditional elective share, offering instead a "year's support" award. Louisiana, with its civil-law tradition, protects children through forced heirship rather than protecting the spouse.
