Sclerophyll

Sclerophyll is a type of vegetation characteristic of Mediterranean-climate regions and other areas with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. The word comes from the Greek sklēros (hard) and phyllon (leaf), referring to the hard, leathery leaves typical of sclerophyll plants. These leaves are usually evergreen, small, thick and stiff, with a waxy coating that reduces water loss during the dry season.

Characteristics

Sclerophyll leaves are adapted to conserve water and to survive nutrient-poor soils. Their hardness comes from a high proportion of sclerenchyma tissue and lignin, which also deters herbivores and slows decomposition of leaf litter. Thick cuticles, sunken stomata and hairs further reduce transpiration. Although long associated with drought adaptation, sclerophylly is now also understood as an adaptation to low phosphorus and other mineral deficiencies, allowing leaves to be long-lived and economical with nutrients.

Distribution

Sclerophyll vegetation occurs in the five Mediterranean-climate regions of the world: the Mediterranean Basin (maquis and garrigue), California (chaparral), central Chile (matorral), the Cape region of South Africa (fynbos), and southern and southwestern Australia (mallee and sclerophyll forests and heaths). Australia is dominated by sclerophyll communities: eucalypts with hard leaves form wet and dry sclerophyll forests across the continent's east and southwest, while Banksia, Hakea and other genera dominate heathlands.

Fire ecology

Most sclerophyll regions are fire-prone, and their plants show adaptations such as serotinous cones and fruits that release seed after fire, lignotubers and epicormic buds for resprouting, and smoke- or heat-triggered seed germination. In Australia, Aboriginal fire-stick farming shaped sclerophyll landscapes for tens of thousands of years.
