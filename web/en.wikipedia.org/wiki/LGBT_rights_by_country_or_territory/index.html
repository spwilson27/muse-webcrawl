LGBT rights by country or territory vary widely across the world. Laws concerning lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender people differ in the legality of same-sex sexual activity, recognition of same-sex relationships and families, anti-discrimination protections, military service, adoption, and legal gender recognition. As of the mid-2020s, same-sex marriage is legal in nearly forty countries, concentrated in Western Europe, the Americas, and parts of Asia-Pacific, while in more than sixty jurisdictions same-sex relations remain criminal offences.

Legality of same-sex activity

The Netherlands was the first country to open marriage to same-sex couples, in 2001. Decriminalisation spread through the late 20th and early 21st centuries — England and Wales in 1967, the whole United States via Lawrence v. Texas (2003), India via the Supreme Court's Navtej Singh Johar judgment (2018), and Botswana, Angola, Bhutan, Singapore, and several Caribbean states in the following years. Conversely, penalties range from fines and imprisonment to, in a small number of countries including Iran, Saudi Arabia, and Uganda (under its 2023 Anti-Homosexuality Act), the death penalty.

Rights and protections

Many jurisdictions ban discrimination in employment and services on grounds of sexual orientation and, increasingly, gender identity. The European Union requires such protections in employment. Adoption and parenting rights largely track marriage recognition. Transgender rights — including legal gender change, access to healthcare, and protection from conversion practices, which a growing number of countries ban — remain especially uneven and politically contested.

International frameworks

The United Nations Human Rights Council has appointed an Independent Expert on sexual orientation and gender identity, and the Yogyakarta Principles (2006, supplemented 2017) set out how international human-rights law applies to LGBT people. ILGA World publishes the most widely cited annual survey, State-Sponsored Homophobia (now Laws on Us).
