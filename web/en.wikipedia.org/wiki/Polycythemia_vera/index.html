Polycythemia vera

TypeMyeloproliferative neoplasm

Polycythemia vera (PV) is a chronic blood cancer of the myeloproliferative neoplasm group, in which the bone marrow produces too many red blood cells, and often excess white blood cells and platelets as well. The resulting increase in blood viscosity raises the risk of thrombosis, including stroke, heart attack, and unusual-site clots such as Budd–Chiari syndrome.

PV causes an abnormal increase in circulating red blood cells.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are diagnosed incidentally from a high haemoglobin or haematocrit on routine blood tests. Symptoms may include headaches, dizziness, visual disturbance, a ruddy complexion, gout, an enlarged spleen, and aquagenic pruritus — intense itching after contact with warm water — which is characteristic of the disease.

Cause

PV is driven by acquired mutations in haematopoietic stem cells. The JAK2 V617F mutation, discovered in 2005, is present in about 95% of patients and constitutively activates signalling downstream of the erythropoietin receptor; most of the remainder carry mutations in JAK2 exon 12.

Treatment

Management aims to reduce thrombotic risk: therapeutic venesection (phlebotomy) to keep haematocrit below 0.45, low-dose aspirin, and cytoreductive drugs such as hydroxycarbamide or interferon in higher-risk patients. The JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib is used for resistant disease. PV can progress over decades to myelofibrosis or, less commonly, acute leukaemia.
