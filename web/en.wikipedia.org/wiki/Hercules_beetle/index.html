The Hercules beetle (Dynastes hercules) is a species of rhinoceros beetle native to the rainforests of Central and South America. It is among the largest beetles in the world and is famous for the enormous horns of the males, which may make up more than half of the animal's total body length; males can reach about 17 cm (7 in) including the horn. The species is also noted for its great strength relative to its size and for the ability of its wing cases to change colour with humidity.

Description

Adult males bear two horns: a long thoracic horn extending forward from the prothorax, armed with short teeth on its underside, and a head horn that curves upward to meet it, forming a pincer used in contests with rival males over feeding sites and mates. Females lack horns entirely. The elytra are typically olive-green to yellowish in dry conditions and turn black when humidity rises, owing to changes in a spongy layer beneath the cuticle.

Life cycle

Females lay eggs in decaying wood. The larvae are large, C-shaped grubs that feed on rotting wood and may spend up to two years developing before pupating. Adults feed on sap and fruit and live for several months.

Strength

Hercules beetles have long been cited as among the strongest animals relative to body mass, capable of carrying loads many times their own weight; frequently quoted figures assert up to 850 times body mass, though measured studies give more modest values. In contests, males use the horn pincer to prise rivals off branches and drop them to the forest floor.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges from southern Mexico through Central America into northern South America, including Colombia and Ecuador, in lowland and montane rainforest. It is widely kept and bred by insect hobbyists, and selectively bred specimens are traded, particularly in Japan.
