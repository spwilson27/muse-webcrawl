Osseointegration

Osseointegration (from Latin os, "bone", and integrare, "to make whole") is the direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the surface of a load-bearing artificial implant, without intervening soft tissue. The concept was developed by Swedish orthopaedic surgeon Per-Ingvar Brånemark, who observed in the 1950s that titanium chambers placed in rabbit bone could not be removed once healed.

Bone in direct contact with an implant surface

Mechanism

After implant placement, proteins adsorb to the oxide layer of the titanium surface, followed by clot formation, migration of osteogenic cells, and deposition of new bone directly on the implant. Micromotion beyond roughly 50–150 micrometres during healing favours fibrous encapsulation instead of integration, so primary stability and an undisturbed healing period are important.

Applications

Osseointegration underlies modern dental implants, introduced clinically by Brånemark's team from 1965, as well as bone-anchored hearing systems, craniofacial prostheses, joint replacement stems and direct skeletal attachment of limb prostheses for amputees. Surface treatments — roughening, anodising and coatings — are used to accelerate and strengthen integration.
