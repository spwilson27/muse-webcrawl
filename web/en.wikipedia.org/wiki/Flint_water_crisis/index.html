The Flint water crisis was a public health crisis that started in 2014 after the drinking water for the city of Flint, Michigan was contaminated with lead and possibly Legionella bacteria. In April 2014, during a financial crisis, state-appointed emergency managers changed the city's water source from treated Detroit Water and Sewerage Department water (sourced from Lake Huron and the Detroit River) to the Flint River. Residents complained about the taste, smell, and appearance of the water. Officials failed to apply corrosion inhibitors to the water, which resulted in lead from aging pipes leaching into the water supply, exposing around 100,000 residents to elevated lead levels.

Background

Flint, a majority-African American city of about 100,000 people, had been under the control of a series of state-appointed emergency managers since 2011 because of a financial emergency. To save money, the city planned to join the Karegnondi Water Authority and, in the interim, switched its water source to the Flint River on April 25, 2014, treating the water at the city's own plant.

Contamination

Unlike the Detroit supply, the Flint River water was not treated with orthophosphate corrosion inhibitor. The more corrosive river water caused lead to leach from lead service lines and solder into drinking water. General Motors stopped using the water at its Flint engine plant in October 2014 because it was corroding parts. Residents reported rashes, hair loss, and discolored water, but officials repeatedly insisted the water was safe.

In 2015, research by Virginia Tech professor Marc Edwards and his team, and blood-lead testing by Flint pediatrician Dr. Mona Hanna-Attisha, showed sharply elevated lead levels in the water and in children's blood. Their findings were initially dismissed by state officials before being confirmed.

Legionnaires' disease

An outbreak of Legionnaires' disease in Genesee County in 2014–2015 killed at least 12 people and sickened dozens more; it was later linked to the water switch. The outbreak was not publicly announced by the governor until January 2016.

Response and aftermath

Flint switched back to Detroit water in October 2015. A state of emergency was declared by the city, the state, and President Barack Obama in January 2016, authorizing federal aid and National Guard distribution of bottled water and filters. Criminal charges were brought against a number of state and local officials, though many charges were later dropped or dismissed. In 2020, Michigan reached a settlement of about $600 million with Flint residents. A program to replace the city's lead and galvanized service lines was largely completed by the early 2020s, and Flint's water has since met federal standards.
