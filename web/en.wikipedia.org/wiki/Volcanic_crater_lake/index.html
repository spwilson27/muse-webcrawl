Volcanic crater lake

A volcanic crater lake is a lake that fills a volcanic crater or caldera. Such lakes form when precipitation, groundwater, or surface water collects in the depression left by an eruption more rapidly than it is lost to evaporation, leakage, or outflow. Because the crater often has no inlet streams, crater lakes are frequently exceptionally clear, and their waters may be strongly influenced by volcanic gases dissolving from below.

Formation

Crater lakes occupy craters formed by explosive eruptions or by the collapse of a volcano's summit into an emptied magma chamber, producing a caldera. Rain and snowmelt accumulate in the bowl once volcanic activity subsides enough for water to persist. Some lakes are fed by hydrothermal springs at the crater floor, which can keep them warm and mineral-rich.

Characteristics and hazards

Many crater lakes are acidic, sometimes extremely so: Kawah Ijen in Indonesia has a turquoise lake with a pH near 0.5, among the most acidic natural waters on Earth. Volcanic gases such as carbon dioxide can accumulate in deep lake water and, if suddenly released — a limnic eruption — can asphyxiate people and animals nearby, as happened at Lake Nyos in Cameroon in 1986, killing about 1,746 people. Other famous crater lakes include Crater Lake in Oregon, the deepest lake in the United States at about 594 metres; Lake Toba in Sumatra, the world's largest volcanic lake, filling the caldera of a supervolcano; Lake Taupō in New Zealand; and the coloured lakes of Kelimutu in Indonesia.

Monitoring

Because changes in temperature, colour, level, or chemistry can precede eruptions, crater lakes on active volcanoes such as Ruapehu (New Zealand) and Poás (Costa Rica) are closely monitored by volcanologists.
