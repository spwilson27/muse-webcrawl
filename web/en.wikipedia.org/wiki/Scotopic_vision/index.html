Scotopic vision

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scotopic vision is the vision of the eye under low-light levels. The term comes from the Greek skotos, meaning "darkness", and -opia, meaning "a condition of sight". In the human eye, cone cells are nonfunctional in low visible light; scotopic vision is produced exclusively through rod cells, which are most sensitive to wavelengths of light around 498 nm (green-blue) and are insensitive to wavelengths longer than about 640 nm (red).

Properties

Under scotopic conditions (luminance below roughly 10−2 cd/m²):

  • Colour perception is absent; the world appears in shades of grey.
  • Visual acuity is poor, because many rods converge onto single ganglion cells and because rods are absent from the fovea—so faint objects are best seen slightly off-axis ("averted vision").
  • Sensitivity is greatest after some 30 minutes of dark adaptation as the photopigment rhodopsin regenerates.
  • Peak sensitivity shifts toward shorter (bluer) wavelengths compared with daylight vision—the Purkinje effect, noticeable at dusk when reds darken faster than blues.

Mesopic and photopic vision

Scotopic vision contrasts with photopic vision, colour vision under well-lit conditions mediated by cone cells, and mesopic vision, the intermediate combination of rods and cones typical of twilight and ordinary indoor lighting. Red light is often used to illuminate instruments at night (as in astronomy and aviation) because rod cells are insensitive to it, preserving dark adaptation while cones can still read the display.
