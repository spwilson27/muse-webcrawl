Max Bill

Max Bill (22 December 1908 – 9 December 1994) was a Swiss architect, artist, painter, typeface designer, industrial designer, and graphic designer, one of the central figures of Concrete art and of twentieth-century Swiss design. A student of the Bauhaus, he became the leading theorist and practitioner of art based on mathematical thinking.

Early life and education

Bill was born in Winterthur, Switzerland. After an apprenticeship as a silversmith and study at the Kunstgewerbeschule in Zurich, he attended the Bauhaus in Dessau from 1927 to 1929, studying with Josef Albers, Wassily Kandinsky, and Paul Klee. Returning to Zurich, he worked as an architect, painter, and graphic designer, and in 1930 built his own house and studio at Zumikon. In 1936 he formulated the principles of Concrete art in a manifesto, extending Theo van Doesburg's 1930 coinage, and organized exhibitions that established the movement internationally.

Ulm and design

In 1953 Bill co-founded the Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm (Ulm School of Design) in Germany with Inge Aicher-Scholl and Otl Aicher, designing its buildings and serving as its first rector. The school sought to revive the Bauhaus ideal, uniting design, science, and technology. Bill left in 1957 amid disagreements over direction. His industrial designs include the Ulm stool, clocks and watches for Junghans, and the iconic Helvetica-era Swiss railway aesthetics; his graphic design helped define the "Swiss Style" of typography.

Art and legacy

Bill's paintings and sculptures explore series, permutations, and geometric structures such as the Möbius strip, notably in his Endless Ribbon sculptures. He won the Grand Prize at the first São Paulo Biennale in 1951 and represented Switzerland at the Venice Biennale. He also served in the Swiss parliament (1967–1971), campaigning for environmental and cultural causes. He died in Berlin in 1994.
