Money multiplier

[Image placeholder: deposit multiplication process]

In monetary economics, the money multiplier is the maximum amount of money that the banking system generates with each unit of reserves. In the traditional textbook model, an initial deposit is repeatedly lent out and redeposited: each bank keeps a fraction of deposits as reserves and lends the remainder, so total deposits expand by a multiple of the initial injection of base money.

Formula

If the reserve requirement is rr, the simple deposit multiplier is

multiplier = 1 / rr

Taking account of the public's currency holdings, the broader money multiplier (for M1 relative to the monetary base) is commonly written as

m = (1 + c) / (c + r)

where c is the currency–deposit ratio and r is the total reserve–deposit ratio, including excess reserves.

Example

With a 10 percent reserve requirement, an initial deposit of $1,000 allows the bank to lend $900; the borrower spends the loan, which is deposited elsewhere, allowing a further loan of $810, and so on. The process converges to total deposits of $10,000 — ten times the original deposit.

Criticism

Many economists and central banks dispute the causal story of the multiplier model. In practice, banks create deposits by lending when they judge loans profitable, and reserves are supplied by the central bank as needed to meet payment demands and any reserve requirements; this "endogenous money" view, publicly endorsed in a 2014 Bank of England bulletin, inverts the textbook chain of causation. Several major economies, including the United States since 2020, have no reserve requirements at all, yet money creation continues to be constrained by capital, liquidity regulation and credit demand.
