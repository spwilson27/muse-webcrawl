Spacecraft attitude determination and control

Spacecraft attitude determination and control (ADCS) is the subsystem of a spacecraft responsible for determining its orientation (attitude) in space and controlling that orientation so that antennas, solar arrays, and scientific instruments point in the desired directions. Attitude is typically described relative to an inertial reference frame or relative to the Earth, the Sun, or another celestial body.

Attitude determination

Attitude determination uses sensors to measure the spacecraft's orientation. Common sensors include Sun sensors, which measure the direction to the Sun; Earth horizon sensors; magnetometers, which measure the local magnetic field; star trackers, which compare observed star patterns against an onboard catalogue for high accuracy; and gyroscopes, which measure angular velocity. Sensor measurements are fused using estimation algorithms such as the Kalman filter and deterministic methods such as TRIAD and QUEST.

Attitude control

Control is achieved with actuators. Reaction wheels and momentum wheels exchange angular momentum with the spacecraft body to rotate it without expending propellant. Control moment gyroscopes provide larger torques. Magnetic torquers (magnetorquers) interact with the Earth's magnetic field and are common on small satellites in low Earth orbit. Thrusters provide control on larger spacecraft and are also used to desaturate reaction wheels. Some spacecraft use passive stabilization, such as gravity-gradient stabilization or spin stabilization.

Modes of operation

Typical ADCS modes include detumbling after separation from the launch vehicle, Sun acquisition, nominal Earth-pointing or inertial-pointing operations, and safe modes that keep power-positive orientation during anomalies.
