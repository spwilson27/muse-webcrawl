Suit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A conventional two-piece suit: matching jacket and trousers worn with shirt and tie.

A suit, also called a lounge suit, business suit, or dress suit, is a set of clothes made from the same fabric, consisting of at least a jacket and trousers. Lounge suits are the most common style of Western formal and business dress, originating in Britain in the 19th century, and are worn with a collared shirt and often a necktie.

History

The modern suit traces its origins to the simplified court dress and military uniforms of the 17th and 18th centuries, and to the country clothing of British landowners. In the 19th century, the frock coat gave way to the morning coat and then to the short lounge jacket; by the early 20th century the three-piece suit (jacket, waistcoat, trousers) had become standard business attire. After the Second World War the two-piece suit predominated, and from the late 20th century business dress codes relaxed in many industries, though the suit remains standard in law, finance, government and formal occasions.

Components and styles

A suit may be two-piece or three-piece. Jackets are single- or double-breasted, typically with notched or peaked lapels, and are made bespoke (individually patterned), made-to-measure, or ready-to-wear. Common fabrics include wool and wool blends, linen and cotton for summer; classic patterns include pinstripe, chalk stripe, herringbone, houndstooth and Prince of Wales check. Suit trousers traditionally match the jacket exactly; the full ensemble is completed by a dress shirt, tie, belt or braces, and dress shoes.

Women's suits

Suits for women, including the skirt suit and the trouser suit (pantsuit), developed from tailored riding and walking costumes in the late 19th century and became mainstream professional dress during the 20th century.
