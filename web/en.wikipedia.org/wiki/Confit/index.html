Classic dishConfit de canard (duck confit)

Confit (French, pronounced /kɔ̃fi/; from the verb confire, "to preserve") is any of various kinds of food that have been immersed in a substance for both flavour and preservation. In the case of meats, the substance is traditionally their own rendered fat, and the food is cooked by slow poaching in it — a very old technique, in use well before the advent of refrigeration, that made meat keep for months.

Method

The classic preparation of duck confit (confit de canard) begins by salt-curing duck legs, often with herbs, for a day or more. The salt is rinsed off, the legs are submerged in rendered duck fat, and the vessel is kept at a gentle heat (about 90–100 °C) for several hours until the meat is tender but not falling apart. Cooled and covered in its fat, the confit was traditionally stored in earthenware pots in a cool cellar.

Other confits

The word is used more broadly: confit d'oignons is onions slowly caramelised in oil or butter; garlic confit is cloves poached in oil; fruit confits are fruits poached in sugar syrup (from which the English word confection derives); and fruit confits may also be preserved in alcohol. The fat renders the meat unctuous and, when the skin is crisped in a hot pan before serving, gives confit its characteristic contrast of textures.

Confit of duck or goose is a staple of south-western French cuisine, served classically with potatoes sautéed in the same fat.
