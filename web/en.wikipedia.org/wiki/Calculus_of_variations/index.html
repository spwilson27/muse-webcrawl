Calculus of variations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Several curves between two points, the lowest-energy one highlighted]

The calculus of variations is the branch of mathematical analysis that finds functions which maximise or minimise quantities (called functionals) that depend on whole functions — the shortest path, the fastest descent curve, the surface of least area.

Its founding problem was the brachistochrone (1696): Johann Bernoulli challenged mathematicians to find the curve down which a bead slides fastest between two points. Newton solved it overnight (anonymously, but Bernoulli recognised 'the lion by his claw'); the answer is a cycloid, not the straight line or the circular arc intuition suggests.

Euler–Lagrange

Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange systematised the subject in the 1750s through the Euler–Lagrange equation, which a minimising function must satisfy. Least-action principles in physics — the bedrock of mechanics and field theory — are statements of the calculus of variations.

Modern optimal control, economics and machine learning (variational inference) are its descendants.
