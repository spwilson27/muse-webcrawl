Attic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An attic is the space found directly below the pitched roof of a house or other building. Also known as a loft or garret, an attic may serve as storage, as living space, or as a buffer zone housing insulation, ventilation, and building services. Because it sits at the top of the building, the attic experiences the greatest temperature swings in the structure.

Design and use

Attics arise naturally from pitched-roof construction, which sheds rain and snow. In many houses the attic is unfinished and used for storage of seldom-used items, reached by a fixed staircase, a ladder, or a pull-down stair. Finished attics — converted into bedrooms, offices, or studios — typically require dormer windows or skylights for light and headroom, proper insulation, and compliance with building codes for ceiling height and emergency egress.

The attic plays an important role in building performance: insulation laid on the attic floor and ventilation through soffit and ridge vents limit heat loss in winter and reduce moisture and overheating in summer. Poorly sealed attics are a common source of ice dams on roofs in cold climates.

History and culture

The word "attic" derives from the Attic style of classical architecture, in which a low decorative story above the main cornice — an "attic story" — was a feature of Greek-influenced building. In nineteenth-century Paris, garret rooms under the roofs of apartment houses became associated with impoverished artists and students, giving rise to the romantic image of the starving artist in a garret. Attics also feature prominently in literature and film as places of storage, secrets, and confinement, from the hidden wife in Jane Eyre to the diary-writing refuge in The Diary of Anne Frank (whose annexe was in an upper rear part of a building, often depicted as attic-like).
