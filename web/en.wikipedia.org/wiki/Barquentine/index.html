Barquentine

A barquentine (also barkentine or schooner barque) is a sailing vessel with three or more masts; square rig on the foremast only, and fore-and-aft rig on all the other masts. The type combined the windward efficiency of the square rig for ocean passages with the smaller crew requirements of fore-and-aft sails.

Characteristics

Barquentines typically have three to five masts. The foremast carries square sails — courses, topsails, topgallants, and sometimes royals — while the main, mizzen, and any additional masts carry gaff sails, with staysails between the masts and jibs at the bow. Because fore-and-aft sails need fewer hands to work them, the rig became popular in the late 19th century for long-distance cargo trades as owners sought to cut crew costs.

History and use

The rig emerged in the 19th century and was widely used by merchant fleets of Britain, Germany, France, and the United States, including large steel barquentines in the nitrate and grain trades around Cape Horn. A number survive or have been reproduced as sail-training ships, such as the Gazela of Philadelphia and several modern training vessels. The four-masted barquentine Juan Sebastián de Elcano serves the Spanish Navy.
