Train station

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A train station, railroad station (American English), railway station (British English) or simply station is a railway facility where trains stop to load or unload passengers, freight or both. It generally consists of at least one platform, one track and one station building providing such ancillary services as ticket sales, waiting rooms and baggage and freight handling. Stations may be connected with bus terminals and metro, tram or rapid transit stations.

Terminology

In British English "railway station" is standard, while "train station" — once criticised by usage writers — has become the more common term in everyday speech. In American English the older term was "depot" (from the French dépôt), still used for small stations, and many great terminals bear the name "Union Station" because they were shared by several railroad companies. A large, principal station at the end of a line is a terminus or terminal.

Facilities

Stations vary from unstaffed halts with a single platform to vast terminal complexes. Typical facilities include platforms (side, island or bay), a station building with ticket office or ticket machines, waiting areas, departure boards and public address systems, footbridges, subways or lifts for accessibility, and interchange with buses, taxis and cycle parking. Historically many stations also handled parcels and freight, had goods sheds, refreshment rooms and station hotels, and generated their own architectural styles: the great nineteenth-century termini such as London Paddington, Paris Gare du Nord and New York's Grand Central Terminal are landmarks of iron, glass and masonry.

Configuration

Stations may be through stations, where tracks run past the platforms, or terminal stations, where trains must reverse out. Platforms are arranged to allow level boarding where possible; platform height and length standards vary by country. Junction stations add interchange between lines, and some stations are split across levels, as with Berlin Hauptbahnhof. Border stations historically contained customs and immigration facilities.

History

The earliest stations were built for the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830): Liverpool Road station in Manchester, now part of the Science and Industry Museum, is the world's oldest surviving passenger terminus. As railways spread, stations became civic monuments and centres of town life; the station clock and standard time itself ("railway time") entered public life through them. In the twentieth century, car and air competition closed thousands of stations, notably in Britain under the Beeching cuts of the 1960s, while in recent decades many countries have invested in new and rebuilt stations for high-speed and urban rail.

Busiest stations

By passenger numbers, the world's busiest station is Shinjuku Station in Tokyo, used by an average of around 3.5 million passengers a day. Others among the busiest include Shibuya and Ikebukuro in Tokyo, Paris Gare du Nord (the busiest in Europe), London Waterloo, and New York Penn Station. India's Howrah Junction is among the largest by platform count.
