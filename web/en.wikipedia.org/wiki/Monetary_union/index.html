Monetary union

A monetary union (also known as a currency union) is an intergovernmental agreement that involves two or more states sharing the same currency. These states may not necessarily rely upon further integration (such as a common central bank or fiscal policy harmonisation; such steps form an economic and monetary union). When the agreement formally involves the policy of a single central bank, the grouping is sometimes called a currency union; when it involves a single currency but multiple issuing authorities, it may be termed informal.

Types

Informally, a single currency circulates de facto, as with the United States dollar in several Caribbean and Pacific states. Formally, states adopt a shared currency through a treaty-based central bank, as in the West African and Central African CFA franc zones and the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union. Multilateral unions may involve a completely new currency, exemplified by the euro, introduced in 1999 by the member states of the European Union's Economic and Monetary Union.

Theory

The theory of optimum currency areas, developed by Robert Mundell and others, weighs the benefits of a union — reduced transaction costs, elimination of exchange-rate risk, and price transparency — against the loss of independent monetary policy and the exchange rate as an adjustment mechanism for asymmetric shocks. Fiscal transfers and labour mobility can compensate for that loss.

Existing unions

  • Eurozone — 20 European Union member states using the euro under the European Central Bank.
  • CFA franc zones — the West African (XOF) and Central African (XAF) monetary unions, whose currencies are pegged to the euro.
  • Eastern Caribbean Currency Union — eight island states and territories using the Eastern Caribbean dollar.
  • Common Monetary Area — South Africa, Lesotho, Namibia, and Eswatini, whose currencies are pegged to the South African rand.
  • Brunei–Singapore — an interchangeability agreement under which each state's currency is accepted at par in the other.

Historical unions

Historical examples include the Latin Monetary Union (1865–1927) and the Scandinavian Monetary Union (1873–1914), both coinage standards based on gold and silver, and the monetary unions dissolved with the breakups of the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia in the 1990s.
