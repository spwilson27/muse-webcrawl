The Reign of Terror (French: la Terreur) was a period of the French Revolution when, following the creation of the First Republic, a series of massacres and numerous public executions took place in response to revolutionary fervour, anticlerical sentiment, and accusations of treason by the Committee of Public Safety. It lasted from 5 September 1793, when the National Convention declared terror "the order of the day", to 27 July 1794 (9 Thermidor Year II), when Robespierre and his allies were overthrown.

Background

By the summer of 1793 France was at war with much of Europe, the Vendée was in revolt, federalist uprisings challenged Paris, the currency was collapsing, and the sans-culottes demanded price controls. Under this pressure the Convention empowered the Committee of Public Safety as an emergency government, created the Revolutionary Tribunal, passed the Law of Suspects (17 September 1793), and imposed the General Maximum on prices.

Course of the Terror

The Terror consumed the Revolution's own leaders: the Hébertists were guillotined in March 1794 and the Dantonists, including Georges Danton, in April. The Law of 22 Prairial (10 June 1794) stripped the accused of legal defence and made death the only sentence, accelerating executions in Paris — the "Great Terror". Prominent victims included Marie Antoinette, the Duke of Orléans, Antoine Lavoisier, and André Chénier. In the provinces, repression was far bloodier: in the Vendée and at Lyon and Nantes (where Jean-Baptiste Carrier's drownings, the noyades, became notorious), tens of thousands died.

End and aftermath

As the military emergency receded after the victory at Fleurus (26 June 1794), the Terror lost its justification. On 27 July 1794 the Convention turned on Robespierre; he and his associates were arrested and executed the following day. The Thermidorian Reaction dismantled the machinery of the Terror, repealed the Law of Prairial and the Maximum, and closed the Jacobin Club.

Death toll and historiography

Donald Greer's classic study counted 16,594 death sentences by revolutionary tribunals across France; total deaths attributed to the Terror, including summary executions, prison deaths, and the Vendée, are commonly estimated at 30,000–50,000. Historians continue to debate whether the Terror was an inevitable product of circumstance or an ideological choice, a debate sharpened by historians such as François Furet against the older "circumstantial" school.
