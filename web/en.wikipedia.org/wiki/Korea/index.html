Korea is a peninsular region in East Asia consisting of the Korean Peninsula, Jeju Island, and smaller islands. Since the end of World War II in 1945 it has been divided, roughly along the 38th parallel, between two states: North Korea (the Democratic People's Republic of Korea) in the north and South Korea (the Republic of Korea) in the south — a division cemented by the Korean War (1950–1953) and an armistice rather than a peace treaty. Korea was a single polity for most of its recorded history, from the unification of the peninsula by Silla in 668 through the Goryeo and Joseon dynasties to the Korean Empire, before Japanese rule (1910–1945).

Etymology

The English name "Korea" derives from Goryeo (高麗), the dynasty that ruled the peninsula from 918 to 1392 and was known to merchants travelling the Silk Road. Koreans call their land Hanguk (한국, in the South) or Joseon (조선, in the North), and the peninsula Hanbando (한반도).

Geography

The peninsula extends about 1,100 km southward from the north-east Asian mainland, bordered by China and Russia to the north along the Yalu (Amnok) and Tumen rivers, the Yellow Sea to the west, and the Sea of Japan (East Sea) to the east. It is mountainous — the Taebaek range runs down the east coast, and the highest peak is Paektu Mountain (2,744 m) on the northern border, a volcano sacred in Korean tradition. Major rivers include the Han, Nakdong and Geum. The climate is temperate with four seasons, hot humid summers influenced by the East Asian monsoon, and cold dry winters.

History

According to tradition, the first Korean kingdom, Gojoseon, was founded in 2333 BC by the legendary Dangun. The Three Kingdoms — Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla — contested the peninsula until Silla unified most of it in 668. The Goryeo dynasty (918–1392) gave the country its name and produced the Tripitaka Koreana woodblocks; the Joseon dynasty (1392–1897) ruled for over five centuries from Hanyang (Seoul) under Neo-Confucian principles, and saw the creation of the Korean alphabet, Hangul, in 1443 under King Sejong. After a brief Korean Empire (1897–1910), Japan annexed Korea in 1910. Liberation in 1945 was followed by division, the establishment of two rival states in 1948, and the Korean War, which devastated the peninsula and ended in stalemate. In the decades since, South Korea has industrialised into one of the world's largest economies and a major cultural exporter, while North Korea has remained an isolated, nuclear-armed state under the Kim family.

Culture

Koreans share a language (Korean, written in Hangul) and many cultural practices across the division: Confucian-influenced family ethics, shamanic and Buddhist traditions, and a distinctive cuisine centred on rice, soups, kimchi and shared side dishes (banchan). In the 21st century the South Korean cultural wave (Hallyu) — K-pop, television dramas and film — has given Korean culture global reach. Roughly 7.3 million ethnic Koreans live overseas, including large diasporas in China, the United States and Japan.
