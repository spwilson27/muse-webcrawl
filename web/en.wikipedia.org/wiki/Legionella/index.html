Legionella is a genus of pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria that includes the species Legionella pneumophila, which causes legionellosis (Legionnaires' disease and the milder Pontiac fever). More than 60 species are known. Legionella species are ubiquitous in freshwater environments and in soil; in the wild they live as intracellular parasites of free-living amoebae and other protozoa, a lifestyle that pre-adapts them to survive inside human macrophages.

History

The genus was named after the 1976 outbreak of severe pneumonia among attendees of an American Legion convention at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel in Philadelphia, which killed 29 people. In 1977 Joseph McDade and Charles Shepard at the US Centers for Disease Control isolated the causative bacterium, which proved to belong to a previously unknown genus. Retrospective testing showed that earlier unexplained outbreaks, including a 1968 outbreak of "Pontiac fever" in Michigan, had also been caused by Legionella.

Biology and ecology

Legionella are aerobic, motile, weakly staining Gram-negative rods that have unusual nutritional requirements, including L-cysteine and iron; they grow on buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) agar. They thrive in warm water (roughly 25–45 °C) and multiply within biofilms and protozoan hosts. Human-made water systems — cooling towers, evaporative condensers, hot-water systems, showers, hot tubs, decorative fountains and hospital plumbing — can amplify the bacteria and generate infectious aerosols.

Pathogenesis and disease

Infection occurs by inhaling contaminated aerosol droplets; person-to-person transmission is essentially absent. Inside the lung, L. pneumophila is taken up by alveolar macrophages, in which it uses a Dot/Icm type IV secretion system to create a protective vacuole and replicate — the same machinery it uses in amoebae. The result ranges from the self-limiting, flu-like Pontiac fever to Legionnaires' disease, a potentially fatal pneumonia, especially in older smokers and immunocompromised patients. L. pneumophila serogroup 1 accounts for the large majority of human cases; other species such as L. longbeachae (associated with potting mix) are regionally important.

Detection and control

Diagnosis is by culture on BCYE agar, urinary antigen testing (mainly for L. pneumophila serogroup 1), or PCR. Control in buildings relies on water-safety plans: keeping hot water hot and cold water cold, flushing infrequently used outlets, and disinfecting cooling towers and spa pools, under guidelines and regulations such as ASHRAE Guideline 12/Standard 188 and national codes.
