Interpretations of quantum mechanics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of Schrödinger's cat in a box, both alive and dead in superposition]
Schrödinger's cat: the icon of the measurement problem.

An interpretation of quantum mechanics is an attempt to explain how the mathematical theory of quantum mechanics corresponds to physical reality. Although the theory's predictions are uncontroversial and spectacularly confirmed, its central objects — the Wave function, superposition, measurement-induced change — admit multiple readings, and physicists and philosophers disagree about what the theory says the world is like.

The principal interpretations include the Copenhagen interpretation (wave function as complete description; collapse upon measurement; complementarity), the Many-worlds interpretation (no collapse; universal wave function branching), pilot-wave/de Broglie–Bohm theory (particles with definite positions guided by a nonlocal wave), objective-collapse theories (GRW: spontaneous, physical collapse), relational and QBist views (states as relations or as an agent's information), and consistent histories. The measurement problem — why definite outcomes occur at all — motivates them all.

Status

Most interpretations are empirically equivalent; they differ in ontology and in heuristic fruitfulness (many-worlds for quantum computing's self-image, pilot-wave for some numerical methods, collapse models are actually testable and are being constrained by experiments). Surveys of physicists find Copenhagen-type views still the plurality position, with many-worlds second and a large undecided bloc — a state of affairs unchanged in outline since the 1930s debates between Bohr and Einstein over the EPR paradox.
