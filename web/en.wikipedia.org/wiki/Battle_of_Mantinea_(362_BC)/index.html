Battle of Mantinea (362 BC)

The Second Battle of Mantinea was fought on 4 July 362 BC near Mantinea in Arcadia, Greece, between the Thebans and their allies, led by Epaminondas, and a coalition of Sparta, Athens, Elis, Mantinea and other states. The Theban army won the day through Epaminondas's refined oblique attack, but the general's death in the moment of victory deprived Thebes of its leader, and the battle ended the Theban hegemony of Greece without establishing any lasting settlement.

Background

Thebes's ascendancy after Leuctra (371 BC) rested on Epaminondas's invasions of the Peloponnese and the liberation of Messenia, which permanently weakened Sparta. By 362 BC the Arcadian League had split, with Mantinea defecting to the Spartan side, and Athens had joined Sparta against its former ally. Epaminondas marched into the Peloponnese for a final campaign: after a daring but unsuccessful dash at Sparta itself — defended in the aged king Agesilaus's absence by his son Archidamus — and a failed attempt on Mantinea, he resolved to force a decisive battle before his term of command expired.

The battle

Xenophon's Hellenica and later sources describe Epaminondas's masterstroke: he marched his army as if retiring to camp, lulling the enemy, then suddenly formed for battle with his infantry in an oblique order — the left wing massed deep like the prow of a trireme, screened by cavalry interleaved with light infantry (hamippoi) — and struck the Spartan right. The Theban cavalry routed their opposite numbers, and the deep Theban phalanx broke the Spartan line. At the crisis of the pursuit Epaminondas was struck by a spear (a Spartan, Gryllus — son of the historian Xenophon — or a Mantinean was variously credited) and carried from the field dying. His last orders, learning that his chosen successors had also fallen, were to make peace; the Theban army, leaderless, let the victory slip away.

Aftermath

A general peace conference confirmed little more than that Messenia remained independent and Greece exhausted. Xenophon closed his history with the reflection that after Mantinea there was "even more confusion and disorder in Greece than before" — a vacuum that within a generation Philip II of Macedon would fill at Chaeronea (338 BC).
