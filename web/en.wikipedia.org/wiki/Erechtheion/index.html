The Erechtheion or Erechtheum is an ancient Greek temple on the north side of the Acropolis of Athens in Greece. Built between 421 and 406 BC in the Ionic order, it housed some of Athens's most sacred cults and relics, including the ancient olive-wood image of Athena Polias, the marks of Poseidon's trident, and the sacred olive tree of Athena. The temple is named for Erechtheus, a legendary early king of Athens.

Architecture

The building has an unusually irregular plan, split across different ground levels to accommodate several shrines and the sacred objects within. Its most famous feature is the southern Porch of the Caryatids, whose roof is supported by six draped female figures. The original caryatids are now preserved in the Acropolis Museum and the British Museum, with replicas in place on the building.

History

Construction began during the Peloponnesian War and was completed in 406 BC. In later centuries the building served successively as a Byzantine church, a residence under Frankish and Ottoman rule, and the harem of the Turkish commandant of the Acropolis. It was badly damaged during the Greek War of Independence. Lord Elgin removed one of the caryatids and a column to Britain in the early 19th century. Major restoration work by the Greek archaeological service, completed in the late 20th century, stabilised the monument.

Sacred features

Within and around the temple were shown the salt-water well said to have sprung from Poseidon's trident during his contest with Athena for patronage of the city, and the olive tree Athena created in the same contest, a descendant of which still grows beside the building.
