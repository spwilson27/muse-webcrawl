Diet-heart hypothesis

The diet-heart hypothesis (also called the lipid hypothesis) is the long-standing scientific proposition that dietary saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, and that elevated blood cholesterol in turn causes atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. In its classical form it holds that reducing saturated fat intake — and replacing it with unsaturated fats — lowers serum LDL cholesterol and thereby reduces the incidence of coronary heart disease.

Origins

Early support came from cholesterol-feeding experiments in rabbits by Nikolai Anitschkov in 1913, and from mid-twentieth-century metabolic ward studies showing that saturated fats raised, and polyunsaturated fats lowered, serum cholesterol. The American physiologist Ancel Keys became the hypothesis's most influential advocate: his Seven Countries Study (begun 1958) reported strong ecological correlations between saturated fat intake, cholesterol levels and coronary mortality across populations.

Dietary guidelines

From the 1960s onward the American Heart Association and, later, national governments adopted the hypothesis in dietary advice. The 1977 United States Senate Select Committee's Dietary Goals and the first U.S. Dietary Guidelines (1980) recommended reducing total and saturated fat, advice echoed by the United Kingdom and many other countries.

Evidence and criticism

Randomised trials of cholesterol-lowering diets, and the later success of statins in reducing events by lowering LDL, are generally regarded as supporting a causal role for LDL cholesterol in atherosclerosis — a position formalised in the related lipid hypothesis. Critics have questioned how strongly dietary saturated fat itself, as opposed to blood LDL, drives disease; noted the weak or inconsistent results of some low-fat diet trials such as the Women's Health Initiative; and pointed to the role of replacement nutrients, arguing that replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrate shows little benefit, whereas replacement with polyunsaturated fat does. Meta-analyses remain divided on the size of the saturated-fat effect when replacement nutrients are specified.

Current consensus

Most mainstream cardiology and nutrition bodies continue to advise limiting saturated fat and emphasise overall dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet, while the debate continues over the strength of the diet-heart chain's first link — from dietary fat to clinical events — as distinct from the well-established causal role of LDL cholesterol.
