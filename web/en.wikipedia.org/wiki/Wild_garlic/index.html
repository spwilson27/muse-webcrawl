Wild garlic

Ramsons in flower in a European woodland.

Wild garlic is a common name applied to several species of the genus Allium. In Europe the name usually denotes ramsons (Allium ursinum), a bulbous perennial flowering plant that carpets damp woodlands in spring. In North America it may refer to species such as Allium vineale (crow garlic) or the ramp, Allium tricoccum.

Ramsons

Ramsons grow in moist, deciduous woodland across Europe and northern Asia. The broad, lance-shaped leaves appear in early spring and give off a strong garlic odour when crushed. White, star-shaped flowers bloom in rounded clusters from April to June. All parts of the plant are edible, and the leaves are widely foraged for pesto, soups and salads; foragers must take care not to confuse them with the poisonous leaves of lily of the valley.

Other species

Allium vineale, sometimes called wild garlic or crow garlic, is a perennial bulb native to Europe and naturalised in North America and Australia, where it is often a weed of pasture and cereals. The North American ramp (Allium tricoccum) is a prized spring vegetable in Appalachia. All share the characteristic oniony-garlic scent produced by sulfur compounds such as allicin.

Uses

Wild garlic species have a long history of culinary and folk-medicinal use. Modern studies of Allium ursinum suggest mild beneficial effects on blood pressure and cholesterol, similar to those attributed to cultivated garlic.
