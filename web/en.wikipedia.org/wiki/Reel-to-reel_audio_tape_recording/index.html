Reel-to-reel audio tape recording


A reel-to-reel tape recorder; tape travels from the supply reel to the takeup reel.

Reel-to-reel audio tape recording, also called open-reel recording, is a form of magnetic tape audio recording in which the tape is held on a removable takeup reel separate from the recording machine, wound on an open supply reel and threaded past the tape heads to the takeup reel, rather than enclosed in a cassette.

History

The technology descends from the German Magnetophon recorders of the 1930s, brought to the United States after the Second World War. Bing Crosby's investment in Ampex helped popularise tape for broadcast, and by the 1950s reel-to-reel machines dominated professional studios and were sold to home hi-fi enthusiasts. Multitrack reel-to-reel recorders enabled modern studio production techniques such as overdubbing and became the backbone of recording until digital formats displaced them in the 1980s and 1990s.

Operation

Tape width is commonly 1/4 inch for stereo home and professional use, and up to 2 inches for 24-track studio machines. Tape speed, measured in inches per second (ips), trades fidelity against playing time: 15 and 30 ips are professional standards, while 7½ and 3¾ ips were common domestically. Sound quality depends on track format (full-, half-, or quarter-track), speed, and tape formulation.

Decline and revival

Cassette and then digital recording ended the format's mainstream use, but reel-to-reel survives in audiophile circles and some studios that value its sound character. New tape formulations and refurbished machines support a small revival, and prerecorded tapes are again being issued.
