Andalusi Romance, also called Mozarabic (Spanish: mozárabe), was a continuum of closely related Romance dialects spoken in the parts of the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule, Al-Andalus, from the ninth century until about the seventeenth century. It was the everyday language of much of the population — Christians living under Muslim rule (the Mozarabs), as well as many Muslims and Jews — alongside the prestigious written language, Andalusi Arabic.

History

After the Umayyad conquest of Hispania in 711, the Latin-derived vernacular of the peninsula's inhabitants gradually absorbed a large Arabic vocabulary while Arabic itself became the language of government and high culture. Andalusi Romance survived longest in rural areas and among Christian communities; as the Christian kingdoms of the north expanded during the Reconquista, the dialects retreated southward, and Mozarabic-speaking communities that migrated north were assimilated into Castilian, Aragonese, and Catalan speech. The language lingered in the Kingdom of Granada until the fall of the Nasrid emirate in 1492 and among the Moriscos until their expulsion in 1609.

Sources

Because Andalusi Romance was rarely written directly, it is known mainly from indirect witnesses: the kharjas, short verse-final refrains in Romance embedded in Arabic and Hebrew muwashshah poems, rediscovered in 1948 and among the earliest lyric texts in any Romance language; aljamiado texts written in Arabic script; botanical and agricultural treatises recording plant names; Romance place names and loanwords preserved across Iberia; and word lists such as the Latin–Arabic glossary of Leiden.

Features and legacy

Andalusi Romance preserved archaic features lost elsewhere in Ibero-Romance, such as initial Latin F- (where Castilian developed h- and later silence) and the outcome of Latin -CT- as -/xt/- rather than -ch-. Its greatest legacy is the heavy stratum of Arabic-influenced and Mozarabic-transmitted vocabulary in Spanish and Portuguese — words for irrigation, agriculture, architecture, and administration — and its trace in the toponymy of southern Iberia.
