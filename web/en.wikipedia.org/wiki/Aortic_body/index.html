Aortic body

The aortic bodies are small clusters of chemoreceptors and supporting cells located along the aortic arch, the great curved artery leaving the heart. Along with the carotid bodies, they form part of the peripheral chemoreceptor system that monitors the chemical composition of arterial blood and helps regulate breathing and circulation.

Aortic bodies are located near the aortic arch

Structure

Several aortic bodies are typically present: one near the junction of the ascending aorta and the arch, others between the arch and the pulmonary artery, and near the origin of the subclavian arteries. Like the carotid body, an aortic body consists of glomus (type I) cells, which act as sensory transducers, enveloped by sustentacular (type II) cells and richly supplied with capillaries and nerve fibers. Sensory signals travel to the brainstem chiefly via the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X).

Function

Aortic body chemoreceptors respond primarily to decreased partial pressure of oxygen (hypoxia) in arterial blood, and also to increased carbon dioxide and decreased blood pH. Their stimulation triggers reflex increases in the rate and depth of ventilation, along with cardiovascular adjustments. In humans the carotid bodies are generally regarded as the dominant peripheral chemoreceptors for ventilatory control, with the aortic bodies playing a smaller, complementary role that may be more important in cardiovascular reflexes and in early life.

Clinical significance

Tumors of the aortic bodies, called paragangliomas (historically chemodectomas when arising here), are rare, usually benign growths that may occur near the aortic arch and in the aortopulmonary region. Aortic bodies are also studied in relation to sleep apnea and the pathophysiology of chronic hypoxia.
