Spiropyran

A spiropyran is an organic chemical compound characterized by a spiro carbon linking two heterocyclic ring systems, one of which is a pyran. Spiropyrans are best known for their photochromic properties: under ultraviolet light they convert reversibly to a planar, strongly coloured open form called a merocyanine, and revert to the colourless closed form with visible light or heat.

Photochromism

In the closed spiropyran form, the two ring systems are held perpendicular to one another about the spiro carbon, so conjugation is interrupted and the compound is essentially colourless. Absorption of UV light breaks the carbon–oxygen bond of the pyran ring, allowing the molecule to unfold into the flat merocyanine structure with an extended conjugated system that absorbs strongly in the visible region. The process is reversible, making spiropyrans molecular switches.

Applications

Because the two forms differ dramatically in colour, polarity and binding behaviour, spiropyrans are used in research on optical data storage, photochromic lenses and inks, chemical sensors, logic gates at the molecular scale, and light-controlled materials and drug delivery systems. Derivatives such as spirooxazines share similar behaviour and are widely used in commercial photochromic eyewear.

History

The photochromism of spiropyrans was first reported in the early twentieth century, and the class was studied systematically from the 1950s onwards as part of the broader development of photochromic materials.
