Speedy trial

In criminal law, the right to a speedy trial is a human and civil right under which a prosecutor may not arbitrarily and indefinitely delay the trial of a criminal defendant. The right is enshrined in the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution, in the Speedy Trial Act of 1974, in most U.S. state constitutions, and internationally in Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Article 14 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The right protects defendants against prolonged pre-trial incarceration, anxiety, and the impairment of their defense as evidence decays and witnesses disappear.

United States law

In Barker v. Wingo (1972), the Supreme Court adopted a four-factor balancing test: (1) the length of the delay, (2) the reason for the delay, (3) the defendant's assertion of the right, and (4) prejudice to the defendant. In Doggett v. United States (1992) the Court held that an 8½-year delay caused by government negligence warranted dismissal even without a showing of specific prejudice. The federal Speedy Trial Act imposes firmer deadlines: an indictment must issue within 30 days of arrest and trial must begin within 70 days thereafter, subject to numerous "excludable" periods for motions and continuances. Violation can result in dismissal, with or without prejudice.

History and other jurisdictions

The right has roots in the Assize of Clarendon (1166) and Magna Carta's promise that justice would not be delayed. Canada guarantees trial within a reasonable time under section 11(b) of the Charter — in R v Jordan (2016) its Supreme Court set presumptive ceilings of 18 and 30 months — while India's Supreme Court has read a speedy-trial right into Article 21 of its constitution.
