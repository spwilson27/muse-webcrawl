Bill of pains and penalties

Hero image: the 1820 proceedings in the House of Lords on the Bill of Pains and Penalties against Queen Caroline.

A bill of pains and penalties is a legislative act in the British tradition that imposes punishment — short of, or different from, that imposed by a bill of attainder — on a named individual or group without a judicial trial. Where a bill of attainder imposed the death penalty and forfeiture for treason or felony, a bill of pains and penalties inflicted a lesser punishment, such as imprisonment, banishment, fines, or the loss of titles and civil rights.

Nature and procedure

Bills of pains and penalties were a form of legislative trial conducted by Parliament. The accused could be represented by counsel and call witnesses, and the proceedings often resembled a court case, with the House of Lords hearing evidence. However, the standards of evidence and procedure were political rather than judicial, and members voted according to conscience or interest. The device allowed Parliament to punish conduct that was not criminal under existing law, or where a prosecution in the ordinary courts was judged impractical.

Notable examples

The most famous use was the Bill of Pains and Penalties of 1820, introduced by the government of Lord Liverpool on behalf of George IV to dissolve his marriage to Queen Caroline and deprive her of her title and rights, on grounds of adultery. After a sensational public hearing, the bill's majority dwindled and it was abandoned; Caroline was cheered by the London crowds. Earlier examples include proceedings against Bishop Francis Atterbury in 1723, who was deprived of his offices and banished for Jacobite plotting, and bills against participants in the Jacobite risings.

Constitutional position

Bills of pains and penalties are generally regarded as incompatible with modern notions of the separation of powers and the rule of law, since the legislature acts as prosecutor, judge, and jury. In the United States, the federal Constitution and state constitutions prohibit bills of attainder, a prohibition the courts have extended to legislative punishments of this lesser kind; the test cases include Cummings v. Missouri and Ex parte Garland (1867), which struck down test oaths as legislative punishment. In the United Kingdom, the procedure has fallen into complete disuse, the 1820 bill being the last major instance.
