Violence in ice hockey

Violence in ice hockey refers to the physical aggression that has been a part of the sport since its early days, including body checking, fighting, and illegal stick work. Hockey is a full-contact sport in which hard body checks are legal, but deliberate attempts to injure — such as cross-checking, boarding, spearing, and butt-ending — are penalized. Fighting occupies a distinctive place: it is penalized in professional North American hockey but, unlike in most sports, does not usually result in ejection.

Fighting and the enforcer

In the National Hockey League, players who fight receive five-minute major penalties rather than game misconducts (unless they are the instigator late in a game or accumulate other infractions). This has historically sustained the role of the "enforcer" or "goon," a player whose job was to protect star teammates and retaliate against opponents. Although staged and spontaneous fights have declined sharply since the 1990s — and by the 2020s dedicated enforcers had largely disappeared — fighting remains a debated part of hockey culture.

Notable incidents and reform

Violent incidents have periodically led to criminal charges and rule changes, such as the Marty McSorley stick-swinging incident in 2000 and the Todd Bertuzzi attack on Steve Moore in 2004, which ended Moore's career. Concerns about concussions and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) have driven tighter rules on hits to the head, stronger suspensions, and, in junior hockey, outright bans on fighting. International and European hockey, by contrast, has always ejected players for fighting.
