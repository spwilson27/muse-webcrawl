Division of labour

[Image: workers each performing one task along a pin-making workshop bench, as in Smith's example]

The division of labour is the separation of a production process into distinct tasks, each performed by different workers or groups. It is the opening theme of Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776): his pin factory, where ten men dividing the eighteen operations of pin-making could produce some 48,000 pins a day against perhaps twenty each working alone, dramatised the productivity of specialisation.

Effects and critiques

Smith attributed the gains to increased dexterity, saved time between tasks, and machinery invented by specialists; he also held that the division of labour is limited by the extent of the Market — hence the gains from Free trade — and, in a passage less often quoted, worried that repetitive work could render workers 'as stupid and ignorant as it is possible for a human creature to become', arguing for public education in reply. Karl Marx made alienation under the capitalist division of labour a centrepiece of critique; Henry Ford's assembly line (1913) carried the principle to mass production, and today's global supply chains divide labour across countries by task, governed by Comparative advantage. The social division of labour — occupational structure as a whole — is a founding topic of sociology (Durkheim, 1893) as well as Economics.
