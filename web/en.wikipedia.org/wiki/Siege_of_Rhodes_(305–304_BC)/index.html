Siege of Rhodes (305–304 BC)

The siege of Rhodes in 305–304 BC was one of the most celebrated sieges of the ancient world. Demetrius Poliorcetes ("the Besieger"), son of Antigonus I Monophthalmus, attacked the wealthy island city of Rhodes with an enormous army and some of the largest siege machines ever built, in an attempt to break Rhodes' alliance with Ptolemaic Egypt. After a year of operations the Rhodians held out, Demetrius withdrew under a negotiated peace, and the city sold his abandoned siege equipment to finance the building of the Colossus of Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

Background

Rhodes, a rich maritime republic, traded with all the successor kingdoms but was closest to Ptolemy I of Egypt. When Antigonus demanded that Rhodes join his war against Ptolemy and the city refused, Demetrius was sent with roughly 40,000 troops and a vast fleet to compel it.

The siege

Demetrius's engineers constructed extraordinary machines: floating siege towers mounted on linked ships to attack the harbour mole, an armoured "tortoise" housing a giant battering ram some 55 metres long worked by a thousand men, and above all the Helepolis ("Taker of Cities"), a wheeled iron-plated tower perhaps 40 metres high, bristling with catapults and manned by hundreds of soldiers. The Rhodians answered with counterworks, raised and thickened walls, night sorties that burned machines, fireships and underwater obstacles against the floating batteries, and diplomacy — Ptolemy, Lysimachus, and Cassander all sent supplies and reinforcements. A final great assault on the city by land in 304 BC penetrated briefly into the streets before being driven back.

Settlement and legacy

With both sides exhausted and other wars pressing, a settlement was negotiated: Rhodes would remain free and autonomous, ally with Antigonus except against Ptolemy, and keep the abandoned siege train. The Rhodians dedicated statues and sold the equipment — tradition says for 300 talents — to fund the Colossus, a giant bronze statue of their patron god Helios, completed about 280 BC.
