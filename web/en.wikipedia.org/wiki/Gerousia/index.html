Gerousia

The Gerousia (Greek: γερουσία, "council of elders") was the Spartan council of elders, one of the principal institutions of the constitution of ancient Sparta. Together with the two kings, it was the most powerful body in the Spartan state and the main check on the popular assembly.

Composition

The Gerousia consisted of 30 members: the two Spartan kings (who sat by right) and 28 gerontes — men over the age of 60 who had completed their military service and were elected for life by the assembly (the Apella) from among the aristocratic families. Election was by acclamation: candidates passed before the assembly in an order chosen by lot, and judges shut in a nearby building, unable to see the candidates, declared the winner by the loudness of the shouting — a method Aristotle criticized as childish.

Powers and functions

The Gerousia prepared business for the assembly: no measure could be discussed by the Apella unless the gerontes approved it first (probouleusis), and the council could veto assembly decisions it considered harmful. It also sat as the supreme criminal court, trying cases involving the death penalty, exile, and loss of civil rights — including cases against the kings themselves. Members of the Gerousia were not subject to the scrutiny (euthyna) applied to other officials, which made their life tenure a considerable source of power.

Role in the constitution

According to tradition, the Gerousia was established by the lawgiver Lycurgus, and its creation is described in the Great Rhetra preserved by Plutarch. Ancient writers including Aristotle and Polybius saw the Gerousia as the aristocratic element in Sparta's admired "mixed constitution", balancing the monarchical kings, the democratic assembly, and the annually elected ephors — although the ephorate, not mentioned in the original rhetra, later grew to rival the council in power.

Other gerousiai

Councils of elders called gerousiai existed in many other Greek cities and, in the Hellenistic and Roman periods, in cities throughout the Greek East and in bodies such as the Jewish council in Jerusalem, where the term was used for local governing councils distinct from the Sanhedrin.
