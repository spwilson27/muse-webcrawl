Time inconsistency

Labelled placeholder: divergence between a plan made today and choices made tomorrow.

In economics, dynamic inconsistency or time inconsistency is a situation in which a decision-maker's preferences change over time in such a way that a preference can become inconsistent at another point in time. This can be thought of as there being many different "selves" within decision makers, with each "self" actually representing the decision maker at a specific point in time; the inconsistency then occurs when all preferences are not aligned.

Monetary policy

The concept was formalised in a celebrated 1977 paper by Finn E. Kydland and Edward C. Prescott, "Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal Plans". A central bank that announces low inflation to anchor expectations may later be tempted to exploit the short-run trade-off and inflate — but rational agents anticipate this, producing an "inflation bias" with no output gain. The analysis underpins arguments for rules-based policy, central bank independence, and inflation targeting, and contributed to Kydland and Prescott's 2004 Nobel Prize.

Behavioural economics

In individual choice, time inconsistency is modelled with hyperbolic discounting: people disproportionately prefer smaller-sooner rewards over larger-later ones when the sooner reward is imminent, reversing choices they made earlier. This explains procrastination, undersaving and addiction, and motivates commitment devices — from pension auto-enrolment to Ulysses contracts.
