Phosphatase


A phosphatase is an enzyme that removes a phosphate group from its substrate by hydrolysis — the opposite reaction to that catalysed by kinases.

A phosphatase is an enzyme that uses water to cleave a phosphoric acid monoester into a phosphate ion and an alcohol (or another free hydroxyl group). Phosphatases thereby oppose the action of kinases, which add phosphate groups using ATP. Together, phosphorylation and dephosphorylation form one of the cell's principal regulatory mechanisms.

Protein phosphatases

Protein phosphatases remove phosphate groups from amino-acid residues of proteins. They are classified by the residues they target: protein serine/threonine phosphatases (such as PP1, PP2A, PP2B/calcineurin and PP2C) and protein tyrosine phosphatases, along with dual-specificity enzymes acting on both. Calcineurin is the target of the immunosuppressant drugs ciclosporin and tacrolimus. Dysregulation of phosphatases is implicated in cancer, diabetes and neurological disease; the tumour suppressor PTEN is a lipid phosphatase that dephosphorylates PIP3.

Other phosphatases

Alkaline phosphatase works best at alkaline pH and is widely measured in clinical blood tests, where elevated levels can indicate liver or bone disease. Acid phosphatase is found in the prostate and lysosomes. Nucleotidases, phytases and sugar phosphatases act on non-protein substrates, and phosphatases are used routinely in molecular biology laboratories — for example, to prevent re-ligation of digested plasmid DNA.
