The Apple M1 is a series of ARM-based systems on a chip (SoCs) designed by Apple Inc. as the central processing unit (CPU) and graphics processing unit (GPU) for its Mac desktops, laptops, and the iPad Pro and iPad Air. Announced on 10 November 2020, it was the first Apple silicon chip built for Macs and the centrepiece of Apple's two-year transition away from Intel processors, which Macs had used since 2006. The M1 was first used in the MacBook Air, 13-inch MacBook Pro, and Mac mini, and later in the 24-inch iMac, iPad Pro (5th generation), and iPad Air (5th generation).

Design

The M1 is fabricated on TSMC's 5-nanometre process and contains 16 billion transistors. Its CPU uses a hybrid ("big.LITTLE"-style) configuration of four high-performance "Firestorm" cores and four high-efficiency "Icestorm" cores, derived from those in the A14 Bionic. The chip integrates an Apple-designed GPU with seven or eight cores, a 16-core Neural Engine capable of 11 trillion operations per second, the Secure Enclave, image signal processor, and media encode/decode engines supporting formats such as H.264, HEVC, and ProRes acceleration in later variants.

A defining feature is the unified memory architecture: the CPU, GPU, and other processors share a single pool of LPDDR4X memory (8 or 16 GB) packaged with the chip, reducing data copying and improving efficiency, at the cost of memory not being upgradeable after purchase.

Performance and reception

The M1 was widely praised on release for delivering performance comparable to or better than the Intel chips it replaced — especially in single-core benchmarks — while consuming far less power, enabling the fanless design of the MacBook Air and markedly longer battery life in the MacBook Pro. Because it uses the ARM instruction set, Intel-based Mac applications run through Apple's Rosetta 2 binary translation layer, generally with good performance. Criticisms focused on limited external display support (a single external display on the first M1 laptops), few ports, and non-upgradeable memory and storage.

Variants

Apple extended the line with the M1 Pro and M1 Max (October 2021), with more CPU and GPU cores, memory bandwidth, and display support for the 14- and 16-inch MacBook Pro, and the M1 Ultra (March 2022), which fuses two M1 Max dies using a packaging interconnect Apple calls UltraFusion and was used in the Mac Studio. The base M1 was succeeded by the Apple M2 in June 2022.
