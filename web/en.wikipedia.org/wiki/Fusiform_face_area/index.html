Fusiform face area

The FFA lies in the fusiform gyrus of the ventral temporal cortex.

The fusiform face area (FFA) is a region of the human fusiform gyrus, part of the ventral stream of the visual system, that is specialised for facial recognition. It is situated on the underside of the temporal lobe (Brodmann area 37) and responds much more strongly to faces than to other objects in functional MRI studies. The area was characterised in the 1990s by Nancy Kanwisher and colleagues, whose 1997 paper established it as a key example of functional specialisation in the brain.

Function and debate

Damage to the region (usually on the right) can cause prosopagnosia, the inability to recognise faces, while leaving other object recognition largely intact — evidence for a dedicated face-processing module. The FFA is part of a wider face network including the occipital face area and the posterior superior temporal sulcus, which handles changeable aspects of faces such as gaze and expression.

However, the extent of the FFA's specificity is debated. It also responds, more weakly, to objects of visual expertise — for example, in bird watchers viewing birds, or car experts viewing cars — leading some researchers to argue it is an expertise area rather than a face module per se. Others note activation for body parts and some non-face categories. Intracranial recordings and stimulation studies (which can briefly distort face perception) support a central, though not exclusive, role in face identity processing.

Development

Face-selective responses emerge early in development and the FFA continues to mature through adolescence, increasing in size and selectivity. In congenital prosopagnosia the area may be present but poorly connected to the rest of the face network. A homologous region exists in macaque monkeys (the middle face patch), enabling comparative research.
