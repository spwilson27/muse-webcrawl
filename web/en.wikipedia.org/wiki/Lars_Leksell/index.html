Lars Leksell (23 November 1907 – 12 January 1986) was a Swedish physician and professor of neurosurgery at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm. He is widely credited as the father of stereotactic radiosurgery and is best known for inventing the Gamma Knife, a device that delivers focused beams of gamma radiation to treat tumours and other disorders deep within the brain without open surgery.

Life and career

Leksell studied medicine at the Karolinska Institute, completing his medical degree in the 1930s and his doctoral thesis in 1945 on the action potential of motor nerves. He trained as a neurosurgeon under Herbert Olivecrona, the founder of Swedish neurosurgery, and succeeded him as professor and head of neurosurgery at the Serafimer Hospital and later the Karolinska Hospital.

Stereotactic surgery

In 1949 Leksell introduced a stereotactic frame based on a polar coordinate (arc-centred) system, which allowed instruments to be guided to targets within the brain with millimetre precision. Unlike earlier Cartesian frames, his design ensured that a probe introduced along the arc would always reach the centre point, making it adaptable to X-rays, electrodes, needles, and later radiation beams.

Radiosurgery and the Gamma Knife

Leksell coined the term "radiosurgery" and, with radiobiologist Börje Larsson, explored the use of cross-fired proton and X-ray beams from 1951 onwards. In 1967–68 they completed the first Gamma Knife unit at Sophiahemmet in Stockholm, using multiple cobalt-60 sources aimed through a collimator helmet at a single intracranial target. The device was initially used for functional disorders and later became a standard treatment for arteriovenous malformations, vestibular schwannomas, and small brain tumours. In 1972 Leksell co-founded the company Elekta to commercialise the technology.

Legacy

Leksell's work laid the foundation for modern stereotactic and functional neurosurgery worldwide. Hundreds of Gamma Knife centres now treat tens of thousands of patients each year, and his stereotactic frame remains in clinical use in both its original and adapted forms.
