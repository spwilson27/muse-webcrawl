A boiling water reactor (BWR) is a type of light water nuclear reactor used for the generation of electrical power. It is the second most common type of electricity-generating nuclear reactor after the pressurized water reactor (PWR). The BWR was developed in the mid-1950s at Argonne National Laboratory and by General Electric.

Principle of operation

In a BWR, the reactor core heats water, which is allowed to boil directly inside the pressure vessel. The resulting steam is separated from the remaining water by steam separators and dryers at the top of the vessel and is then used to drive a turbine generator. After passing through the turbine, the steam is condensed and returned to the vessel as feedwater. Because the steam is generated in the core itself, a BWR needs no separate steam generators, unlike a PWR with its pressurized primary loop.

Reactivity is controlled by cruciform control blades inserted from below the core — gravity cannot be relied upon to drop rods in during an accident, so hydraulic drives insert them — and by varying the recirculation flow through the core: increasing flow sweeps out steam voids, improves moderation, and raises power. The negative void coefficient means that increased boiling reduces reactivity, giving the design an inherent self-limiting characteristic.

Design generations

GE produced successive BWR product lines (BWR/1 through BWR/6) with different containment designs, from the small Mark I pressure-suppression containment used at many early plants to the larger Mark III. Later designs include the Advanced Boiling Water Reactor (ABWR), first operated at Kashiwazaki-Kariwa in Japan, and the Economic Simplified BWR (ESBWR) with natural-circulation cooling and passive safety systems.

Safety systems and the Fukushima accident

BWRs rely on emergency core cooling systems such as high- and low-pressure injection and automatic depressurization. The 2011 Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster involved GE BWR/3 and BWR/4 units with Mark I containments; loss of all AC power led to core damage and hydrogen explosions, prompting worldwide reviews of Mark I venting and flood protection.

Comparison with PWR

The BWR operates at a lower pressure (about 7 MPa, versus roughly 15 MPa in a PWR), which reduces the mechanical demands on the pressure vessel, but the turbine and much of the secondary plant handle slightly radioactive steam and must be treated as radiologically controlled areas. BWR control rods enter from the bottom, and the top of the vessel remains free for steam separation equipment and refuelling access.
