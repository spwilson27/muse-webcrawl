Mondrian dress

The Mondrian dress is a shift dress designed by the French couturier Yves Saint Laurent in 1965 for his autumn/winter collection, inspired by the abstract paintings of the Dutch artist Piet Mondrian. The collection — consisting of six cocktail dresses and a small number of related designs — is among the most famous in 20th-century fashion, and the dress has become an icon of the dialogue between art and fashion.

Mondrian dress by Yves Saint Laurent, autumn/winter 1965.

Design

The dresses were A-line sleeveless shift dresses made of wool jersey, decorated with blocks of white, red, blue and yellow separated by black lines — reproducing the geometry of Mondrian's mature neoplasticist canvases, such as Composition with Red, Blue and Yellow. Rather than printing the design onto finished cloth, Saint Laurent's ateliers constructed each dress so that the coloured blocks were individual pieces of fabric seamed together, with the black bands masking the joins; the dresses were carefully engineered so the seams followed the "grid" while the garment retained its clean, flat silhouette.

Reception and influence

Presented in August 1965, the collection was an immediate sensation, hailed by fashion journalists as the look of the season and widely credited with popularising the shift dress and with bringing pop art and op art aesthetics into mainstream fashion. Inexpensive copies flooded shops around the world, making the Mondrian dress one of the most imitated designs of the 1960s. The dresses, originally sold for several thousand new francs, are now held by major museums, including the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, and originals command high prices at auction.

The collection marked a turning point in Saint Laurent's career, demonstrating that haute couture could engage directly with contemporary art and youth culture. Saint Laurent went on to create further art-homage collections, including dresses referencing pop art (1966), Picasso and Matisse.
