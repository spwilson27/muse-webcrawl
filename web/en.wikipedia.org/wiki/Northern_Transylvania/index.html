Northern Transylvania (Romanian: Transilvania de Nord; Hungarian: Észak-Erdély) is a region in north-western Romania, part of the historical region of Transylvania. It is best known internationally for its transfer between Romania and Hungary in the mid-20th century: awarded to Hungary by the Second Vienna Award of 1940, it was returned to Romania after the Second World War.

History

Transylvania belonged to the Kingdom of Hungary for centuries before 1918, when, following the First World War, it was united with Romania under the Treaty of Trianon (1920). Hungary never fully accepted the loss. On 30 August 1940, under pressure from Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, the Second Vienna Award transferred the northern part of Transylvania — about 43,000 km² with some 2.5 million inhabitants of mixed Romanian and Hungarian population — to Hungary.

Second World War period

The 1940–44 period saw tensions and atrocities on both sides, including massacres of Romanian civilians by Hungarian troops (as at Ip and Trăznea) and reprisals against Hungarians in 1944. The region's Jewish population was deported to Auschwitz in May–June 1944 under Hungarian administration, with catastrophic loss of life. Romanian and Soviet troops retook the territory in the autumn of 1944, and the 1947 Treaty of Paris formally restored Northern Transylvania to Romania.

Today

The region contains a substantial Hungarian minority, concentrated in the Székely counties and in cities such as Cluj-Napoca (Kolozsvár), which remains a major university and cultural centre for both Romanians and Hungarians. Disputes over language rights, autonomy and historical memory continue to feature in Romanian–Hungarian relations.
