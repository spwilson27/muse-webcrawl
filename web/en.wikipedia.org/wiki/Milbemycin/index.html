Milbemycin

The milbemycins are a family of macrocyclic lactones produced by fermentation of Streptomyces species, closely related to the avermectins. Like the avermectins, they are potent antiparasitic agents active against nematodes and arthropods, acting on glutamate-gated chloride channels in invertebrate nerve and muscle cells. The best-known members are milbemycin oxime and moxidectin.

Discovery

Milbemycins were first isolated in the 1970s from Streptomyces hygroscopicus subsp. aureolacrimosus in Japan, where they were identified as acaricides and anthelmintics. The milbemycin A3/A4 mixture was developed as the agricultural miticide milbemectin, while derivatives were selected for animal health.

Veterinary uses

Milbemycin oxime, the oxime derivative of milbemycins A3 and A4, is widely used in dogs and cats for the prevention of heartworm disease (Dirofilaria immitis) and for control of intestinal worms including hookworms, roundworms, and whipworms. It is sold alone (Interceptor) and in combinations with lufenuron (Sentinel), praziquantel, or the neonicotinoid/spinosad partners in multi-parasite products. Moxidectin, a semisynthetic milbemycin, is used in livestock, dogs, and — since its 2018 FDA approval — in humans for the treatment of onchocerciasis (river blindness).

Mechanism and safety

Milbemycins bind glutamate-gated chloride channels, increasing chloride permeability, hyperpolarizing nerve and muscle cells, and causing flaccid paralysis and death of the parasite. In mammals these channels are confined to the central nervous system and protected by the blood–brain barrier; certain dog breeds carrying the MDR1 (ABCB1) mutation are more sensitive to macrocyclic lactones, though milbemycin oxime is considered to have a comparatively wide safety margin at label doses.
