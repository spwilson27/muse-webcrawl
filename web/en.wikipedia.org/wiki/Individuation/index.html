Individuation

Individuation is a concept with distinct uses in philosophy, psychology, and biology. Most prominently, in the psychology of Carl Gustav Jung, individuation is the lifelong process by which a person integrates the conscious and unconscious parts of the psyche to become a distinct, indivisible whole — the psychological individual, or Self. In philosophy, the principle of individuation (principium individuationis) concerns what makes a thing a particular individual rather than another of the same kind, a problem running from Aristotle and the scholastics to Leibniz and Schopenhauer.

In Jungian psychology

Jung regarded individuation as the central process of human development, especially in the second half of life. He described it as the process by which the ego comes into relationship with the Self — the archetype of wholeness that is both the centre and the totality of the psyche. Unlike Freud's model centred on childhood, Jung's individuation requires confronting and integrating unconscious contents: the persona (the social mask) must be recognised as partial, the shadow (repressed and disowned aspects of the personality) acknowledged and assimilated, and the contrasexual archetypes anima (in men) and animus (in women) withdrawn from projection. The goal is not perfection but wholeness — becoming "an individual", distinct from both conformity and mere individualism.

Stages and elements

Jungian analysis supports individuation through dream interpretation, active imagination, and attention to symbols and synchronicities. The process often manifests in mandala imagery, which Jung took as spontaneous representations of the ordering Self. Because the unconscious is also collective, individuation involves engaging with archetypes of the collective unconscious rather than merely personal material. Jung emphasised that individuation does not isolate a person from society; the more individuated a person becomes, the more genuinely they can relate to others.

In philosophy

Scholastic philosophers debated what individuates a substance: for Thomas Aquinas, material substances are individuated by designated matter (materia signata); for Duns Scotus, by a positive individuating formality he called haecceitas ("thisness"). Leibniz held that each monad is unique, a view summarised as the identity of indiscernibles. Schopenhauer used principium individuationis for space and time themselves: the forms through which the one indivisible Will appears as a multiplicity of separate individuals.

In biology

In biology, individuation refers to the delimitation of an individual organism — a question complicated by colonial organisms, symbiosis, chimeras, and clonal reproduction — and to the processes of development by which parts of an organism become distinct and specialised.
