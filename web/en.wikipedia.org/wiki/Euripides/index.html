Euripides

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A marble herm of Euripides from the Roman period: the tragedian with furrowed brow and long philosopher's beard]

Euripides (c. 480 – c. 406 BC) was the third and most controversial of the great Athenian tragedians. He wrote some 90 plays, won fewer prizes than his rivals in his lifetime, and after death became the most popular tragedian of the ancient world — the one from whom most surviving Greek tragedies (18 plays, plus the satyr-play Cyclops) descend.

His work unsettles: gods behave ignobly, heroes argue like lawyers, enslaved women and madmen get the great speeches. Medea (431 BC), Hippolytus (428 BC), The Trojan Women (415 BC), Electra, Helen, Iphigenia among the Taurians, and the posthumous Bacchae — his last, most terrifying play, in which Dionysus destroys the king who denied him — span war protest, psychological drama and romance.

Reputation in Athens

Aristophanes caricatured Euripides mercilessly — as a greengrocer's son corrupting Athens with clever slaves and adulterous queens — yet quoted him endlessly; the parodies are themselves evidence of his fame. He spent his last two years at the Macedonian court of Archelaus, dying there in 406 BC, the same year as Sophocles.

Why so many plays survive

Euripides' Hellenistic popularity made him a school author, and chance preserved two groups: ten 'select' plays with commentary, and a run of an alphabetical complete edition. His rationalism, rhetoric and sympathy for outsiders made him the ancient tragedian most legible to modern readers; Shaw, Eliot and feminist critics alike have claimed him.
