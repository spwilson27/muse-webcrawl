Tenderloin, San Francisco

The Tenderloin is a neighbourhood in downtown San Francisco, California, roughly bounded by Van Ness Avenue on the west, the Civic Center and Market Street to the south, and Geary and Post Streets to the north. Long known for its single-room-occupancy hotels, poverty, homelessness and street-level drug activity, it is also one of the city's most historically and ethnically diverse neighbourhoods, home to a large Southeast Asian community in its "Little Saigon" section.

History

The district developed after the 1906 earthquake and fire, when cheap hotels and apartment buildings were rapidly constructed for working-class residents. It acquired a reputation as a vice district of gambling halls, speakeasies, jazz clubs and theatres; notable venues included the Black Hawk jazz club, where Miles Davis recorded live albums. The origin of the name is uncertain — a common story links it to the New York Tenderloin and to police officers who, enriched by graft in the district, could afford tenderloin steaks.

Community and character

The Tenderloin has a high concentration of social services, supportive housing and nonprofit organizations serving low-income residents, seniors, families and people with disabilities. In the 1980s, refugees from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos settled in the area, giving rise to Little Saigon along Larkin Street. The Tenderloin National Forest, a community garden in a former alley, and the Tenderloin Museum reflect local organizing to preserve the neighbourhood's history.

Issues

The neighbourhood has some of the city's highest rates of homelessness, open-air drug use and overdose deaths, particularly associated with the fentanyl crisis, and it was among the areas hardest hit by the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2020 the University of California Hastings (now UC Law SF) sued the city over conditions in the Tenderloin, leading to a settlement to reduce tents on sidewalks.
