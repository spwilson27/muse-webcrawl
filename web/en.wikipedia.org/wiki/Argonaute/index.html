The Argonaute protein family is central to RNA silencing processes, including RNA interference (RNAi) and microRNA-mediated gene regulation. Argonaute proteins bind small guide RNAs — such as siRNAs and miRNAs — and form the core of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which they guide to complementary target RNAs for cleavage or repression.

Structure and mechanism

Argonaute proteins are about 100 kDa in size and consist of four main domains: N, PAZ, MID, and PIWI, arranged in a bilobed structure with a channel that cradles the guide RNA. The PAZ domain anchors the 3′ end of the guide and the MID domain binds its 5′ phosphate. The PIWI domain adopts an RNase H-like fold and, in catalytically active ("slicer") Argonautes such as human AGO2, cleaves the target RNA opposite the middle of the guide.

Subfamilies and roles

The family divides into the Ago subfamily, expressed widely and acting in miRNA and siRNA pathways, and the Piwi subfamily, expressed mainly in germ cells, which binds piRNAs and silences transposable elements. Humans have eight Argonaute genes: AGO1–AGO4 and PIWIL1–PIWIL4. Argonaute proteins also occur in bacteria and archaea, where prokaryotic Argonautes defend against foreign nucleic acids and have been explored as programmable tools alongside CRISPR systems.

Name

The family is named after the argonaute mutant of the plant Arabidopsis (AGO1), described in 1998, whose pointed, curled leaves resembled a small squid; the name alludes to the argonauts of Greek myth and the paper nautilus Argonauta.
