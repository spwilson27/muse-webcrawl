Sit-in

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sit-in or sit-down is a form of direct action and nonviolent protest in which one or more people occupy an area — for example a segregated lunch counter, a government office or a university building — and refuse to leave until their demands are met, the action is forcibly ended, or they are arrested. Sit-ins are designed to create a moral and public-relations dilemma for authorities: evicting peaceful seated protesters generates damaging publicity, while tolerating them concedes the protesters' claim on the space.

History

The tactic descends from the labour movement's sit-down strikes of the 1930s and was adapted by civil rights activists. Early episodes included sit-ins by the Congress of Racial Equality in the 1940s and student actions in the late 1950s, but the tactic became nationally prominent with the Greensboro sit-ins that began on 1 February 1960, when four African American students from North Carolina A&T sat at the whites-only Woolworth lunch counter in Greensboro, North Carolina. Within weeks the movement spread to dozens of Southern cities; the Nashville sit-ins, conducted by students trained in nonviolence by James Lawson, led in May 1960 to Nashville becoming the first major Southern city to begin desegregating its lunch counters. Sit-ins helped spur the founding of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and contributed directly to the climate that produced the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which outlawed segregation in public accommodations.

Later uses

Students used sit-ins against the Vietnam War and university complicity in it, and the tactic has since been adopted by disability-rights activists (notably ADAPT), anti-apartheid and divestment campaigns, environmental movements such as school-strike climate protests, and Occupation-style movements. Variants include the "die-in", "teach-in" and the occupation of administrative offices.
