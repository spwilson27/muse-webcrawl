Activity tracker

An activity tracker, also known as a fitness tracker, is a device or application for monitoring and tracking fitness-related metrics such as distance walked or run, calorie consumption, and in some cases heartbeat and quality of sleep. The term is primarily used for dedicated electronic monitoring devices that are synced, in many cases wirelessly, to a computer or smartphone for long-term data tracking. There are also independent smartphone and social-media applications.

History

The concept of tracking physical activity dates back to the pedometer, a mechanical step-counting device described as early as the 18th century and popularised in Japan in the 1960s under the name manpo-kei ("10,000 steps meter"). The modern electronic activity tracker emerged in the late 2000s. Fitbit, founded in 2007, released its first clip-on tracker in 2009. Nike and Jawbone followed with wrist-worn devices, and the category expanded rapidly with smartwatches such as the Apple Watch (2015), which integrated activity tracking into a general-purpose wearable.

Technology

Most trackers use a three-axis accelerometer to detect movement and estimate step counts, distance and sleep patterns. Many include optical heart-rate sensors based on photoplethysmography, which shines green light into the skin and measures blood-flow changes. Higher-end devices add GPS for route tracking, altimeters for elevation, gyroscopes, and sensors for blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), skin temperature and electrocardiogram (ECG) readings. Data are typically transferred via Bluetooth to a companion app, where algorithms convert raw motion signals into estimates of steps, calories burned, sleep stages and exercise sessions.

Accuracy and criticism

Studies have found that step counts from popular trackers are generally accurate within a few percent under controlled conditions, while estimates of energy expenditure can err by 20 percent or more. Critics have raised privacy concerns about the collection and sale of health-related data, as well as questions about whether tracker use produces lasting improvements in health behaviour. Some research suggests modest increases in daily step counts among users, particularly when trackers are combined with goal-setting or social features.
