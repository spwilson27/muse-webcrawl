Baluster

Turned balusters supporting a handrail.

A baluster is a vertical moulded shaft, square, or lathe-turned form in stone, wood or metal, such as that found in a staircase, parapet, or balustrade. A row of balusters topped by a handrail or coping is called a balustrade. Balusters support the handrail of a stair or the coping of a parapet, and also serve a safety function by closing the gap beneath the rail.

Form and history

The classic baluster profile resembles a vase or bulbous urn: a base (torus), a swelling belly or bulb, a collar, and a capital supporting the rail. The word derives from the Italian balaustro, from balaustra, the wild pomegranate flower, whose bud the profile resembles. Assyrian palace reliefs show early baluster-like window colonnettes, and Roman examples survive, but the form became ubiquitous in Renaissance Italy, where architects such as Michelangelo used monumental balusters on the Campidoglio in Rome. Giuliano da Sangallo is credited with popularising the balustrade in the late fifteenth century.

Materials and construction

Stair balusters in domestic construction are commonly turned wood or forged metal; exterior balustrades historically used stone or cast stone. Modern building codes regulate baluster spacing — in many jurisdictions the gaps must be small enough that a 4-inch (100 mm) sphere cannot pass through, to protect children. In North America, stair balusters are sometimes informally called spindles.

Profile terminology

Terms for the parts of a turned baluster include the pommel or base block, the torus (a rounded moulding at the base), the collar (a ring around the shaft), the baluster shaft itself, and the capital at the top. Profiles vary by period and regional style, from slender vase shapes to barley-twist and square Jacobean forms.
