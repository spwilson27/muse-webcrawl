Kumina

Kumina is an Afro-Jamaican religion and cultural tradition that developed in St. Thomas Parish in eastern Jamaica, largely from Kongo indentured labourers who arrived after emancipation in the mid-19th century. Its name derives from the Kikongo word kumina, meaning coming together or possession. The tradition centres on ceremonies of drumming, dancing and song — in a language preserving many Kikongo words — through which ancestral spirits are invited to possess participants.


Kumina drummers. The drums are played with the hands and a pair of sticks (katta).

Beliefs and practice

Kumina cosmology recognises a supreme god (often called King Zambi or God Almighty) and a hierarchy of spirits including sky gods, earth gods and ancestral spirits. Ceremonies are held for births, funerals, tombstoning, healing and thanksgiving, and typically last through the night. Two main drums are used: the bandu (or kbandu), a larger lead drum, and the smaller "playing cast" or cyas; both are goatskin-headed and accompanied by shakers (shakas), scrapers and singing. Dancers move counter-clockwise in a ring, and a possessed dancer may be guided by a "Kumina king" or "queen" — hereditary ritual leaders.

Influence

Kumina strongly influenced Jamaican popular music: its drumming underpins the nyabinghi rhythms of Rastafari and appears in reggae recordings. The tradition was documented extensively by scholars including Monica Schuler and Maureen Warner-Lewis, and celebrated practitioners such as Imogene "Queenie" Kennedy preserved its repertoire in the 20th century.
