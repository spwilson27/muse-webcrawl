The terms Anno Domini (AD) and before Christ (BC) are used when designating years in the Julian and Gregorian calendars. The term anno Domini is Medieval Latin and means "in the year of the Lord". The system was devised by the monk Dionysius Exiguus in AD 525 to replace the Diocletian era in his Easter tables.

History

Dionysius Exiguus, a Scythian monk working in Rome, calculated that Jesus was born in the year he designated 1 AD, counted from the traditional Incarnation. His system spread through the works of the Venerable Bede, whose Ecclesiastical History of the English People (731) used it extensively and popularized the practice of counting years backwards before the Incarnation as "before Christ".

The system gradually replaced regnal years and other era systems across Western Europe, becoming standard by the later Middle Ages, and it was carried worldwide with European expansion. The Gregorian calendar reform of 1582 retained the Dionysian epoch.

Conventions

There is no year zero in the system: the year 1 BC is followed directly by AD 1. Dionysius's calculation is now generally considered to be a few years off, since Herod the Great, who was alive at the time of Jesus's birth according to the Gospel of Matthew, died in 4 BC. Astronomers use a system with a year 0 for calculation, equivalent to 1 BC.

Traditionally, AD is placed before the year number (AD 2026) and BC after it (44 BC). In many modern contexts, the religiously neutral equivalents CE (Common Era) and BCE (Before the Common Era) are used with identical numbering.
