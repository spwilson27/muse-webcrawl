Percomorpha is a large clade of ray-finned fishes (Actinopterygii) that includes the perches, groupers, wrasses, tunas, flatfishes, seahorses, gobies, cichlids and many other familiar groups. With well over 17,000 described species in more than 280 families, percomorphs account for roughly half of all fish species and about a quarter of all vertebrate species, making them the most diverse radiation of vertebrates on Earth. They dominate marine coastal waters, coral reefs and many freshwater systems.

Characteristics

Percomorphs are united by features including spines in the dorsal and anal fins, ctenoid scales in most groups, a distinctive arrangement of the jaw and suspensorium bones, and pelvic fins positioned forward on the body, often directly beneath the pectorals. Beyond these traits their body plans are extraordinarily varied: from the flattened flounders and the upright seahorses to the deep-bodied angelfishes and the streamlined tunas.

Evolution and classification

Fossil evidence suggests percomorphs originated in the Late Cretaceous and underwent an explosive radiation around and after the end-Cretaceous mass extinction 66 million years ago, filling marine niches vacated by extinct groups. Their internal classification was long considered one of the hardest problems in ichthyology — the group was nicknamed a "bush at the top" of the fish tree. Large-scale DNA sequencing studies in the 2010s, notably the phylogenetic classification of bony fishes by Betancur-R. and colleagues, resolved Percomorpha into a series of major series and orders, including Perciformes (in a restricted sense), Syngnathiformes, Gobiiformes, Ovalentaria (containing cichlids and their relatives), Carangiformes (including flatfishes) and Eupercaria.

Ecological and economic importance

Many of the world's most important food fishes — tunas, mackerels, sea basses, snappers, groupers and perches — are percomorphs, as are the great majority of tropical aquarium fishes. Cichlids of the African Great Lakes are textbook examples of explosive adaptive radiation and speciation.
