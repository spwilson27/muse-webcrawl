An inductor, also called a coil or choke, is a passive two-terminal electrical component that stores energy in a magnetic field when electric current flows through it. It typically consists of an insulated wire wound into a coil, often around a core of air, iron, or ferrite that increases the inductance. The defining property, inductance, is measured in henries (symbol H) and quantifies the component's tendency to oppose changes in current, as described by Faraday's law of induction and Lenz's law.

Inductors come in many forms, from tiny surface-mount chips to large power chokes.

Physics

For an ideal inductor, the voltage across it is proportional to the rate of change of current: V = L · dI/dt. The energy stored is E = ½LI². Inductance of a solenoid increases with the square of the number of turns and with the permeability and cross-sectional area of the core, and decreases with length. Practical inductors also exhibit resistance, parasitic capacitance, and core losses; magnetic cores can saturate at high currents, causing inductance to fall.

Applications

Inductors are essential in filters (especially LC and RLC circuits), tuned circuits and radio-frequency oscillators, transformers, motors and generators, energy storage in switch-mode power supplies, and as chokes that block high-frequency alternating current while passing direct current. In power electronics, large inductors smooth current in converters and drives; in audio and signal circuits, small inductors shape frequency response.

Types

Common constructions include air-core coils, iron-core and ferrite-core inductors, toroidal inductors (which confine magnetic flux and reduce interference), laminated-core power chokes, and planar or integrated inductors fabricated on circuit boards and chips. Variable inductors adjust inductance by moving a core or selecting taps.
