Taxus baccata is a species of evergreen tree in the family Taxaceae, known as the English yew or European yew. Native to Europe, north-west Africa and south-west Asia, it is a slow-growing, exceptionally long-lived conifer, often planted in churchyards and formal gardens. All parts of the plant except the fleshy seed covering are highly poisonous.

Description

Yew is a small to medium-sized tree, typically 10–20 m tall with a trunk that becomes thick and fluted with age. The leaves are dark green, flat needles arranged spirally but appearing two-ranked. The species is mostly dioecious: male plants shed clouds of pollen in spring, while female plants produce seeds each partly enclosed in a bright red, berry-like cup called an aril — the only non-toxic part of the plant, its sweet flesh eaten and dispersed by birds.

Toxicity and medicine

Yew contains taxine alkaloids, cardiotoxins that have killed livestock and people; historically the tree was associated with death and graveyards. Yet it is also a source of medicine: the bark of its relative the Pacific yew yielded paclitaxel (Taxol), a major chemotherapy drug, and compounds from T. baccata needles are now used as precursors for semi-synthetic taxanes such as docetaxel.

Longevity and culture

Individual yews may live for many centuries — claims of 2,000 years or more are made for churchyard trees such as the Fortingall Yew in Scotland and the Llangernyw Yew in Wales, though dating hollow trunks is unreliable. Yew wood is hard and elastic, prized for the medieval English longbow, and the tree remains a potent symbol of both death and eternal life in British folklore.
