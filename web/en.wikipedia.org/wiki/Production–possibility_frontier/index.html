Production–possibility frontier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The production–possibility frontier (PPF), also called the production possibilities curve, is a model in economics showing the maximum combinations of two goods (or services) that an economy can produce using all of its resources and technology fully and efficiently.

Interpretation

Points on the frontier are productively efficient: more of one good can only be obtained by producing less of the other, which illustrates opportunity cost. Points inside the frontier are attainable but inefficient (resources are unemployed or misallocated); points outside are unattainable with current resources and technology.

Shape and shifts

The frontier is usually drawn concave ("bowed out") because resources are not equally suited to both goods, so opportunity cost increases as production specialises. Economic growth, better technology or more resources shift the frontier outward; war, disaster or resource depletion shift it inward. A technological improvement in one industry rotates the frontier outward along that good's axis.

Uses

The PPF is a staple of introductory economics, used to teach scarcity, choice, efficiency (including Pareto efficiency at a tangency with social preferences) and the potential gains from trade via comparative advantage.
