Needle lace is a type of lace created using a needle and thread to stitch up hundreds of small stitches to form the lace itself. It is considered by many to be the highest form of lace-making: the work is built up stitch by stitch, entirely independent of any woven ground, which gives the maker complete freedom of design. Pure needle lace emerged in Italy in the early 16th century from cutwork and reticella embroidery.

Technique

The design is drawn on parchment or card. Along the outlines, "couching" threads are basted down through the pattern. The worker then fills the shapes with closely worked buttonhole (blanket) stitches over laid foundation threads, building up areas of solid cloth stitch, open meshes, and raised, padded outlines. When the piece is finished, the basting threads are cut away from behind and the lace is released from the pattern. A single elaborate piece could occupy a skilled lacemaker for months.

History and varieties

The first printed pattern book devoted to needlework of this kind was Federico Vinciolo's Les singuliers et nouveaux pourtraicts (1587). From reticella — geometric lace worked over a grid of drawn threads — Venetian makers developed punto in aria ("stitches in the air"), free of any fabric base. Venetian gros point of the 17th century, with its bold raised scrolls, was among the most prized luxury goods in Europe. France responded by founding royal manufactures: the point d'Alençon and point de Sedan were developed under Colbert to rival Venice, and Alençon, with its fine hexagonal ground, became known as the "queen of lace". Related traditions include Armenian needle lace (worked around a circular motif), Irish Kenmare and Youghal laces, and the needle laces of Burano, which is still made today.

Decline and revival

Handmade needle lace could not compete with machine-made lace after the early 19th century, and the industry collapsed in most regions. It survives as a heritage craft, taught in lace schools in Burano, Alençon and elsewhere, and is recognised in several national inventories of intangible cultural heritage.
