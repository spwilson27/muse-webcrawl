Atlatl

Atlatl
TypeSpear-thrower
OriginUpper Paleolithic
Word originNahuatl ahtlatl

An atlatl (from Nahuatl ahtlatl) is a spear-thrower: a hand-held tool that uses leverage to achieve greater velocity and distance in dart or spear throwing. It consists of a shaft with a cup or spur at one end that holds the butt of the dart, effectively lengthening the thrower's arm and multiplying the force of the throw.

History

Spear-throwers are among the oldest mechanical projectile devices. The earliest secure examples date to the Upper Paleolithic of Europe (Solutrean, c. 17,000 years ago), and the technology spread worldwide. In the Americas the atlatl was used for thousands of years, by cultures from the Arctic to Mesoamerica, where Aztec warriors used it in battle against the Spanish. In Australia, the analogous device is the woomera.

Mechanics and use

The dart rests along the thrower, its butt seated against the spur. A throwing motion swings the atlatl up and forward, the extra leverage accelerating the flexible dart, which flexes and stores energy in flight. Skilled users can hurl darts at over 100 km/h. Many atlatls carry a stone "bannerstone" or atlatl weight, whose purpose — balance, silencing, or symbolism — remains debated.

Modern use

Atlatl throwing survives as a sport, with competitions organised by bodies such as the World Atlatl Association, and it is legal for hunting in some jurisdictions.
