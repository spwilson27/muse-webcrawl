Alexander von Humboldt

Friedrich Wilhelm Heinrich Alexander von Humboldt
Born: 14 September 1769, Berlin, Prussia
Died: 6 May 1859, Berlin
Fields: Natural history, geography, physical geography, biogeography
Known for: Humboldtian science; Cosmos; American expedition (1799–1804); vegetation zones and isotherms
Brother: Wilhelm von Humboldt

Alexander von Humboldt (1769–1859) was a Prussian polymath, geographer, naturalist, and explorer, regarded as a founder of modern geography and biogeography. His quantitative, interconnected view of nature — "Humboldtian science" — influenced Darwin and generations of scientists.

American expedition

With Aimé Bonpland, Humboldt travelled through Spanish America (1799–1804) — Venezuela, the Orinoco, Cuba, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Mexico — collecting tens of thousands of plant specimens, mapping the Casiquiare canal, climbing Chimborazo to a then-record altitude, and studying the cold Pacific current now bearing his name (the Humboldt Current).

Science

Humboldt pioneered isotherm maps, altitudinal vegetation zones, and the idea of nature as a web of connections, anticipating ecology and recognising human-caused environmental change around Venezuela's Lake Valencia. His multi-volume Personal Narrative and later Cosmos (1845–1862) attempted a unified physical description of the universe.

Later life and legacy

A later expedition crossed Russia to Siberia in 1829. More species and places are named after Humboldt than after almost anyone else, including the Humboldt penguin and numerous mountains, rivers, and currents. He died in Berlin in 1859, aged 89.
