A movie projector is an opto-mechanical device for displaying motion picture film by projecting it onto a screen. A projector advances the film intermittently so that each frame stops briefly in front of a bright light source and lens; a rotating shutter blocks the light while the film moves and typically flashes each frame two or three times, raising the flicker rate above the threshold of perception.

Mechanism

The key elements are the film transport (sprockets and an intermittent mechanism, often a Geneva drive or "Maltese cross"), the lamp house — historically carbon arc, later xenon arc lamps — the shutter, the projection lens, and the sound reader for the optical soundtrack printed along the film's edge. In theatrical practice, film runs at 24 frames per second, with reels changed over between two projectors or spooled onto large platters.

History

Projectors developed rapidly in the 1890s with devices such as the Lumière brothers' Cinématographe (1895), which combined camera and projector. Formats proliferated for home and amateur use — 16 mm (1923), 8 mm (1932), and Super 8 (1965) — while 35 mm remained the theatrical standard, joined by 70 mm for prestige productions. From the early 2000s, digital cinema projectors using DLP or LCoS technology and standardised by the Digital Cinema Initiatives largely replaced film projectors in commercial cinemas.
