Emancipation Proclamation

The Emancipation Proclamation, officially Proclamation 95, was a presidential proclamation and executive order issued by United States President Abraham Lincoln on 1 January 1863, during the American Civil War. It declared that all persons held as slaves within the rebellious Confederate states "are, and henceforward shall be free". A preliminary proclamation had been issued on 22 September 1862, warning that freedom would take effect in areas still in rebellion on 1 January.

Background and drafting

Lincoln, personally opposed to slavery, initially framed the war as a struggle to preserve the Union. Pressure from abolitionists, congressional confiscation acts, and the military logic of depriving the Confederacy of slave labour pushed him toward emancipation as a war measure under his authority as commander-in-chief. He read a draft to his cabinet on 22 July 1862; Secretary of State William Seward advised waiting for a military success so the measure would not seem a cry of desperation. The Union victory at Antietam on 17 September provided the occasion, and the preliminary proclamation followed five days later.

Scope and effect

The final proclamation applied to the ten states still in rebellion, naming them individually; it exempted the loyal border states (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri) and Union-occupied areas of the Confederacy, including Tennessee and specified parishes of Louisiana and counties of Virginia (which became West Virginia). Of roughly 4 million enslaved people, some 3.2 million were within its declared scope, though freedom in practice followed the advance of Union armies. The proclamation also authorised the enlistment of Black men into the Union armed forces; about 180,000 served in the United States Colored Troops and the Navy.

The proclamation transformed the war's character, made abolition an explicit Union aim, and discouraged British and French recognition of the Confederacy. Because it rested on wartime powers, Lincoln pressed Congress to secure abolition permanently, achieved by the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in December 1865, which abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime.

Legacy

The Emancipation Proclamation is one of the most famous documents in American history. Frederick Douglass called it "the first step on the part of the nation in its departure from the thralldom of the ages". The anniversary of its issuance, 1 January, is marked by Watch Night services in African American churches, and Juneteenth (19 June) commemorates the enforcement of the proclamation in Texas in 1865.
