Promised Land

The Promised Land (Hebrew: Ha'aretz hamuvtakhat) is the land which, according to the Hebrew Bible, God promised and subsequently gave to Abraham and his descendants. In modern contexts, the phrase "Promised Land" is used metaphorically for any place or condition of hoped-for happiness, freedom or fulfilment, and in Jewish and Christian tradition it remains a central theological and national concept associated with the Land of Israel.

Biblical account

In the Book of Genesis, God promises Abram (Abraham) the land of Canaan: "To your offspring I give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates" (Genesis 15:18). The promise is renewed to Isaac and Jacob, and after the Exodus from Egypt the Israelites under Moses journey toward the land, described as "a land flowing with milk and honey" (Exodus 3:8). Moses views the land from Mount Nebo but dies before entering it; under Joshua the Israelites conquer and settle Canaan.

Boundaries

Different biblical passages give different extents: Genesis 15 gives the maximal span from the "river of Egypt" (often identified with the Wadi el-Arish or the Nile) to the Euphrates, while Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 describe narrower boundaries roughly corresponding to the territory of ancient Israel and Judah between the Mediterranean Sea and the Jordan River and beyond.

Metaphorical use

The phrase entered wider usage through the King James Bible. In African-American spirituals and in the rhetoric of the civil rights movement, the Promised Land symbolized freedom and equality; Martin Luther King Jr.'s final speech ("I've Been to the Mountaintop", 1968) famously drew on Moses viewing the land he would not enter. The term is also applied to emigrant destinations and utopian hopes in many cultures.
