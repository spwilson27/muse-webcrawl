Sprechstimme

Sprechstimme (German, "speech-voice") is a vocal technique that lies between speaking and singing: the performer articulates precisely notated pitches and rhythms but does not sustain the pitch as in singing, instead gliding immediately away from it, giving the effect of heightened, melodised speech. In scores it is usually indicated by a cross through the stem of a note, or by small crosses in place of noteheads.

History

Precursors appear in Engelbert Humperdinck's Königskinder (1897) and in melodrama traditions in which actors spoke over music. The technique is most famously associated with Arnold Schoenberg, who used it in the Gurre-Lieder and, above all, in Pierrot Lunaire (1912), where a speaking voice (Sprechstimme) delivers Albert Giraud's poems in Otto Erich Hartleben's translation. Schoenberg wrote detailed prefaces explaining that the indicated pitches should be transformed into a speech melody rather than sung.

Related techniques and usage

Alban Berg used Sprechstimme extensively in Wozzeck and Lulu, devising intermediate forms between pure speech, half-singing, and full song. The related term Sprechgesang ("speech-song") is often used interchangeably, though some writers distinguish it as closer to singing. Because no performer reproduces the pitches exactly, the notation has been a perennial subject of debate and of widely differing performance practice.
