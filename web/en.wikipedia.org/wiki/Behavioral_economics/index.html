Behavioral economics

[Image: a shopper pausing between two nearly identical products on a supermarket shelf]

Behavioral economics (behavioural economics) studies how psychological, cognitive and social factors affect economic decisions, systematically testing the rational-agent assumptions of neoclassical theory against experiments and field data.

Findings and figures

Its landmark results include prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979): people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses around a reference point, are loss-averse, and misjudge probabilities; bounded rationality and heuristics-and-biases (Herbert Simon; Tversky and Kahneman); present bias and hyperbolic discounting; fairness and reciprocity in games; and default effects — exploited by 'nudge' policy (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008) in pension auto-enrolment, organ donation and tax letters. Daniel Kahneman (2002) and Richard Thaler (2017) received Nobel Memorial Prizes; Vernon Smith shared the 2002 prize for experimental method. Behavioural insights units in governments apply its findings, while critics debate replicability, paternalism and how far findings overturn, rather than refine, standard Microeconomics and Macroeconomics.
