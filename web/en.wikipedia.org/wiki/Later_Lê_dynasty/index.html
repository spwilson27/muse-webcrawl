The Later Lê dynasty (Vietnamese: triều Hậu Lê), also called the Lê dynasty, was the longest-ruling dynasty of Vietnam, reigning from 1428 to 1789 with an interruption by the Mạc dynasty (1527–1533, though the Mạc held the north until 1592). It was founded by Lê Lợi, the hero of the Lam Sơn uprising that expelled the Ming Chinese occupation forces, who ascended the throne as Emperor Lê Thái Tổ in 1428.

Golden age

Under Emperor Lê Thánh Tông (r. 1460–1497) the dynasty reached its zenith. He reorganised the administration on Confucian lines, promulgated the Hồng Đức legal code, expanded the examination system, and in 1471 conquered the kingdom of Champa's capital at Vijaya, extending Vietnamese territory far to the south. His reign is remembered as a golden age of Vietnamese culture and statecraft.

Decline and division

After Lê Thánh Tông the dynasty weakened. In 1527 the court official Mạc Đăng Dung seized the throne, founding the Mạc dynasty. Loyalists under Nguyễn Kim restored the Lê emperors in the south in 1533, and his son-in-law Trịnh Kiểm laid the foundation of the Trịnh lords' dominance. Following decades of war, the Trịnh defeated the Mạc in 1592 and restored the Lê court in Hanoi — but real power thereafter lay with the Trịnh lords in the north and the rival Nguyễn lords in the south, who fought the Trịnh–Nguyễn Civil War (1627–1672) while the Lê emperors reigned as figureheads. This arrangement gave the period its Vietnamese name, "the king Lê, the lord Trịnh" (vua Lê, chúa Trịnh).

Downfall

The Tây Sơn rebellion, beginning in 1771, swept away both the Nguyễn and the Trịnh and finally the Lê dynasty itself in 1789, when the last emperor, Lê Chiêu Thống, fled to China. The Tây Sơn victory at Ngọc Hồi–Đống Đa over the Qing expeditionary force sent to restore him sealed the dynasty's end.
