Alternative wine closure

Alternative wine closures are substitute closures used in the wine industry in place of traditional cork stoppers. The emergence of these alternatives has grown in recent years as a response to cork taint (caused mainly by the compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole, or TCA) and to concerns about consistency, cost, and oxygen management in bottled wine.

Screw caps

The most widespread alternative is the screw cap (notably the Stelvin closure), widely adopted in Australia and New Zealand from the early 2000s, where the large majority of wines are now sealed this way. Screw caps provide a consistent, near-hermetic seal; modern caps can be manufactured with liners of controlled oxygen transmission. Research indicates screw-capped wines avoid TCA taint entirely, though winemakers must manage the risk of reductive (sulphur-like) aromas in wines sealed with very low oxygen ingress.

Synthetic corks

Synthetic stoppers, made from extruded or moulded food-grade plastics (e.g. Nomacorc), mimic cork's appearance and can be removed with a corkscrew. They avoid TCA contamination, though early versions allowed excessive oxygen ingress over long storage; newer co-extruded designs perform more consistently.

Glass closures

Glass stoppers such as Vinolok/Vinoseal seal with an inert O-ring and are reusable and recyclable; they are used mainly for premium wines and spirits. Other alternatives include crown caps (common for sparkling wine ageing before disgorgement), the Zork closure, and technical/agglomerate corks (such as Diam), in which cork granules are treated to remove TCA and reconstituted with microspheres.

Debate

Traditionalists argue that natural cork's slow oxygen exchange benefits long-term ageing and that the ritual of cork removal is part of wine culture, while advocates of alternatives cite reliability, the elimination of cork taint (historically affecting a small but significant percentage of bottles), and sustainability analyses comparing cork forests with aluminium and plastic production.
