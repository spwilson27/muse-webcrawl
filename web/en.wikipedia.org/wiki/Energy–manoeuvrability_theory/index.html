Energy–manoeuvrability theory

Energy–manoeuvrability theory is a model of aircraft performance. It was developed by USAF Colonel John Boyd and mathematician Thomas P. Christie at Eglin Air Force Base in the 1960s, and describes an aircraft's performance as a function of its specific energy — the sum of its kinetic and potential energy — and its ability to change that energy state.

An energy–manoeuvrability diagram for a fighter aircraft.

Theory

The key quantity is specific excess power, Ps = (T − D)V / W, where T is thrust, D drag, V velocity and W weight. Plotting Ps contours over altitude and speed produces E-M diagrams that allow designers and pilots to compare aircraft and select manoeuvres that preserve or gain energy relative to an opponent.

Influence

The theory profoundly influenced the design of the F-16 Fighting Falcon and the requirements for the F-15 Eagle, shifting fighter design away from maximum speed toward sustained manoeuvrability and rapid transients.
