Tarsila do Amaral

Tarsila de Aguiar do Amaral (1 September 1886 – 17 January 1973) was a Brazilian painter, draftswoman and translator, regarded as one of the leading Latin American modernist artists. Her painting Abaporu (1928) inspired the Antropofagia ("Cannibalist") movement of Brazilian modernism.

Life and work

Born into a wealthy family of coffee planters in São Paulo state, Tarsila studied art in São Paulo and then in Paris, where she worked at the Académie Julian and with Fernand Léger. Back in Brazil she joined the modernist Grupo dos Cinco with Anita Malfatti, Menotti Del Picchia, Mário de Andrade and Oswald de Andrade, whom she later married.

Phases

Her work is often divided into phases: the Pau-Brasil period (from 1924), using bright colours and Brazilian themes in a simplified, cubist-influenced style, following Oswald's Pau-Brasil manifesto; the Antropofagia period (1928–1930), whose dreamlike paintings — Abaporu, a gift to Oswald, took its name from Tupi words for "the man who eats people" and prompted his 1928 Cannibalist Manifesto urging Brazilian culture to devour and transform foreign influences; and a later, socially engaged phase in the 1930s, including depictions of workers and the poor, before a return to landscape and portraiture.

In 2018 the Museum of Modern Art in New York mounted the first major North American retrospective of her work.
