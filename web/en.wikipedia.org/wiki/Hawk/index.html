Hawk

Hawks are birds of prey of the family Accipitridae, a group that also includes eagles, kites, harriers and Old World vultures. The name is applied especially to the "true hawks" of the genus Accipiter (goshawks and sparrowhawks) and, in North America, to the soaring hawks of the genus Buteo, which in Britain are called buzzards. Hawks are found on every continent except Antarctica.

Characteristics

Hawks are medium-sized diurnal raptors with sharp hooked beaks, powerful talons and exceptionally keen eyesight — among the sharpest in the animal kingdom, with visual acuity several times that of humans, adapted for spotting prey from great distances. Accipiters have short, rounded wings and long tails for manoeuvring through woodland after birds, while buteos have broad wings and short, wide tails for soaring over open country after small mammals and reptiles. Females are typically larger than males (reversed sexual dimorphism).

Behaviour and diet

Hawks hunt by surprise pounces, stoops or still-hunting from perches, feeding on mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and insects according to species. Many species are migratory, and their autumn movements are counted at famous watch sites such as Hawk Mountain in Pennsylvania and Cape May in New Jersey. Hawks are generally monogamous; both parents help rear the young.

Hawks and humans

Hawks have long been trained in the sport of falconry — goshawks and sparrowhawks were prized hunting birds. Persecution and pesticides such as DDT reduced many populations in the twentieth century, but legal protection and pesticide bans have allowed strong recoveries; the red-tailed hawk and Cooper's hawk are now common even in cities. In figurative usage, a "hawk" is a person who advocates an aggressive foreign policy, in contrast to a "dove".

Selected species

  • Eurasian sparrowhawk (Accipiter nisus)
  • Red-tailed hawk (Buteo jamaicensis)
  • Cooper's hawk (Accipiter cooperii)
  • Harris's hawk (Parabuteo unicinctus)
