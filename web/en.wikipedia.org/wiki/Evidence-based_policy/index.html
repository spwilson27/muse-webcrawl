Evidence-based policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Evidence-based policy (or evidence-informed policy) is an approach to public policy in which decisions are grounded in rigorously established objective evidence rather than in ideology, anecdote or raw political bargaining alone. Modelled on evidence-based medicine (itself from the 1990s), it gained governmental form in the UK after 1997 (“what matters is what works”; the Modernising Government white paper, 1999) and in the spread of randomised evaluations in development economics (the work recognised by the 2019 Nobel Memorial Prize), “What Works” centres, nudge units (the UK's Behavioural Insights Team, 2010) and policy labs, and statutory evidence-building (the US Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act, 2018).

Its machinery — systematic reviews, RCTs, cost–benefit analysis, administrative data — sharpens some decisions (programme design, targeting) while its critics note that the hardest questions (distribution, values, foreign policy) are not evidence-decidable, that evidence is selected politically (“policy-based evidence-making” in the sceptics' jibe), and that average effects travel poorly across contexts. The mature literature reframes the goal as evidence-informed judgement within democratic politics rather than technocratic replacement of it.
