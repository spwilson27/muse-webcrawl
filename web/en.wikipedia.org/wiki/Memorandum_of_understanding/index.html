Memorandum of understanding

Memorandum of understanding
Abbreviation: MoU
Type: Statement of intent / agreement
Used in: Business, government, international relations
Legal force: Usually non-binding, but depends on wording and intent
Representatives signing a memorandum of understanding, a formal document recording the parties' shared intentions and the framework for future cooperation.

A memorandum of understanding (MoU) is a type of agreement between two (bilateral) or more (multilateral) parties. It expresses a convergence of will between the parties, indicating an intended common line of action. It is often used either in cases where parties do not wish to enter into a legally binding contract, or in situations where the parties cannot create a legally enforceable agreement. It is a more formal alternative to a gentlemen's agreement.

Binding or non-binding?

Whether an MoU is legally binding depends not on its title but on the parties' intent as shown by its language and the circumstances: a document labelled "memorandum of understanding" that contains all the elements of a contract — offer, acceptance, consideration, and an intention to create legal relations — may be enforced as one, while a document labelled "contract" lacking those elements may not. In practice, many MoUs state expressly that they are not legally binding, while creating strong political or moral commitments. Some provisions, such as confidentiality or governing-law clauses, are sometimes made binding even in an otherwise non-binding MoU.

Uses

In business, MoUs commonly record the framework of a proposed transaction before definitive agreements are negotiated. In government and international relations, they are widely used between agencies and states to structure cooperation — for example on defence, trade facilitation, or regulatory cooperation — where a treaty would be too slow or too formal. The 2015 Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action on Iran's nuclear programme and many climate and development partnerships were framed in non-treaty instruments of this family.

Related instruments

Related documents include letters of intent, term sheets, heads of terms, and joint declarations. In international law, the distinction turns on whether the parties intend to create obligations governed by international law; if they do, the instrument is a treaty whatever it is called, as the International Court of Justice confirmed in the Qatar v. Bahrain case concerning the 1990 Doha Minutes.
