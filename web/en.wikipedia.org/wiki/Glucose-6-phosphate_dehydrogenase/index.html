Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase


Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase catalyses the first step of the pentose phosphate pathway.

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD or G6PDH; EC 1.1.1.49) is a cytosolic enzyme that catalyses the first and rate-limiting step of the pentose phosphate pathway, a metabolic pathway that supplies reducing energy to cells and provides ribose 5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis.

Reaction and function

G6PD converts glucose 6-phosphate into 6-phosphoglucono-δ-lactone, reducing NADP+ to NADPH in the process. The NADPH generated is essential for maintaining the supply of reduced glutathione, which protects cells — especially red blood cells, which lack mitochondria and rely on this pathway as their only source of NADPH — from oxidative damage by reactive oxygen species and peroxides. G6PD is a housekeeping enzyme expressed in all tissues, active as a dimer or tetramer, and tightly regulated by the NADPH/NADP+ ratio, insulin, and other signals.

Genetics

The human G6PD gene lies on the long arm of the X chromosome (Xq28) and spans about 18.5 kilobases with 13 exons. More than 200 mutations have been described, almost all of which reduce enzyme stability or activity rather than abolishing it entirely. Because the gene is X-linked, males are hemizygous and either normal or deficient, while females may be homozygous or heterozygous; heterozygous females are mosaics of normal and deficient red cells owing to X-chromosome inactivation.

Clinical significance

Inherited deficiency of the enzyme — glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency — is the most common human enzyme defect, affecting an estimated 400–500 million people worldwide, particularly in Africa, Asia and the Mediterranean. Most deficient individuals are asymptomatic except when red cells are stressed by certain drugs (such as the antimalarial primaquine), infections, or ingestion of fava beans, which can precipitate acute haemolytic anaemia. The geographical distribution of G6PD deficiency overlaps that of malaria, and the trait is thought to have been selected because deficient red cells are less hospitable to Plasmodium parasites.
