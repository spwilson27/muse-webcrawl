Plunge diving

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plunge diving is a foraging technique used by some birds — and, in a different sense, by certain other animals — in which the animal dives from the air into water at high speed to seize prey below the surface. It is most famously practised by gannets and boobies (family Sulidae), which may enter the water at speeds approaching 100 km/h (60 mph), and also by terns, kingfishers, pelicans, tropicbirds and some raptors such as the osprey.

Mechanism

A plunge-diving bird typically cruises or hovers above the water, spots fish or squid, then folds its wings and dives steeply in a streamlined posture. The momentum of the dive carries the bird several metres below the surface, after which it may swim briefly using wings or feet to pursue prey. Structural adaptations protect specialists: gannets have no external nostrils, air sacs beneath the skin cushion the impact, strong neck musculature, and binocular vision for judging distance through the air–water interface.

Groups that plunge-dive

  • Sulids — gannets and boobies, the most committed specialists.
  • Terns — generally shallower dives from hovering flight.
  • Brown pelican — dives from heights of up to 20 m, rotating to protect its throat pouch.
  • Kingfishers — dive from perches or hovering to take small fish.
  • Ospreys and sea eagles — plunge feet-first to snatch fish near the surface.

Other usages

The term is also applied to the high-speed entry of diving mammals and to human cliff diving. In cetacean biology, related lunge-feeding behaviours differ in that the animal accelerates underwater rather than falling from the air.
