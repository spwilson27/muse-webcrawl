Dutch literature

Dutch literature comprises all writings in the Dutch language, principally from the Netherlands and Dutch-speaking Flanders in Belgium, and includes works from the medieval period to the present, as well as a body of colonial and postcolonial writing from Suriname, the Caribbean and Indonesia.

Medieval and Renaissance

Early Dutch writing includes the verse romance tradition exemplified by Van den vos Reynaerde (Of Reynaert the Fox), a celebrated Middle Dutch beast epic, and the works of the mystic Hadewijch. In the 17th-century Dutch Golden Age, poetry and drama flourished: Joost van den Vondel, the national poet, wrote the tragedy Gijsbrecht van Aemstel and the epic Lucifer; P. C. Hooft and Gerbrand Bredero shaped drama and lyric poetry, while Jacob Cats became the country's most-read moralizing poet.

18th and 19th centuries

The epistolary novel Sara Burgerhart (1782) by Betje Wolff and Aagje Deken marked a high point of the Enlightenment. In the 19th century, Multatuli's Max Havelaar (1860), an indictment of Dutch colonial abuses in Java, became the most famous Dutch novel of its era; the Tachtigers (Movement of Eighty) renewed poetry and prose in the 1880s.

20th century and after

Postwar literature was dominated for decades by "the great three": Willem Frederik Hermans, Gerard Reve and Harry Mulisch, alongside Anne Frank, whose diary became one of the world's most read books. Later writers of international note include Cees Nooteboom, Hella Haasse, Arnon Grunberg and Herman Koch, while Flemish authors such as Hugo Claus contributed major Dutch-language works.
