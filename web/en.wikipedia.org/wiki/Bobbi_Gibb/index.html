Roberta Louise "Bobbi" Gibb (born November 2, 1942) is an American former runner who was the first woman to run the entire Boston Marathon, completing the race unofficially in 1966 after being denied entry because of her sex. Her run challenged prevailing beliefs that women were physiologically incapable of running 26.2 miles and helped pave the way for women's official participation in marathons.

The 1966 Boston Marathon

Gibb fell in love with running while studying in Boston and trained up to 40 miles a day, largely in secret. She applied for the 1966 Boston Marathon and received a letter from race director Will Cloney stating that women were not physiologically capable of running marathon distances and that the rules forbade it. Undeterred, she traveled from California by bus, hid in bushes near the Hopkinton start wearing her brother's shorts and a hooded sweatshirt, and joined the race when the gun fired. Once fellow runners realized a woman was among them, they were supportive; Gibb finished in 3 hours, 21 minutes, and 40 seconds — ahead of roughly two-thirds of the field — to the attention of the press and Massachusetts governor John Volpe, who congratulated her.

Later runs and recognition

Gibb ran Boston again in 1967 and 1968 unofficially. In 1967 Kathrine Switzer ran with an official number under controversial circumstances; women were not officially admitted to Boston until 1972, and a women's Olympic marathon did not exist until 1984. In 1996, the Boston Athletic Association recognized Gibb and other early women runners, awarding her medals for her 1966, 1967, and 1968 finishes and inscribing her name on the Boston Marathon memorial.

Life beyond running

Gibb became a lawyer, an artist and sculptor, and later worked in neuroscience research on neurodegenerative diseases. She wrote the memoir Wind in the Fire and remains active as an advocate for women in sport and for research into Alzheimer's disease.
