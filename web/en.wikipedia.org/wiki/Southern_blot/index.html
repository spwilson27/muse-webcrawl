AnalyteDNA

A Southern blot is a method used in molecular biology to detect a specific DNA sequence in a DNA sample. It combines transfer of electrophoretically separated DNA fragments to a membrane with detection by hybridisation to a labelled nucleic-acid probe. The technique is named after its inventor, the British biologist Edwin Southern, who published it in 1975.

Procedure

  1. Genomic DNA is cut into fragments with one or more restriction enzymes.
  2. Fragments are size-separated by agarose gel electrophoresis.
  3. The DNA is denatured in the gel and transferred ("blotted") by capillary action or vacuum onto a nitrocellulose or nylon membrane, preserving the fragment pattern.
  4. The membrane is incubated with a labelled probe — historically radioactive, now often chemiluminescent or fluorescent — complementary to the target sequence.
  5. After washing away unbound probe, the position of the target fragment is visualised, revealing its size and presence.

Applications

Southern blotting was foundational for gene mapping, detecting transgene insertions and copy number, identifying restriction fragment length polymorphisms (RFLPs) in early DNA fingerprinting, and diagnosing genetic disorders such as Huntington's disease (via repeat-expansion sizing). It has largely been supplanted by PCR-based methods and sequencing, but remains a gold standard for analysing large structural changes and repeat expansions too long for routine amplification.

Related techniques

The success of the Southern blot inspired punning names for analogues: the northern blot for RNA, the western blot for proteins, and further variants (southwestern, northwestern, far-western) for molecular interactions.
