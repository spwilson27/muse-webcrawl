Transitive verb

A transitive verb is a verb that accepts one or more objects. This contrasts with intransitive verbs, which do not have objects. Transitivity is traditionally thought of as a global property of a clause, by which activity is transferred from an agent to a patient.

Types

Monotransitive verbs take a single direct object ("She kicked the ball"). Ditransitive verbs take two objects, an indirect and a direct object ("He gave her a book"). Some languages and analyses also recognize tritransitive verbs. Many verbs are ambitransitive, usable in both transitive and intransitive frames — "The dog is eating" versus "The dog is eating his food". The valency of a verb can change through voice: passivization demotes the subject and promotes the object ("The ball was kicked").

Cross-linguistic notes

Transitivity is a central parameter in typology: case-marking and agreement patterns (nominative–accusative versus ergative–absolutive) are defined relative to the single argument of an intransitive verb versus the two core arguments of a transitive one. Hopper and Thompson proposed that transitivity is a gradient property involving features such as volitionality, affectedness of the object, and aspect.
