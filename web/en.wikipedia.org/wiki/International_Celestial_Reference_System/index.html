International Celestial Reference System

The International Celestial Reference System (ICRS) is the current standard celestial reference system adopted by the International Astronomical Union (IAU). Its origin is at the barycenter of the Solar System, and its axes are intended to be "fixed" with respect to the distant universe.

Realization

The ICRS is realized by the International Celestial Reference Frame (ICRF), a catalogue of precise positions of extragalactic radio sources — mostly quasars — measured by very-long-baseline interferometry. Because quasars are so distant, their apparent proper motions are negligible, making them ideal fixed reference points. The third realization, ICRF3, was adopted in 2019 and contains over 4,500 sources. At optical wavelengths, the Gaia space mission provides a corresponding frame.

Relation to other systems

The ICRS axes are aligned close to the mean equator and equinox of J2000.0, but are defined by the quasar positions rather than by Earth's rotation, so coordinates in the ICRS do not rotate with time. It replaced the FK5 star catalogue as the fundamental reference system on 1 January 1998.

Use

The ICRS underpins precision astrometry, spacecraft navigation, Earth-orientation measurement, and the coordinate grids used by modern star catalogues and telescopes.
