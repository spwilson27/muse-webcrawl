The Battle of Chancellorsville, fought from 30 April to 6 May 1863 in Spotsylvania County, Virginia, is widely regarded as Robert E. Lee's greatest victory — a triumph of audacity against an army more than twice his size — but it cost the Confederacy Lieutenant General Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson, mortally wounded by friendly fire.

Background

Union Major General Joseph Hooker, commanding the Army of the Potomac with some 133,000 men, devised a brilliant plan: while Sedgwick's wing demonstrated at Fredericksburg, the main body would march upriver, cross the Rappahannock and Rapidan, and turn Lee's left. The crossing (27–30 April) was flawlessly executed, but on 1 May, meeting resistance in the tangled Wilderness thickets, Hooker lost his nerve and fell back to a defensive position around the Chancellorsville crossroads.

The battle

Lee, with about 60,000 men, divided his inferior army twice in the face of the enemy. Leaving Jubal Early's division to hold Fredericksburg, Lee faced Hooker while Jackson took 28,000 men on a 12-mile flank march across the Union front on 2 May. At about 5:30 p.m. Jackson's corps burst from the woods onto the unsuspecting Union XI Corps, which was cooking supper, and rolled it up in a rout. That night, reconnoitring between the lines, Jackson was shot by his own pickets; he died of pneumonia on 10 May after his arm was amputated.

On 3 May the Confederates fought their way through to reunite, shelling Hooker's headquarters — a cannonball striking a pillar against which Hooker leaned concussed him. Lee then turned to defeat Sedgwick at Salem Church (3–4 May). Hooker withdrew across the river on the night of 5–6 May.

Casualties and significance

The Union lost about 17,300 men; the Confederacy about 13,000, including the irreplaceable Jackson. Emboldened, Lee undertook his second invasion of the North, which ended at Gettysburg two months later.
