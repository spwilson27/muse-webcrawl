Lactase persistence

TraitContinued lactase production into adulthood
GeneLCT (regulation via enhancer in MCM6)

Lactase persistence is the continued activity of the enzyme lactase in adulthood, allowing digestion of lactose, the sugar in milk. In most mammals and in about two-thirds of the world's humans, lactase production declines after weaning ("lactase non-persistence"), making large quantities of fresh milk hard to digest. Lactase persistence is a classic example of recent human evolution driven by culture — specifically, by the domestication of dairy animals.

Genetics

The trait is controlled not by the lactase gene (LCT) itself but by single-nucleotide variants in an enhancer region within the neighbouring MCM6 gene on chromosome 2, which keep LCT switched on in adults. Different mutations arose independently in different populations: the −13.910*T variant in Europe, distinct variants among East African pastoralists such as the Maasai, and others in the Middle East — a textbook case of convergent evolution.

History and selection

Ancient DNA shows the European variant was rare in early Neolithic farmers and rose sharply over the last ~5,000–10,000 years, among the strongest selection signals in the human genome — though recent research suggests it spread especially during periods of famine and disease, when the consequences of drinking milk while intolerant were most severe, rather than through a steady advantage. Frequencies today exceed 90% in Scandinavia and the British Isles and fall below 10% in much of East Asia.
