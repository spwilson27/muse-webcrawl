Ground deformation

Ground deformation is the change in shape of the Earth's surface — uplift, subsidence or horizontal movement — caused by natural processes such as volcanic activity, earthquakes, tectonics and groundwater changes, or by human activities such as mining, fluid extraction and construction loading.

Causes

Volcanoes deform as magma moves beneath them, inflating before eruptions and subsiding as magma withdraws. Earthquakes displace the ground suddenly along faults, and the crust continues to move afterwards in postseismic relaxation. Regions can subside when groundwater, oil or gas is withdrawn, as in Mexico City, Jakarta and California's Central Valley, and can rebound where ice sheets have melted, as in Fennoscandia and Hudson Bay (glacial isostatic adjustment). Loading from reservoirs, permafrost thaw and landslides also deform the surface.

Measurement

Deformation is measured with a range of geodetic techniques: continuous GPS/GNSS stations and levelling networks track movement at points; tiltmeters and extensometers record local changes at volcanoes; and satellite interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) maps deformation over wide areas with millimetre-to-centimetre precision by comparing radar images taken at different times. Monitoring ground deformation is a core method in volcano surveillance and in assessing hazards from subsiding cities and unstable slopes.
