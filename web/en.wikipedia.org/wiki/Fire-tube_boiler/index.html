Fire-tube boiler

Fire-tube boiler
TypeSteam boiler
PrincipleHot combustion gases pass through tubes surrounded by water
Typical pressureUp to about 25 bar (packaged units)
Contrast withWater-tube boiler

A fire-tube boiler is a type of boiler invented in 1828 by Marc Seguin, in which hot gases pass from a fire through one or more tubes running through a sealed container of water. The heat of the gases is transferred through the walls of the tubes by thermal conduction, heating the water and ultimately creating steam.

Operation

The fire heats air which passes through tubes inside the boiler shell; the surrounding water absorbs the heat and boils. Fire-tube boilers are classified by the number of times the hot gases pass through the boiler: a two-pass boiler sends the gases through once in each direction, while three-pass and four-pass designs route the gases back and forth additional times to extract more heat and improve efficiency.

Types

  • Lancashire boiler — a long horizontal shell with two large internal flues, widely used in mills.
  • Cornish boiler — similar but with a single flue.
  • Locomotive boiler — compact multi-tubular design used on steam locomotives, with many small fire tubes and a separate firebox.
  • Scotch marine boiler — a short, large-diameter shell with one or more furnaces, used aboard ships and in stationary service.
  • Vertical fire-tube boiler — an upright design used for small steam demands.
  • Packaged boiler — modern shop-assembled units, usually three-pass, fired by oil or gas.

Advantages and limitations

Fire-tube boilers are relatively inexpensive, compact and easy to operate, and the large water volume gives a reserve of steam that tolerates fluctuating demand. However, the large pressurised shell limits them to lower pressures and capacities than water-tube boilers, and a shell failure can release a large amount of energy explosively — the cause of many historical boiler explosions, which led to inspection regimes and the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code.
