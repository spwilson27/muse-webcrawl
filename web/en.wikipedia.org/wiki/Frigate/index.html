A frigate is a type of warship with a long and varied history. In the age of sail, frigates were fast, three-masted, square-rigged ships carrying their guns on a single deck, used for scouting, cruising and commerce raiding. In modern navies, a frigate is a surface combatant — usually smaller than a destroyer and larger than a corvette — built for escort duties, anti-submarine warfare and general patrol.

A modern frigate: the workhorse escort of most of the world's navies.

Sailing frigates

The classic frigate emerged in the mid-18th century, combining speed with enough firepower (typically 24 to 44 guns) to overpower smaller cruisers while outrunning ships of the line. Frigates served as the "eyes of the fleet", raided commerce and carried dispatches. Famous examples include the French Hermione and the American "super-frigates" such as USS Constitution ("Old Ironsides"), launched in 1797 and still in commission. Steam, shell guns and armour rendered the type obsolete by the mid-19th century.

Modern frigates

The name was revived in the Second World War for British anti-submarine escort vessels larger than corvettes. Post-war frigates evolved into multi-role ships with guided missiles, helicopters and advanced sonar: examples include the American Oliver Hazard Perry class, the Franco-Italian FREMM frigates, and the British Type 23 and forthcoming Type 26. Definitions vary by navy — the United States reclassified many of its frigates as cruisers in 1975 and retired the type before reviving it with the Constellation class in the 2020s.
