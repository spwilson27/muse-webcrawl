Digest (Roman law)

The Digest, also known as the Pandects (Latin: Digesta seu Pandectae), is a compendium of Roman law compiled by order of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I. Promulgated on 16 December 533, it forms the second and by far the largest part of the Corpus Juris Civilis, Justinian's codification of Roman law. The Digest consists of excerpts from the writings of classical Roman jurists, arranged in fifty books.

Compilation

In 530, Justinian appointed a commission headed by the quaestor Tribonian to extract everything of practical value from the works of the classical jurists. The commissioners read some 2,000 books containing over three million lines, and reduced them to about 150,000 lines — roughly one twentieth of the original. The work was completed with remarkable speed in three years. Excerpts are attributed to thirty-nine jurists, with Ulpian contributing about a third of the whole and Paul about a fifth; other major sources include Papinian, Gaius, Modestinus, Pomponius, and Julian.

Structure

The fifty books are divided into titles, each devoted to a specific legal topic, and the titles into fragments (laws), each headed by the name of the jurist and the work from which it was taken. The first book deals with general principles and the administration of law; later books cover private law, crime, and finally a book of definitions and maxims (including many of the famous regulae iuris).

Influence

Lost to the West for centuries, the Digest was rediscovered in Italy around the late eleventh century — a famous manuscript, the Littera Florentina, came to Florence. Its study at Bologna under Irnerius and the glossators revived academic legal study in Europe and profoundly shaped the civil law tradition. The Digest remains the principal source for classical Roman private law.
