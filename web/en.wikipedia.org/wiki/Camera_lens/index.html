A camera lens (also known as a photographic lens or photographic objective) is an optical lens or assembly of lenses used in conjunction with a camera body and mechanism to make images of objects either on photographic film or on other media capable of storing an image.

Design

A lens consists of several individual glass elements arranged in groups, figured and coated to control aberrations such as spherical aberration, chromatic aberration, coma, astigmatism, field curvature and distortion. An adjustable diaphragm (aperture) controls the amount of light admitted and the depth of field; a focusing mechanism moves elements to set the plane of sharp focus.

Key properties

The principal characteristics of a lens are its focal length, which determines the angle of view and magnification, and its maximum aperture, expressed as an f-number. Lenses are broadly classified as wide-angle, normal, telephoto and macro; zoom lenses vary focal length continuously, while prime lenses have a fixed focal length. Specialised designs include fisheye, tilt–shift and anamorphic lenses.

Lens mounts and history

Interchangeable lenses attach via standardised bayonet mounts (such as Canon EF, Nikon F, and the mirrorless mounts), each with its own register distance and electronic contacts. Early cameras used simple meniscus and achromatic doublet lenses; landmark designs such as the Petzval portrait lens (1840), the Cooke Triplet (1893) and the Zeiss Tessar (1902) successively improved speed and sharpness. Modern lenses add multi-coating, low-dispersion glass, aspheric elements, autofocus motors and optical image stabilisation.
