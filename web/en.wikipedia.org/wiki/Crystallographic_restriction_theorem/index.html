Crystallographic restriction theorem

The crystallographic restriction theorem states that the rotational symmetries of a crystal lattice in two or three dimensions are limited to rotation orders 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. In other words, a periodic lattice can be invariant under rotations through 360°, 180°, 120°, 90° or 60°, but not under a rotation of order 5 or of order 7 or higher.

Proof idea

The result follows from the requirement that a rotation preserving a lattice must, when expressed in a lattice basis, have an integer trace. For a rotation through angle θ, the trace is 2 cos θ (in two dimensions), so 2 cos θ must be an integer between −2 and 2, giving cos θ ∈ {−1, −1/2, 0, 1/2, 1}, which corresponds exactly to orders 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. An intuitive geometric proof considers the shortest lattice vector: rotating it repeatedly must generate other lattice vectors, and the distances between their endpoints rule out fivefold and higher rotations.

Consequences

The theorem underlies the classification of the 230 space groups and explains why ordinary crystals cannot have the fivefold symmetry of a regular pentagon or icosahedron. Quasicrystals, discovered by Dan Shechtman in 1982, display fivefold diffraction symmetry precisely because they are ordered but not periodic, and so escape the theorem's assumptions.
