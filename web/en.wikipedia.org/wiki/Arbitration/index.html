Arbitration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arbitration is a form of alternative dispute resolution (ADR) in which parties agree to submit their dispute to one or more arbitrators, who make a binding decision (an award). It is distinguished from mediation, where the third party only facilitates settlement, and from litigation in public courts: arbitration is private, usually faster and procedurally flexible, and awards are enforceable internationally under the New York Convention of 1958, ratified by the great majority of trading nations (~170 parties).

Use and procedure

Commercial parties choose arbitration for neutrality (avoiding each other's home courts), confidentiality and cross-border enforceability, under institutional rules (ICC, LCIA, SIAC, AAA/ICDR) or ad hoc arrangements modelled on UNCITRAL rules. Employment and consumer contracts in some countries (notably the US) commonly mandate arbitration and class-action waivers — a practice legislatures and courts contest. A distinct, politically sensitive species is investor–state dispute settlement (ISDS), under which investors sue host states before arbitral tribunals for expropriation or unfair treatment; high-profile awards and concerns about regulatory chill have driven reform and withdrawal debates in the EU and elsewhere.
