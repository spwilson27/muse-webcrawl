Anticholinergic

An anticholinergic agent is a substance that blocks the action of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at synapses in the central and peripheral nervous system. These agents inhibit the parasympathetic nervous system by selectively blocking the binding of acetylcholine to its receptors in nerve cells. The nerve fibres of the parasympathetic system are responsible for the involuntary movement of smooth muscles present in the gastrointestinal tract, urinary tract, lungs, and many other parts of the body.

MechanismBlockade of muscarinic and/or nicotinic acetylcholine receptors
ExamplesAtropine, scopolamine, oxybutynin, ipratropium, benztropine
EffectsDry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, tachycardia
Antidote (overdose)Physostigmine

Pharmacology

Anticholinergics are generally divided into two categories based on their receptor targets: antimuscarinic agents, which block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, and antinicotinic agents, which block nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction and autonomic ganglia. Most drugs referred to clinically as anticholinergics are antimuscarinics. Atropine, derived from plants such as deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna), is the prototype of the class.

Medical uses

  • Respiratory disorders — inhaled agents such as ipratropium and tiotropium are used as bronchodilators in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Gastrointestinal disorders — agents such as dicyclomine reduce gut spasm in irritable bowel syndrome.
  • Urinary incontinence — oxybutynin and tolterodine relax the bladder detrusor muscle in overactive bladder.
  • Motion sickness and nausea — scopolamine (hyoscine) is used in transdermal patches.
  • Parkinson's disease — benztropine and trihexyphenidyl help reduce tremor.
  • Ophthalmology — atropine and tropicamide drops dilate the pupil for examination.
  • Anaesthesia — used to reduce secretions and counteract bradycardia.

Adverse effects

The characteristic side effects of anticholinergics are summarised in the mnemonic "blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare": dilated pupils and blurred vision, dry mouth and skin, flushing, confusion and delirium, and raised body temperature. Older adults are particularly susceptible to cognitive effects, and long-term use of drugs with strong anticholinergic burden has been associated in observational studies with an increased risk of dementia. Overdose produces anticholinergic syndrome, which can be treated with the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor physostigmine.

Plant sources

Several plants of the nightshade family produce tropane alkaloid anticholinergics, notably deadly nightshade, henbane, mandrake, and Datura species. These have long histories in medicine, ritual, and poisoning.
