Bayes' theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram showing a prior probability distribution being updated by new evidence into a posterior distribution]

Bayes' theorem (also Bayes' law or Bayes' rule) describes the probability of an event in terms of prior knowledge of conditions related to the event. It is named after the statistician and minister Thomas Bayes, whose work on the problem was published posthumously in 1763, and it is a foundation of Bayesian probability and statistics.

Statement

In its common form, P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B): the probability of hypothesis A given evidence B equals the likelihood of the evidence given the hypothesis, multiplied by the prior probability of the hypothesis, divided by the overall probability of the evidence. The prior P(A), the likelihood P(B|A) and the evidence P(B) are the theorem's main ingredients.

Applications

Bayes' theorem is used in medical diagnosis (updating the chance of a disease given a test result), spam filtering, machine learning classifiers, forensic analysis and scientific inference more generally. A common caution is the base rate fallacy: ignoring the prior frequency of a condition can make even an accurate test misleading.
