A wireless LAN (WLAN) is a wireless computer network that links two or more devices within a limited area such as a home, school, computer laboratory, campus, or office building. Devices connect through wireless access points using radio waves, most commonly following the IEEE 802.11 family of standards marketed as Wi-Fi.

History

In 1971, Norman Abramson connected seven computers across Hawaii using packet radio in the ALOHAnet system, an early forerunner of wireless networking. The FCC's 1985 decision to open several frequency bands for unlicensed use spurred commercial development. The IEEE 802.11 standard was first released in 1997, providing data rates up to 2 Mbit/s; the 802.11b amendment (1999) reached 11 Mbit/s and was popularized by Apple's AirPort and other consumer products. Successive amendments — 802.11a/g, 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4), 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5), 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6/6E), and 802.11be (Wi-Fi 7) — raised throughput into the multi-gigabit range.

Architecture

Most WLANs operate in infrastructure mode, in which stations communicate through one or more access points wired to a distribution system, typically Ethernet. A set of access points sharing a service set identifier (SSID) can cover a large area and support roaming. In ad hoc (independent) mode, stations communicate directly without an access point.

Security

Because radio signals extend beyond physical premises, WLAN security relies on authentication and encryption. The original Wired Equivalent Privacy (WEP) protocol proved cryptographically weak and was deprecated in 2004. Its successors — WPA, WPA2, and WPA3 — progressively strengthened key management and encryption, with WPA3 adding individualized data encryption and protection against offline dictionary attacks.
