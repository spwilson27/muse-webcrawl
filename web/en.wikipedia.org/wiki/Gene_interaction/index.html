Gene interaction

Gene interaction refers to the combined effects of two or more genes on a single phenotypic trait — that is, situations in which the effect of one gene depends on the presence or state of another gene. The classic form of gene interaction is epistasis, in which the expression of one gene masks or modifies the effect of another.

Types

  • Complementary gene action — two genes must both be functional to produce the wild-type phenotype (e.g., flower colour in sweet pea, giving a 9:7 ratio in the F2 generation).
  • Duplicate gene action — either of two genes alone suffices for the phenotype (15:1 ratio).
  • Recessive epistasis — a homozygous recessive genotype at one locus masks another locus (9:3:4 ratio, e.g., coat colour in Labrador retrievers).
  • Dominant epistasis — a dominant allele at one locus masks another locus (12:3:1 ratio).
  • Suppressor interactions — a mutation at one locus reverses the effect of a mutation at another.

Relation to Mendel's laws

Gene interactions explain the modified dihybrid ratios observed when two genes affect the same trait, departing from the 9:3:3:1 ratio expected for independently acting genes. William Bateson and Reginald Punnett documented such cases in the early 1900s; Bateson coined the word "epistasis".

Modern usage

In quantitative and systems genetics, genetic interaction is studied on large scales by combining mutations (for example double-knockout screens in yeast) and measuring whether the combined fitness differs from expectation — negative (aggravating) interactions include synthetic lethality, while positive (alleviating) interactions indicate genes acting in the same pathway or complex.
