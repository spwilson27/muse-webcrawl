Selectable marker

Placeholder image: only transformed cells survive on selective medium.

A selectable marker is a gene introduced into a cell, typically on a plasmid, that confers a trait allowing the cell to survive under conditions that kill or inhibit cells lacking the gene. Selectable markers are fundamental tools of molecular biology: they allow researchers to identify the small fraction of cells that have successfully taken up foreign DNA.

Common markers

In bacteria, antibiotic resistance genes (such as bla, conferring resistance to ampicillin, or genes for kanamycin resistance) are standard; cells are grown on medium containing the antibiotic so only transformants grow. In yeast and other eukaryotes, auxotrophic markers such as URA3 or LEU2 complement a metabolic deficiency, allowing selection on minimal medium. In mammalian cell culture, genes conferring resistance to drugs such as neomycin (G418), hygromycin, or puromycin are widely used, sometimes combined with negative (counter-) selection markers.

Use in genetic engineering

Because stable transformation is rare, selectable markers are nearly always included in constructs for making transgenic organisms and knockout cell lines. Concerns about antibiotic resistance markers in commercial genetically modified crops have led to research into marker-free transformation and marker excision systems, such as Cre-lox recombination.
