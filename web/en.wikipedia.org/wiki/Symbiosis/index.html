Symbiosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Symbiosis is any type of close and long-term biological interaction between two organisms of different species. In the broad (de Bary, 1879) sense it includes mutualism (both benefit), commensalism (one benefits, the other is unaffected) and parasitism (one benefits at the other's expense). Some modern usage restricts symbiosis to mutualism alone.

Mutualisms are structural to life: the mitochondria and chloroplasts in eukaryotic cells began as symbiotic Bacteria; most land plants partner with mycorrhizal fungi for nutrients; corals depend on internal algae; ruminants, termites and humans all rely on gut microbiomes for digestion. Lichens — fungus plus alga or cyanobacterium — colonise bare rock and tundra.

Costs and conflicts

Symbioses sit on a continuum: partners cheat, hosts impose sanctions, and mutualisms can slide towards parasitism as conditions change. Cleaner fish that nip mucus, and fig wasps that fail to pollinate, show the policing problems every partnership must solve.

Parasitism

Parasites — from malaria's Plasmodium to tapeworms and brood-parasitic cuckoos — are by some estimates the commonest lifestyle on Earth, shaping host evolution in perpetual arms races (the Red Queen dynamic).
