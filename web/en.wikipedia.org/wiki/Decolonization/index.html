Decolonization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Decolonization is the undoing of colonialism — the process by which colonies become independent of the colonising country. Through the twentieth century it dismantled the European overseas empires (and, in different senses, the Japanese and Ottoman ones), creating the majority of today's states: from the independence of India and Pakistan (1947) and Indonesia and the Philippines in the 1940s, through the "Year of Africa" (1960), to the Portuguese colonies (1975), Zimbabwe (1980) and Namibia (1990), with Hong Kong (1997) and Macau (1999) closing the classic era by transfer rather than full independence.

Drivers and forms

Causes combined colonial nationalism and armed struggle, metropolitan exhaustion after the Second World War, superpower and UN pressure (the 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples), and changing norms that made empire illegitimate. Transfers of power ranged from negotiated constitutional transitions to protracted wars (Algeria, Vietnam, Angola, Mozambique), and partitions and communal violence scarred several (India/Pakistan, Palestine). The UN's decolonisation machinery (the Special Committee, C-24) still lists Non-Self-Governing Territories, and "decolonisation" has been extended metaphorically to culture, knowledge and institutions — usages contested in contemporary debates.

Legacy

Postcolonial states inherited colonial borders (locked in by the OAU's 1964 uti possidetis decision), economies and institutions; the politics of development, the Non-Aligned Movement and North–South diplomacy all descend from decolonisation's settlement, as do continuing disputes over restitution of cultural property and reparations.
