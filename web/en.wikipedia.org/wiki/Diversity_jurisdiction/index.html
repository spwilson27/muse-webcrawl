Diversity jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diversity jurisdiction
Legal basisU.S. Const. art. III; 28 U.S.C. § 1332
Amount in controversyMore than $75,000 (since 1997)
Key doctrineComplete diversity (Strawbridge v. Curtiss, 1806)

In the United States, diversity jurisdiction is the authority of the federal district courts to hear civil cases in which the parties are citizens of different states (or a citizen of a state and a foreign citizen) and the amount in controversy exceeds a statutory threshold — currently more than US$75,000. It is one of the two principal heads of federal subject-matter jurisdiction, the other being federal question jurisdiction. The constitutional basis is Article III, Section 2, which extends the judicial power to controversies "between Citizens of different States."

Rationale

The traditional justification, voiced by Alexander Hamilton in The Federalist No. 80, is protection against local bias: an out-of-state litigant might face prejudice in the home courts of an opponent, so a neutral federal forum is provided. Critics have long questioned whether such bias persists, and proposals to abolish or curtail diversity jurisdiction recur in debates over federal court caseloads, but Congress has retained it.

Requirements

Under the complete diversity rule announced in Strawbridge v. Curtiss (1806), no plaintiff may be a citizen of the same state as any defendant. Citizenship for individuals means domicile; a corporation is a citizen both of its state of incorporation and of the state of its principal place of business (its "nerve centre," per Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 2010). Unincorporated associations such as partnerships and LLCs take the citizenship of every member, which can complicate or defeat diversity. The amount in controversy must exceed $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs, measured by the plaintiff's good-faith claim at filing.

Removal and the Erie doctrine

A case filed in state court that could have been brought in federal court may generally be removed by the defendant, though not if any defendant is a citizen of the forum state. Because diversity cases usually arise under state law, the federal court applies state substantive law under the Erie doctrine (Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 1938), while federal procedural rules govern the conduct of the litigation. Supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367 allows related claims or parties to be heard together, subject to statutory exceptions designed to preserve the complete-diversity rule. The Class Action Fairness Act of 2005 created a separate, "minimal diversity" basis for large class actions exceeding $5 million.
