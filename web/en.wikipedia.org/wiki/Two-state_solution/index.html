Two-state solution

The two-state solution is a proposed framework for resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict by establishing two states for two peoples: Israel for the Jewish people and Palestine for the Palestinian people, side by side in peace and security.

Proposed statesIsrael and Palestine
Key basis1967 borders, with land swaps

History

The idea dates to the 1937 Peel Commission and the 1947 UN Partition Plan (Resolution 181), which proposed Jewish and Arab states; Israel was established in 1948 while the Arab state was not. After the 1967 Six-Day War, UN Security Council Resolution 242's "land for peace" formula underpinned diplomacy. The 1993–95 Oslo Accords created the Palestinian Authority as an interim step, and the 2000 Camp David Summit, 2001 Taba talks, 2002 Arab Peace Initiative, and the Annapolis and Kerry negotiations all centred on two states.

Core issues

Negotiations address borders (generally the 1967 lines with mutually agreed swaps), the status of Jerusalem as capital of both states, security arrangements, Israeli settlements, and Palestinian refugees' claims. Alternatives debated include a one-state solution and confederation models.

Support and obstacles

The two-state framework has long been endorsed by the UN, the US, the EU and the Arab League, though public support on both sides has fluctuated and settlement expansion, violence, and political divisions are cited as obstacles.
