Aspen

Aspens
Kingdom: Plantae
Family: Salicaceae
Genus: Populus
Section: Populus sect. Populus

Aspen is a common name for certain tree species of the genus Populus, in the willow family Salicaceae. The aspens comprise section Populus of the genus, which includes the Eurasian aspen (Populus tremula), the North American quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides), the bigtooth aspen (Populus grandidentata), the Chinese aspen (Populus adenopoda), and the Japanese aspen (Populus sieboldii), among others.

Characteristics

Aspens are medium-sized deciduous trees, typically 15–30 metres tall, with smooth pale bark that is white, greenish or grey, marked by black horizontal scars and prominent black knots. Their leaves are nearly round with flattened petioles (leaf stalks), which cause the leaves to tremble or "quake" in even light breezes — a trait reflected in names such as quaking aspen and trembling aspen and in the Latin epithet tremula. In autumn the foliage turns brilliant gold, yellow or, more rarely, orange and red.

Reproduction and ecology

Aspens reproduce both by seed and, especially, by root suckers, producing large clonal colonies in which all the stems are genetically identical and connected by a shared root system. The most famous example is Pando, a quaking aspen colony in Utah consisting of tens of thousands of stems, considered one of the largest and oldest living organisms on Earth. Aspens are pioneer species that colonise disturbed sites after fire, and their bark, leaves and twigs are important food for beavers, deer, elk, grouse and many other animals.

Uses

Aspen wood is soft, light and pale, and is used for pulp and paper, matches, excelsior, oriented strand board, and sauna benches, since it does not splinter easily or overheat. Aspens are also widely planted as ornamental and shelterbelt trees, valued for their autumn colour and rapid growth.
