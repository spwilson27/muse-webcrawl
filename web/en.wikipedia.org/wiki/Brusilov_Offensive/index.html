The Brusilov Offensive (4 June – 20 September 1916) was the largest and most successful Russian operation of the First World War, launched by General Aleksei Brusilov's Southwestern Front against Austria-Hungary in Galicia and Volhynia. It inflicted losses from which the Austro-Hungarian army never fully recovered, but at a cost that helped exhaust the Russian Empire itself.

Methods

Brusilov rejected the era's reliance on massed artillery and narrow breakthrough sectors. After careful aerial reconnaissance and the construction of full-scale replica trenches for rehearsal, his four armies attacked simultaneously along a broad front, with short, intense bombardments, saps dug to within metres of the enemy wire, and reserves positioned close behind the assault waves — tactics anticipating later infiltration methods.

Course

The offensive shattered the Austro-Hungarian Fourth and Seventh Armies, taking Lutsk and Czernowitz and hundreds of thousands of prisoners in the first weeks. German reinforcements were rushed east, pressure on France at Verdun and on Italy in the Trentino was relieved, and Romania was emboldened to join the Allies in August — with disastrous results for Romania. Russian advances stalled by September amid supply failures, the failure of neighbouring fronts to attack in support, and mounting losses.

Consequences

Austro-Hungarian casualties are estimated at well over half a million, including some 400,000 prisoners; Russian losses were comparable. The offensive destroyed Austria-Hungary's capacity for independent operations, deepened its dependence on Germany, and — by consuming Russia's last reserves of trained manpower — contributed to the collapse of the Tsarist regime in 1917.
