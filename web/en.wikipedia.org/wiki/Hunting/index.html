Hunting is the human practice of seeking, pursuing, capturing, and killing wildlife or feral animals. The most common reasons are to harvest food and useful animal products (fur, hide, bone), for recreation and trophy, to remove dangerous predators, and to manage wildlife populations. Humans have hunted since prehistory; hunting was a critical component of the subsistence of early human societies, and the endurance-running or "persistence hunting" hypothesis links it to human evolution. In modern societies, hunting is most commonly a regulated recreational activity.

History

Stone-tipped spears appear in the archaeological record over 300,000 years ago, and hunting large game with coordinated groups plausibly contributed to the development of language, toolmaking, and social cooperation. With the Neolithic transition, domestication replaced hunting as the main source of meat in farming societies, but hunting persisted as subsistence for many peoples and as the privileged sport of elites—from medieval European deer parks and fox hunting to falconry in the Islamic world and Asia.

Modern hunting

Conservation laws, pioneered in North America in the early 20th century (the "North American Model of Wildlife Conservation"), fund habitat protection through hunting licence fees and excise taxes such as the Pittman–Robertson Act (1937). Regulated hunting controls overabundant populations (e.g., deer) but remains controversial: debates surround trophy hunting of big game, hunting with hounds (banned in England and Wales by the Hunting Act 2004), lead ammunition's poisoning of scavengers, and poaching, which threatens species such as elephants and rhinoceroses. Subsistence hunting retains legal protection for many Indigenous peoples.

Types

  • Big-game hunting (deer, elk, moose, bear, African plains game)
  • Waterfowl and upland bird hunting
  • Small-game hunting and varmint hunting
  • Trapping and hunting with dogs (including fox hunting)
  • Falconry and bowhunting
