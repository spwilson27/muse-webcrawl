Cytoarchitecture

Cytoarchitecture (Greek κύτος "cell" + ἀρχιτεκτονική "architecture") is the study of the cellular composition of the body's tissues under the microscope. The term is most often applied to the arrangement of neurons in the central nervous system, especially the layered organisation of the cerebral cortex, where differences in cell type, size, density and lamination define anatomically and functionally distinct areas.

Cerebral cortex

The neocortex typically has six layers, numbered I to VI from the surface inwards, whose relative development varies between regions: the primary visual cortex has a prominent layer IV (receiving thalamic input), while motor cortex has a thick layer V containing the giant Betz cells and lacks a distinct layer IV. Korbinian Brodmann used Nissl-stained cytoarchitectonic differences to define his famous map of 52 Brodmann areas (1909), many of which correspond to functional regions. Other classical maps include those of von Economo and Koskinas.

Methods

Cytoarchitectonic study traditionally relies on cell-body stains such as Nissl (cresyl violet) staining, complemented by myeloarchitecture (myelin stains), immunohistochemistry for cell-type-specific markers, and modern observer-independent statistical mapping of cell density profiles. Cytoarchitectonic abnormalities, such as cortical dyslamination, are investigated in developmental disorders and psychiatric disease.
