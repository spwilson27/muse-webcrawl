Viscous friction

Viscous friction arises from shear between adjacent layers of fluid

Viscous friction is the resistance experienced by a body moving through a fluid, or between layers of fluid moving at different velocities, arising from the fluid's viscosity. In its simplest (linear) form the frictional force is proportional to velocity, F = −c v, in contrast to dry (Coulomb) friction, which is approximately independent of speed, and to quadratic drag, which dominates at high Reynolds numbers.

Physical origin

In a Newtonian fluid the shear stress between layers is proportional to the velocity gradient: τ = μ(du/dy), where μ is the dynamic viscosity. When a solid body moves slowly through such a fluid — the regime of creeping or Stokes flow — the total drag is linear in velocity. For a sphere of radius r this gives Stokes' law, F = 6πμr v. Viscous friction also acts within machinery wherever lubricating films separate moving parts, and it is the mechanism that dissipates energy in dashpots and viscous dampers.

Applications

Viscous friction governs the terminal velocity of small particles settling in liquids, the behaviour of oil films in journal bearings, the damping of galvanometer and seismometer suspensions, and the fall of raindrops at small sizes. In control theory and mechanical modelling it appears as the linear damping term in the equation of motion, producing exponential decay of oscillations when it is the dominant loss mechanism. At higher speeds the drag transitions to the quadratic regime described by the drag equation, so viscous (linear) friction is characteristic of low-Reynolds-number motion, microfluidics and highly viscous media such as honey or pitch.
