Medical research

Medical research, also called biomedical research or health research, is research conducted to aid and support the development of knowledge in the field of medicine, including the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of disease.

Types of research

  • Basic research: fundamental work on biological processes, such as cell biology and genetics, that underpins later medical advances.
  • Preclinical research: testing of candidate drugs and devices in vitro and in animal models before human use.
  • Clinical research: studies involving patients and healthy volunteers, most notably clinical trials, which evaluate the safety and efficacy of interventions in phased trials.
  • Translational research: the "bench to bedside" process of turning laboratory findings into clinical applications.
  • Epidemiological research: the study of the distribution and determinants of disease in populations.
  • Health services research: study of how healthcare is delivered, financed, and accessed.

Funding and institutions

Medical research is funded by governments, industry, and charities. In the United States, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) is the largest public funder, supporting tens of thousands of grants. Major charitable funders include the Wellcome Trust and the Gates Foundation. Pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies fund a large share of clinical trials aimed at drug approval.

Ethics and oversight

Research involving human subjects is governed by principles set out in documents such as the Nuremberg Code and the Declaration of Helsinki, requiring informed consent and independent review by an institutional review board or research ethics committee. Historical abuses, including the Tuskegee syphilis study, shaped modern oversight. Animal research is likewise regulated and remains publicly debated.

Publication

Results are disseminated through peer-reviewed journals such as The Lancet, The New England Journal of Medicine, and JAMA, with increasing use of preprint servers. Registration of clinical trials before they begin is now standard practice, intended to reduce publication bias.
