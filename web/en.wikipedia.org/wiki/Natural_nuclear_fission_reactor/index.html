Natural nuclear fission reactor

A natural nuclear fission reactor is a uranium deposit where self-sustaining nuclear chain reactions have occurred naturally. The only known examples are the fossil reactors at Oklo in Gabon, West Africa, where about sixteen zones of uranium ore underwent fission roughly 1.7 billion years ago.

Discovery

The phenomenon was discovered in 1972 when French scientists analysing uranium ore from the Oklo mine found that the proportion of the fissile isotope uranium-235 was significantly lower than the standard 0.72% found everywhere else on Earth. The deficit, together with characteristic fission-product isotope patterns, showed that some of the uranium-235 had been consumed by fission in the distant past. Physicist Paul Kuroda had predicted in 1956 that natural conditions could once have allowed such reactions.

How they worked

Two billion years ago, radioactive decay had not yet reduced the natural abundance of uranium-235 as far as today: it then made up about 3% of natural uranium, comparable to the fuel of a modern light-water reactor. At Oklo, rich uranium ore bodies saturated with groundwater achieved criticality, with water acting as a neutron moderator. The reactors appear to have operated cyclically — heating until the water boiled away, shutting down, cooling and refilling — for several hundred thousand years, at an average power of about 100 kilowatts.

Scientific significance

The Oklo reactors provide a natural laboratory for studying the long-term migration of fission products in rock, informing the design of geological repositories for nuclear waste. Measurements of isotope ratios at Oklo have also been used to test whether the fine-structure constant has changed over geological time, with results consistent with it remaining constant.
