Supermassive black hole

A supermassive black hole (SMBH or sometimes SBH) is the largest type of black hole, with its mass being on the order of hundreds of thousands, to billions of times the mass of the Sun (M☉). Black holes are a class of astronomical objects that have undergone gravitational collapse, leaving behind spheroidal regions of space from which nothing can escape, not even light.

The black hole at the centre of M87, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope

Properties

Observational evidence indicates that almost every large galaxy has a supermassive black hole at its centre. The Milky Way hosts Sagittarius A*, with about 4.3 million solar masses, while the black hole in Messier 87 is roughly 6.5 billion solar masses. Because a supermassive black hole's event horizon is so large, its average density can be surprisingly low — comparable to or less than that of water for the most massive examples. Accreting supermassive black holes power active galactic nuclei and quasars, among the most luminous objects in the universe.

Formation

How supermassive black holes form remains an open question. Proposed channels include the growth of stellar-mass "seed" black holes by accretion and mergers, the direct collapse of giant gas clouds in the early universe, and runaway collisions in dense star clusters. Their presence in quasars less than a billion years after the Big Bang requires either massive seeds or episodes of super-Eddington accretion.

Observation

Masses are measured from the orbits of stars and gas near the galactic centre, reverberation mapping of broad-line regions, and maser disks. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first direct image of a black hole's shadow (M87*), followed in 2022 by an image of Sagittarius A*. The correlation between black hole mass and the properties of the host galaxy bulge (the M–sigma relation) suggests that black holes and galaxies co-evolve.
