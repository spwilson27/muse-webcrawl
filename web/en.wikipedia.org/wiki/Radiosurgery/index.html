Radiosurgery is surgery using radiation, that is, the destruction of precisely selected areas of tissue using ionizing radiation rather than excision with a blade. Like other forms of radiation therapy, it is usually used to treat cancer. Radiosurgery was originally defined by the Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell as "a single high dose fraction of radiation, stereotactically directed to an intracranial region of interest".

In stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), the word "stereotactic" refers to a three-dimensional coordinate system that enables accurate correlation of a virtual target seen in diagnostic images with the actual target position in the patient. The treatment delivers many individually weak beams of radiation that converge on the target, so the target receives a high dose while surrounding tissue receives a much lower one.

History

Leksell developed the concept in the late 1940s and 1950s at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm, building a stereotactic frame to localize intracranial targets. The first Gamma Knife, using cobalt-60 sources arranged in a hemispherical array, treated patients from the late 1960s. Later, linear accelerator (LINAC)-based systems and the robotic CyberKnife broadened the technique to tumours throughout the body, where it is often called stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) when fractionated.

Indications

Radiosurgery is used for brain metastases, meningiomas, acoustic neuromas (vestibular schwannomas), pituitary adenomas, arteriovenous malformations, and trigeminal neuralgia. It is particularly valuable for lesions that are deep-seated or inoperable, and for patients unfit for open surgery.
