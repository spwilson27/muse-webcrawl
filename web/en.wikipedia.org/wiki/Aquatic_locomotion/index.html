Aquatic locomotion or swimming is biologically propelled motion through a liquid medium. It is the primary means of movement for most fish, marine mammals, many invertebrates and aquatic birds, and is studied as a problem in biomechanics and fluid dynamics.

Modes of swimming

Swimmers propel themselves in several ways. Most fish swim by undulating the body and caudal fin (BCF locomotion) or by oscillating paired fins (median and paired fin, MPF locomotion). Squid, octopus and jellyfish use jet propulsion, expelling water from a cavity. Water striders and some beetles row with specialised legs, while penguins and sea turtles "fly" underwater, generating lift with flippers.

Physics

The Reynolds number determines whether viscous or inertial forces dominate: microscopic organisms live at low Reynolds numbers where drag is severe and reciprocal strokes fail, whereas large swimmers exploit inertia and vortex shedding. Efficient cruisers such as tuna minimise drag with fusiform bodies, stiff crescent tails and a narrow caudal peduncle.

Evolution

Swimming evolved independently in many lineages; secondarily aquatic tetrapods such as whales, seals and ichthyosaurs converged on streamlined forms. Aquatic locomotion research informs the design of underwater robots and autonomous vehicles.
