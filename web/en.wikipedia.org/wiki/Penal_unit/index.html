Penal unit

Penal formations were often assigned the most dangerous duties.

A penal unit is a military formation composed of personnel assigned as a punishment or under coercion—such as convicted soldiers, political prisoners, or civilian convicts—and typically employed on especially dangerous or undesirable duties, sometimes under threat of further punishment for retreat or refusal. Penal units have been used by various armies, most notoriously in the Second World War.

History

The Soviet Union made extensive use of penal battalions (shtrafbaty) and penal companies after Order No. 227 of July 1942 ("Not a step back"). Officers and soldiers accused of cowardice, desertion, or disciplinary offences could be sent to such units to "atone with blood", and barrier troops were sometimes stationed behind them. Hundreds of thousands of men passed through Soviet penal formations, which were used for assaults, mine clearance, and reconnaissance by fire with correspondingly high casualties.

Nazi Germany maintained probationary and penal formations including the 999th Light Afrika Brigade, the Dirlewanger Brigade (recruited in part from convicted poachers and later criminals and prisoners, and responsible for extensive atrocities), and SS and Wehrmacht probationary units. Other examples include penal labour and combat units in various earlier and later conflicts, and the recruitment of prisoners by the Russian Wagner Group during the Russo-Ukrainian War from 2022.

Characteristics

Common features include assignment of the riskiest missions, harsher discipline, reduced equipment or support, and the promise—honoured inconsistently—that surviving service would restore rank, rights, or freedom. The practice is frequently criticized as a form of forced labour and a violation of prisoners' rights, and the wartime conduct of some penal units, particularly Dirlewanger's, made them subjects of war-crimes prosecutions.
