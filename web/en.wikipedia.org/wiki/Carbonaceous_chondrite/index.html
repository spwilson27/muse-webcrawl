Carbonaceous chondrite

Carbonaceous chondrites are a class of chondritic meteorites distinguished by their high content of carbon compounds, water-bearing minerals and other volatiles. They are among the most primitive materials in the Solar System — chemically close to the composition of the Sun — and are prized by scientists because they preserve organic matter and presolar grains from before the planets formed.

Composition

Like all chondrites, carbonaceous chondrites consist of chondrules — millimetre-sized silicate spheres that formed as molten droplets in the early solar nebula — set in a fine matrix, together with calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs), the oldest dated solids in the Solar System at about 4.567 billion years. Unlike ordinary chondrites, they were never strongly heated or melted, so they retain volatile elements in near-solar proportions. CI chondrites match the Sun's photosphere in composition (excluding gases) more closely than any other material.

Organic matter

The Murchison meteorite (Australia, 1969) yielded more than 70 kinds of amino acids, along with nucleobases, sugars, carboxylic acids and insoluble macromolecular organic matter. These compounds formed abiotically in space or on the meteorite's parent asteroid, demonstrating that the building blocks of life can arise without biology — a key line of evidence in origin-of-life research. Many carbonaceous chondrites also show evidence of liquid water on their parent bodies, which altered their minerals and may have driven this organic chemistry.

Classification

Groups are named after a type specimen: CI (Ivuna), CM (Mighei), CO (Ornans), CV (Vigarano), CK (Karoonda), CR (Renazzo), CB (Bencubbin) and CH (high-metal). They differ in chondrule size, metal content and degree of aqueous alteration and thermal processing.

Sample return

Because carbonaceous asteroids are the parent bodies of these meteorites, they have been targeted by sample-return missions: JAXA's Hayabusa2 returned material from asteroid Ryugu (2020) and NASA's OSIRIS-REx returned material from Bennu (2023), both confirming close kinship with CI/CM chondrites.
