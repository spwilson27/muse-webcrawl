Film studies is an academic discipline that deals with various theoretical, historical, and critical approaches to cinema as an art form and a medium. Sometimes subsumed within the broader field of media studies, it analyses film's narrative, aesthetic, cultural, economic, and political dimensions rather than teaching the practical craft of filmmaking.

History

Serious writing about cinema began with early theorists such as Ricciotto Canudo, who in 1911 proclaimed film the "seventh art," and Soviet montage theorists including Lev Kuleshov and Sergei Eisenstein. Hugo Münsterberg's The Photoplay (1916) and Béla Balázs's works pioneered psychological and aesthetic analysis. Film studies entered universities in earnest after the Second World War, expanding rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s; the first dedicated departments and doctoral programmes appeared alongside the rise of film societies and auteurist criticism.

Major approaches

  • Formalism and mise-en-scène: analysis of how films are put together — editing, cinematography, composition, sound.
  • Auteur theory: originating with Cahiers du cinéma critics such as François Truffaut, the view of the director as the film's principal creative author.
  • Genre studies: how categories like the western, film noir, or the musical organise production and audience expectation.
  • Psychoanalytic and feminist theory: Laura Mulvey's 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" introduced the concept of the "male gaze."
  • Spectatorship and reception studies: how audiences actually interpret films, influenced by cultural studies.
  • Historical and industrial approaches: including David Bordwell, Janet Staiger, and Kristin Thompson's The Classical Hollywood Cinema (1985) and the "new film history."

Film theory

Film theory asks what distinguishes cinema from other arts: André Bazin championed photographic realism and the long take; montage theorists saw meaning in the collision of shots; semioticians such as Christian Metz treated film as a language-like system; cognitivists later challenged psychoanalytic orthodoxy with models based on perception and inference. The digital turn has renewed debates about indexicality, medium specificity, and the boundary between cinema and other moving-image forms.

Criticism and scholarship

Film studies is served by journals such as Screen, Film Quarterly, Cinema Journal (now JCMS), and Cahiers du cinéma, and by scholarly societies including the Society for Cinema and Media Studies. Its methods increasingly intersect with television studies, game studies, and platform studies.
