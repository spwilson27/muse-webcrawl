Andrew Jackson Downing (31 October 1815 – 28 July 1852) was an American landscape designer, horticulturist and writer, a prominent advocate of the Gothic Revival and Italianate styles in American architecture and the founder of American landscape architecture. He edited The Horticulturist magazine and championed public parks, an advocacy that helped inspire New York's Central Park.

Life and work

Downing was born in Newburgh, New York, where his father ran a nursery. His first book, A Treatise on the Theory and Practice of Landscape Gardening, Adapted to North America (1841), was the first of its kind published in the United States and a lasting success. It was followed by Cottage Residences (1842), The Fruits and Fruit Trees of America (1845) and The Architecture of Country Houses (1850), which together shaped middle-class domestic taste across the young republic.

Downing promoted picturesque, naturalistic planting over formal geometry, and modest homes embellished with verandas, gables and climbing plants, arguing that a beautiful home environment elevated the character of its inhabitants. In 1850 he brought the young English architect Calvert Vaux to America as his partner; Vaux, with Frederick Law Olmsted, later designed Central Park.

Public grounds and death

In 1851 President Millard Fillmore commissioned Downing to plan the public grounds of Washington, D.C. — the Mall, the President's Park and the Smithsonian grounds. Before the work was complete, Downing died aged 36 in the burning of the steamboat Henry Clay on the Hudson River on 28 July 1852.

Legacy

Downing Park in Newburgh and a memorial urn placed by the Smithsonian commemorate him. His ideas on rural cemeteries, suburban homes and urban parks decisively shaped nineteenth-century American landscapes.
