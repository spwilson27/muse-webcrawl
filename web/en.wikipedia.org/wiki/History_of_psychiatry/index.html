History of psychiatry

The history of psychiatry is the historical development of the medical specialty devoted to the diagnosis, prevention, study, and treatment of mental disorders. The earliest accounts of mental illness appear in ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian texts, where disturbed behaviour was often attributed to supernatural causes such as the displeasure of gods or possession by evil spirits.

Ancient and medieval periods

Greek physicians such as Hippocrates proposed naturalistic explanations, associating mental disorders with imbalances of the four humours. The Roman physician Galen elaborated humoral theory, which dominated Western medicine for over a millennium. In the medieval Islamic world, hospitals known as bimaristans in cities such as Baghdad and Damascus provided care for the mentally ill, and scholars including Avicenna described conditions resembling melancholia and mania.

Asylums and moral treatment

In early modern Europe, people with mental illness were increasingly confined in asylums, madhouses, and poorhouses, often in harsh conditions. The late eighteenth century saw reform movements: Philippe Pinel in Paris famously removed chains from patients at the Bicêtre and Salpêtrière hospitals, and William Tuke founded the York Retreat in England, advocating "moral treatment" based on kindness, routine, and humane surroundings.

Nineteenth century

The nineteenth century saw the growth of large public asylums and the professionalisation of psychiatry. Emil Kraepelin developed an influential classification distinguishing dementia praecox (later schizophrenia) from manic-depressive illness. Jean-Martin Charcot studied hysteria and hypnosis at the Salpêtrière, influencing a young Sigmund Freud.

Twentieth century

The early twentieth century was marked by psychoanalysis, founded by Freud, and by somatic treatments of varying value, including insulin coma therapy, electroconvulsive therapy, and lobotomy. The introduction of chlorpromazine in the 1950s launched the psychopharmacology era. From the 1960s, deinstitutionalisation movements in many countries closed large asylums in favour of community care. The publication of the DSM-III in 1980 standardised diagnostic criteria, and advances in neuroscience and genetics reshaped research.
