Sir John Frederick William Herschel, 1st Baronet KH FRS (7 March 1792 – 11 May 1871) was an English polymath active as a mathematician, astronomer, chemist, inventor and experimental photographer. The only son of the astronomer William Herschel, he extended his father's surveys of the heavens to the southern hemisphere and laid foundations for the modern naming of the constellations' stars and for photographic practice.

Early life and education

Herschel was born at Slough and educated briefly at Eton before entering St John's College, Cambridge, in 1809. There, with Charles Babbage and George Peacock, he founded the Analytical Society to promote continental (Leibnizian) calculus in Britain. He graduated as senior wrangler and first Smith's prizeman in 1813 and was elected a fellow of the Royal Society the same year.

Astronomy

From 1816 Herschel assisted his father and then continued the family programme of "sweeping" the sky, cataloguing nebulae and double stars in the northern hemisphere. In 1834 he sailed to the Cape of Good Hope, where from Feldhausen he carried out a systematic survey of the southern sky, observing the return of Halley's Comet and the Great Eruption of Eta Carinae. His Results of Astronomical Observations made at the Cape of Good Hope (1847) and his General Catalogue of Nebulae and Clusters (1864) — later the basis of the New General Catalogue (NGC) — were standard references for decades.

Herschel named seven moons of Saturn (Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea, Titan and Iapetus received their present names from him) and four moons of Uranus (Titania, Oberon, Ariel and Umbriel). He also investigated stellar magnitudes and the distribution of stars, and his Outlines of Astronomy (1849) was a popular textbook.

Photography and chemistry

In 1819 Herschel discovered that sodium thiosulfate ("hypo") dissolves silver salts, the basis of photographic fixing. In 1839, on hearing of the daguerreotype, he made his own photographs on paper within days, coined the terms "photography", "negative" and "positive", and in 1842 invented the cyanotype process — later used for blueprints. His botanical cyanotypes influenced the work of Anna Atkins.

Other work and later life

Herschel's Preliminary Discourse on the Study of Natural Philosophy (1830) influenced Charles Darwin, who called him a formative influence. He served as Master of the Mint (1850–1855), was created a baronet in 1838, and received the Copley Medal twice. He died at Collingwood, his home in Kent, in 1871 and was buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton; Darwin was later buried beside him.
