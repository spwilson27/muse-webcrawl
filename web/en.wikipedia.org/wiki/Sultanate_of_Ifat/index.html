Sultanate of Ifat

The Sultanate of Ifat at its greatest extent, c. 14th century

The Sultanate of Ifat (1285–1415) was a medieval Muslim sultanate in the eastern Horn of Africa. Founded by the Walashma dynasty in the late 13th century, it was centred in the ancient city of Zeila and included parts of what are now eastern Ethiopia, Djibouti and Somaliland. Ifat was a major commercial and religious power, controlling important trade routes between the Ethiopian highlands and the Red Sea coast.

History

According to tradition, the sultanate was founded around 1285 by Umar Walashma (or his son Ali in some accounts), who established the Walashma dynasty. Ifat consolidated several smaller Muslim principalities in the region, including Fatagar, Dawaro, Bale and Sharkha. Its armies campaigned westward toward the Shewa plateau, bringing it into recurring conflict with the Christian Solomonic Empire of Ethiopia.

Under Sultan Haqq ad-Din I (reigned in the late 14th century), Ifat launched major offensives against Emperor Amda Seyon I, who responded with devastating counter-campaigns described in the royal chronicle The Glorious Victories of Amda Seyon. Haqq ad-Din was killed in battle in 1376 or 1386, and his brother Sa'ad ad-Din II continued the resistance from Zeila.

In the early 15th century, Emperor Yeshaq I pursued Sa'ad ad-Din to the island of Zeila, where the sultan was killed in 1415. The Walashma sultans fled to Yemen, but later returned and founded the successor Adal Sultanate, which would renew the struggle in the following century.

Trade and culture

Ifat's wealth rested on trade. Caravans carried ivory, slaves, gold and hides from the interior to the ports of Zeila and Berbera, returning with cloth, beads and metal goods. Archaeological remains include the ruins of mosques, tombs and settlements, notably at sites in the Chercher Mountains and around Harar. The sultanate was also an important centre for the spread of Islam in the Horn of Africa.
