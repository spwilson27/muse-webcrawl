History of gunpowder

The history of gunpowder begins in Tang-dynasty China, where alchemists seeking an elixir of immortality discovered the explosive mixture of saltpetre, sulfur and charcoal that went on to transform warfare, mining and engineering worldwide.

Chinese invention

Taoist alchemists mixing saltpetre (potassium nitrate) with sulfur and honey recorded dangerous deflagrations by the 9th century; the first written formula appears in the Wujing Zongyao of 1044. Song-dynasty armies deployed gunpowder in fire arrows, bombs, the fire lance and, by the late 13th century, true metal-barrelled guns against the Jin and Mongols.

Transmission westward

Gunpowder knowledge travelled along Mongol-era routes to the Islamic world and Europe. Hasan al-Rammah described rockets and purified recipes c. 1270; in Europe Roger Bacon and the pseudo-Marcus Graecus recorded formulas in the 13th century, and cannon appear in European records by the 1320s. The Ottoman Empire, Safavid Persia and Mughal India — the "gunpowder empires" — built states around firearms and artillery.

Gunpowder warfare and industry

Corned (granulated) powder, cast bronze and iron cannon, and handguns reshaped siegecraft: Constantinople fell to Ottoman great bombards in 1453, and star forts evolved in response. Gunpowder also blasted mines, canals and tunnels. State powder mills — and their catastrophic explosions — were fixtures of early modern industry, and figures such as Lavoisier rationalised French saltpetre production.

Replacement

From the mid-19th century, black powder was progressively displaced: guncotton, nitroglycerin, dynamite (Nobel, 1867) and smokeless powder (Poudre B, 1884; cordite) offered greater power and less smoke. Black powder survives today in fireworks, fuses, historical re-enactment and some blasting, a legacy of a millennium of use.
