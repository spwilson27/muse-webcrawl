Salt water taffy

Salt water taffy is a variety of soft taffy originally produced and marketed in the Atlantic City, New Jersey area of the Jersey Shore starting in the late 19th century. Despite the name, the candy does not contain appreciable amounts of sea water; it does contain salt and water alongside sugar, cornstarch, corn syrup, glycerine, butter, and flavorings. The finished pieces are twisted and wrapped in wax paper, and are sold in an enormous range of colors and flavors.

Origin

The most popular legend holds that in August 1883 a storm tide flooded the Atlantic City candy shop of David Bradley, soaking his stock of taffy with Atlantic Ocean water. When a young customer asked for taffy the next day, Bradley jokingly offered her "salt water taffy," and the name stuck. Bradley's mother, in the back of the shop, liked the name and the family began marketing the candy under it.

Joseph Fralinger popularized the candy by giving it away in boxes as a souvenir and was the first to package it in one-pound boxes for boardwalk tourists; his Fralinger's remains a well-known brand, joined by James's and others along the Jersey Shore. The name "salt water taffy" was the subject of litigation reaching the United States Supreme Court in 1923 (Hildreth v. Mastoras), which held that the term had become generic for the type of candy and could not be monopolized.

Production

Taffy is made by boiling the ingredients to the soft-crack stage, then pulling the mass — traditionally on a hook, now by machine — to aerate it and give it a light, chewy texture. After pulling, the taffy is rolled, extruded, cut into bite-sized pieces, and machine-wrapped in wax paper. Common flavors include vanilla, chocolate, strawberry, lemon, molasses, peppermint, and more unusual varieties such as root beer and chili-mango.

Although strongly associated with Atlantic City, salt water taffy is sold in beach towns across the United States and Canada, and it is frequently marketed as a souvenir of seaside resorts.
