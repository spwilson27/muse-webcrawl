AB toxin

AB toxins
Two-component bacterial exotoxins
A: active/enzymatic component
B: binding and translocation component.

AB toxins are a class of bacterial protein toxins made from two functionally distinct parts. The B component binds to receptors on a target cell and promotes uptake or delivery, while the A component is an enzyme that enters the cytosol and modifies a cellular target. The separation of binding from catalytic activity makes AB toxins powerful and specific, and it has made them important models in cell biology.

Structure and variants

Some AB toxins consist of one A subunit linked to one B subunit, while others are AB5 toxins in which five B subunits form a ring that binds cell-surface glycans. After attachment, toxins may be endocytosed and travel through endosomes or retrograde pathways to the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum. From there the A chain can cross into the cytosol, sometimes exploiting protein-quality-control machinery.

Examples

Well-known examples include cholera toxin and the related heat-labile enterotoxin of Escherichia coli, pertussis toxin, diphtheria toxin, Shiga toxins and the botulinum and tetanus neurotoxins in a broader functional sense. Their A components may ADP-ribosylate G proteins or elongation factors, cleave SNARE proteins, or depurinate ribosomal RNA. The biochemical specificity explains the distinct diseases they cause, from profuse diarrhoea to paralysis.

Medical and research significance

Because AB toxins enter cells so efficiently, researchers use modified or detoxified components to study membrane trafficking and as tools for targeted delivery. Vaccine design often aims to neutralise the B binding step or detoxify the A enzyme while preserving immunogenicity. Understanding these toxins remains important for treating bacterial diseases and for assessing biological-threat risks.
