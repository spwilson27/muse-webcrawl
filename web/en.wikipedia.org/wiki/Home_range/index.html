Home range

A home range is the area in which an animal lives and moves on a periodic basis. It is related to the concept of an animal's territory, but differs from it: a territory is the portion of the home range that the animal actively defends against others of its species, whereas the home range itself need not be defended and may overlap extensively with the ranges of neighbours.

The concept was first set out by W. H. Burt in 1943, who defined the home range as the area traversed by an individual in its normal activities of food gathering, mating and caring for young, excluding occasional exploratory excursions. An animal's attachment to its home range is an example of site fidelity, and knowledge of a familiar area is thought to improve foraging efficiency and escape from predators.

Measurement

Home ranges are usually estimated from repeated observations of an individual's location, obtained by direct observation, capture–recapture, or telemetry such as radio collars and GPS tags. Common estimators include the minimum convex polygon drawn around location points, and kernel density estimation, which produces utilisation distributions showing the intensity with which different parts of the range are used; the intensively used centre of the range is called the core area.

Variation

Home range size varies enormously between species and individuals. It tends to increase with body size and energy requirements, to be larger in carnivores than in herbivores of similar mass, and to expand when food is scarce. Many species shift or enlarge their ranges seasonally, and migratory animals maintain separate breeding and wintering ranges connected by migration routes.
