Cabaret is a 1972 American musical drama film directed by Bob Fosse and starring Liza Minnelli, Michael York and Joel Grey. Set in Berlin during the Weimar Republic in 1931, under the growing shadow of the Nazi Party, the film is loosely based on the 1966 Broadway musical Cabaret by Kander and Ebb, which was itself adapted from Christopher Isherwood's 1939 novel Goodbye to Berlin and John Van Druten's 1951 play I Am a Camera.

The film centres on American cabaret singer Sally Bowles, who performs at the Kit Kat Klub, and her relationship with British academic Brian Roberts, who has come to Berlin to complete his doctorate. Their lives become entangled with wealthy playboy Maximilian von Heune and Jewish heiress Natalia Landauer as the political situation around them deteriorates.

Plot

In 1931 Berlin, young American Sally Bowles performs at the Kit Kat Klub. She meets Brian Roberts, a shy British writer who has moved into her boarding house while finishing his doctorate and supporting himself by teaching English. Although Brian initially resists Sally's advances — hinting that his previous relationships with women have failed — the two become lovers and close companions.

Sally befriends Fritz Wendel, a German gigolo who wishes to marry into money. The pair then meet Maximilian von Heune, a rich, bisexual baron who lavishes gifts on them and seduces them both. Fritz, meanwhile, pursues Natalia Landauer, a wealthy Jewish department-store heiress, but conceals from her that he is himself Jewish. As Nazi violence increases in the streets — intercut with the Kit Kat Klub's musical numbers, presided over by the sinister, grinning Master of Ceremonies — Sally becomes pregnant, and Brian proposes marriage. She ultimately obtains an abortion without telling him, fearing a life of domestic obscurity in Cambridge. Brian leaves Berlin, while Sally returns to the stage as life in the club, and the city, slides toward catastrophe.

Unlike most film musicals of the period, nearly all the songs are performed as diegetic numbers on the Kit Kat Klub stage, commenting ironically on the action. The only exception is "Tomorrow Belongs to Me," sung in a beer garden.

Production

Director and choreographer Bob Fosse, fresh from the commercial failure of Sweet Charity (1969), fought to direct the film, convincing producer Cy Feuer that his experience in Germany and his dark, realistic approach suited the material. Several songs from the stage show were dropped, new ones written, and the plot was reworked to follow Isherwood's stories more closely: Sally became American (as in the novel), Brian replaced the American Cliff Bradshaw, and the Fräulein Schneider–Herr Schultz subplot was reduced. The film was shot on location in West Germany, including in Munich and Berlin.

Reception and legacy

Cabaret was a critical and commercial success, grossing more than $42 million against a modest budget. It won eight Academy Awards at the 45th Academy Awards — including Best Director (Fosse), Best Actress (Minnelli) and Best Supporting Actor (Grey) — remarkably, without winning Best Picture, which went to The Godfather. Its eight wins remain the most ever for a film that did not win Best Picture.

The film revitalised the musical genre with its mature themes and is widely regarded as one of the greatest musicals ever made. In 1995 it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant."
