Crystal methamphetamine

Methamphetamine (contracted from N-methylamphetamine) is a potent central nervous system stimulant that is mainly used as a recreational drug and less commonly as a treatment for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and obesity. It was discovered in 1893 and exists in two enantiomers: levorotatory and dextrorotatory. The dextrorotatory form (dextromethamphetamine) is the stronger stimulant and is the form most associated with both therapeutic use (sold as Desoxyn in the United States) and abuse.

Pharmacology

Methamphetamine acts primarily by increasing the activity of dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin in the brain. It enters neurons through monoamine transporters, disrupts vesicular storage, and reverses transporter function, causing large increases in synaptic monoamine levels. It is metabolized in part to amphetamine, itself an active stimulant. The result is increased wakefulness, energy, euphoria, appetite suppression, and elevated heart rate and blood pressure.

Effects and harms

At therapeutic doses, methamphetamine improves attention in ADHD. Recreational use, especially of the crystalline form ("crystal meth"), carries high risk of addiction, psychosis, cardiovascular damage, severe dental decay ("meth mouth"), and neurotoxicity affecting dopamine and serotonin systems. Chronic use is associated with cognitive impairment, violent behavior, and increased risk of Parkinson's disease. Withdrawal is characterized by fatigue, depression, and intense craving.

History and regulation

Methamphetamine was first synthesized in Japan in 1893 by Nagai Nagayoshi from ephedrine, and crystallized in 1919 by Akira Ogata. It was widely distributed to military personnel in World War II (e.g., as Pervitin in Germany) and sold over the counter for many conditions after the war, leading to epidemics of abuse. Today it is a Schedule II controlled substance in the United States and similarly restricted in most countries; illicit production led to controls on precursors such as pseudoephedrine.
