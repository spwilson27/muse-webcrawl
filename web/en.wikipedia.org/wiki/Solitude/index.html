Solitude

Solitude is a state of seclusion or isolation, a lack of contact with other people. Unlike loneliness, which is an unpleasant feeling of unwanted isolation, solitude is often chosen and can be experienced positively — as an opportunity for reflection, creativity, rest, or spiritual practice. Solitude may be sought for short periods or, in the case of hermits and some religious contemplatives, for much of a lifetime.

Psychology

Research in psychology distinguishes solitude from social isolation: voluntarily chosen solitude is associated with benefits including emotional regulation, self-reflection, and restoration of attention, whereas prolonged involuntary isolation is a risk factor for depression and cognitive decline. Developmental psychologists note that the capacity to be alone, described by Donald Winnicott, is an important sign of emotional maturity. Adolescents and adults differ in how much solitude they need and enjoy, and personality traits such as introversion influence preference for time alone.

Religious and philosophical traditions

Many religious traditions value solitude: Jesus is described as withdrawing to lonely places to pray; Buddhist and Hindu practitioners undertake solitary retreats; Christian hermits and anchorites lived in seclusion, and monastic orders such as the Carthusians structure life around individual cells. Philosophers and writers, from Montaigne to Thoreau at Walden Pond, praised solitude as a condition for self-knowledge and original thought.

Enforced solitude

Solitude can also be imposed as punishment, most controversially in solitary confinement in prisons, which many medical and human-rights bodies regard as harmful and potentially torturous when prolonged. Involuntary solitude also occurs among castaways, prisoners of war, and people marooned by circumstance.
