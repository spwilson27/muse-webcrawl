History of banking

The history of banking begins with ancient grain loans and temple deposits, develops through medieval Italian merchant banks and early modern public banks, and culminates in the central banks, joint-stock banks and electronic finance of the modern world.

Ancient origins

Loans of grain and livestock were recorded in Mesopotamia by 2000 BCE; the Code of Hammurabi regulated lending, and Babylonian temples and private houses such as the Egibi accepted deposits and made loans. In Greece and Rome, money-changers at the trapeza (table, hence "bank" via the Italian banco) exchanged coin and lent at interest.

Medieval and Renaissance banking

Medieval Europe's ban on usury pushed credit into bills of exchange. Italian families — the Bardi, Peruzzi and above all the Medici (bank founded 1397) — built networks across Europe, financing trade, kings and popes; their failures (Edward III's default ruined the Bardi) taught hard lessons about sovereign risk. Double-entry bookkeeping spread with the trade.

Early modern public banks

The Bank of Amsterdam (1609) provided a stable deposit currency; Sweden's Riksbank (1668) and the Bank of England (1694) — founded to fund war against France — pioneered the central bank model, issuing notes and managing public debt. The South Sea Bubble of 1720 and John Law's Mississippi scheme demonstrated the dangers of paper credit.

The modern era

The 19th century saw joint-stock and investment banks (Barings, Rothschilds, later J.P. Morgan) finance railways and industry, while cooperative and savings banks served ordinary people. The gold standard linked currencies until 1914. The 20th century brought deposit insurance and central banking orthodoxy after 1929, US Glass-Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, post-1970s deregulation, electronic trading, and the 2008 financial crisis with its "too big to fail" rescues. Digital and mobile banking now dominate retail finance.
