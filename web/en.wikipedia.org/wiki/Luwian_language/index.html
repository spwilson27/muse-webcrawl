Luwian (sometimes spelled Luvian) is an extinct language of the Anatolian branch of the Indo-European language family, spoken in western and southern Anatolia and northern Syria during the Bronze and early Iron Ages. It was a close relative of Hittite, and the Luwians were one of the major peoples of the Hittite world; after the collapse of the Hittite Empire around 1180 BC, Luwian-speaking Neo-Hittite (Syro-Hittite) states flourished in south-eastern Anatolia and Syria for several more centuries.

Attestation and scripts

Luwian is attested in two writing systems. Cuneiform Luwian appears mainly in ritual texts from the Hittite capital Hattusa, written on clay tablets in the Old Babylonian-derived cuneiform also used for Hittite. Hieroglyphic Luwian is written in the native Anatolian hieroglyphic script, used for monumental inscriptions on stone and for seals; its inscriptions date from about the 14th century BC to around 600 BC, with the great bulk belonging to the Neo-Hittite states of the Iron Age. The decipherment of the hieroglyphs, advanced by the bilingual Karatepe inscription (Phoenician and Luwian) discovered in 1946–47, confirmed the language's Indo-European character.

Linguistic features

Luwian belongs to the "Luwic" subgroup of Anatolian, together with Lycian and Carian. Characteristic features include the genitival adjectives used instead of a true genitive case, a distinctive i-mutation in noun declension, and extensive use of sentence-initial particle chains and enclitic pronouns. Verb endings and vocabulary are close to Hittite but systematically different, for example the Luwian verb a- "to do/make" and the use of -mi conjugation endings.

History of scholarship

The prominence of Luwian speakers has led some scholars, notably in the controversial 2016 popular work by Eberhard Zangger and in more mainstream research by Fred Woudhuizen and others, to emphasise a "Luwian civilisation" of the Late Bronze Age Aegean-Anatolian interface; the suggestion that Luwians were behind the Trojan War tradition remains a minority hypothesis.
