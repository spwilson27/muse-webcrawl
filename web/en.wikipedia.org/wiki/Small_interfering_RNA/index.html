Small interfering RNA (siRNA), sometimes known as short interfering RNA or silencing RNA, is a class of double-stranded non-coding RNA molecules, typically 20–24 (normally 21) base pairs in length, that operate within the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway. siRNAs interfere with the expression of specific genes with complementary nucleotide sequences by degrading mRNA after transcription, preventing translation.

Structure and biogenesis

siRNAs have a well-defined structure: a short double-stranded RNA with phosphorylated 5′ ends and hydroxylated 3′ ends bearing two overhanging nucleotides. In cells they are produced when the enzyme Dicer cleaves longer double-stranded RNA or short hairpin precursors into 21–23 nucleotide fragments. Exogenous siRNAs can also be introduced directly, including synthetic molecules used in research and therapy.

Mechanism

One strand of the siRNA (the guide strand) is loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), whose core protein is an Argonaute protein (Ago2 in humans). RISC uses the guide strand to find mRNAs with complementary sequence; Ago2 then cleaves the target mRNA, which is degraded, silencing the corresponding gene. Because a single loaded RISC can destroy many mRNA molecules, RNAi is catalytic and potent.

Applications

siRNAs are a standard laboratory tool for knocking down gene expression in cultured cells and model organisms. They are also medicines: patisiran (Onpattro), approved in 2018 for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis, was the first FDA-approved siRNA drug, followed by agents such as givosiran, lumasiran, and the cholesterol-lowering drug inclisiran. Delivery to target tissues, stability, and avoiding off-target silencing remain central challenges addressed by chemical modification and lipid nanoparticle formulations.

History

RNA interference was described in 1998 by Andrew Fire and Craig Mello in Caenorhabditis elegans, work recognised by the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Short siRNAs were identified as the intermediates of the pathway in plants and flies around 1999–2001, and Thomas Tuschl's group showed in 2001 that synthetic 21-nucleotide siRNAs could silence genes in mammalian cells, launching the field's therapeutic era.
