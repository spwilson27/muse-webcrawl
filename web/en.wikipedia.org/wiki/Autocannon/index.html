An autocannon is a fully automatic weapon that fires explosive or armour-piercing shells of 20 mm calibre or larger at a rapid rate. Autocannon bridge the gap between machine guns, which fire solid bullets, and artillery pieces, which are generally not automatic. They are mounted on aircraft, naval vessels, armoured vehicles and in anti-aircraft roles.

History

The first practical autocannon appeared in the First World War, notably the German 2 cm Becker cannon and the British QF 1-pounder "pom-pom", derived from Maxim's design. In the Second World War autocannon became the standard armament of fighter aircraft — such as the 20 mm Hispano-Suiza HS.404 and the German MG 151 — and of light anti-aircraft guns like the 40 mm Bofors and the 20 mm Oerlikon. Post-war development produced revolver cannon such as the Mauser BK-27 and rotary cannon such as the six-barrelled 20 mm M61 Vulcan.

Mechanisms

Autocannon operate by several principles. Gas-operated and recoil-operated guns use the energy of the fired cartridge; revolver cannon use a rotating cylinder of chambers to achieve high rates of fire in a single barrel; rotary (Gatling-type) guns rotate multiple barrels; and externally powered weapons, such as the M242 Bushmaster chain gun, use an electric motor and chain drive, which handles misfires without stopping. Shells may be high-explosive, incendiary, armour-piercing or programmable air-burst types.

Applications

Typical installations include the 30 mm GAU-8 Avenger of the A-10 Thunderbolt II, the 25 mm M242 on the Bradley fighting vehicle, naval close-in weapon systems such as Goalkeeper and Phalanx (the latter a rotary cannon), and helicopter turrets. Infantry fighting vehicles commonly carry 20–40 mm autocannon capable of defeating lightly armoured vehicles and engaging low-flying aircraft and helicopters.
