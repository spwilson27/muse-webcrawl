Translation is the communication of the meaning of a source-language text by means of an equivalent target-language text. The English word derives from the Latin translatio, "a carrying across". Translation has been practised since antiquity — among the earliest evidence are bilingual inscriptions such as the Rosetta Stone, whose parallel texts in Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Ancient Greek allowed the decipherment of hieroglyphs in the 1820s — and it remains essential to diplomacy, trade, literature, religion, and science.

The discipline that studies translation systematically is called translation studies. Central debates in the field concern the tension between fidelity to the source text's wording (formal equivalence) and fidelity to its effect on readers (dynamic or functional equivalence), and the perennial claim — summed up in the Italian pun traduttore, traditore, "the translator is a traitor" — that something is always lost.

History

Translation was central to the spread of religions: the Hebrew Bible was translated into Greek as the Septuagint in the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE; Buddhist sutras were carried from India to China by translators such as Kumārajīva; and St Jerome's Latin Vulgate (late 4th century CE) became the standard Bible of Western Christianity for a millennium. In the medieval Islamic world, the translation movement centred on Baghdad rendered Greek science and philosophy into Arabic, whence much of it passed into Latin in 12th-century Toledo and Sicily. The printing press and the Reformation made Bible translation into vernaculars a mass phenomenon — Luther's German Bible (1522–1534) and the English King James Version (1611) shaped their languages profoundly.

Theory

Roman Jakobson distinguished intralingual translation (rewording within one language), interlingual translation (between languages), and intersemiotic translation (between sign systems, as when a novel becomes a film). Eugene Nida, drawing on Bible translation, contrasted formal and dynamic equivalence. Later scholars such as Lawrence Venuti argued that translators tend to "domesticate" texts — making them read fluently in the target culture at the cost of foreignness — and called for making the translator's work visible. Descriptive translation studies, associated with Gideon Toury, examines translations as facts of the target culture governed by norms, while "skopos theory" holds that a translation should be judged by its purpose.

The notion of untranslatability concerns words and structures with no close counterpart in another language — culture-specific terms, puns, metre, and grammatical categories such as evidentiality. Philosophers including Quine have pressed a stronger claim of the indeterminacy of translation, though working translators generally treat such problems as matters of degree and strategy rather than impossibility.

The translation process

A translator analyses the source text — its meaning, register, style, and purpose — then drafts a target text and revises it, often with the help of dictionaries, terminology databases, parallel texts, and, increasingly, computer-assisted translation (CAT) tools that store previous translations in "translation memories". Professional associations and bodies such as the International Federation of Translators set standards; sworn or certified translators produce legally recognised translations of official documents. Interpretation — the spoken counterpart of translation — is a separate profession with its own techniques (simultaneous and consecutive interpreting).

Machine translation

Machine translation (MT) began with rule-based systems in the 1950s, moved to statistical methods trained on large parallel corpora in the 1990s and 2000s (the first generation of widely used online translators), and since about 2016 has been dominated by neural machine translation, which produces markedly more fluent output. Large language models have since further improved quality, especially for high-resource language pairs. MT is now routine for gisting and high-volume content, but human translators remain standard for literature, law, marketing, and other high-stakes texts; the professional workflow increasingly involves post-editing machine output.

Specialised translation

  • Literary translation — fiction, poetry, and drama, where style and voice are paramount.
  • Legal translation — contracts, statutes, and court documents, requiring precision and knowledge of two legal systems.
  • Technical and scientific translation — manuals, patents, and research, dominated by terminology.
  • Localisation — adapting software, websites, and games to a locale's language, conventions, and culture.
  • Audiovisual translation — subtitling and dubbing for film and television.
