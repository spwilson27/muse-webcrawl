Carl O. Sauer

Carl Ortwin Sauer (December 24, 1889 – July 18, 1975) was an American geographer at the University of California, Berkeley. He was a leading figure in the development of cultural geography and a critic of environmental determinism.

Career and ideas

Sauer received his PhD from the University of Chicago in 1915 and joined Berkeley in 1923, chairing its geography department for over three decades. In his influential 1925 paper "The Morphology of Landscape", he proposed the concept of the cultural landscape — the natural landscape as reshaped by human culture over time.

Contributions

Sauer's research ranged widely: field studies of Mexico and Latin America, the history of agriculture and plant domestication in Agricultural Origins and Dispersals (1952), and early warnings about human destruction of the environment. His 1955 symposium "Man's Role in Changing the Face of the Earth" anticipated modern environmental history and human-impact science.

The Berkeley school of geography he founded emphasised fieldwork, historical depth, and regional study, shaping generations of geographers.
