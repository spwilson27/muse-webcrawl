T-score

The term T-score is used in two related statistical senses. Most commonly, in bone densitometry, a T-score is the number of standard deviations by which a patient's measured bone mineral density (BMD) differs from the mean BMD of a healthy young adult reference population. More generally, in statistics a T-score can denote a standardised score derived from Student's t-distribution, or a linear transformation of a z-score to a scale with mean 50 and standard deviation 10, used in some psychological and educational testing.

Bone densitometry

T-scores are obtained from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans, most often of the lumbar spine and hip. The World Health Organization diagnostic categories for postmenopausal women and men aged 50 and over are:

  • T-score ≥ −1.0: normal bone density.
  • T-score between −1.0 and −2.5: low bone mass (osteopenia).
  • T-score ≤ −2.5: osteoporosis.
  • T-score ≤ −2.5 with a fragility fracture: severe (established) osteoporosis.

The T-score is distinguished from the Z-score, which compares the patient's BMD with the mean for people of the same age, sex and ethnicity; Z-scores are preferred in younger patients and children. Fracture risk roughly doubles for each standard deviation the T-score falls below the young adult mean, and T-scores are combined with clinical risk factors in tools such as FRAX to estimate the 10-year probability of fracture.

Psychometric usage

In test theory, a T-score is a standard score with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, defined as T = 50 + 10z. It is used, for example, in the reporting scales of the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI), where scores of 65 and above are typically considered clinically significant.
