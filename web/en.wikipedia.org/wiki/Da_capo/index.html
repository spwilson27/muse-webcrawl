Da capo

Da capo (Italian: [da (k)ˈkaːpo], literally "from the head") is a musical term that instructs a performer to repeat a piece of music from its beginning. It is usually abbreviated D.C. The instruction saves the composer from having to write out repeated material in full. In small pieces the instruction may be equivalent to a simple repeat sign, but in larger works it carries considerable structural significance.

Variants

Two common variants qualify the instruction. Da capo al fine ("from the head to the end-mark") directs the performer to return to the beginning and play through to the place marked fine. Da capo al coda directs the player to repeat from the beginning and, on reaching a marker (often a cross-like symbol), jump to a concluding passage called the coda. Together with the related instruction dal segno (D.S.), "from the sign", the da capo belongs to the family of navigation marks that allow compact notation of music with repeated sections.

Da capo aria

The most famous application of the repeat is the da capo aria, the dominant form of operatic aria in the late Baroque era. In this form, used extensively by composers such as Handel and Hasse, a first section (A) is followed by a contrasting middle section (B); the performer then repeats the first section — da capo — giving an overall ternary (ABA) structure. The repeated A section was conventionally ornamented and embellished by the singer, providing an opportunity for virtuoso display. The da capo aria fell from favour in the later 18th century as composers such as Gluck sought greater dramatic continuity, and it is closely associated with the conventions of opera seria.
