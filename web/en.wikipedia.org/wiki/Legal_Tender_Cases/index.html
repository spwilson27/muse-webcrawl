The Legal Tender Cases were a series of United States Supreme Court decisions in 1870–71 on whether the federal government could issue paper money ("greenbacks") and make it legal tender for the payment of debts. After first holding the Legal Tender Acts unconstitutional in Hepburn v. Griswold (1870), the Court reversed itself the following year in Knox v. Lee and Parker v. Davis (1871), upholding Congress's power.

Background

To finance the Civil War, Congress passed the Legal Tender Acts of 1862–63, authorising United States Notes — greenbacks — and making them acceptable for most debts. Creditors who had contracted in gold coin challenged being forced to accept depreciated paper. In Hepburn, a 4–3 majority led by Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase — ironically the Treasury Secretary who had issued the greenbacks — held the Acts unconstitutional as applied to debts contracted before their passage.

Reversal

On the very day Hepburn was announced, President Grant nominated two new justices, William Strong and Joseph P. Bradley, who supplied the votes to rehear the question. In 1871 a 5–4 Court held that the power to issue legal-tender paper money could be implied from Congress's war, borrowing, and currency powers combined with the Necessary and Proper Clause. A later case, Juilliard v. Greenman (1884), extended the holding to peacetime. The episode remains a classic, controversial example of court-packing pressure and rapid self-reversal.
