Wayland's Smithy

Wayland's Smithy is a Neolithic long barrow and chamber tomb located near Ashbury in Oxfordshire, England, close to the Ridgeway and the Uffington White Horse. It is one of the best-known megalithic monuments in Britain and is in the care of English Heritage.

Description and history

The monument was built in two phases in the fourth millennium BC. An earlier timber mortuary house and stone-chambered barrow, containing the remains of at least 15 people, was later enclosed within a larger trapezoidal long barrow about 56 metres long, with a stone façade and a transepted chamber opening to the south. Excavations in 1962–63 revealed the second chamber's burials, including eight individuals.

Folklore

The barrow takes its name from Wayland the Smith, the legendary master blacksmith of Germanic and Norse mythology. Local tradition held that a traveller whose horse had lost a shoe could leave the animal and a silver coin at the tomb and return to find the horse shod and the coin gone. The legend was popularised by Sir Walter Scott's novel Kenilworth and Rudyard Kipling's Puck of Pook's Hill.
