Early music revival

The early music revival is a renewed interest in and performance of music from earlier historical periods — broadly, medieval, Renaissance, and Baroque music — that gathered momentum in the late nineteenth and especially the twentieth century. It is closely associated with the historically informed performance movement, which seeks to recreate the sound world of the era in which a work was composed, using period instruments, historical tunings, and performance conventions documented in treatises.

Origins

Precursors include Felix Mendelssohn's celebrated 1829 revival of Bach's St Matthew Passion in Berlin, which reintroduced Baroque masterworks to concert audiences, and nineteenth-century scholarly editions of early repertory. In England, Arnold Dolmetsch (1858–1940) pioneered the building of period instruments — recorders, viols, harpsichords, and lutes — and promoted the performance of early music in domestic and concert settings.

Twentieth century

After the Second World War, figures such as Noah Greenberg (New York Pro Musica), Nikolaus Harnoncourt and Gustav Leonhardt in Vienna and Amsterdam, and later Christopher Hogwood, Trevor Pinnock, and John Eliot Gardiner in Britain, brought historically informed performance from the margins to the mainstream of classical recording. Ensembles such as Concentus Musicus Wien, Musica Antiqua Köln, The English Concert, and the Academy of Ancient Music demonstrated that period-instrument performance could be both scholarly and commercially successful; by the 1980s and 1990s period practice had spread to Classical and even Romantic repertory.
