DNA supercoil

DNA supercoiling refers to the over- or under-winding of a DNA strand, and is an expression of the strain on that DNA. The amount of supercoiling in a closed circular molecule is quantified by its linking number; supercoils partition between twist (winding of the strands about the axis) and writhe (coiling of the axis itself). DNA that is underwound is said to be negatively supercoiled, the normal state of cellular DNA, while overwound DNA is positively supercoiled.

Biological role

Supercoiling is important for DNA packaging within all cells: negative supercoiling compacts the molecule and facilitates strand separation, which is needed for replication and transcription. As polymerases move along DNA they generate positive supercoils ahead and negative supercoils behind (the "twin supercoiled domain" model), and topoisomerase enzymes — including DNA gyrase in bacteria and topoisomerases I and II in eukaryotes — continuously adjust the supercoil density. Inhibiting these enzymes is the mechanism of several antibacterial and anticancer drugs.

Geometry

For a closed DNA molecule the linking number Lk is the sum of twist Tw and writhe Wr, an invariant described by the White–Fuller theorem. Relaxed B-DNA has about 10.5 base pairs per turn; deviations from the relaxed linking number are expressed by the superhelical density.
