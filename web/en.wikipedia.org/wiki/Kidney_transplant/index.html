Kidney transplant

A kidney transplant is the organ transplant of a kidney into a patient with end-stage kidney disease. It is generally the preferred treatment for kidney failure, offering better survival and quality of life than long-term dialysis, though recipients require lifelong immunosuppression.

Procedure

The donor kidney is usually placed in the iliac fossa of the pelvis, connected to the iliac vessels and the bladder, while the patient's own kidneys are normally left in place. Donors may be living (often relatives) or deceased. Because a person can live healthily with one kidney, the kidney is among the most commonly donated organs.

History

The first successful kidney transplant was performed in 1954 at Boston's Peter Bent Brigham Hospital by Joseph Murray, between identical twins — avoiding rejection and earning a share of the 1990 Nobel Prize; cyclosporine in the 1980s greatly improved outcomes with unrelated grafts.

Risks and outcomes

Risks include rejection, infection and side effects of immunosuppressants. Average graft survival is roughly 10–15 years for deceased-donor and 15–20 years for living-donor kidneys.
