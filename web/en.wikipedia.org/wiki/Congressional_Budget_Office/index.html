The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) is a federal agency within the legislative branch of the United States government that provides budget and economic information to Congress. Created by the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, the CBO produces independent analyses of budgetary and economic issues, most prominently formal cost estimates ("scores") of nearly every bill reported by a congressional committee, estimating how the legislation would affect federal revenues, spending and the deficit over five- and ten-year windows. Its estimates are decisive in practice: under congressional budget rules, a damaging CBO score can kill a bill, and reconciliation legislation must satisfy targets measured by the CBO.

Functions

The CBO each year publishes The Budget and Economic Outlook, projecting federal deficits, debt and economic variables under current law for the coming decade, together with a long-term outlook extending decades ahead. It prepares cost estimates for reported legislation (more than 500 formal estimates in a typical year, plus thousands of informal ones), analyses of the president's budget proposals, studies of policy options, and estimates of the cost of federal mandates imposed on state, local and tribal governments and the private sector under the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995. In health policy, CBO models of insurance coverage and premiums have made it a central arbiter in debates such as those over the Affordable Care Act and proposals to repeal or replace it.

Organisation and independence

The CBO's director is appointed jointly by the Speaker of the House and the President pro tempore of the Senate, after considering the recommendations of the Budget Committees, for a four-year term. Its staff of roughly 270 — mostly economists and public-policy analysts — is hired on professional merit, and the agency describes its work as strictly nonpartisan, making no policy recommendations. It is advised by a panel of economic advisers drawn from academia and research institutions. Alice Rivlin, the founding director, established the agency's reputation for independence. Despite this, CBO estimates regularly attract political attack: its use of conventional rather than fully "dynamic" macroeconomic scoring in headline estimates has been debated, with the agency publishing supplementary analyses of major bills' macroeconomic effects since rules changes in 2015.
