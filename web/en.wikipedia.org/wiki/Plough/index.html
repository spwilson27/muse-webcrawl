Plough

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plough (US spelling plow) is a farm tool for loosening or turning soil before sowing seed or planting. Ploughs were traditionally drawn by oxen or horses and are now in most areas pulled by tractors. Ploughing aerates the soil, buries crop residues and weeds, and brings fresh nutrients to the surface, preparing a seedbed for the new crop.

History

The earliest ploughs were digging sticks and hoes; scratch ploughs (ards) that merely scored the soil appear in the ancient Near East and Egypt by the 4th millennium BC. The heavy wheeled mouldboard plough, able to turn the dense soils of northern Europe, spread during the early Middle Ages and greatly increased agricultural productivity. Iron shares, and later steel ploughs such as John Deere's self-scouring steel plough of 1837, opened heavy prairie soils to farming. In the twentieth century, tractor-drawn multi-furrow and reversible ploughs became standard, though conservation agriculture has increasingly promoted reduced or no-till farming to limit erosion and soil-carbon loss.

Types

  • Mouldboard plough — cuts a furrow with a share and turns it over with a curved mouldboard.
  • Disc plough — uses rolling concave discs; useful in hard or sticky soils.
  • Chisel plough — loosens soil without inverting it.
  • Reversible plough — carries two sets of bodies so furrows can be turned the same way on return passes.
