The Curtis Act of 1898 was a United States federal law that extended the allotment policy of the Dawes Act to the "Five Civilized Tribes" — the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee) and Seminole nations — in Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma). Named for its sponsor, Senator Charles Curtis of Kansas (himself of Kaw ancestry, and later Vice President), it provided for the survey and division of the tribes' communally held lands into individual allotments, the enrolment of tribal citizens, and the abolition of tribal governments and courts, paving the way for Oklahoma statehood in 1907.

Background

The Dawes Act of 1887 had authorised allotment of reservation lands elsewhere but expressly exempted the Five Tribes, whose lands were held under separate treaties. Pressure from railroads, settlers and land speculators, together with the assimilationist programme of federal Indian policy, led Congress to create the Dawes Commission in 1893 to negotiate the tribes' consent to allotment. When negotiations stalled, Congress imposed the process by statute in the Curtis Act, enacted on 28 June 1898 as part of an Indian appropriations bill.

Provisions and effects

The Act authorised federal surveys of tribal domains, directed the Dawes Commission to enrol tribal citizens (producing the Dawes Rolls), and provided for allotments to each enrolled member, with "surplus" lands opened to settlement and townsite development. It invalidated tribal laws, abolished tribal courts, and subjected the territory to federal law. Tribal governments were scheduled for dissolution in 1906 (though several in fact persisted in reduced form). Through allotment and the later sale of restricted lands, the Five Tribes lost control of most of their roughly 19 million acres in Indian Territory, and the enrolment records created under the Act remain the basis for citizenship determinations in the successor tribal nations today.
