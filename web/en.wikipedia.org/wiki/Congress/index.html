Congress

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A congress is a formal meeting or assembly of representatives. In politics, the term most often denotes a national legislature, above all the Congress of the United States — the bicameral federal legislature composed of the Senate and the House of Representatives. Many other states, especially in Latin America and the Philippines, also call their legislatures congresses, and the word is used for the governing assemblies of political parties, as in India's Indian National Congress.

The United States Congress meets in the Capitol in Washington, D.C. Article I of the United States Constitution vests all legislative powers of the federal government in Congress, enumerating powers including taxation, borrowing, commerce among the states, coinage, and declarations of war, together with the power to make all laws “necessary and proper” for executing them.

The United States Congress

The Senate has 100 members, two from each state, serving staggered six-year terms; the House has 435 voting members apportioned among the states by population, serving two-year terms. Legislation must pass both chambers in identical form and be presented to the President, who may sign or veto it; a veto may be overridden by two-thirds of both houses. Congress also holds powers of impeachment, oversight of the executive through committees and investigations, and — in the Senate — advice and consent to treaties and appointments.

Other congresses

Party congresses are central in many political systems: the National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party, meeting roughly every five years, formally elects the party's Central Committee. In several Latin American republics the Congress is the ordinary name of the bicameral legislature, while international gatherings — from the Congress of Vienna (1815) onward — use the word for diplomatic assemblies.
