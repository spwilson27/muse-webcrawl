Alan Greenspan (born 6 March 1926) is an American economist who served as the 13th chairman of the Federal Reserve from 1987 to 2006. Appointed by President Ronald Reagan, he was reappointed by three subsequent presidents and served the second-longest tenure in the office, overseeing a long period of economic expansion and low inflation in the United States.

Early life and career

Greenspan studied economics at New York University and briefly played clarinet and saxophone professionally. Before entering government he ran the economic consulting firm Townsend-Greenspan & Co. In the 1950s and 1960s he was a close associate of the philosopher Ayn Rand, contributing essays to her Objectivist publications, though he later moderated his views in public life. He chaired the Council of Economic Advisers under President Gerald Ford (1974–1977).

Federal Reserve chairmanship

Greenspan took office days before the stock market crash of October 1987, responding with a prompt provision of liquidity. His tenure included the management of the 1997 Asian financial crisis and the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management. In a famous December 1996 speech he questioned whether "irrational exuberance" had inflated asset prices. His preference for low interest rates after the dot-com bubble was later criticised for fuelling the housing bubble that preceded the financial crisis of 2007–08; in congressional testimony in 2008 he acknowledged a "flaw" in his free-market ideology. His memoir, The Age of Turbulence, was published in 2007.
