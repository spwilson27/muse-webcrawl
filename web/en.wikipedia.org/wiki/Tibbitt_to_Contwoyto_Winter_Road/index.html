Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road

RegionNorthwest Territories and Nunavut, Canada
SurfaceAbout 85–90% frozen lake ice
SeasonRoughly late January to early April
PurposeSupply of diamond mines

The Tibbitt to Contwoyto Winter Road is a seasonal ice road in northern Canada, the longest heavy-haul ice road in the world. It runs roughly 600 kilometres north from Tibbitt Lake, near the end of the Ingraham Trail east of Yellowknife in the Northwest Territories, to Contwoyto Lake in Nunavut. About 85 to 90 percent of its length lies across the frozen surfaces of a chain of lakes, including the great Mackay Lake, linked by short portages over land. For roughly eight to ten weeks each winter, it is the sole surface lifeline of the region's diamond mines.

Construction and operation

Construction begins in December, when crews flood and grade the lake ice to build it up to load-bearing thickness; the road typically opens to light traffic in late January and to full loads once the ice exceeds safe thicknesses, closing again as the spring thaw approaches, usually in late March or early April. The road is built and operated by a joint venture of the mining companies it serves. Traffic travels in convoys dispatched at intervals, at tightly controlled speeds — loaded trucks may be limited to 25 km/h or less over the ice — because the pressure wave a fast-moving truck creates under the ice can damage the surface. Breakdown camps and security patrols are stationed along the route, and every load of the year's fuel, cement, explosives, machinery and construction material for the mines must move within the short season, typically several thousand truckloads.

The mines served

The road supplies the Ekati and Diavik diamond mines, the (now closed) Snap Lake and Jericho mines, the Lupin gold mine site on Contwoyto Lake, and exploration camps along its length, with spur roads branching to each property. Without the winter road, these remote operations would depend entirely on air freight, which is prohibitively expensive for bulk goods.

In popular culture

The road's drama — cracking ice, whiteouts and extreme cold — made it a setting for the first seasons of the television series Ice Road Truckers, which brought the winter-road system to a wide audience.
