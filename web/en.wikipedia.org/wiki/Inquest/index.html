Inquest

An inquest is a judicial inquiry in common law jurisdictions, particularly one held by a coroner, into the circumstances of a death that is sudden, unexplained, violent, or occurred in state custody. The purpose of an inquest is fact-finding: to establish who the deceased was and how, when and where they died. It is not a trial and does not determine criminal or civil liability, although its findings may prompt further proceedings or recommendations to prevent future deaths.

England and Wales

Under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, a coroner must investigate a death where the cause is unknown, the death was violent or unnatural, or the deceased died in custody or state detention. Most inquests are held without a jury, but a jury is required in certain cases, such as deaths in custody where the cause is not natural. The coroner (or jury) records a conclusion — short-form conclusions include natural causes, accident or misadventure, suicide, unlawful killing, industrial disease and open conclusion; narrative conclusions describing the circumstances in more detail are also common. Where an inquest reveals risks of future deaths, the coroner may issue a Report to Prevent Future Deaths (Regulation 28 report). Under Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights, "enhanced" (Middleton) inquests inquire more broadly into the circumstances of deaths involving the state.

Scotland and other jurisdictions

Scotland has no coroners; sudden deaths are investigated by the procurator fiscal, and a Fatal Accident Inquiry may be held before a sheriff under the Inquiries into Fatal Accidents and Sudden Deaths etc. (Scotland) Act 2016. Coronial inquests exist in many other common law countries — Ireland, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Hong Kong among them — while in the United States the system varies by state and county between coroners and medical examiners.

Notable inquests

High-profile inquests in Britain have included those into the Hillsborough disaster (the 2014–16 inquests concluded the 97 victims were unlawfully killed), the death of Diana, Princess of Wales (2007–08), and the 7 July 2005 London bombings (2010–11).
