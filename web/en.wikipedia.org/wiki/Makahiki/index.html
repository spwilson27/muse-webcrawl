Makahiki

Makahiki was an ancient Hawaiian New Year festival in honor of the god Lono, associated with fertility, agriculture, and peace. The celebration began with the rising of the Makaliʻi (Pleiades) constellation in the evening sky, roughly in mid-November, and lasted about four lunar months, through January or February.

The season

During the Makahiki, warfare was forbidden under kapu, and the work of the harvest season gave way to feasting, sport, and ceremony. A tall standard bearing the image of Lono — a crosspiece hung with white kapa cloth, feathers, and fern fronds — was carried clockwise around each island in a great procession, receiving hoʻokupu (offerings) of food and goods from each district (ahupuaʻa) along the way.

Games and ritual

The festival was famed for its athletic contests and games, including boxing, wrestling, javelin throwing (ʻoʻo ihe), foot races, sliding on paper-mulberry sleds (hōlua), bowling-like ʻulu maika, and mock battles fought with thrown spears blunted or padded to limit injury. At the season's end, chiefs and priests performed closing rites, taxes in the form of produce were settled, and the god's image was sent out to sea on a canoe laden with offerings, symbolizing Lono's departure before the return of Kū and the resumption of war and ordinary labor.

Historical note

Captain James Cook arrived in Hawaiʻi during the Makahiki season of 1778–79, and some scholars have argued that his reception — and later fatal conflict at Kealakekua Bay — was shaped by the festival's ritual calendar. The festival declined after the abolition of the kapu system in 1819, but cultural revivals have restored Makahiki celebrations on several islands in modern times.
