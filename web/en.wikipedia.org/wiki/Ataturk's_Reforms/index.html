Atatürk's reforms

Atatürk's reforms (Turkish: Atatürk İnkılâpları) were a series of sweeping political, legal, cultural, social, and economic changes implemented in the early years of the Republic of Turkey under its founder and first president, Mustafa Kemal Atatürk. Their aim was to transform the former Ottoman Empire into a secular, modern nation-state aligned with contemporary Western civilisation.

Political and legal reforms

The sultanate had been abolished in 1922; the Republic was proclaimed on 29 October 1923, and the caliphate abolished in 1924. Turkey adopted a new civil code (1926, based on the Swiss code), a penal code modelled on Italy's, and a Swiss-style commercial law, replacing sharia-based law in civil matters. Women gained the right to vote in local elections in 1930 and in national elections in 1934 — earlier than in several European countries.

Cultural and social reforms

The most famous measure was the Alphabet Reform of 1928, which replaced the Arabic-based Ottoman Turkish script with a Latin-based alphabet, accompanied by a mass literacy campaign. Other reforms included the Hat Law (1925) promoting Western dress, the adoption of the Gregorian calendar and international time, the Surname Law (1934) — under which Mustafa Kemal received the name Atatürk, "Father of the Turks" — the unification and secularisation of education, and the language reform carried out by the Turkish Language Association from 1932.

Economic reforms

The state promoted industrialisation, nationalised foreign-owned utilities, founded state banks and enterprises (such as Sümerbank and Etibank), built railways, and adopted five-year plans in the 1930s under the policy of etatism.

Legacy

The reforms remain foundational to the Turkish Republic and are protected in the constitution, though their interpretation — especially the strict secularism (laiklik) — has been continuously debated in Turkish politics.
