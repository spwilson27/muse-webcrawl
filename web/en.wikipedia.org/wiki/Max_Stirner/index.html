Max Stirner (25 October 1806 – 26 June 1856), born Johann Kaspar Schmidt, was a German philosopher who is considered a forerunner of nihilism, existentialism, psychoanalytic theory, postmodernism and individualist anarchism. His principal work, The Ego and Its Own (Der Einzige und sein Eigentum, published in late 1844 and dated 1845), argues that religion, morality, the state and other social institutions are mere "spooks" (Spuk) — fixed ideas that dominate the individual mind — and that the self-interested individual, the "Unique One" (der Einzige), should subordinate all such abstractions to his or her own will.

Life

Schmidt was born in Bayreuth, Bavaria. He studied philosophy at the University of Berlin, where he attended lectures by Hegel, and later worked as a teacher at a school for young women in Berlin. He took the pen name "Stirner" (from Stirn, forehead) from a school nickname referring to his high forehead. In Berlin he frequented the circle of Young Hegelians known as Die Freien ("The Free Ones"), which included Bruno Bauer and, briefly, Friedrich Engels and Karl Marx.

The Ego and Its Own appeared in October 1844 and was immediately controversial. Although initially well received, it made Stirner no fortune; a business venture in milk distribution failed, and he spent his last years in poverty, translating works of political economy and writing a History of Reaction (1852). He died in Berlin in 1856, reportedly from an infected insect bite.

Philosophy

The Ego and Its Own

Stirner's book opens with the declaration "All things are nothing to me" and develops a radical egoism: concepts such as God, Man, the State, the People, morality and even truth are "spooks" — abstractions that claim authority over the individual. Against them Stirner sets the egoist, who treats everything, including other people, as his "property" in the sense of what he can make his own through his power. In place of society he proposes a "union of egoists" (Verein von Egoisten), a voluntary association individuals enter and leave at will without obligation.

Influence

Marx and Engels devoted the largest section of The German Ideology (1845–46) to a polemic against Stirner ("Saint Max"), which ironically preserved interest in his work. After near-oblivion, Stirner was rediscovered in the 1890s, partly through the advocacy of John Henry Mackay, and influenced individualist anarchists, Nietzsche scholarship (though Nietzsche's direct debt remains disputed), and later existentialist and post-structuralist thought.
