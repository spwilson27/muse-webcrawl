Capuchin Crypt

LocationVia Vittorio Veneto 27, Rome, Italy
ChurchSanta Maria della Concezione dei Cappuccini
Established1631 (remains moved); chapels arranged 1732–1775
ContainsRemains of ~3,700 Capuchin friars

The Capuchin Crypt is a small space comprising several tiny chapels located beneath the church of Santa Maria della Concezione dei Cappuccini on the Via Veneto near Piazza Barberini in Rome, Italy. It contains the skeletal remains of 3,700 bodies believed to be Capuchin friars buried by their order, arranged along the walls and in decorative patterns. The Catholic order insists that the display is not meant to be macabre, but a silent reminder of the swift passage of life on Earth and of human mortality — a memento mori.

History

When the friars arrived at the church in 1631, they brought 300 cartloads of the remains of deceased brethren from their former friary. Soil from Jerusalem was used in the burial ground. As friars died, their bones were exhumed after decades of decomposition to make room for new burials, and the bones were added to the displays in the crypt's five chapels — of skulls, pelvises, shin bones, and full skeletons. Between roughly 1732 and 1775 the arrangements took their present form.

Description

Individual bones are nailed to walls in intricate patterns, forming rosettes, coats of arms, clocks, and even a skeleton representing Death with scales and a scythe. Some skeletons, still wearing their brown habits, recline in arched niches. A plaque in one chapel, in three languages, reads: "What you are now, we once were; what we are now, you shall be." Famous visitors struck by the crypt include the Marquis de Sade, Mark Twain, and Nathaniel Hawthorne.

Similar ossuaries

The crypt belongs to a wider tradition of ossuary chapels, including the Sedlec Ossuary in the Czech Republic, the Capela dos Ossos in Évora, Portugal, and the Capuchin Catacombs of Palermo, Sicily.
