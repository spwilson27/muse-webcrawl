Constitution of 3 May 1791

[Hero image placeholder: Jan Matejko's painting of the adoption of the Constitution of 3 May 1791]

The Constitution of 3 May 1791 (Polish: Konstytucja 3 maja), titled the Governance Act, was adopted by the Great Sejm (parliament) of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. It is often described as the first modern written national constitution in Europe and the second in the world, after the United States Constitution of 1788. It was in effect for less than 19 months, until the Commonwealth's defeat in the War in Defence of the Constitution.

Provisions

The constitution introduced political equality between townspeople and the nobility, placed the peasants under the protection of the law and government, abolished the destructive liberum veto and the right of confederation, and established a constitutional monarchy with a hereditary throne (to pass to the Saxon Wettin dynasty) and separation of powers among a legislative Sejm, an executive council (the Guardians of the Laws) headed by the king, and an independent judiciary. Roman Catholicism remained the dominant religion, with freedom of worship guaranteed to other faiths.

Adoption and aftermath

King Stanisław August Poniatowski and reformers such as Hugo Kołłątaj and Ignacy Potocki pushed the act through the Sejm on 3 May 1791 amid scenes of popular enthusiasm. Opponents, backed by Catherine the Great of Russia, formed the Targowica Confederation and invited Russian intervention. The resulting war ended with the Second Partition of Poland in 1793 and annulment of the constitution.

Legacy

3 May is celebrated in Poland as a national holiday (Constitution Day), and the constitution remains a powerful symbol of Polish statehood and Enlightenment reform.
