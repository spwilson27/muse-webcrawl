Myocarditis, also known as inflammatory cardiomyopathy, is an acquired cardiomyopathy due to inflammation of the heart muscle. Symptoms may include shortness of breath, chest pain, decreased ability to exercise, and an irregular heartbeat. The duration of problems can vary from hours to months. Complications may include heart failure due to dilated cardiomyopathy or cardiac arrest.

Myocarditis is most often due to a viral infection. Other causes include bacterial infections, certain medications, toxins, and autoimmune disorders. A diagnosis may be supported by an electrocardiogram (ECG), increased troponin, heart MRI, and occasionally a heart biopsy. An ultrasound of the heart is important to rule out other potential causes such as heart valve problems.

The heart muscle (myocardium) is the site of inflammation in myocarditis.

Signs and symptoms

The presentation of myocarditis ranges from asymptomatic cases detected only by transient ECG changes to fulminant disease with cardiogenic shock. Common features include:

  • Chest pain, often sharp or stabbing and sometimes mimicking a heart attack
  • Shortness of breath, at rest or on exertion
  • Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance
  • Palpitations and arrhythmias
  • Signs of heart failure, including swelling of the legs and breathlessness when lying flat
  • Fever and other features of the triggering infection

In infants and young children, symptoms may be non-specific, including poor feeding, irritability, and rapid breathing. Myocarditis is an important cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes.

Causes

Infectious

Viruses are the most commonly identified cause. Frequently implicated agents include coxsackievirus B, adenovirus, parvovirus B19, human herpesvirus 6, Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2. Bacterial causes include Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease), and disseminated tuberculosis. The parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, the cause of Chagas disease, is a major cause of myocarditis and chronic cardiomyopathy in Latin America.

Non-infectious

Autoimmune and inflammatory conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus, sarcoidosis, and giant cell myocarditis can involve the heart muscle. Drugs implicated include clozapine, anthracycline chemotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Hypersensitivity (eosinophilic) myocarditis may follow drug exposure, and toxins such as alcohol and cocaine are also recognised causes.

Mechanism

Most cases are thought to proceed in phases: direct viral injury to cardiomyocytes, followed by an innate and adaptive immune response that can itself damage the myocardium, and in some patients a chronic phase of autoimmune injury and remodelling leading to dilated cardiomyopathy. Molecular mimicry between viral antigens and cardiac proteins, particularly cardiac myosin, is thought to contribute.

Diagnosis

Findings that support the diagnosis include elevated cardiac troponin and inflammatory markers, ECG abnormalities such as ST-segment changes and conduction blocks, and impaired ventricular function on echocardiography. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can demonstrate myocardial oedema and late gadolinium enhancement; the updated Lake Louise criteria are used to interpret these findings. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the reference standard and, under the Dallas criteria, shows inflammatory infiltrate with necrosis of adjacent myocytes; it is reserved for selected cases, particularly where giant cell or eosinophilic myocarditis is suspected.

Treatment

Treatment is mainly supportive. Heart failure is treated with standard guideline-directed therapy, arrhythmias are managed as they arise, and patients are advised to abstain from competitive sport during recovery, typically for three to six months. Fulminant cases may require mechanical circulatory support such as ECMO. Immunosuppression is used for specific forms, including giant cell myocarditis, cardiac sarcoidosis, and eosinophilic myocarditis; routine immunosuppression or intravenous immunoglobulin is not recommended for typical viral myocarditis.

Prognosis

Many patients with mild disease recover completely. Others develop chronic dilated cardiomyopathy requiring long-term therapy, heart transplantation, or die suddenly. Fulminant myocarditis paradoxically carries a relatively good long-term prognosis if the patient survives the acute phase. In one large series of biopsy-proven cases, long-term outcomes were worst for giant cell myocarditis.

Epidemiology

The global incidence is estimated at roughly 1 to 10 cases per 100,000 people per year, though underdiagnosis is likely. Myocarditis disproportionately affects young men. It accounts for a significant proportion of sudden cardiac deaths in people under 40 and in competitive athletes.

History

Inflammation of the heart muscle was recognised in the nineteenth century, but the modern concept developed after the Second World War with the recognition of coxsackievirus infections. The Dallas criteria for histopathological diagnosis were published in 1987. The term "myocarditis" was popularised following studies of epidemics of viral illness with cardiac involvement.
