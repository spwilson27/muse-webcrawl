Colour (flag)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Colour

TypeMilitary flag
Used byInfantry regiments and other units, chiefly in Commonwealth armies

A colour is a type of ceremonial flag carried by military units, most prominently infantry regiments of British and Commonwealth armies. Colours embody the honour, spirit and traditions of a regiment, bearing its battle honours and, in the British tradition, being treated with profound reverence — historically, soldiers would defend the colours to the death, and the loss of a colour to the enemy was considered a grave disgrace.

Description

In the British Army, infantry battalions typically carry two colours: the King's Colour (or Sovereign's Colour), based on the Union Flag, and the Regimental Colour, which is in the regiment's facing colour and bears its badge, motto and battle honours. Guards regiments reverse this arrangement. Colours are made of silk, richly embroidered, and are mounted on pikes topped with a royal crest finial.

History

The practice descends from the standards and ensigns of early modern warfare, when brightly coloured flags served as rallying points in battle amid smoke and confusion. As tactics changed in the late 19th century, colours ceased to be carried into action — the last British colours carried in battle were at Laing's Nek in 1881 — but they remain central to ceremonial life, being paraded on occasions such as Trooping the Colour.

Consecration and laying up

New colours are consecrated in a religious ceremony before presentation. When colours are retired, they are "laid up" in a church, cathedral or other significant building, where they hang until they disintegrate; they are never destroyed deliberately. Retired colours of disbanded regiments remain objects of regimental commemoration.

Other traditions

Similar practices exist in other armed forces. In the United States, units carry organizational colors; in France, infantry regiments carry drapeaux; naval ensigns and RAF squadron standards serve a comparable role.
