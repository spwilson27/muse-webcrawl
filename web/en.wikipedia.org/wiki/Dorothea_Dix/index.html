Dorothea Dix

Dorothea Lynde Dix (April 4, 1802 – July 17, 1887) was an American advocate on behalf of people with mental illness who, through a vigorous programme of lobbying state legislatures and the United States Congress, created the first generation of American mental asylums. During the Civil War, she served as Superintendent of Army Nurses for the Union Army.

Early life

Dix was born in Hampden, Maine, and had an unhappy childhood. She ran schools for girls in Boston and wrote textbooks, including Conversations on Common Things (1824), before ill health forced her to give up teaching. In 1841, while teaching a Sunday school class at a jail in East Cambridge, Massachusetts, she discovered mentally ill inmates confined in unheated cells, and began her campaign for reform.

Asylum reform

Dix investigated conditions across Massachusetts and in 1843 presented a famous memorial to the state legislature describing the abuse and neglect of the "insane poor" in jails and almshouses. Her lobbying led to the expansion of the state asylum at Worcester and, over the following decades, to the founding or enlargement of more than thirty hospitals for the mentally ill across the United States and Canada. A federal bill to fund asylums through land grants passed Congress in 1854 but was vetoed by President Franklin Pierce. She later campaigned in Europe, prompting reforms in Scotland and elsewhere.

Civil War and later life

In 1861 Dix was appointed Superintendent of Army Nurses, organising and training female nurses for Union hospitals and setting strict standards for recruits. After the war she resumed her advocacy until shortly before her death in Trenton, New Jersey, in 1887.
