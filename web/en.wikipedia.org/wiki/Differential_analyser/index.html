The differential analyser is a mechanical analogue computer designed to solve differential equations by integration, using wheel-and-disc mechanisms to perform the integration. It was one of the most powerful calculating devices in use from the 1930s until it was superseded by electronic analogue and then digital computers in the 1950s.

History

The concept of using wheel-and-disc integrators in a machine for solving differential equations goes back to Lord Kelvin and his brother James Thomson in the 1870s, but practical machines awaited a means of amplifying the feeble torque of an integrator output. The first modern differential analyser was built at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology under Vannevar Bush and completed in 1931, using mechanical torque amplifiers. In Britain, Douglas Hartree and Arthur Porter built a machine at the University of Manchester (1934–35), initially from Meccano parts, and full-scale machines followed at Cambridge and elsewhere.

Operation

Quantities in the machine are represented by rotations of shafts. The problem is set up by interconnecting integrators, adders, multipliers and function tables (on which an operator tracks a curve with a pointer) with shafts and gears so that the machine embodies the equations to be solved. As the machine runs — originally driven by electric motors — solutions are traced on output drawing tables or read from counters. Typical machines could solve ordinary differential equations up to about the sixth order with an accuracy of roughly 0.1–1 per cent.

Uses and legacy

Differential analysers were used for ballistics and fire-control calculations, electrical network and power-system studies, aeronautics, and problems in physics and engineering that resisted closed-form solution. During the Second World War they were important computational resources in both Britain and the United States. Their role in the history of computing is significant: they established the institutional culture of large-scale scientific computation that electronic computers inherited.
