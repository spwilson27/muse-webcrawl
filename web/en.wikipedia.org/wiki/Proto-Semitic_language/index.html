Proto-Semitic language

Proto-Semitic is the reconstructed proto-language of the Semitic language family, the common ancestor of languages including Akkadian, Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, and Ethiopic languages such as Ge'ez and Amharic. It is generally thought to have been spoken in the Levant or northern Arabia during the Bronze Age, roughly the fourth millennium BCE, before the family divided into its East and West branches.

Phonology

The Proto-Semitic consonant inventory is reconstructed with a series of "emphatic" consonants — realized in most daughter languages as ejectives, pharyngealized, or glottalized sounds — alongside plain voiced and voiceless series, two pharyngeals, glottals, and three sibilants whose exact phonetics are debated. The vowel system is reconstructed with three short vowels */a, i, u/ and corresponding long vowels.

Grammar

Proto-Semitic nouns are reconstructed with a three-case system (nominative, accusative, genitive), three numbers (singular, dual, plural), and a predominantly consonantal root-and-pattern morphology: words are built by interdigitating a consonantal root, typically triliteral, with vowel templates. The verbal system combined prefix and suffix conjugations whose original aspectual values are debated, and word order was most likely verb–subject–object.

Homeland

Proposed homelands include the Levant, the Syro-Arabian desert fringe, and (in older scholarship) the Arabian Peninsula; a Levantine origin is now widely favoured on linguistic and archaeological grounds, within the broader Afroasiatic homeland debate.
