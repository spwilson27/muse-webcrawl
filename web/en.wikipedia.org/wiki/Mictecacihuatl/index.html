Mictecacihuatl
Role: Queen of Mictlan, goddess of death
Consort: Mictlantecuhtli
Culture: Aztec (Mexica)
Mictecacihuatl as depicted in Aztec codex imagery

Mictecacihuatl (from Nahuatl Mictecacihuatl, "Lady of the Dead") is the Aztec goddess of death and queen of Mictlan, the lowest and northernmost level of the underworld, which she rules together with her husband Mictlantecuhtli. Her role was to watch over the bones of the dead and to preside over Aztec festivals of the dead, which over time blended with the Catholic All Saints' and All Souls' observances to contribute to the modern Day of the Dead (Día de los Muertos).

Mythology

According to Aztec myth, Mictecacihuatl was sacrificed as an infant and then grew to adulthood in Mictlan. She is typically depicted with a defleshed, skeletal body, a skull-like face with an open jaw said to swallow the stars during the day. In the creation myth, Quetzalcoatl descended into Mictlan to retrieve the bones of earlier humanity from the death deities; after trickery and a fall that broke the bones — the mythic explanation for differing human statures — he escaped with them to recreate humankind.

Modern veneration

Mictecacihuatl is popularly identified with "La Calavera Catrina" imagery and is sometimes conflated with the folk saint Santa Muerte, though the two figures are distinct. She remains a prominent symbol in Day of the Dead celebrations, where offerings (ofrendas) of food, marigolds and candles guide ancestral spirits back to the world of the living.
