Ultras

Ultras are a type of association football fan characterised by fanatical support for their club. The term originated in Italy in the late 1960s and spread across Europe, North Africa, and much of the world. Ultra groups are typically organised independently of their clubs, occupy designated sections of stadiums (often behind the goals), and are known for elaborate visual and vocal displays.

An ultras tifo: large choreographed banners are a hallmark of ultra culture.

Characteristics

Core features of ultra groups include continuous chanting and singing throughout matches, the use of flags, banners, drums, and pyrotechnics, and the creation of large choreographed displays called tifos. Groups are usually self-financed through membership fees and merchandise and maintain a strong code of loyalty and secrecy. Identity is often tied to the locality and to rivalries with other groups; some groups adopt explicit political identities, ranging across the ideological spectrum.

History

The first groups calling themselves ultras formed in Italy around 1968–1971 (among them Sampdoria's Ultras Tito Cucchiaroni and Torino's Ultras Granata), drawing on older Italian supporter traditions and on influences from English fans and Brazilian torcidas. The culture spread through continental Europe in the 1970s and 1980s and became prominent in the Balkans, North Africa—where Egyptian and Moroccan groups became especially prominent—and Latin America in related forms such as the barras bravas.

Controversies

Ultras have been associated in some places with violence, organised confrontations with rival fans and police, and racist displays, leading to bans, stadium closures, and police attention. In Egypt, ultras groups such as Ultras Ahlawy played a visible role in the 2011 revolution and subsequent clashes, and Egyptian ultras were eventually banned by court order in 2015. Supporters of the movement emphasise its communal, creative, and anti-commercial aspects, including campaigns against modern football's commercialisation.
