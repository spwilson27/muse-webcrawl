The Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D major was composed by Maurice Ravel between 1929 and 1930. It was commissioned by the Austrian pianist Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm during the First World War and commissioned works for the left hand alone from many leading composers. The concerto is written in a single continuous movement and is noted for its dark orchestral colouring and jazz-inflected episodes.

Background

Ravel composed the work at the same time as his Piano Concerto in G major, deliberately contrasting the two: the G major concerto is light and Mozartian, while the Left Hand concerto is a single-span, more sombre structure. Ravel studied the existing left-hand repertoire, including Saint-Saëns's études, to master the illusion of a two-handed texture from one hand.

Music

The work opens in the depths of the orchestra — contrabassoon and divided double basses — before the piano enters with a sweeping cadenza. Thumb-played melodies in the middle register, supported by arpeggiated figuration, create the impression of two hands. A central scherzo-like section draws on rhythms suggestive of jazz, which Ravel admired. The whole concerto is unusually concise for its richness of material.

Premiere and the Wittgenstein dispute

Wittgenstein gave the premiere in Vienna in November 1931 with the Vienna Symphony under Robert Heger. Dissatisfied with Ravel's orchestration, Wittgenstein altered the score in rehearsal and performance, provoking a famous quarrel; he later acknowledged he had been wrong and the two were reconciled. The concerto has since become a cornerstone of the left-hand repertoire, performed by pianists such as Alfred Cortot (who initially tried to play it with both hands before Ravel objected), Robert Casadesus and Leon Fleisher.
