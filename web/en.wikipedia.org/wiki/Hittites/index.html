Hittites

Hittite Empire
Flourishedc. 1650 – c. 1180 BC
CapitalHattusa (near modern Boğazkale, Turkey)
LanguageHittite (Nesite), Luwian, Akkadian (diplomacy)
ScriptCuneiform; Anatolian hieroglyphs
Major rulersHattusili I, Suppiluliuma I, Muwatalli II, Hattusili III

The Hittites were an Anatolian people who established one of the great empires of the ancient Near East, centered at Hattusa in north-central Anatolia (modern Turkey). At its height in the fourteenth century BC the Hittite Empire rivaled Egypt, Babylon, and Assyria, controlling much of Anatolia and northern Syria. The Hittite language is the earliest well-attested member of the Indo-European language family.

History

Hittite-speaking peoples were established in central Anatolia by the early second millennium BC. Labarna and Hattusili I (c. 1650 BC) are regarded as founders of the Old Kingdom; Mursili I famously raided Babylon around 1595 BC. After a period of weakness, Suppiluliuma I (c. 1344–1322 BC) created the Empire, defeating Mitanni and installing vassal kingdoms across Syria. His successors fought Egypt for control of the Levant, culminating in the Battle of Kadesh (1274 BC) under Muwatalli II against Ramesses II — among the largest chariot battles ever fought. The peace treaty that followed (c. 1259 BC), concluded by Hattusili III, is the earliest surviving international treaty of parity; a copy hangs at the United Nations headquarters. Around 1180 BC the empire collapsed amid the Late Bronze Age collapse, though "Neo-Hittite" city-states in southeastern Anatolia and Syria endured into the eighth century BC.

Civilization

Excavations at Hattusa (from 1906) recovered royal archives of tens of thousands of cuneiform tablets — treaties, letters, annals, rituals, and a law code notably milder than its Mesopotamian counterparts, favoring compensation over retribution. In 1915–17 Bedřich Hrozný deciphered Hittite and proved it Indo-European, transforming the reconstruction of that language family. Hittite religion was polytheistic and absorptive — "the thousand gods of Hatti" — with great state festivals and storm-god (Teshub) and sun-goddess cults. Monumental art survives in the Lion and Sphinx Gates of Hattusa and the rock sanctuary of Yazılıkaya.

Rediscovery

The Bible's references to Hittites were long doubted until archaeology revealed the empire — a classic case of a "lost" civilization restored by excavation. Whether the biblical Hittites were the imperial people themselves or the later Neo-Hittite states remains debated.
