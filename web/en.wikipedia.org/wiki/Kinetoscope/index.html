Kinetoscope

The Kinetoscope is an early motion picture exhibition device, developed in the laboratory of Thomas Edison, principally by William Kennedy Laurie Dickson, between 1889 and 1892. It was designed for films to be viewed by one individual at a time through a peephole viewer window at the top of a cabinet.

Design

Inside the cabinet, a loop of 35 mm film — the format Dickson standardized, with four pairs of sprocket holes per image — ran continuously over a bank of spools, passing between an electric lamp and a shutter, while the viewer watched through a magnifying peephole. Films typically ran for well under a minute. The system grew out of Edison's earlier Kinetograph camera, patented alongside the viewing machine.

Commercial debut

The first commercial exhibition took place on 14 April 1894, when the Holland Brothers opened a Kinetoscope parlor in New York City with ten machines. Kinetoscope parlors spread quickly across the United States and Europe, showing short subjects such as dancers, boxers, vaudeville acts, and scenes staged at Edison's Black Maria studio in West Orange, New Jersey.

Legacy

Although Edison initially dismissed projection, the success of projected film by 1896 led him to market the Vitascope projector. The Kinetoscope's single-viewer format faded, but the 35 mm film standard it established remained the industry norm for more than a century.
