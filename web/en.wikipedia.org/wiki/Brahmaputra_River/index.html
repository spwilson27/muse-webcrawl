The Brahmaputra is a trans-boundary river which originates in southwestern Tibet as the Yarlung Tsangpo, flows east across the Tibetan Plateau, cuts through the Himalayas in some of the world's deepest gorges, and enters India in Arunachal Pradesh, where it is known as the Siang and Dihang. Joined by the Dibang and Lohit it becomes the Brahmaputra proper in the Assam valley, then flows into Bangladesh as the Jamuna, where it merges with the Ganges (Padma) and Meghna to form the world's largest river delta before emptying into the Bay of Bengal.

Course

At roughly 3,969 km long, the Brahmaputra is one of the great rivers of Asia. In Tibet it flows for over 1,600 km at elevations above 4,000 m, making it one of the highest major rivers in the world. Near Namcha Barwa it makes a hairpin bend through the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon — among the deepest canyons on Earth — and descends to the plains of Assam, where it becomes a vast braided river up to 10–20 km wide in the monsoon season, dotted with river islands including Majuli, one of the world's largest.

Hydrology and flooding

The river has one of the largest average discharges of any river and carries an enormous sediment load eroded from the young Himalayas. Seasonal monsoon flooding is a defining hazard of life in Assam and Bangladesh, replenishing soils while regularly displacing millions. In Hindu tradition the Brahmaputra is unusual in bearing a masculine name — "son of Brahma" — and in being worshipped as a male river deity.

Disputes and development

Chinese dam-building on the Yarlung Tsangpo, including the Zangmu dam and a planned very large project at the Great Bend, has raised concerns in India and Bangladesh over dry-season flows, while India has its own large hydropower plans for the Siang basin. No comprehensive water-sharing treaty covers the Brahmaputra basin.
