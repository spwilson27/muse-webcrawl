Eucharist in the Catholic Church

The Eucharist in the Catholic Church is the sacrament, celebrated in the Mass, in which — according to Catholic doctrine — bread and wine are changed into the body and blood of Jesus Christ. The Catholic Church teaches that the Eucharist is the "source and summit of the Christian life": it re-presents (makes present) the sacrifice of Christ on the Cross and is the sacramental meal of the New Covenant, instituted at the Last Supper.

Doctrine

At the consecration during Mass, the priest, acting in persona Christi, speaks the words of institution over bread and wine. Catholic theology, defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and reaffirmed at the Council of Trent, holds that by transubstantiation the whole substance of the bread is changed into the substance of Christ's body, and the whole substance of the wine into his blood, while the outward appearances (accidents) of bread and wine remain. Christ is believed to be present whole and entire under each species, so communicants receive the full Christ whether they receive the host, the chalice, or both.

Practice

Catholics in a state of grace may receive Holy Communion; those conscious of mortal sin are expected to confess first. Children normally receive their First Communion around the age of reason (about seven). The faithful are obliged to receive Communion at least once a year (the Easter duty) and to attend Mass on Sundays and holy days of obligation. Because the consecrated host is believed to be Christ himself, it is treated with intense reverence: reserved in tabernacles, carried in procession (notably at Corpus Christi), and exposed for adoration in a monstrance.

The validity of the sacrament requires wheaten bread and natural grape wine; unleavened bread is customary in the Latin Church, while the Eastern Catholic Churches use leavened bread.
