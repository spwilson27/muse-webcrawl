Mesoamerican codex

Mesoamerican codices are manuscripts produced by Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica before and during the Spanish conquest, written in the Indigenous pictorial and hieroglyphic traditions. They take the form of screenfolds made of bark paper (amate) or deerhide, painted on both sides and folded like an accordion, as well as single sheets and, in the colonial period, bound books combining native imagery with alphabetic text in Nahuatl, Maya, Mixtec, and Spanish.

Contents and genres

Pre-Columbian codices fall into several genres: divinatory almanacs and ritual calendars recording the 260-day cycle and its patron deities; astronomical tables, such as the Venus and eclipse tables of the Maya Dresden Codex; historical and genealogical narratives, especially the Mixtec codices that trace dynastic histories of kingdoms such as Tilantongo; and cartographic histories linking territory to lineage. After the conquest, Indigenous scribes produced tribute lists, land documents, censuses, and annals, including famous works such as the Codex Mendoza, compiled around 1541 for the Spanish Crown, and the Florentine Codex, Bernardino de Sahagún's great ethnographic compendium prepared with Nahua scholars.

Survival and destruction

Only a small number of pre-conquest codices survive — around fifteen screenfolds are generally accepted as pre-Columbian, including the Maya Dresden, Madrid, and Paris codices and the Mixtec and Borgia-group manuscripts. During the conquest and evangelisation campaigns, Spanish friars destroyed large numbers of Indigenous books as idolatrous; Diego de Landa's account of burning Maya books at Maní in 1562 is the most infamous episode. Surviving codices are held in libraries and museums in Europe and Mexico and are listed under the names of their repositories or former owners.
