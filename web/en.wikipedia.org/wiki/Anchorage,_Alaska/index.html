Anchorage (Dena'ina: Dgheyay Kaq') is the largest city in Alaska and its principal commercial centre, with a municipal population of about 291,000 at the 2020 census — more than a third of the state's residents. It lies on a triangular peninsula in Southcentral Alaska at the head of Cook Inlet, between the Knik and Turnagain Arms, backed by the Chugach Mountains.

History

The area was long inhabited by the Dena'ina Athabascan people. Captain James Cook explored the inlet in 1778. Anchorage itself was founded in 1914 as a tent-city construction headquarters for the Alaska Railroad at the mouth of Ship Creek, and was chosen over older settlements precisely because its sheltered waters offered ships a good anchorage. The town grew slowly until the Second World War, when military bases (Elmendorf Air Force Base and Fort Richardson, now Joint Base Elmendorf–Richardson) and the Alaska Highway spurred expansion. On Good Friday 1964 the city was devastated by the magnitude 9.2 Great Alaska earthquake, the most powerful recorded in North America, which dropped parts of Fourth Avenue by several metres. The discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 and construction of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline in the 1970s made Anchorage the administrative hub of Alaska's oil industry.

Economy and transport

Ted Stevens Anchorage International Airport is among the world's busiest cargo airports, a refuelling crossroads between Asia and North America. The Port of Alaska handles most freight entering the state. Tourism, healthcare, the military and state government are major employers. Anchorage is the starting point of the Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race, ceremonially begun each March.

Geography and climate

Anchorage has a subarctic climate with long, cold, snowy winters and mild summers with very long daylight. The municipality encompasses most of Chugach State Park, and wildlife including moose and bears is common within city limits.
