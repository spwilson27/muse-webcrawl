The radio spectrum is the part of the electromagnetic spectrum corresponding to radio waves — frequencies from about 3 hertz to 300 gigahertz. Different bands of the spectrum have very different propagation characteristics and are allocated to different services: broadcasting, mobile telephony, radar, satellite communication, radio astronomy, amateur radio, and many others.

Bands and propagation

Low-frequency waves (LF, MF) travel long distances as ground waves and by reflection from the ionosphere, which is why AM radio can be received far beyond the horizon at night. Shortwave (HF) signals bounce between the ionosphere and the Earth for intercontinental communication. Frequencies above HF (VHF, UHF, SHF) travel essentially in straight lines — line of sight — and are used for FM radio, television, mobile phones, Wi-Fi, radar, and satellite links. Microwave bands can carry large amounts of data but are attenuated by rain and require clear paths.

Management

Because transmissions interfere with one another, the spectrum is a regulated resource. The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) coordinates global allocations through World Radiocommunication Conferences, dividing the world into three regions; national regulators such as the Federal Communications Commission in the United States license users and auction commercially valuable bands. Certain bands, such as the 2.4 GHz ISM band, are set aside for unlicensed low-power use, enabling Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.
