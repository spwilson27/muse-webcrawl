Prelude (music)

A prelude (German: Präludium or Vorspiel; Latin: praeludium; French: prélude) is a short piece of music, the form of which may vary from piece to piece. While, during the Baroque era, for example, it may have served as an introduction to a following fugue or suite movement, in the Romantic era it became a self-contained character piece.

History

The earliest surviving preludes are short organ works from the 15th century, used to introduce church services and test the tuning of the instrument. Lutenists developed improvisatory preludes to warm up the fingers. In the Baroque era, Johann Sebastian Bach paired preludes with fugues in all 24 major and minor keys in the two books of The Well-Tempered Clavier.

Romantic era

Frédéric Chopin transformed the prelude into an independent miniature with his 24 Preludes, Op. 28, one in each key, ranging from the brief "Raindrop" prelude to stormy dramatic pieces. Later composers — Debussy, Rachmaninoff, Scriabin and Shostakovich — also wrote prelude cycles. Rachmaninoff's Prelude in C-sharp minor became one of the most famous piano pieces of its era.

Opera

In opera, the prelude (or overture/prelude distinction) sets the mood before the curtain rises; Wagner's concept of the Vorspiel, as in Lohengrin and Tristan und Isolde, made the prelude an integral dramatic statement drawing on the opera's leitmotifs.
