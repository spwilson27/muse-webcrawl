Contributory negligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Contributory negligence is a common-law defence in tort under which a plaintiff whose own negligence contributed to their injury may be barred from recovering damages from the defendant. In its classical, harsh form — traceable to Butterfield v. Forrester (1809) — any fault of the plaintiff, however small, defeated the claim entirely, subject to mitigations such as the last-clear-chance doctrine.

Most jurisdictions have replaced it with comparative negligence (comparative fault), apportioning damages by responsibility: “pure” comparative fault (plaintiff recovers even at 99 per cent fault, reduced accordingly) in states such as California (Li v. Yellow Cab, 1975) and New York; “modified” systems barring recovery at 50 or 51 per cent fault in most other US states; and statute-based apportionment in England and Wales (Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945) and the Commonwealth. Pure contributory negligence survives in only a handful of US jurisdictions (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia, and the District of Columbia) — a persistence routinely criticised by scholars and reform commissions.
