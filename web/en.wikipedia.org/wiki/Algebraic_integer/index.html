Algebraic integer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebraic number theory, an algebraic integer is a complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. The set of all algebraic integers forms a ring, and the notion generalises the ordinary integers: indeed, the algebraic integers that are rational numbers are exactly the ordinary integers, a fact that follows from the rational root theorem.

Examples

The Gaussian integers a + bi (with a, b ordinary integers) are algebraic integers in the field of Gaussian rationals. So are the Eisenstein integers, and roots of unity. In a quadratic field Q(√d), the algebraic integers are a + b√d when d is not congruent to 1 modulo 4, while for d ≡ 1 (mod 4) they take the form (a + b√d)/2 with a and b of the same parity — the reason numbers like the golden ratio (1 + √5)/2 count as integers in this extended sense.

Properties

The algebraic integers within a fixed number field form its ring of integers, a Dedekind domain in which ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals even when the elements themselves do not factor uniquely. Algebraic integers are closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication, and every algebraic integer's minimal polynomial over the rationals has integer coefficients. The concept, developed by Dedekind and others in the 19th century, is central to the study of Diophantine equations and class field theory.
