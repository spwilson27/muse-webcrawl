Ex parte Milligan

Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866), is a landmark United States Supreme Court case concerning the trial of civilians by military commissions. The Court held that the military trial of Lambdin P. Milligan, a civilian citizen of Indiana who was not in the military or naval forces, was unconstitutional where the civil courts were open and functioning.

Background

In 1864 Milligan, a Democratic politician and member of the Sons of Liberty, was arrested and tried by a military commission in Indiana on charges of conspiracy against the government, inciting insurrection, and aiding the Confederacy. He was sentenced to death. Milligan petitioned for a writ of habeas corpus, challenging the commission's jurisdiction.

Decision

Justice David Davis, writing for the majority, held that martial law cannot arise merely from a threatened invasion and cannot be applied where the courts are open and their process unobstructed; the Constitution's guarantees apply "equally in war and in peace". Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase concurred in the result but argued Congress could have authorized such commissions, though it had not.

Legacy

The case remains a leading authority on the limits of military jurisdiction over civilians and is frequently cited in debates over the detention and trial of terrorism suspects.
