Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot (June 1, 1796 – August 24, 1832) was a French physicist and military engineer whose 1824 treatise Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire laid the foundations of thermodynamics. In it he described the idealised heat engine cycle that bears his name and proved that the maximum efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the temperatures between which it operates — a result central to the later formulation of the second law of thermodynamics.

Life

Carnot was the son of Lazare Carnot, a leading mathematician and statesman of revolutionary France. He studied at the École Polytechnique and served as an officer in the engineering corps, increasingly devoting himself to the theory of steam engines, then central to industrial and military power but poorly understood.

His book, published at his own expense in 1824, attracted little attention during his lifetime. Carnot died of cholera in Paris in 1832 at the age of 36, and much of his work was burned after his death in accordance with practice for cholera victims, though his notes survived.

Scientific contribution

Working within the caloric theory, which treated heat as a conserved fluid, Carnot nevertheless reached conclusions that outlived that framework. The Carnot cycle — a reversible cycle of isothermal and adiabatic expansions and compressions — sets an upper bound on efficiency that no real engine can exceed.

Émile Clapeyron revived Carnot's ideas in 1834, and in the 1850s Rudolf Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) reconciled them with the emerging principle of energy conservation, founding classical thermodynamics and the concept of entropy.
