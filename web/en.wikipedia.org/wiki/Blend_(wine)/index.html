Blend (wine)

A blend, in wine, is a wine made from more than one grape variety or from grapes grown in different vineyards, vintages or fermented in different vessels. Blending is one of the winemaker's principal tools for achieving balance, complexity and consistency. Many of the world's most celebrated wine styles — Bordeaux, Champagne, Rioja, Châteauneuf-du-Pape and most Port — are traditionally blends, in contrast to varietal wines labelled by a single dominant grape.

Reasons for blending

Winemakers blend to combine the strengths of different grapes: one variety may contribute colour and tannin, another aroma and perfume, a third acidity or alcohol. Blending also spreads risk against poor weather in a particular vineyard parcel and allows a house style to be maintained from year to year. Non-vintage Champagne, for example, blends wines from several years to achieve a consistent character.

Blending may occur at several points: grapes may be co-fermented together (a "field blend" when the vines are interplanted), wines may be blended after fermentation and before ageing, or finished wines may be blended shortly before bottling. In many appellations the permitted varieties and proportions are regulated by law.

Famous blends

The classic Bordeaux blend combines Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, and sometimes Petit Verdot and Malbec. The southern Rhône blend known as "GSM" mixes Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre. Chianti historically blended Sangiovese with Canaiolo and white grapes, while modern "Super Tuscan" blends often combine Sangiovese with Cabernet Sauvignon or Merlot.

Labelling

Labelling laws differ by country. In the European Union a wine may be labelled with a single variety if it contains at least 85% of that grape; in the United States the minimum is generally 75%. Wines not meeting the threshold, or made from several varieties, are marketed as blends, proprietary names, or under regional appellations such as Bordeaux AOC.
