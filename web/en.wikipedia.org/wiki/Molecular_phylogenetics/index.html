Molecular phylogenetics

Molecular phylogenetics is the branch of phylogeny that analyses genetic, hereditary molecular differences, predominantly in DNA sequences, to gain information on an organism's evolutionary relationships. From these analyses, scientists can determine the processes by which diversity among species has been achieved and estimate the evolutionary distance between taxa and the times at which they diverged.

History

The field grew out of work in the 1960s by Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling, who proposed the concept of the molecular clock, and from early comparisons of protein sequences such as cytochrome c. The development of DNA sequencing in the 1970s and 1980s, and later of high-throughput sequencing, transformed systematics: relationships that morphology could not resolve — for example, the three-domain classification of life proposed by Carl Woese from ribosomal RNA sequences — became accessible to direct investigation.

Methods

A molecular phylogenetic study typically proceeds by selecting one or more genes or genomic regions, sequencing them in the organisms of interest, aligning the sequences, and inferring a phylogenetic tree using methods such as maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood, or Bayesian inference. Models of sequence evolution are chosen to account for differing rates and patterns of substitution. The resulting tree is tested for statistical support, commonly with bootstrap values.

Applications

Molecular phylogenetics is used throughout biology: in taxonomy and conservation, in reconstructing the history of languages of genes and gene families, in epidemiology to track the spread of pathogens such as influenza and coronaviruses, and in forensics and agriculture.
