Scion (botany)

In plant physiology and horticulture, a scion is a detached living portion of a plant — such as a bud or shoot — that is joined to the stock (root system and lower stem) of another plant in grafting. The scion becomes the upper, fruit- or flower-bearing part of the combined plant, while the stock provides the roots.

Role in grafting

Grafting is used to propagate plants that do not come true from seed, to combine desirable characteristics of two plants — for example, the fruit quality of one variety with the disease resistance or dwarfing habit of another's rootstock — and to repair damaged trees. The scion is selected from wood of the desired cultivar, usually one-year-old growth bearing several healthy buds. The cambium layers of scion and stock must be aligned so that the two can grow together and form a functional vascular connection.

Selection and storage

Scion wood is typically cut during the dormant season, when the buds are not yet growing, and stored cool and moist (often refrigerated in sealed plastic) until grafting in late winter or early spring. For bud grafting (budding), performed in summer, a single bud with a shield of bark is used instead of a multi-bud shoot.

Genetics

The scion retains its own genetic identity: fruit produced on a grafted branch is genetically that of the scion cultivar, not of the rootstock. This property — unlike seed propagation, which mixes the genes of two parents — is what allows centuries-old fruit cultivars to be maintained clonally. Rarely, graft chimeras can arise at the union, with tissue of both partners growing together.
