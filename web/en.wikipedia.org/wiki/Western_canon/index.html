Western canon
ScopeLiterature, philosophy, music, art of the Western world
Key advocateHarold Bloom, The Western Canon (1994)

The Western canon is the body of books, music, and art that scholars generally accept as the most important and influential in shaping Western culture. It includes works of fiction, nonfiction, poetry, drama, philosophy, and the visual and musical arts, from Homer and the Bible through Dante, Shakespeare, and Cervantes to the major figures of the modern era.

Harold Bloom's canon

The phrase gained prominence with literary critic Harold Bloom's 1994 book The Western Canon: The Books and School of the Ages, which placed Shakespeare at the centre of the canon and listed hundreds of essential works in an appendix organised by era — the Theocratic, Aristocratic, Democratic, and Chaotic ages. Bloom defended the canon on aesthetic grounds, arguing that canonical works are distinguished by "strangeness", originality, and mastery rather than by their social utility.

The canon debate

From the 1960s onward, the canon became a central battleground in the "culture wars" of higher education. Critics argued that the traditional canon over-represented "dead white males" and excluded women, minorities, and non-Western voices, reflecting the power structures that formed it. Allan Bloom's The Closing of the American Mind (1987) defended traditional curricula, while reforms at universities such as Stanford (whose Western Culture requirement was replaced in 1988) became flashpoints. Defenders of reform replied that a canon is a living selection, continually revised by each generation.

Related concepts include the Great Books programmes of Columbia University and the University of Chicago, and Mortimer Adler's Great Books of the Western World (1952).
