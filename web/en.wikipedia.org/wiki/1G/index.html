1G refers to the first generation of wireless telephone technology, comprising analogue cellular mobile telecommunication systems introduced in the late 1970s and 1980s and superseded by digital 2G systems in the 1990s. Unlike earlier mobile radio services, in which a single powerful transmitter covered a whole city, 1G systems divided coverage into many small "cells," each served by its own base station, allowing frequencies to be reused across a metropolitan area and greatly increasing capacity.

Standards and deployment

Several incompatible 1G standards were developed. Japan's Nippon Telegraph and Telephone (NTT) launched the first commercial cellular network in Tokyo in 1979. The Nordic Mobile Telephone (NMT) system, launched in 1981 in Scandinavia, was the first to offer international roaming. In North America, the Advanced Mobile Phone System (AMPS), developed by Bell Labs, was deployed commercially in Chicago in 1983; the iconic Motorola DynaTAC 8000X, the first handheld mobile phone, was an AMPS device. Other systems included Total Access Communication System (TACS) in the United Kingdom and Italy, C-450 in West Germany, and Radiocom 2000 in France.

Characteristics and limitations

1G networks carried voice as frequency-modulated analogue signals, typically in the 450 MHz and 800–900 MHz bands, with data services essentially limited to voice calls; throughput for modem data was around 2.4 kbit/s at best. Security was poor: calls were unencrypted and could be intercepted with radio scanners, and phone cloning fraud was widespread. Handsets were large, heavy and expensive, with short battery life. Despite these limitations, 1G established the cellular concept, the handover of calls between cells, and the commercial mobile telephone market.

Transition to 2G

Beginning with Finland's GSM network in 1991, digital 2G systems offered encrypted calls, greater capacity through digital compression, and data services such as SMS text messaging. Operators progressively shut down their 1G networks during the 1990s and 2000s to refarm the spectrum; AMPS service in the United States ended in 2008, although a few NMT 450 networks in remote regions of northern Europe survived somewhat longer.
