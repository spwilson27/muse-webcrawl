Dashpot

A dashpot, also known as a damper, is a mechanical device that resists motion via viscous friction. The resulting force is proportional to the velocity, but acts in the opposite direction, slowing the motion and absorbing energy. It is commonly used in conjunction with a spring (which acts to resist displacement). The process and instrumentation drawing (P&ID) symbol for a dashpot is a filled cylinder with a piston.

Types

The two most common types of dashpots are linear dashpots, which resist motion along a straight line, and rotary dashpots, which resist rotation about an axis. A linear dashpot consists of a piston moving inside a sealed cylinder filled with a viscous fluid such as silicone oil; motion of the piston forces the fluid through small orifices, generating a damping force. Rotary dashpots use a vane or disc rotating in a fluid-filled chamber.

Theory

In an idealized dashpot, damping is linear: the force is proportional to velocity, F = −c·v, where c is the viscous damping coefficient with units of force per unit velocity. Linear dashpots are a standard element in models of vibration and control systems, for example in the mass–spring–damper model used to analyse the ride and handling of vehicles.

Applications

Dashpots are used as shock absorbers in vehicle suspensions, in door closers that prevent slamming, in soft-close drawers and toilet seats, in pneumatic timers and escapements, and in seismic protection devices for buildings, where they dissipate kinetic energy as heat.
