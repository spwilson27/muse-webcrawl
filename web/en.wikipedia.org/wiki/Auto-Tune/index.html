Auto-Tune is an audio processor developed by Antares Audio Technologies (formerly Antares Audio Technologies and Antares) that uses a proprietary device to measure and correct pitch in vocal and instrumental performances. Released in 1997, it was originally intended to discreetly correct off-key pitches to the nearest true semitone. When set to extreme, fast retune speeds, it produces a distinctive robotic, warbling vocal effect that became one of the most recognisable sounds in popular music.

History

Auto-Tune was created by Andy Hildebrand, an engineer who had worked in seismic data analysis for the oil industry, adapting techniques used to map underground structures by autocorrelation. It was introduced at the 1996 Audio Engineering Society convention and shipped in 1997. The effect was first used prominently — and controversially, since producers initially kept it secret — on Cher's 1998 hit "Believe", where the stepped, robotic vocal became a deliberate stylistic feature dubbed the "Cher effect".

Use in popular music

In the 2000s the rapper T-Pain popularised overt Auto-Tune as an instrument in its own right in hip hop and R&B, provoking both imitation and backlash; Jay-Z's 2009 single "D.O.A. (Death of Auto-Tune)" protested its overuse. Artists including Kanye West (on the album 808s & Heartbreak), Bon Iver, Daft Punk, Travis Scott and many pop producers have used pitch correction variously as a subtle polish, an expressive device or a genre marker. Auto-Tune has also been widely adopted in regional styles such as raï, reggaeton and the Maghrebi chaabi scene.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have charged that Auto-Tune erodes the value of natural singing ability and homogenises pop vocals; the magazine Time named it one of the 50 worst inventions in 2010. Defenders reply that it is simply another studio tool, comparable to reverb or compression. The term "Auto-Tune" has become a registered trademark but is often used generically for pitch correction software, and the technology received a Technical Grammy Award in 2023 in recognition of its impact on recording.
