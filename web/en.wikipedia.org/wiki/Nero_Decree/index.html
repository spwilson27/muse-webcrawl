Nero Decree

The Nero Decree (German: Nerobefehl) was issued by Adolf Hitler on 19 March 1945, ordering the destruction of German infrastructure to prevent its use by Allied forces as they penetrated deep within Germany. It was officially titled Demolitions on Reich Territory Decree and has subsequently become known as the Nero Decree, after the Roman emperor Nero, who supposedly ordered the Great Fire of Rome.

Background

By early 1945 Germany's military situation was hopeless: Allied armies were crossing the Rhine in the west while the Red Army advanced on Berlin from the east. Hitler, increasingly detached, resolved that if the war was lost, the German nation should perish with him.

Contents

The decree ordered that all military, industrial, transportation, and communication facilities, as well as all stores and supplies within Reich territory that the enemy could use, were to be destroyed. In his accompanying remarks to Albert Speer, Hitler declared that if the war was lost, the nation would also perish, and that there was no need to take into consideration the basis of even a most primitive existence for the German people.

Response and non-implementation

The decree was deliberately disobeyed by Albert Speer, Minister of Armaments and War Production, who was appalled by the order and lost faith in the Nazi leader. Speer travelled around Germany persuading commanders and officials to ignore or delay the demolitions, and on 29 March persuaded Hitler to rescind some of the measures. Many regional leaders likewise quietly failed to carry out the destruction, though some demolitions of bridges and facilities did occur in the war's final weeks.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the decree and Speer's resistance to it formed part of Speer's defence; Speer claimed his opposition to the order demonstrated his break with Hitler, a claim that historians have since examined critically.
