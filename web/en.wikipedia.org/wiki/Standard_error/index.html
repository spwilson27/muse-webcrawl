Standard error

The standard error (SE) of a statistic (usually an estimate of a parameter) is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution, or an estimate of that standard deviation. If the statistic is the sample mean, it is called the standard error of the mean (SEM).

Standard error of the mean

The sampling distribution of the sample mean is generated by repeated sampling from the same population. Its standard deviation — the standard error of the mean — equals the population standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size:

SEx̄ = σ / √n

In practice the population standard deviation σ is unknown, so the standard error is usually estimated using the sample standard deviation s: SE ≈ s / √n. The standard error decreases as the sample size grows, reflecting that the sample mean becomes a more precise estimate of the population mean.

Interpretation

The standard error quantifies the precision of an estimate: a smaller standard error implies a tighter clustering of sample estimates around the true value. It is fundamental to confidence intervals and hypothesis testing: roughly, a 95% confidence interval for a mean is the estimate plus or minus about 1.96 standard errors (for large samples).

Terminology

The term was introduced by George Udny Yule in 1897. It should not be confused with the standard deviation, which describes the spread of individual observations rather than the precision of an estimate; because of this confusion, some statisticians have encouraged reporting confidence intervals instead of standard errors.
