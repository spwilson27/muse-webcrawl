Tadeusz Kutrzeba (15 April 1886 – 8 January 1947) was a Polish general, commander of the Poznań Army during the German invasion of Poland in 1939, and a military historian. He is best known for planning and leading the Battle of the Bzura, the largest Polish counter-offensive of the September Campaign.

Early life and career

Kutrzeba was born in Kraków, then part of Austrian Galicia. He studied at a military technical academy in the Austro-Hungarian Empire and served as an engineer officer in the Austro-Hungarian Army during the First World War. After Poland regained independence in 1918 he joined the Polish Army, served in the Polish–Soviet War, and became one of the army's leading staff officers and theorists. From 1928 to 1935 he was commandant of the Higher War School in Warsaw, shaping the education of a generation of Polish officers. In the 1930s he was also involved in preparing Polish defence plans against Germany.

September Campaign 1939

On the outbreak of the Second World War in September 1939, Kutrzeba commanded the Poznań Army in western Poland. As German forces advanced, he proposed and, after obtaining authorisation, launched a counter-offensive southward across the Bzura River beginning on 9 September. The attack initially surprised the German 8th Army and recaptured towns including Łęczyca and Łowicz, but overwhelming German superiority in armour and air power, and the collapse of neighbouring Polish forces, doomed the operation. The Polish forces were encircled and largely destroyed by 22 September. Kutrzeba reached Warsaw with remnants of his army, took part in the final defence of the capital, and after its capitulation on 28 September 1939 went into German captivity, spending the rest of the war in prisoner-of-war camps, including Oflag VII-A Murnau.

Later life

After liberation in 1945 Kutrzeba went to London, where he headed a historical commission documenting the September Campaign. He died in London on 8 January 1947. His works on the 1939 campaign, notably his study of the Battle of the Bzura published posthumously, remain important sources for the military history of the campaign.
