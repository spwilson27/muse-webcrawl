Replisome

The replisome is a complex molecular machine that carries out replication of DNA. The replisome first unwinds double-stranded DNA into two single strands. For each of the resulting single strands, a new complementary sequence of DNA is synthesised, so that the replisome at each replication fork duplicates the genome accurately and rapidly.

Components

In bacteria such as E. coli, the replisome includes the DnaB helicase, which unwinds the duplex; the DnaG primase, which lays down RNA primers; DNA polymerase III holoenzyme, whose β sliding clamp confers processivity; and single-strand binding proteins (SSB) that protect the exposed templates. In eukaryotes, the corresponding machinery includes the CMG helicase (Cdc45–MCM–GINS), DNA polymerases ε (leading strand) and δ (lagging strand), the PCNA sliding clamp loaded by RFC, and RPA.

Function

Because DNA polymerases synthesise only in the 5′→3′ direction, one new strand (the leading strand) is made continuously while the other (the lagging strand) is made discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, later joined after primer removal. Physical coupling of the two polymerases in the replisome coordinates both reactions as the fork advances — in bacteria at up to roughly 1,000 nucleotides per second.
