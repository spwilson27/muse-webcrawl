Endoderm

Germ layer formation during gastrulation; endoderm is shown innermost

The endoderm is the innermost of the three primary germ layers in the early embryo of animals, the other two being the ectoderm (outermost) and the mesoderm (middle). During gastrulation, cells that ingress through the primitive streak (in amniotes) or migrate inward at the blastopore (in other animals) form the endoderm, which displaces the hypoblast and lines the future gut cavity.

Derivatives

Endoderm gives rise to the epithelial lining of much of the digestive tract and respiratory system, as well as to several internal organs. Its principal derivatives include:

  • The epithelial lining of the gastrointestinal tract (except parts of the mouth and anus derived from ectoderm), and its associated glands
  • The liver and pancreas, which bud from the foregut endoderm
  • The lining of the respiratory tract, including the trachea, bronchi and alveoli of the lungs
  • The thyroid and parathyroid glands, thymus, and the epithelial lining of the pharynx and Eustachian tube
  • The lining of the urinary bladder and parts of the urethra
  • In plants, the term is also applied by analogy in some older literature, though "endoderm" is principally an animal embryology term

Development

Endoderm specification is controlled by signalling pathways including Nodal, Wnt/β-catenin and FGF, and by transcription factors such as Sox17, FoxA and GATA family proteins. In the mouse, definitive endoderm emerges during gastrulation around embryonic day 6.5–7.5. The endodermal sheet then folds into a gut tube — foregut, midgut and hindgut — from which organs bud during subsequent development.
