The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (PRWORA) is a United States federal law passed by the 104th United States Congress and signed into law by President Bill Clinton. The law fundamentally restructured the American welfare system, implementing major changes to the nation's social welfare policy and fulfilling Clinton's 1992 campaign promise to "end welfare as we know it".

Provisions

The Act replaced the Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) entitlement programme with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), a block grant to the states. It imposed work requirements on recipients, set a five-year lifetime limit on federally funded cash assistance, gave states wide discretion in programme design, tightened eligibility for Supplemental Security Income for children, restricted benefits for legal immigrants, and strengthened child-support enforcement. It also included measures promoting marriage and reducing out-of-wedlock pregnancies.

Background and passage

Clinton vetoed two earlier Republican welfare bills before signing a third, with the election of 1996 approaching. The bill passed with bipartisan support, though it was controversial within the administration; several officials resigned in protest.

Effects and debate

Caseloads fell dramatically in the following years — by more than half within five years — while employment among single mothers rose during the strong late-1990s economy. Scholars debate the law's effect on deep poverty: critics argue that TANF reaches few poor families today and that the safety net for the poorest children weakened, especially in recessions, while supporters cite increased work and reduced welfare dependence.
