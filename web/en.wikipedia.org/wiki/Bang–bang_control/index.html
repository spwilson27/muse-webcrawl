Bang–bang control

FieldControl theory
Also calledOn–off control, two-step control
Output statesFully on or fully off only
Common examplesThermostats, tank level switches

In control theory, a bang–bang controller (also called an on–off controller or two-step controller) is a feedback controller that switches abruptly between two states, typically fully on and fully off. Such controllers are the simplest form of feedback control and appear in most household thermostats.

Operation

The controller compares the measured variable with a setpoint and drives the actuator to one of its two extremes. A heating thermostat switches the furnace fully on when temperature falls below the setpoint and fully off above it. To prevent rapid cycling ("chattering") at the setpoint, practical devices add hysteresis — a deadband between the on and off thresholds — or a minimum on/off time.

Optimal control

Bang–bang control also arises in optimal control theory: for systems linear in the control input with the input bounded, the time-optimal control (for example, moving a mass to rest at a target position in minimum time) consists of applying maximal control in one direction and then the other, switching at most a finite number of times. This follows from Pontryagin's maximum principle and is the origin of the name.

Limitations

Because the output never modulates, bang–bang control produces a persistent oscillation (limit cycle) around the setpoint rather than settling exactly on it. Where precision matters, proportional or PID control is preferred; where cost and simplicity dominate, on–off control remains standard in appliances, refrigeration, and level and pressure switches.
