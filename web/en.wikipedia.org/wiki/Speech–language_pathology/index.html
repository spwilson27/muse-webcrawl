Speech–language pathology

Speech–language pathology (also known as speech and language therapy) is a healthcare and education field concerned with the assessment, diagnosis, and treatment of communication disorders and swallowing disorders. Practitioners, called speech-language pathologists (SLPs) or speech therapists, work with clients across the lifespan, from infants with feeding difficulties to adults recovering from stroke.

Scope of practice

SLPs address disorders of:

  • Speech sounds — articulation and phonological disorders, apraxia, and dysarthria
  • Language — receptive and expressive language delays and disorders, and aphasia
  • Fluency — stuttering and cluttering
  • Voice and resonance — hoarseness, vocal nodules, and resonance disorders
  • Social communication — pragmatic difficulties, including in autism spectrum disorder
  • Cognitive-communication — attention, memory, and executive-function deficits after brain injury
  • Swallowing and feeding — dysphagia across the lifespan
  • Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) — selecting and supporting communication devices and systems

Settings and training

Speech-language pathologists work in schools, hospitals, rehabilitation facilities, skilled nursing facilities, private practice, and research. In the United States, the profession requires a master's degree, supervised clinical fellowship, and certification by the American Speech–Language–Hearing Association (ASHA), along with state licensure; in the United Kingdom, practitioners register with the Health and Care Professions Council and are represented by the Royal College of Speech and Language Therapists.
