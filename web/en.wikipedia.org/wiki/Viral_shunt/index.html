Viral shunt

The viral shunt is a mechanism that prevents marine microbial particulate organic matter (POM) from migrating up trophic levels into animals and instead recycles it into dissolved organic matter (DOM), which can be readily taken up again by microorganisms. The term, introduced by Wilhelm and Suttle in 1999, describes the ecological consequence of the enormous rates of viral infection and lysis in the oceans.

Process

Viruses are the most abundant biological entities in seawater, and they infect an estimated 20–40% of marine bacteria each day. When an infected cell lyses, its carbon and nutrients are released largely as dissolved and colloidal matter and cell debris rather than being transferred to grazers such as zooplankton. The liberated DOM fuels further bacterial growth, which supports more viral production — a positive feedback that "shunts" carbon away from higher trophic levels and into the microbial loop.

Significance

Estimates suggest the viral shunt releases on the order of 0.1–0.5 gigatonnes of carbon per day globally, influencing ocean carbon sequestration, nutrient regeneration (especially iron and nitrogen), and the respiration of marine ecosystems. Viral lysis also shapes microbial community composition through "kill the winner" dynamics, maintaining diversity by suppressing dominant strains.
