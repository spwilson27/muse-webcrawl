Novation

Novation, in contract law, is the act of replacing an existing obligation or party with a new one, with the consent of all parties involved. The original contract is extinguished and a new contract takes its place on the agreed terms. A common example is the transfer of a contract from one party to another — for instance, when a business is sold and its supplier contracts are novated to the buyer, or when one bank replaces another as lender under a loan.

Requirements

For a novation to be effective in common-law systems, generally: (1) there must be a valid existing contract; (2) all parties — including the incoming party and the outgoing party — must agree to the novation; (3) the original contract must be extinguished; and (4) a valid new contract must come into being, supported where required by consideration, which is usually found in the discharge of the old obligations. Because novation depends on consent, a party cannot be forced to accept a substitute counterparty.

Novation versus assignment

Novation differs from assignment. An assignment transfers only the benefit (rights) of a contract, not its burden (obligations), and does not require the other contracting party's consent in most cases; the original contract continues in force. A novation, by contrast, transfers both benefit and burden and extinguishes the original contract. Whether a transaction amounts to novation or assignment depends on the intention of the parties, inferred from the documents — language showing that the old contract is cancelled and replaced points to novation.

Uses

Novation is routine in construction projects (novating design consultants to the contractor under design-and-build procurement), corporate transactions, government contracting, and financial markets. In derivatives markets, novation underpins central clearing: trades between two counterparties are novated to a central counterparty (clearing house), which becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer, reducing counterparty risk.

Civil law

The concept descends from Roman law (novatio) and exists in civil-law systems, where it is usually codified — for example in the French Civil Code (articles 1329 et seq.) and in the Louisiana Civil Code — generally with the same core features: extinction of the old obligation and creation of a new one, with the creditor's consent.
