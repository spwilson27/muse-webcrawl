Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) is a molecular cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescent probes that bind to specific sequences of DNA on chromosomes. Developed in the early 1980s from earlier radioactive in situ hybridisation methods, it allows scientists and clinicians to visualise and count particular genes or chromosomal regions directly in cells and tissue sections under a fluorescence microscope.

FISH: coloured dots mark the locations of specific DNA sequences.

Method

A probe — a short piece of DNA complementary to the target sequence — is labelled with a fluorescent dye, directly or via a hapten detected by fluorescent antibodies. Sample DNA (for example, metaphase chromosomes spread on a slide, or interphase nuclei) is denatured, the probe hybridises to its complementary target, unbound probe is washed away, and the signals are observed. Multiple probes with different colours can be used simultaneously (multiplex FISH), and related techniques such as spectral karyotyping and comparative genomic hybridisation extend the approach.

Applications

  • Clinical diagnosis: detection of deletions, duplications and translocations, e.g. HER2 amplification in breast cancer, the BCR–ABL fusion in chronic myeloid leukaemia, and microdeletion syndromes
  • Prenatal and genetic testing: rapid screening for trisomies 13, 18 and 21
  • Cancer cytogenetics: identifying characteristic rearrangements guiding prognosis and therapy
  • Microbiology: identifying bacteria and archaea in environmental and clinical samples using rRNA-targeted probes
  • Research: mapping genes on chromosomes and studying nuclear organisation
