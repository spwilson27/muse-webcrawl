Charing Cross

Charing Cross is a London junction around a monument in the City of Westminster, where six routes meet: the Strand, Whitehall, Cockspur Street, the Mall, Northumberland Avenue and the former Charing Cross Road (now part of St Martin's Place). Distances to London are traditionally measured from Charing Cross, making it the notional centre of the city. Its name derives from the Old English ciering ("river bend," of the Thames) and the Eleanor cross erected here in 1290.

The Victorian replica of the Eleanor cross in the forecourt of Charing Cross station.

History

Charing Cross was the last of the twelve Eleanor crosses marking the funeral route of Eleanor of Castile, wife of Edward I, in 1290; the original was destroyed by Parliament in 1647. A bronze equestrian statue of Charles I, erected in 1675 just south of the junction, still stands on the site and marks the official centre point from which London distances are measured. The present ornate cross, an 1865 replica designed by E. M. Barry, stands outside Charing Cross railway station, which opened in 1864 and serves destinations in south-east England.

The road junction and surroundings

The junction borders Trafalgar Square to the north and the government quarter of Whitehall to the south. Nearby landmarks include the National Gallery, St Martin-in-the-Fields church, Admiralty Arch and Nelson's Column. Charing Cross tube station, on the Northern and Bakerloo lines, serves the area. The street was also famous for its booksellers, commemorated in the bookseller's address "84, Charing Cross Road," made famous by Helene Hanff's book and film.
