Arabic poetry

Arabic poetry (Arabic: الشعر العربي, ash-shiʿr al-ʿarabī) is the earliest form of Arabic literature and, for many centuries, the most prestigious. It survives from the pre-Islamic period (the Jahiliyya), with poems transmitted orally and collected in the 8th and 9th centuries; the most celebrated anthology is the Muʿallaqāt, the "Suspended Odes".

Forms and metres

Classical Arabic verse is built on quantitative metres analysed by the grammarian al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi into sixteen canonical patterns. The dominant form is the qaṣīda, a monorhymed ode of up to about a hundred lines, classically moving from an amatory prelude (nasīb) at an abandoned campsite, through a journey section, to praise of the patron, tribal boasting, or satire. Other major genres include the love lyric (ghazal), elegy (marthiyya), invective (hijāʾ), wine poetry, and later the strophic muwashshaḥ of al-Andalus.

History

Pre-Islamic masters such as Imru' al-Qais, Labid, and Antara ibn Shaddad set the canon. Under the Umayyads and Abbasids, court poets like al-Mutanabbi — often called the greatest Arabic poet — Abu Nuwas, and al-Ma'arri raised the art to new sophistication, while the blind philosopher-poet al-Ma'arri pushed it toward sceptical reflection. Andalusi poets developed new strophic forms that may have influenced European troubadour lyric. In the 20th century, poets such as Nazik al-Malaika and Badr Shakir al-Sayyab pioneered free verse (al-shiʿr al-ḥurr), and figures like Mahmoud Darwish gave Arabic poetry a central place in modern cultural identity.
