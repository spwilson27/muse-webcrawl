The Kurfürstendamm (German pronunciation: [ˌkuːɐ̯fʏʁstn̩ˈdam], colloquially Ku'damm) is one of the most famous avenues in Berlin. The street takes its name from the former Kurfürsten (prince-electors) of Brandenburg. The broad, long boulevard can be compared to the Champs-Élysées in Paris and is lined with shops, houses, hotels and restaurants. In particular, many fashion designers have their shops there, as well as several car manufacturers' showrooms.

History

The avenue was first built around 1542 as a causeway (Damm) of wooden planks from the Berlin City Palace to the Grunewald hunting lodge of Elector Joachim II Hector of Brandenburg. Widened under Elector Frederick William, it later became a fashionable promenade and, after its rebuilding under Otto von Bismarck's initiative in the 1880s — modelled on Parisian boulevards — Berlin's grand commercial avenue.

During the Cold War, the Ku'damm became the commercial centre and showcase of West Berlin, centred on Breitscheidplatz with the Kaiser Wilhelm Memorial Church ruin and the Europa Center. After German reunification in 1990, some of its glamour shifted east to Mitte, but the avenue remains a premier shopping street, especially its western stretch.

Description

The Kurfürstendamm is 3.5 kilometres long, stretching from Breitscheidplatz (with the Gedächtniskirche) past the Uhlandstraße and Fasanenstraße intersections to Rathenauplatz in Halensee, where it merges toward the Grunewald. Side streets such as Joachimsthaler Straße, Leibnizstraße and Bleibtreustraße hold many of its notable cafés, theatres (including the Theater des Westens nearby) and the famous Café Kranzler.
