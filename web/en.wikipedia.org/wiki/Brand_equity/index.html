Brand equity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The customer-based brand equity pyramid (Keller).

Brand equity is a phrase used in the marketing industry to describe the value of having a well-known brand name, based on the idea that the owner of a well-known brand name can generate more money from products with that name than from products with a less well-known name, because consumers believe a product with a well-known name is better than products with lesser-known names. More precisely, brand equity is the added value that a brand endows a product with, reflected in how consumers think, feel, and act toward the brand, and in the prices, market share, and profitability the brand commands.

Definitions and models

The concept emerged in the 1980s amid takeover battles in which companies paid large premiums for branded businesses, prompting marketers and accountants to treat brands as assets. David Aaker defined brand equity as a set of assets and liabilities linked to a brand's name and symbol, grouped into five categories: brand loyalty, brand awareness, perceived quality, brand associations, and other proprietary assets such as trademarks and channel relationships. Kevin Lane Keller's customer-based brand equity (CBBE) model defines brand equity as the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to marketing, and structures brand building as a pyramid: identity (salience), meaning (performance and imagery), response (judgements and feelings), and relationships (resonance).

Measurement

Brand equity is measured from consumer, financial, and market perspectives. Consumer-based measures track awareness, associations, perceived quality, and loyalty through surveys and experiments. Financial approaches estimate the value of the brand as an intangible asset—for example by attributing a share of earnings to the brand (royalty relief or price-premium methods) as in the rankings published by firms such as Interbrand and Brand Finance. Market-based measures infer equity from outcomes such as price premiums, revenue premiums over private-label equivalents, and resilience of demand during downturns or product failures.

Effects and management

Positive brand equity allows firms to charge higher prices, reduces vulnerability to competitors' promotions, increases the effectiveness of advertising, eases entry into new categories through brand extensions, and strengthens bargaining power with retailers. Negative brand equity—when consumers react less favourably to a branded product than to an unbranded equivalent—can arise from scandals, quality failures, or overexposure. Managing brand equity involves consistent product quality, distinctive brand elements, integrated marketing communications, and protection of trademarks. Brand equity is recognised as a marketing asset under accounting standards only in limited circumstances (such as acquisitions) and is otherwise generally not carried on the balance sheet, a longstanding source of debate between marketers and accountants.
