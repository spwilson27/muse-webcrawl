Reform Judaism


A Reform synagogue interior.

Reform Judaism, also known as Liberal Judaism or Progressive Judaism, is a major Jewish denomination that emphasizes the evolving nature of Judaism, the superiority of its ethical aspects to its ceremonial ones, and belief in a continuous revelation, which is closely intertwined with human reason and not limited to the theophany at Mount Sinai.

History

The movement emerged in early 19th-century Germany amid the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah) and emancipation. Lay reformers introduced vernacular sermons, choral music, and shortened services; rabbis such as Abraham Geiger gave the movement theological shape. Carried by German Jewish immigrants to the United States, Reform became the dominant American denomination, codified in the Pittsburgh Platform of 1885. In Britain the related movement is known as Liberal Judaism and Reform Judaism (a separate, slightly more traditional body).

Beliefs and practice

Reform Judaism regards the Torah as divinely inspired but humanly shaped, and treats ritual law as non-binding while elevating ethical commandments. It affirms the autonomy of the individual in deciding which practices to observe, accepts patrilineal descent, ordains women and LGBTQ rabbis, and conducts egalitarian services mixing Hebrew with the vernacular. Platforms adopted in 1937 and 1999 partially reversed earlier classical Reform positions, restoring support for Zionism and many traditional practices.

Organisation

The movement's institutions include the Union for Reform Judaism, the Central Conference of American Rabbis, and the Hebrew Union College. Internationally, the World Union for Progressive Judaism links Liberal and Reform communities in some 50 countries, making it the largest stream of Judaism worldwide by synagogue affiliation.
