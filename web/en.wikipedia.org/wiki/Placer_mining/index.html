Placer mining

Gold panning, the simplest form of placer mining, relies on gravity to separate heavy gold from lighter sediment.

Placer mining (pronounced "plasser") is the mining of stream bed deposits — called placers — for minerals. It is usually done with gold, but can also be used for tin, platinum, and gemstones such as diamonds. Placer mining exploits the fact that heavy, valuable minerals weather out of bedrock and are concentrated by running water in riverbeds, beaches, or residual gravel deposits, where they can be separated by gravity.

Processes

A number of processes are used. In panning, a shallow pan is filled with gold-bearing sand and gravel, submerged in water and shaken so that the gold sinks to the bottom. Sluicing passes water through a riffled box, trapping heavy grains behind the riffles. The rocker box or cradle is a portable high-walled box on rockers that combines sieving and washing. At industrial scale, hydraulic mining uses high-pressure water jets to wash whole hillsides through sluices, while dredging excavates material from riverbeds with floating plant; some deposits are worked by open-pit methods and trommel wash plants.

History

Placer mining was the principal method behind the great gold rushes of the nineteenth century, including the California gold rush of 1849, the Victorian gold rush in Australia, and the Klondike Gold Rush in Canada. The simplicity of the basic technology — a pan or sluice — allowed individual prospectors to participate without large capital. Hydraulic mining was largely curtailed in California after the 1884 court decision in Woodruff v. North Bloomfield Gravel Mining Company, which restricted the dumping of mining debris into rivers.

Environmental effects

Placer mining disturbs stream channels and floodplains, increases turbidity, and can mobilise mercury used in amalgamation. Modern operations are generally required to restore worked ground and manage sediment. In some regions, historic placer workings remain a significant landscape feature.
