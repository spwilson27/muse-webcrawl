German historical school

The German historical school of economics was an approach to economic thought that flourished in Germany in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Its adherents held that economic behaviour could not be captured by universal, timeless laws of the kind proposed by British classical economics, but had to be understood historically, institutionally, and nationally, through empirical and statistical research.

Origins and generations

The school's forerunners included Friedrich List, who argued for national systems of political economy and infant-industry protection. The "older" historical school of Wilhelm Roscher, Bruno Hildebrand, and Karl Knies (1840s–1860s) sought historical "laws of development." The "younger" school, led by Gustav von Schmoller and including Lujo Brentano, Adolf Wagner, and Georg Friedrich Knapp, emphasised detailed empirical monographs and social reform; its members were derided by liberals as Kathedersozialisten ("socialists of the chair"). A "youngest" generation included Werner Sombart and Max Weber.

The Methodenstreit

In 1883 Carl Menger of the Austrian School attacked the historical school's neglect of theory, opening the Methodenstreit (battle of methods) with Schmoller. The dispute over the relative roles of deduction and induction shaped German-speaking economics for decades and contributed to its relative isolation from the emerging neoclassical mainstream.

Influence

Through the Verein für Socialpolitik (Association for Social Policy), founded in 1872, the school influenced Bismarck-era social insurance and German social policy. Its institutional and empirical orientation anticipated American institutional economics; scholars such as Richard T. Ely carried its approach to the United States.
