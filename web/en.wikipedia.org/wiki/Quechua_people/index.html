Quechua people

PopulationSeveral million

The Quechua people (Quechua: Qhichwa runa) are Indigenous peoples of South America who speak a Quechua language, which originated among the peoples of the central Andes. They constitute the largest Indigenous ethnic grouping in the Americas, concentrated in Peru and Bolivia, with significant populations in Ecuador, Colombia, Argentina, and Chile.

History

Quechua spread widely as the administrative language (lingua franca) of the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu) in the 15th and early 16th centuries, and the Spanish colonial administration continued to promote it for evangelization, making it the most widely spoken Indigenous language in the Americas today. Quechua-speaking communities maintained Andean traditions — agriculture with potatoes, quinoa, and maize; camelid herding; reciprocity (ayni) and communal labour (minka) — through the colonial era, republican land reforms, and modern migration to cities.

Culture

Quechua culture blends pre-Columbian and Catholic elements: veneration of Pachamama (Mother Earth), mountain spirits (apus), festivals such as Inti Raymi, distinctive woven textiles and dress (including the montera hat and lliclla shawl), and traditional music. Quechua is co-official in Peru and Bolivia, and intercultural bilingual education is promoted, though the language faces pressure from Spanish.
