Sede vacante (Latin for "the seat being vacant") is the period in the Catholic Church between the end of one pope's reign — by death or by resignation — and the election of a successor. The term is also applied to any episcopal see during a vacancy, and, in a broader sense, to the interregnum of other offices. During a papal sede vacante, the Holy See's coat of arms substitutes the umbraculum (a small canopy) for the papal tiara above the crossed keys of St Peter.

Governance during the vacancy

Under the apostolic constitution Universi Dominici Gregis (1996), ordinary governance of the Church during the vacancy passes to the College of Cardinals acting together, who may handle only routine matters and may make no decision that would bind the future pope. Most heads of dicasteries of the Roman Curia cease to exercise their offices. The Camerlengo administers the temporal goods of the Holy See and, traditionally, formally verifies the pope's death; upon a resignation, he verifies the relevant documents. The Dean of the College summons the cardinals to Rome for the general congregations that precede the conclave.

Recent vacancies

The sede vacante following the death of John Paul II in 2005 lasted 17 days, ending with the election of Benedict XVI. Benedict's resignation on 28 February 2013 produced the first vacancy by renunciation since 1415; it ended after 13 days with the election of Pope Francis. While most vacancies are brief, the longest in history followed the death of Clement IV in 1268 and lasted nearly three years (1268–1271), an ordeal that led to the formal rules of the conclave.

Sedevacantism

The term should not be confused with sedevacantism, the position held by some traditionalist Catholics that the see of Rome is in fact vacant because post–Vatican II popes are, in their view, heretics and therefore not true popes.
