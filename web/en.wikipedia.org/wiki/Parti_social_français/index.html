Parti social français

François de La Rocque, leader of the PSF, addressing supporters in the late 1930s.

The Parti social français (PSF; French Social Party) was a French nationalist political party founded in 1936 by Colonel François de La Rocque, following the banning of his Croix-de-Feu league by the Popular Front government. The PSF rapidly became the largest right-wing party in France before the Second World War, claiming a membership that may have approached one million by 1938.

Ideology

The PSF advocated a programme of social reform within a nationalist and authoritarian-leaning framework: it called for association between labour and capital, reform of the parliamentary system, women's suffrage, family allowances, and paid holidays, while opposing communism, socialism and class struggle. Historians have long debated whether the PSF should be classed as fascist. Many scholars, notably in the tradition of René Rémond, regard it as a conservative, nationalist mass party that accepted, at least tactically, parliamentary legality; other historians argue that its paramilitary origins, leader cult and anti-democratic rhetoric place it close to a distinctively French fascism.

History

Denied the chance to test its strength in a general election before the war, the PSF nevertheless won seats in by-elections and controlled several municipalities. The party was dissolved under the Vichy regime in 1940, though La Rocque himself later worked with the Resistance, was arrested by the Gestapo in 1943 and deported; he died shortly after the war in 1946.
