Frederick Treves

Sir Frederick Treves, 1st Baronet (15 February 1853 – 7 December 1923) was a prominent British surgeon, famous for his association with Joseph Merrick, known as the "Elephant Man", and for performing the appendectomy that saved the life of King Edward VII in 1902.

Career

Born in Dorchester, Dorset, Treves trained at the London Hospital, where he became a lecturer in anatomy and a leading surgeon. He wrote influential surgical texts, including a manual of surgical applied anatomy that long remained a standard reference. In 1884 he encountered Joseph Merrick at the London Hospital and arranged for him to live there, later recounting the story in his memoir The Elephant Man and Other Reminiscences (1923).

Royal surgery

On the eve of Edward VII's coronation in 1902, the King developed appendicitis; Treves drained the abscess and performed the operation that allowed the coronation to proceed weeks later. Treves was created a baronet and appointed Sergeant-Surgeon to the King. He later co-founded the British Red Cross Society.
