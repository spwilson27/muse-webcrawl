Buddhism in the United States

Buddhism in the United States is a religious movement comprising roughly one percent of the U.S. population, making it the country's third-largest religion after Christianity and Judaism by some measures. It encompasses both Asian immigrant communities that brought the religion with them and American converts drawn to meditation, Zen, Tibetan Buddhism, and mindfulness practice.

History

Buddhism first reached the United States in the mid-19th century, carried by Chinese immigrants who arrived during the California Gold Rush and by Japanese immigrants who followed. At the same time, scholars and Transcendentalist writers such as Henry David Thoreau and Ralph Waldo Emerson engaged with translated Buddhist texts. The 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago introduced many Americans to figures such as Anagarika Dharmapala and Soyen Shaku.

In the 1950s and 1960s, Beat Generation writers including Jack Kerouac and Allen Ginsberg popularized Zen. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened the door to teachers from Tibet, Vietnam, Thailand, and Korea — including Thich Nhat Hanh and the 14th Dalai Lama, whose exile after 1959 brought Tibetan Buddhism to wide American attention.

Demography

Surveys by the Pew Research Center have found that about 1% of U.S. adults identify as Buddhist. Asian Americans make up a majority of American Buddhists, with substantial Japanese, Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, and Sri Lankan communities, alongside a large population of converts, who tend to emphasize meditation practice.

Major traditions

Prominent forms include Zen (both Soto and Rinzai), Pure Land Buddhism, Nichiren Buddhism (notably Soka Gakkai), Tibetan Buddhism in its Gelug, Kagyu, and Nyingma schools, and Theravada traditions centered on vipassana (insight meditation), which strongly influenced the modern mindfulness movement.
