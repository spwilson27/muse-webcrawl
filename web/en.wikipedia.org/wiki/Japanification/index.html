Japanification

A deserted shopping street in a provincial Japanese city

Japanification (or Japanization) is a term in economics describing the process by which an advanced economy enters a prolonged period of low growth, low inflation or deflation, very low interest rates, and rising public debt resembling Japan's experience after the collapse of its asset price bubble in the early 1990s — the so-called "Lost Decades."

Background

Japan's stock and real-estate bubbles peaked in 1989–1991. The subsequent balance-sheet recession left banks burdened with bad loans, corporations focused on debt repayment rather than investment, and the Bank of Japan pushed interest rates to zero. Despite massive fiscal stimulus and later quantitative easing, growth and inflation remained subdued for decades, while gross public debt rose above 200 percent of GDP.

Use of the term

Economists and commentators applied "Japanification" to the eurozone after the 2010s debt crisis, and later to other economies facing demographic decline, weak demand, and chronically low inflation. Warning signs typically cited include an aging and shrinking population, a flat yield curve, negative bond yields, and the inability of central banks to lift inflation to target despite unconventional monetary policy.

Debate

Analysts dispute how transferable Japan's experience is: Japan maintained social stability, low unemployment, current-account surpluses, and debt held largely by domestic investors, conditions not shared by all economies said to be "Japanifying." The concept nevertheless remains a common frame in discussions of secular stagnation and the limits of monetary policy.
