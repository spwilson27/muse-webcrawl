Marital rape

Marital rape or spousal rape is the act of sexual intercourse with one's spouse without the spouse's consent. Historically, most legal systems treated marriage as implying irrevocable consent, and rape within marriage was not recognised as a crime. During the later twentieth century, feminist movements and changing understandings of consent led many jurisdictions to criminalise it.

History of the marital exemption

The traditional exemption is commonly traced to a statement attributed to the seventeenth-century English jurist Sir Matthew Hale, who wrote that a husband could not be guilty of raping his wife because by marriage she had given herself to him and could not retract the consent. This doctrine shaped law across the common-law world for centuries. Under coverture, a wife's legal identity was subsumed in her husband's, reinforcing the view that spouses had no separate right to refuse sex.

Legal reform

Reform gathered pace from the 1970s. Sweden had removed the exemption as early as 1965, and many European countries followed in the 1980s and 1990s. The House of Lords abolished the exemption in England and Wales in the case of R v R (1991). In the United States, Nebraska was the first state to abolish the exemption in 1976, and by 1993 marital rape was a crime in all fifty states, though some retained differing penalties or reporting requirements for spousal offences. In a number of countries, particularly parts of Asia and Africa, marital rape remains legal or only partially criminalised, and reform campaigns continue; international bodies such as the United Nations have repeatedly called for its criminalisation everywhere.

Effects and research

Research associates spousal rape with severe physical and psychological consequences for victims, including injury, post-traumatic stress and long-term health problems, often compounded by continued cohabitation with the perpetrator and economic dependence. It is widely considered an under-reported crime, and it frequently occurs alongside other forms of domestic violence. See also Domestic violence and Consent.
