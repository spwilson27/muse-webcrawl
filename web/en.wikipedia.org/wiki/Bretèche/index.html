A bretèche (also spelled bretache or bretesche) is a small projecting structure in medieval fortification. In its stone form it is a small balcony-like projection, usually placed over a gate or doorway of a castle or town wall, with openings in its floor (machicolations) through which defenders could drop stones, shoot arrows or pour boiling liquids on attackers directly below.

Description

The stone bretèche is a rectangular projection carried on corbels, open at the bottom between the corbels, and typically roofed. It is related to the larger bartizan (a corner turret) and to the wooden hoarding or brattice — a temporary timber gallery erected on a wall in time of siege, from which the stone bretèche likely developed as a permanent replacement. Bretèches are common on French, English and crusader castles of the 12th to 15th centuries, frequently above the main entrance, the most vulnerable point of the defences.

Other uses

In some contexts the term was applied to a small latrine projection on a castle wall, and in heraldry a bretèche can appear as a charge. The word derives from Old French, ultimately from a Germanic root related to "board" or "plank", reflecting the wooden origins of the structure.
