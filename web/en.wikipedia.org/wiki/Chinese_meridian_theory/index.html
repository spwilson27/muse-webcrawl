Meridian (Chinese medicine)

Meridian theory (simplified Chinese: 经络; traditional Chinese: 經絡; pinyin: jīngluò) is a central concept in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). The meridian system is a network of channels through which the vital energy known as qi is said to flow throughout the body, connecting organs, tissues, and acupuncture points.

The meridian system

The network is divided into twelve primary meridians (jingmai), each associated with one of the twelve zang-fu organs, and eight extraordinary vessels (qijing bamai). Along the meridians lie acupuncture points (xuewei), at which needles may be inserted or pressure applied to influence the flow of qi.

The twelve primary meridians are the Lung, Large Intestine, Stomach, Spleen, Heart, Small Intestine, Bladder, Kidney, Pericardium, Triple Burner (San Jiao), Gallbladder, and Liver meridians. Six are yin and six are yang, and they form sequential pairs in the daily cycle of qi circulation.

History

The earliest surviving description appears in silk texts excavated from Mawangdui (dated to 168 BC), which describe eleven vessels. The system was codified in the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon), compiled between the 2nd century BC and 2nd century AD.

Scientific evaluation

There is no anatomical or histological basis for the existence of meridians or acupuncture points as distinct structures, and the concept of qi is not supported by modern biomedical science. Effects of acupuncture are generally attributed to placebo effects, local physiological responses, and other mechanisms under investigation.
