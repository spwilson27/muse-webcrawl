Diminished triad

In music theory, a diminished triad is a triad made up of two minor thirds above the root. When a diminished triad consists of a root, a minor third, and a diminished fifth, it is the most dissonant of the common triads in tonal music and functions characteristically as a leading-tone chord or as part of a dominant seventh chord with the root omitted.

The diminished triad is built from a root note, the note a minor third (three semitones) above it, and the note a diminished fifth (six semitones) above the root. In four-part harmony it is frequently doubled at the root because the diminished fifth tends to resolve inward. In a major key, the diminished triad occurs naturally on the seventh scale degree (vii°); in the harmonic minor scale it occurs on the second and seventh degrees.

Because both its intervals above the root are "small", the chord has a tense, unstable quality. The interval between root and fifth is a tritone, the classic source of its dissonance. In figured bass, a diminished triad in root position may be indicated with a small circle or a slashed 5; in popular music chord symbols it is written "dim" or "°", as in Bdim or B°.

Historically the chord appears from the Baroque era onward as an essential colour in minor-key harmony, and its close relative, the half-diminished seventh chord, became a staple of jazz harmony.
