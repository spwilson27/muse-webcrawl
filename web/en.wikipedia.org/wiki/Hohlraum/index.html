Hohlraum

A hohlraum (German for "hollow space" or "cavity") is a cavity whose walls are in radiative equilibrium with the radiant energy inside it. In practice, the term most often denotes the small, high-Z (usually gold or depleted uranium) cylindrical enclosure used in indirect-drive inertial confinement fusion and in nuclear weapons physics.

Inertial confinement fusion

In indirect drive, laser beams or X-rays do not strike the fuel capsule directly. Instead they enter the hohlraum through holes at its ends and heat its inner walls, which re-radiate the energy as a smooth bath of soft X-rays. This radiation symmetrically ablates the surface of the tiny deuterium–tritium capsule suspended at the centre, driving an implosion intended to ignite fusion. Hohlraums are used in this way at facilities such as the National Ignition Facility.

Thermodynamics background

The idealized hohlraum is a physical realization of a blackbody cavity: radiation escaping through a small aperture in the wall approximates perfect blackbody radiation, a concept central to the development of Planck's law of thermal radiation.

Weapons physics

In thermonuclear weapon design, a radiation case or channel functioning as a hohlraum couples X-ray energy from a fission primary to a physically separate secondary stage — the principle underlying the Teller–Ulam design.
