Glasnost

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Glasnost (Russian: гласность, "openness" or "publicity") was a policy of increased openness and transparency in government and the media introduced in the Soviet Union by Mikhail Gorbachev in the mid-1980s. Together with perestroika (economic and political restructuring), it was intended to revitalise Soviet socialism; instead, by permitting free discussion of the system's failures, it helped unleash forces that led to the revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

Implementation

Gorbachev began using the term in 1985–86, initially meaning openness in the work of state institutions. After the Chernobyl disaster of April 1986 — whose initial official silence drew criticism — glasnost expanded dramatically: censorship was relaxed, previously banned books and films were released, political prisoners such as Andrei Sakharov were freed, and newspapers and television began reporting crime, corruption and historical crimes including Stalin's terror. Political reforms followed: contested elections to a new Congress of People's Deputies in 1989, whose sessions were broadcast live to an enthralled public.

Consequences

Open debate exposed the depth of economic stagnation and legitimised criticism of the Communist Party itself. National movements in the Baltic states and other republics used the new freedoms to demand independence, and newly legal public associations eroded the party's monopoly on politics. Historians debate whether Gorbachev intended controlled liberalisation or lost control of the process, but glasnost is widely regarded as the decisive step that made the peaceful end of the Cold War — and the USSR's collapse — possible.
