MOSFET

The metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET, MOS-FET, or MOS FET) is a type of field-effect transistor (FET), most commonly fabricated by the controlled oxidation of silicon. It has an insulated gate, the voltage of which determines the conductivity of the device. This ability to change conductivity with the amount of applied voltage can be used for amplifying or switching electronic signals.

The MOSFET is the basic building block of most modern electronics, and the most frequently manufactured device in history, with an estimated total of 13 sextillion (1.3 × 1022) MOSFETs manufactured between 1960 and 2018. It is the key component of the digital integrated circuits used in computers, smartphones and consumer electronics.

History

The basic principle of the field-effect transistor was first patented by Julius Edgar Lilienfeld in 1925. The MOSFET was later successfully demonstrated by Mohamed M. Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs in 1959, and first reported in 1960. Atalla first proposed the concept of MOS integrated circuits in 1960, and the first MOS chip was built in 1964.

Structure and operation

A MOSFET has four terminals: source, gate, drain and body (or substrate). The gate is separated from the semiconductor channel by a thin insulating layer of silicon dioxide. Applying a voltage to the gate creates an electric field that attracts or repels charge carriers in the channel beneath it, changing its conductivity between source and drain.

Types

MOSFETs are classified by the doping of their channel. In an n-channel device, electrons carry the current and a positive gate voltage enhances conduction; in a p-channel device, holes carry the current. Devices that conduct only when a sufficient gate voltage is applied are called enhancement mode; those that conduct at zero gate bias are depletion mode. The gate voltage at which conduction begins is the threshold voltage.

Applications

Complementary pairs of n- and p-channel devices form CMOS logic, whose near-zero static power consumption made modern microprocessors practical. Power MOSFETs, designed to handle significant currents and voltages, are widely used in power supplies, motor controllers and automotive electronics. MOSFETs are also used as memory cells in flash memory and DRAM, and as image sensors in digital cameras.

Scaling

Decades of miniaturisation, anticipated by Moore's law and described by Dennard scaling, shrank MOSFET dimensions from tens of micrometres to a few nanometres. At the smallest scales, leakage currents and short-channel effects led manufacturers to adopt new structures such as FinFETs and gate-all-around transistors.
