Galician wine


Galician wine is produced in the autonomous community of Galicia in northwestern Spain.

Galician wine is Spanish wine made in the autonomous community of Galicia in the northwest of Spain. Influenced by the Atlantic climate — high rainfall and moderate temperatures — the region is best known for crisp, aromatic white wines, particularly those made from the Albariño grape.

Denominations of origin

Galicia has five established Denominación de Origen (DO) regions: Rías Baixas, famous internationally for Albariño whites grown near the Atlantic coast; Ribeiro, one of Spain's oldest wine regions, known for Treixadura-based whites; Ribeira Sacra, where steep terraced vineyards above the Miño and Sil rivers produce Mencía reds; Valdeorras, noted for Godello whites and Mencía reds; and Monterrei, in the southeast near the Portuguese border.

Grape varieties

White varieties include Albariño, Godello, Treixadura, Loureira, Caíño Blanco, and Torrontés. Red varieties are dominated by Mencía, along with Caíño Tinto, Brancellao, Sousón, and Merenzao. Many vines are trained on pergolas (parras) raised above the ground, a traditional response to the humid climate that improves air circulation and reduces fungal disease.

History

Viticulture in Galicia dates to Roman times and was preserved by monastic orders through the Middle Ages. Ribeiro wines were exported widely in the 16th and 17th centuries. Like the rest of Europe, the region was devastated by phylloxera in the late 19th century, and recovery emphasized indigenous varieties in the late 20th century, when Rías Baixas (established as a DO in 1988) brought Albariño to international prominence.
