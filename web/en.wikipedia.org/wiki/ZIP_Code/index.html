A ZIP Code (an acronym for Zone Improvement Plan) is a system of postal codes used by the United States Postal Service (USPS). Introduced nationwide on 1 July 1963, the basic format consists of five digits. An extended ZIP+4 code, introduced in 1983, adds four further digits after a hyphen to identify a more specific location, such as a city block, an office building or a single high-volume mail recipient. "ZIP Code" was originally registered as a trademark by the Postal Service, though the registration has since expired.

Structure and geography

The first digit of a ZIP Code designates one of ten groups of U.S. states, running from 0 in the Northeast to 9 on the West Coast. The second and third digits together identify a sectional center facility — a regional mail sorting hub — and the final two digits identify a specific post office or delivery zone. ZIP Codes do not strictly follow political boundaries; they follow mail routes, and some span state lines. A handful of codes are famous in their own right, such as 90210 for Beverly Hills, California, and 12345 for a General Electric facility in Schenectady, New York. Low-numbered codes include 00501, assigned to the Internal Revenue Service office in Holtsville, New York.

History and use

ZIP Codes replaced the earlier postal zone numbers used in large cities, helping the Post Office cope with rapidly growing mail volume and enabling automated sorting. ZIP+4, though optional for most mailers, allows still more precise sorting. Beyond mail delivery, ZIP Codes are widely used as a convenient geographic unit for marketing, insurance rating, demographic statistics, sales-tax and eligibility checks, and are often requested in retail and online transactions. The Census Bureau defines ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) to approximate ZIP Code geographies for statistical reporting.
