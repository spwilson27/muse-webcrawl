Carbon in pulp

Carbon in pulp (CIP) is an extraction technique for recovery of gold which has been liberated into a cyanide solution as part of the gold cyanidation process. Introduced in the early 1980s, carbon in pulp is regarded as a simple and cheap process.

Activated carbon acts like a sponge to aurocyanide and other soluble complexes in the pulp, adsorbing the dissolved gold from solution. The loaded carbon is then separated from the pulp, and the gold is stripped from the carbon, typically with a hot caustic cyanide solution, before being recovered by electrowinning.

Process

In the CIP process, ground ore is mixed with a cyanide solution in a series of large agitated tanks. Granular activated carbon, usually derived from coconut shells, is added to the tanks and flows counter-current to the slurry. Because the adsorption of gold onto carbon is much faster than leaching, the carbon captures the gold as it dissolves. Once loaded, the carbon is screened from the slurry, eluted, and regenerated by heating in a kiln so it can be reused.

Related processes

The closely related carbon in leach (CIL) process combines leaching and adsorption in the same tanks, while resin in pulp substitutes ion-exchange resins for activated carbon. CIP is widely used where ores are not "preg-robbing" and where simplicity of operation is valued.
