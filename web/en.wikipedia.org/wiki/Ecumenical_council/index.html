An ecumenical council is a meeting of bishops and other church authorities convened to consider and rule on questions of Christian doctrine, administration and discipline, understood to represent the whole (ecumenical, "world-wide") Church. Decisions of councils regarded as ecumenical carry the highest authority within the traditions that recognise them, though churches differ over which councils qualify.

The first seven councils

Seven councils held between 325 and 787 are recognised as ecumenical by both the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, and most produced the creeds and doctrinal definitions of historic Christianity:

  1. First Council of Nicaea (325) — condemned Arianism and produced the original Nicene Creed.
  2. First Council of Constantinople (381) — completed the Nicene Creed and affirmed the divinity of the Holy Spirit.
  3. Council of Ephesus (431) — defined Christ as one person and Mary as Theotokos ("God-bearer"), against Nestorianism.
  4. Council of Chalcedon (451) — defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human.
  5. Second Council of Constantinople (553).
  6. Third Council of Constantinople (680–681) — condemned monothelitism.
  7. Second Council of Nicaea (787) — restored the veneration of icons.

The Oriental Orthodox churches accept only the first three; the Church of the East accepts only the first two.

Later councils

After the East–West Schism of 1054, councils held in the West continued under papal authority; the Catholic Church counts 21 ecumenical councils in total, including the Councils of Trent (1545–1563), which defined Catholic teaching in response to the Reformation, and the First (1869–1870) and Second (1962–1965) Vatican Councils. Eastern Orthodox theology holds that no council since 787 has the same universally binding status, though some synods, such as the Pan-Orthodox councils of the 14th and 19th centuries, are treated as authoritative.

Authority

In Catholic teaching, an ecumenical council together with the pope exercises supreme authority in matters of faith and morals, and its doctrinal definitions are held to be infallible. In Orthodox teaching, a council's authority rests on its reception by the whole Church. Protestant traditions generally accept the doctrinal conclusions of the early councils in so far as they accord with Scripture.
