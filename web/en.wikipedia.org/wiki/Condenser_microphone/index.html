A condenser microphone, also called a capacitor microphone, is a microphone whose transducer operates on the principle of a capacitor. The diaphragm acts as one plate of a capacitor, and sound vibrations produce changes in the distance between the plates, hence changes in capacitance, which are converted into an electrical signal. Condensers are the standard choice for studio recording and for measurement microphones because of their wide, flat frequency response and sensitivity.

Operation

A thin, electrically conductive diaphragm is suspended close to a rigid backplate. A polarising voltage charges the capsule; when sound moves the diaphragm, the varying capacitance causes a corresponding variation in voltage across a very high-value resistor. Because the capsule's output impedance is extremely high, a buffer amplifier (historically a vacuum tube, now a field-effect transistor) sits immediately behind the capsule inside the microphone body.

Studio condensers are usually powered by 48-volt phantom power delivered over the microphone cable. Electret condenser microphones instead use a permanently charged material, so they need only a small voltage for the buffer amplifier; electrets are used in telephones, headsets, lavalier microphones, and most consumer devices, including miniature MEMS microphones.

History

The condenser microphone was invented at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1916 by E. C. Wente, initially for telephony research and later for early sound film and broadcast. German firms such as Neumann (founded 1928) developed celebrated studio models — the U 47 (1949) and U 87 (1967) — whose sound remains a benchmark in recording studios.

Characteristics

Condenser microphones generally have higher sensitivity and wider frequency response than dynamic microphones, and are made in small- and large-diaphragm versions and in directional patterns from omnidirectional through cardioid to figure-of-eight, often switchable on a single microphone. Their disadvantages include the need for powering, greater fragility, susceptibility to moisture, and, for large-diaphragm models, higher cost.
