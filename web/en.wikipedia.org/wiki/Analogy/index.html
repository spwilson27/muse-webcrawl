Analogy

Analogy maps a system of relations from a familiar source domain onto a less familiar target.
Analogy
DisciplinesCognitive science, philosophy, linguistics, law
Related conceptsMetaphor, simile, proportion, case-based reasoning

Analogy is a comparison between two things, typically for the purpose of explanation or clarification, in which a feature or system of relations in a familiar source is mapped onto a less familiar target. In a narrower classical sense, analogy is a similarity of relations rather than of surface features, expressed as a proportion: A is to B as C is to D. Reasoning by analogy — inferring that because two things agree in some respects they probably agree in another — is a fundamental cognitive process, central to learning, problem solving, science, and legal argument, though its conclusions are always defeasible.

Cognitive science

Research in cognitive science, notably Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory, holds that analogy works by aligning relational structures between domains while discarding most object attributes. Children gradually shift from noticing surface similarity to relational similarity, a change called the relational shift. Analogical reasoning underlies case-based reasoning in artificial intelligence, in which new problems are solved by adapting solutions to similar past cases.

Science and philosophy

Famous scientific analogies include the comparison of the atom to a solar system, of electric current to flowing water, and of natural selection to artificial selection in Darwin's On the Origin of Species. Philosophers such as Mary Hesse distinguished positive, negative, and neutral analogies between a model and its target. In theology, analogical predication — Aquinas's analogia entis — concerns how words can be used of God neither univocally nor equivocally but analogically.

Law

Analogical reasoning is the backbone of common-law precedent: judges decide new cases by analogy with earlier decided cases, distinguishing unfavourable precedents and following favourable ones. Civil-law systems also use reasoning by analogy (analogia legis) to fill gaps in statutes where permitted.

Linguistics

In linguistics, analogy is a process of language change in which irregular forms are regularised on the pattern of more common ones — for example, the spread of regular plural or past-tense endings at the expense of older irregular forms.
