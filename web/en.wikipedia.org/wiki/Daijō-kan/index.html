Daijō-kan

The Daijō-kan (Japanese: 太政官), or Great Council of State, was the highest organ of Japan's premodern imperial government under the Ritsuryō legal system. Created as the codes were implemented in the late 7th and early 8th centuries, it headed a bureaucracy of eight ministries and supervised the administration of the provinces in the emperor's name.

Structure

The council was presided over by the Daijō-daijin (Chancellor of the Realm), a post often left vacant or filled honorarily. Effective leadership fell to the Sadaijin (Minister of the Left) and Udaijin (Minister of the Right), assisted by the Naidaijin and the major and middle counsellors (Dainagon and Chūnagon), who reviewed memorials and drafted imperial edicts. Below the council stood the eight ministries (shō) — among them Central Affairs, Ceremonial, Civil Affairs, Popular Affairs, War, Justice, Treasury and the Imperial Household — along with agencies such as the Jingikan (Department of Divinities), which in protocol ranked alongside the Daijō-kan.

History

As the Fujiwara regents and later cloistered emperors and the shogunates drew real power away from the court, the Daijō-kan became increasingly ceremonial, though its titles remained prized court honours. The institution was briefly revived after the Meiji Restoration of 1868 as the name of the new central government, before being replaced by the cabinet system in 1885.
