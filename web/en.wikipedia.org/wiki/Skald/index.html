A skald, or skáld (Old Norse: [ˈskɔːld]; Icelandic: [ˈskault]), is one of the often named poets who composed skaldic poetry, one of the two kinds of Old Norse poetry, the other being eddic verse. Skalds were court poets attached to Scandinavian and Icelandic kings and earls from the Viking Age into the Middle Ages; their poems praised rulers' deeds, commemorated battles, and preserved genealogies and mythology.

Skaldic verse

Skaldic poetry is distinguished by its extremely intricate metres — most famously dróttkvætt ("court metre"), with strict syllable counts, internal rhyme and alliteration — and by its heavy use of kennings, compressed metaphorical circumlocutions such as "whale-road" for the sea. Poems were orally composed and performed, then preserved in sagas and in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda, which used skaldic verse to teach the art.

Notable skalds

  • Bragi Boddason, often regarded as the first skald (9th century)
  • Þorbjörn Hornklofi and Egill Skallagrímsson
  • Eyvindr skáldaspillir, composer of Hákonarmál
  • Sigvatr Þórðarson, court poet of Olaf II of Norway
  • Snorri Sturluson, whose Háttatal exemplifies the metres

More than 5,000 skaldic verses survive, attributed to some 300 named skalds.
