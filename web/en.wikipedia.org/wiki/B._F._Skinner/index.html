B. F. Skinner

Burrhus Frederic Skinner (March 20, 1904 – August 18, 1990) was an American psychologist, behaviorist, author, inventor, and social philosopher. A professor of psychology at Harvard University from 1958 until his retirement in 1974, he pioneered the experimental analysis of behavior and founded radical behaviorism, a philosophy of science that elaborates on operant conditioning.

Biography

Skinner grew up in Susquehanna, Pennsylvania, studied English literature at Hamilton College, and after a failed attempt at writing fiction turned to psychology at Harvard, earning his PhD in 1931. He taught at the University of Minnesota and Indiana University before returning to Harvard in 1948. His daughter Julie Vargas and Deborah Buzan are known in connection with his controversial "baby tender" or air crib, often misreported as an experiment on his children.

Operant conditioning

Unlike the classical conditioning described by Ivan Pavlov, operant conditioning explains learning through consequences: reinforcement increases behavior and punishment decreases it. Skinner invented the operant chamber, popularly the Skinner box, and the cumulative recorder to study rates of responding in rats and pigeons. He developed schedules of reinforcement, shaping, and programmed instruction, and applied the analysis in The Behavior of Organisms (1938) and Science and Human Behavior (1953). See also behaviorism and John B. Watson.

Major works

His utopian novel Walden Two (1948) imagined a community designed on behavioral principles. Verbal Behavior (1957) extended his account to language and was famously criticized by Noam Chomsky. Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971) argued that belief in autonomous free will hindered a technology of behavior for social problems.

Legacy

Skinner was ranked among the most influential psychologists of the 20th century. His work underlies applied behavior analysis, behavior therapy, and teaching machines that anticipated computer-based instruction, while drawing criticism for determinism and for neglecting internal mental states addressed by cognitive psychology.
