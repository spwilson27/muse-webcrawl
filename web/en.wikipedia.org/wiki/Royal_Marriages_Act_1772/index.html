The Royal Marriages Act 1772 (12 Geo. 3. c. 11) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that required members of the British royal family to obtain the prior consent of the sovereign before marrying, on pain of the marriage being declared void.

Provisions and origins

George III promoted the Act after learning that two of his brothers had married without his approval: Prince Henry, Duke of Cumberland, married the commoner Anne Horton, and Prince William Henry, Duke of Gloucester, had secretly married Maria, the illegitimate daughter of Sir Edward Walpole. The Act applied to all descendants of George II (except the issue of princesses who married into foreign families). A descendant over 25 could marry without consent after giving twelve months' notice to the Privy Council, unless Parliament expressly objected. A marriage contracted without consent was null and void, and anyone officiating at or attending it committed praemunire.

Effects and repeal

The Act caught many later unions, most famously the morganatic-style secret marriage of the Prince Regent to Maria Fitzherbert (1785), which was legally void, and it affected the marriage validity of several Hanoverian and Cambridge princes. Because it applied to thousands of distant descendants of George II, it was long criticised as unworkable. The Perth Agreement of 2011 among Commonwealth realms led to the Succession to the Crown Act 2013, which repealed the 1772 Act and substituted a consent requirement for only the first six persons in the line of succession; the changes took effect on 26 March 2015, retrospectively validating marriages previously void under the Act where the parties were outside the first six in line.
