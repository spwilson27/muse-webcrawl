The Texas Longhorn is an American breed of beef cattle known for its exceptionally long horns, which can span more than two metres tip to tip. It descends from Spanish cattle brought to the Americas from the late 15th century onward, which escaped or were released, became feral in the brush country of Texas and northern Mexico, and over centuries adapted to drought, heat, and poor forage.

History

After the American Civil War, vast herds of wild longhorns — estimated in the millions — were gathered and driven north on trails such as the Chisholm Trail to railheads in Kansas, supplying beef to eastern markets and seeding the ranching industry of the Great Plains. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries the breed was nearly displaced by faster-fattening British breeds and by crossbreeding, until conservation efforts began in 1927, when the U.S. government established a protected herd in the Wichita Mountains of Oklahoma. Texas also maintained a state herd, and the breed recovered as a recognised symbol of Texas heritage.

Characteristics

Longhorns display almost every cattle colour and pattern, have modest birth weights and easy calving, strong maternal instincts, and meat that is notably lean. Their horns — long, often twisting upward — evolved in part for defence against predators in open range conditions. The breed is the official large mammal symbol of Texas, the namesake of the University of Texas at Austin's Texas Longhorns athletic teams, and the ancestor or close relative of other Criollo-derived breeds such as the Florida Cracker and Pineywoods cattle.
