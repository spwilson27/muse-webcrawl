Stadion (unit)

The stadion (plural stadia, Greek στάδιον), also anglicised as stade, was an ancient Greek unit of length, equal to 600 Greek feet (podes). Since the length of the foot varied by city, the stadion also varied: the Attic stadion was about 185 metres, while the Olympic stadion — the length of the footrace course at Olympia — was about 192.3 metres. The unit was used throughout the Hellenistic and Roman worlds for measuring distances.

Eratosthenes and the Earth

The stadion is famous through Eratosthenes' measurement of the Earth's circumference (3rd century BC): knowing the distance between Alexandria and Syene to be 5,000 stadia and the difference in solar angle to be 1/50 of a circle, he computed 250,000 stadia (later adjusted to 252,000). Depending on which stadion he used, his result was accurate to within roughly 1–15% of the true value — a celebrated achievement of ancient science. Posidonius later repeated the exercise with a value that Ptolemy adopted, giving a smaller Earth that may have encouraged Columbus.

Usage

Stadia were the standard unit for itinerary distances in authors such as Herodotus, Strabo and Ptolemy. The word also gave its name to the stadium, the running track itself. In English translations of the New Testament the unit appears as the "furlong" (one-eighth of a Roman mile), as in the distance from Jerusalem to Emmaus given as 60 stadia in Luke's Gospel.
