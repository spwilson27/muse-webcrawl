Rondeau (forme fixe)


Origin: Medieval France, 13th–15th centuries
Form: Two rhymes, refrain structure ABaAabAB
Leading composer: Guillaume de Machaut

The rondeau was a French poetic and musical form of the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries, one of the three formes fixes together with the ballade and the virelai. It was primarily a song form used by the trouvères and by the courtly composers of the ars nova and ars subtilior, setting short lyric poems — usually about love — to music in which a refrain alternates with verses that share its music and rhymes.

Form

In its classic eight-line musical form (the rondeau prime), the poem is built on two rhyme sounds and two musical phrases: the refrain sets the opening lines (AB), a short verse uses the first musical phrase (a), the refrain's first half returns (A), a longer verse uses both phrases (ab), and the full refrain closes the piece (AB) — giving the scheme ABaAabAB. The most prolific and influential composer of the form was Guillaume de Machaut (c. 1300–1377), who set 21 rondeaux; later masters included Guillaume Du Fay and Gilles Binchois in the fifteenth century.

Later development

As a purely literary form the rondeau survived in French poetry, its refrain lines repeated in whole or part. The English-language rondeau of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries (thirteen to fifteen lines, with the opening phrase of the first line as a short refrain) is a later development from the related rondel; John McCrae's "In Flanders Fields" (1915) is the most famous example, though its structure differs from the medieval song form. The musical rondeau should also be distinguished from the Baroque and Classical rondo, though the names share the root rond, "round".
