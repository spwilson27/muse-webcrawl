Snowdon, known in Welsh as Yr Wyddfa (pronounced [ər ˈʊɨ̞ðva]), is a mountain in north-west Wales. With a summit elevation of 1,085 metres (3,560 ft) above sea level, it is the highest mountain in Wales and the highest in the British Isles south of the Scottish Highlands. Snowdon lies within Snowdonia National Park (Parc Cenedlaethol Eryri) and has been designated a national nature reserve for its rare flora and fauna.

Geology and landscape

The rocks of Snowdon were formed by volcanism in the Ordovician period and were later sculpted by glaciation into sharp ridges (arêtes) and pyramidal peaks. The mountain's cwms (cirques) hold glacial lakes such as Llyn Llydaw and Glaslyn. The summit can be reached by several well-known paths, including the Llanberis Path, the Pyg Track, the Miners' Track, and the more demanding Watkin Path; the Crib Goch ridge offers a knife-edge scramble for experienced walkers.

Snowdon Mountain Railway

Since 1896, the Snowdon Mountain Railway, a rack railway, has carried visitors from Llanberis to the summit station. The summit building, Hafod Eryri, opened in 2009, replacing a 1930s structure designed by Clough Williams-Ellis. On clear days the views can extend to Ireland, the Isle of Man, and much of northern England.

Environment and culture

Snowdon supports arctic-alpine plants including the Snowdon lily (Gagea serotina). The mountain figures in Welsh mythology — legend holds that the giant Rhita Gawr is buried on the summit, giving Yr Wyddfa ("the burial mound") its name. In 2022 the Snowdonia National Park Authority voted to use the Welsh names Yr Wyddfa and Eryri in preference to Snowdon and Snowdonia. The mountain is also the busiest in the United Kingdom, attracting hundreds of thousands of walkers annually, and forms one leg of the National Three Peaks Challenge.
