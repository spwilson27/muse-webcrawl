Arctic National Wildlife Refuge

The coastal plain of the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge, with the Brooks Range beyond.

The Arctic National Wildlife Refuge (ANWR, pronounced "an-war") is a national wildlife refuge in northeastern Alaska, United States, administered by the United States Fish and Wildlife Service. Covering about 19.3 million acres (78,000 km²) — an area roughly the size of South Carolina — it is the largest national wildlife refuge in the country. The refuge spans the Brooks Range, boreal forest, tundra coastal plain and Arctic coastline, and supports caribou, polar bears, grizzly bears, musk oxen, and millions of migratory birds.

History and protection

The area was first protected in 1960 as the Arctic National Wildlife Range under President Dwight D. Eisenhower, following a campaign by conservationists including Olaus and Margaret Murie. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 renamed it a refuge, enlarged it, and designated most of it as wilderness, while leaving the 1.5-million-acre coastal plain (the "1002 area") undecided pending study of its oil and gas potential and wildlife values.

Drilling controversy

For decades the refuge has been the central symbol of the American debate over oil drilling in protected lands. The coastal plain is the calving ground of the Porcupine caribou herd and is considered sacred by the Gwich'in people, while some Iñupiat communities support development for its economic benefits. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 mandated an oil and gas leasing program in the 1002 area; a first lease sale was held in January 2021, attracting little industry interest, and the resulting leases were cancelled by the Biden administration in 2023, keeping the issue politically contested.
