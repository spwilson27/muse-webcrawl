Seven deadly sins

The seven deadly sins in allegory

The seven deadly sins, also known as the capital vices or cardinal sins, are a classification of vices in Christian teaching. They are called "deadly" or "capital" (from Latin caput, "head") because they are held to be the sources from which other sins arise. In the standard list settled by Pope Gregory I and systematised by Thomas Aquinas, they are: pride, greed, wrath, envy, lust, gluttony, and sloth.

History

The scheme originates with the 4th-century desert monk Evagrius Ponticus, who listed eight "evil thoughts" (including vainglory and sadness); his pupil John Cassian brought the list west. Gregory the Great (c. 590) revised it into seven, treating pride as the root of all, and folding sadness into sloth (acedia) and vainglory into pride. In the medieval period the sins became a central framework for preaching, confession manuals, and penance, and were memorised by the mnemonic SALIGIA formed from their Latin initials. Aquinas treated them in the Summa Theologica, preferring "capital vices" as the philosophically precise term.

In literature and art

The sins structure Dante's Purgatorio, whose terraces are ordered by disordered love, and animate Chaucer's Parson's Tale, the morality play tradition, Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, and countless visual cycles from cathedral sculpture to Hieronymus Bosch's tabletop of the Seven Deadly Sins. In modern culture they appear in works from the silent film era to the 1995 thriller Se7en, in which a murderer kills according to the list. Catholic doctrine distinguishes these dispositions from "mortal sins" proper: a sin is mortal when it concerns grave matter, committed with full knowledge and deliberate consent.
