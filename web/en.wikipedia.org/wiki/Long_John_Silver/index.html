Long John Silver is a fictional character and the main antagonist in the 1883 novel Treasure Island by Robert Louis Stevenson. Silver is a cunning and opportunistic pirate who was quartermaster under the notorious Captain Flint and who serves as ship's cook aboard the Hispaniola during the voyage to recover Flint's buried treasure.

Character

Silver is a tall, strong man with a crutch, having lost one leg — he claims in the service of his country, though he actually lost it in pirate service under Flint. He keeps a parrot named Captain Flint, after his former captain, which cries "Pieces of eight!" Silver is married to a woman of African descent, who manages his tavern, the Spy-glass, in Bristol in his absence.

Stevenson describes Silver as complex and shifting: he befriends the cabin boy Jim Hawkins, who comes to regard him almost as a father figure, yet he is also the leader of the mutinous pirates plotting to seize the treasure and murder the ship's officers. His ability to switch allegiances and survive every turn of fortune is central to the novel. At the story's end, Silver escapes with a portion of the treasure, and Jim reflects that he is glad to be rid of him while hoping he fares well.

Inspiration

Stevenson partly based Silver's physical appearance on his friend William Ernest Henley, a poet who had lost a leg to tuberculosis of the bone and walked with a crutch. Stevenson acknowledged Henley's "maimed strength and masterfulness" as an inspiration for the character.

In popular culture

Long John Silver has become the archetypal image of the pirate in popular culture: the wooden leg (or crutch), the parrot on the shoulder, and the West Country "arr" speech all derive largely from portrayals of Silver. He has been played on screen by actors including Wallace Beery, Orson Welles, and Robert Newton, whose exaggerated performance in the 1950 Disney film Treasure Island popularised the stereotypical pirate accent. The American seafood restaurant chain Long John Silver's is named after the character.
