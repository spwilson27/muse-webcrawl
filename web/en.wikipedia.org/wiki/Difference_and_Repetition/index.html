Difference and Repetition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Difference and Repetition (French: Différence et répétition) is a 1968 book by the French philosopher Gilles Deleuze. Originally published as his principal doctoral thesis, it is one of the foundational texts of his philosophy. In it, Deleuze argues that difference should not be subordinated to identity, and develops a concept of repetition that is not the repetition of the same but the production of the new. The book has been influential in post-structuralism, literary theory, and contemporary metaphysics.

Summary

Deleuze's central complaint is that western philosophy, from Plato through Hegel, has treated difference as secondary to identity: things are said to differ only insofar as they first share a concept, a genus, or a self-identical being. Deleuze inverts this hierarchy. Difference, he argues, is primary; identity is an effect produced by deeper processes of differentiation. Drawing on thinkers including Duns Scotus, Spinoza, Nietzsche, and Bergson, he develops a univocal ontology in which being is said in a single sense of everything that is, while differing in itself.

The title's second term is equally radical. Repetition, for Deleuze, is not generality — the recurrence of instances under a law — but the return of difference itself. Following Kierkegaard and Nietzsche's doctrine of the eternal return, Deleuze treats repetition as the affirmation of difference: what returns is never the same but always the different. The book also introduces an account of Ideas as differential structures, a critique of the "image of thought" that philosophy presupposes, and a theory of the syntheses of time: habit, memory, and the eternal return.

Structure

The work comprises an introduction and five chapters: "Difference in Itself", "Repetition for Itself", "The Image of Thought", "Ideas and the Synthesis of Difference", and "Asymmetrical Synthesis of the Sensible", followed by a conclusion. Its companion volume, The Logic of Sense (1969), was Deleuze's secondary doctoral thesis.

Reception and influence

Though difficult, the book established Deleuze's reputation as a major philosopher. Michel Foucault famously predicted that "one day this century will be Deleuzian." Difference and Repetition has shaped fields from film studies to political theory, and its concepts of univocity, virtuality, and repetition have been taken up by later thinkers such as Alain Badiou and Manuel DeLanda. The English translation by Paul Patton appeared in 1994.
