Cardiopulmonary bypass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a technique in which a machine temporarily takes over the function of the heart and lungs during surgery, maintaining the circulation of blood and the oxygen content of the body. The CPB machine is commonly referred to as a heart–lung machine, and its operation is the responsibility of a specialist perfusionist. It allows the surgeon to operate on a still, bloodless heart, which makes most modern open-heart surgery possible.

How it works

Venous blood is drained by gravity or vacuum assistance from cannulas in the right atrium or venae cavae into a reservoir, passed through a membrane oxygenator (the artificial "lung") where oxygen is added and carbon dioxide removed, through a heat exchanger that can cool or warm the patient, and then pumped back into the arterial circulation, usually via the ascending aorta. The patient's blood is anticoagulated with heparin to prevent clotting in the circuit, and reversed with protamine at the end. The heart itself is usually arrested with cold potassium-rich cardioplegia solution and the body often cooled (hypothermia) to reduce metabolic demand.

History and uses

John Gibbon performed the first successful open-heart operation using a heart–lung machine in 1953, closing an atrial septal defect; earlier attempts by Clarence Dennis and others had failed. CPB is now used for coronary artery bypass grafting, heart valve repair and replacement, repair of congenital heart defects, heart and lung transplantation, aortic surgery, and removal of intracardiac tumours. Related technology underlies ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) used for prolonged support in intensive care.

Complications

Contact of blood with the artificial circuit triggers a systemic inflammatory response; other complications include stroke and subtle cognitive decline (historically dubbed "pump head"), bleeding, kidney injury, haemolysis and air or particulate embolism. Off-pump coronary surgery and miniaturised circuits were developed partly to mitigate these risks.
