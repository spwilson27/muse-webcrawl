Behçet's disease

Behçet's disease, also called Behçet's syndrome, is a rare chronic inflammatory disorder of unknown cause whose primary manifestation is vasculitis — inflammation of blood vessels throughout the body. It is characterised by recurrent painful mouth ulcers, genital ulcers, eye inflammation (uveitis), and skin lesions. The disease is named after Hulusi Behçet, the Turkish dermatologist who first described the characteristic triple-symptom complex in 1937.

Signs and symptoms

Nearly all patients develop painful aphthous ulcers of the mouth, which may precede other symptoms by years. Genital ulcers occur in roughly three quarters of patients and can scar. Eye involvement, typically a relapsing uveitis affecting both the front and back of the eye, can lead to blindness if untreated. Skin findings include erythema nodosum-like lesions and acneiform eruptions. Less commonly, the disease involves joints, the gastrointestinal tract, the nervous system (neuro-Behçet's), and large blood vessels, where aneurysms and thrombosis can be life-threatening.

Causes and epidemiology

The cause is not fully understood; genetic susceptibility (notably the HLA-B51 allele) combined with environmental or microbial triggers is thought to provoke an aberrant immune response. The disease is most prevalent along the ancient Silk Road, from the Mediterranean basin through the Middle East to East Asia, and is particularly common in Turkey. It usually begins in the third or fourth decade of life.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on criteria such as recurrent oral ulceration plus any two of genital ulceration, eye lesions, skin lesions, or a positive pathergy test. There is no cure; treatment aims to suppress inflammation and prevent organ damage. Colchicine is used for mucocutaneous symptoms, while severe ocular or systemic disease is treated with immunosuppressants such as azathioprine, corticosteroids, and biologic agents including anti-TNF drugs such as infliximab.
