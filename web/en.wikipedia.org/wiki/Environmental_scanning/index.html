Environmental scanning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Environmental scanning is the systematic process by which an organization gathers, analyzes, and uses information about its external environment — political, economic, social, technological, legal, and ecological developments — in order to identify opportunities and threats and to inform strategic planning. The concept was introduced by Francis J. Aguilar in his 1967 book Scanning the Business Environment, and it has since become a standard component of strategic management and competitive intelligence.

Environmental scanning monitors the external environment for emerging trends, opportunities, and threats.

Modes of scanning

Aguilar described four modes of gathering external information:

  • Undirected viewing — general exposure to information with no specific purpose in mind, relying on diverse sources.
  • Conditioned viewing — directed exposure to a particular area or type of information, assessing its significance without active searching.
  • Informal search — a relatively limited, unstructured effort to obtain specific information.
  • Formal search — a deliberate, systematic effort, often a planned research project, to obtain information for a specific issue.

Frameworks

Scanning is commonly organized using frameworks that partition the macro-environment. The best known is PEST analysis (political, economic, social, technological), extended in variants such as PESTLE (adding legal and environmental factors) and STEEPLE. At the industry level, scanning complements tools such as Porter's five forces and feeds directly into the opportunities-and-threats half of a SWOT analysis.

Process and practice

In practice, environmental scanning ranges from informal monitoring by senior managers — reading trade press, attending conferences, talking to customers — to dedicated horizon-scanning units in large corporations and governments. Effective scanning emphasizes the detection of weak signals: early, fragmentary indications of emerging issues that may later become strategically important. Futurists and foresight practitioners extend scanning into scenario planning, constructing alternative futures from the trends and uncertainties the scan reveals. Critics note that scanning can degenerate into information overload, and that organizations frequently collect far more environmental data than they actually use in decisions.
