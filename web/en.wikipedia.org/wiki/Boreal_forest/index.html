The boreal forest, also known as the taiga, is a biome characterised by coniferous forests consisting mostly of pines, spruces and larches. It forms a circumpolar band across the high northern latitudes of North America and Eurasia, lying between the tundra to the north and temperate forest or grassland to the south. It is the world's largest land biome, covering roughly 17 million square kilometres, most of it in Russia and Canada.

Climate

The boreal forest experiences a subarctic climate with very large seasonal temperature ranges. Summers are short, mild and wet, with between one and three months averaging above 10 °C; winters are long, severe and dry, with mean temperatures below freezing for six or more months. Permafrost is widespread in the northern parts of the zone, and the landscape is dotted with lakes, bogs and wetlands left by Pleistocene glaciation.

Flora

A small number of conifer genera dominate: spruce (Picea), pine (Pinus), fir (Abies) and the deciduous conifer larch (Larix), which covers vast areas of Siberia. Broadleaf species such as birch, aspen, poplar and alder occur on warmer or disturbed sites. The forest floor is typically shaded and slow to decompose, producing acidic, nutrient-poor podzol soils.

Fauna

Characteristic animals include moose, woodland caribou, lynx, wolf, brown bear, wolverine, snowshoe hare and beaver, together with huge summer populations of migratory songbirds and waterfowl that breed in the wetlands. Insect outbreaks and wildfire are major natural disturbances.

Ecology and carbon

Boreal forests and their soils store an enormous quantity of carbon — by some estimates more than tropical forests — largely in peatlands and permafrost soils. Climate warming threatens to release this carbon through thawing, drying and increased fire frequency, making the biome a key element in global climate feedbacks.
