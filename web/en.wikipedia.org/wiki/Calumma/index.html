Calumma

Calumma parsonii, the largest member of the genus.

Calumma is a genus of chameleons endemic to Madagascar. The genus contains some of the largest chameleons in the world, including Parson's chameleon (Calumma parsonii), which can exceed 60 cm in length, as well as many smaller forest species. Roughly 40 species are recognised, most of them inhabitants of the humid eastern rainforests, though some occur in drier western and northern regions and on offshore islands such as Nosy Be.

Characteristics

Members of Calumma typically have laterally compressed bodies, casques on the head, and, in males of many species, distinctive rostral (nose) appendages used in display and species recognition. Like other chameleons they possess zygodactylous feet, independently mobile eyes and long, projectile tongues for capturing insect prey. Colour change is well developed and used for camouflage and communication.

Habitat and conservation

Most species are arboreal specialists of primary forest and are highly sensitive to habitat loss. Deforestation for agriculture and logging has placed many Calumma species under threat; several are listed as Endangered or Critically Endangered by the IUCN, and the genus is included in CITES Appendix II, restricting international trade.
