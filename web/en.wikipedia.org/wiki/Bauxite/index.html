Bauxite is a sedimentary rock with a relatively high aluminium content; it is the world's main source of aluminium and gallium. It consists mainly of the aluminium minerals gibbsite, boehmite and diaspore, mixed with iron oxides (which give much bauxite its reddish-brown colour), the clay mineral kaolinite and small amounts of anatase and rutile. Bauxite was named after the village of Les Baux-de-Provence in southern France, where it was recognised in 1821 by geologist Pierre Berthier.

Formation

Bauxite forms by intensive lateritic weathering of aluminosilicate rocks in tropical and subtropical climates: rain leaches silica and soluble elements away, leaving a residue concentrated in aluminium and iron hydroxides. Most deposits occur as shallow, near-surface layers that can be mined by open-pit methods, often with topsoil and overburden replaced during rehabilitation.

Production and processing

Australia is the largest producer, followed by Guinea (which holds the largest reserves) and China. Almost all mined bauxite is refined into alumina (aluminium oxide) by the Bayer process, in which hot caustic soda dissolves the aluminium minerals; most alumina is then smelted into aluminium metal by the Hall–Héroult process. A residue of the Bayer process, alkaline "red mud", is a major waste-management challenge. Smaller quantities of bauxite are used directly in cements, abrasives and refractories.
