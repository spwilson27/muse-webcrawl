Radiocarbon dating

Radiocarbon dating (also carbon-14 dating) is a method for determining the age of organic material, developed by Willard Libby at the University of Chicago in the late 1940s, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960.

Living organisms exchange carbon with the atmosphere, maintaining a steady proportion of radioactive carbon-14. When an organism dies, intake stops and the carbon-14 decays with a half-life of about 5,730 years; measuring what fraction remains dates the death, reliably back to roughly 50,000–60,000 years. Accelerator mass spectrometry now counts carbon-14 atoms directly, needing only milligrams of sample. Calibration against tree rings corrects for past fluctuations in atmospheric carbon-14; contamination and reservoir effects are known pitfalls.

Applications

Radiocarbon dating transformed archaeology and Quaternary science, dating everything from the Dead Sea Scrolls and Ötzi the Iceman to the spread of farming. Libby's early demonstration samples included wood from Egyptian tombs of known historical age.
