Mammoth Cave National Park

Mammoth Cave National Park is a national park in south-central Kentucky, United States, encompassing portions of Mammoth Cave, the longest known cave system in the world. Since surveys connected the Flint Ridge cave system in 1972, more than 420 miles (676 km) of surveyed passageways have been documented — nearly twice the length of the next longest known cave, Mexico's underwater Sac Actun system.

Geology

The cave formed in thick Mississippian limestone strata capped by a layer of sandstone, which protects the passages from rapid erosion. Water from the Green River and surface streams dissolved the limestone over millions of years, producing vast horizontal passages, vertical shafts, stalactites, stalagmites and gypsum formations.

History

Indigenous peoples mined minerals in the cave for thousands of years; several mummified remains have been recovered. According to legend, the cave was rediscovered by settlers in 1797 when John Houchin chased a wounded bear to its entrance. During the War of 1812 the cave was mined for saltpeter used in gunpowder. In the 19th century the enslaved guide Stephen Bishop explored and mapped much of the cave, crossing the Bottomless Pit and discovering many of its famous features. In 1843 a tuberculosis hospital experiment was briefly conducted inside the cave by Dr John Croghan.

The park was authorised in 1926, assembled through private purchases and eminent domain, and dedicated in 1941. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1981 and an International Biosphere Reserve in 1990.

Ecology and tours

The cave ecosystem hosts cave-adapted species including eyeless fish, cave shrimp and several bat species threatened by white-nose syndrome. Ranger-led tours range from short lantern tours of the historic section to strenuous crawling tours. Above ground, the park protects hardwood forest and the Green River corridor.
