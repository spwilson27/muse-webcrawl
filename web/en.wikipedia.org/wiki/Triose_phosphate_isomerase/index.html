Triose phosphate isomerase

Triose phosphate isomerase (TIM, also written triosephosphate isomerase, EC 5.3.1.1) is an enzyme that catalyzes the reversible interconversion of the triose phosphate isomers dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and D-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GAP). It plays a central role in glycolysis and is famous in enzymology as a "catalytically perfect" enzyme: its reaction rate is limited only by how fast substrate can diffuse to it.

Function in metabolism

In the fourth step of glycolysis, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is split into DHAP and GAP, but only GAP proceeds directly through the pathway. TIM rapidly converts DHAP into GAP, so both halves of the sugar yield product. The enzyme also functions in gluconeogenesis, the Calvin cycle, and glycerol metabolism.

Structure and mechanism

TIM is a homodimer whose each subunit folds into the classic TIM barrel (an α/β barrel of eight parallel β-strands surrounded by eight α-helices), the first such fold discovered and one of the most common enzyme architectures. Catalysis proceeds through an enediol intermediate using a glutamate base and a histidine residue in the active site. Inherited deficiency of TIM in humans is a rare but severe multisystem disorder involving hemolytic anemia and progressive neurological dysfunction.
