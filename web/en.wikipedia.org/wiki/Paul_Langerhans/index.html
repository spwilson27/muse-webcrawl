Paul Wilhelm Heinrich Langerhans (25 July 1847 – 20 July 1888) was a German pathologist, physiologist and biologist, credited with the discovery of the cells that secrete insulin, named after him as the islets of Langerhans, and of the dendritic skin cells known as Langerhans cells.

Early life and education

Langerhans was born in Berlin, the son of a prominent physician and political figure. He studied medicine at the University of Jena and in Berlin, where he worked under the pathologist Rudolf Virchow and the physiologist Julius Cohnheim. While still a student, using gold chloride staining techniques, he described the star-shaped dendritic cells of the epidermis that now bear his name, initially interpreting them as nerve cells.

Discovery of the pancreatic islets

In his doctoral thesis of 1869, Beiträge zur mikroskopischen Anatomie der Bauchspeicheldrüse ("Contributions to the microscopic anatomy of the pancreas"), Langerhans described small, sharply demarcated clusters of cells scattered through the pancreas, distinct from its enzyme-secreting acinar tissue. He frankly admitted that he did not know their function. Decades later, after diabetes mellitus was linked to the pancreas, Édouard Laguesse proposed in 1893 that these "îlots de Langerhans" produced an internal secretion regulating blood sugar — the hormone insulin, isolated in 1921–22.

Later life

Langerhans became a professor of pathological anatomy at the University of Freiburg, but tuberculosis forced him to abandon his academic career in 1874. Seeking a milder climate, he settled on the island of Madeira, where he practised medicine, studied marine zoology — describing numerous worm species — and wrote a handbook for travellers to the island. He died at Funchal on 20 July 1888, shortly before his 41st birthday, of kidney failure complicating his tuberculosis.

Eponyms
