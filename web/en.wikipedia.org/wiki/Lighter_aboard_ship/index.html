The lighter aboard ship (LASH) system is a method of cargo transport in which unpowered barges, called lighters, are loaded aboard a larger mother vessel for ocean transport and floated or lifted off at the destination, allowing cargo to reach shallow or unequipped ports and inland waterways without transshipment.

The system was developed in the 1960s by the American shipbuilding engineer Jerome L. Goldman. The first LASH vessel, Acadia Forest, entered service in 1969. The idea paralleled containerisation but aimed at ports where container terminals did not exist: the lighter itself acts as the unit load, and can be towed up rivers to the final destination.

Operation

A LASH ship carries scores of identical lighters in its holds and stacked on deck. At the stern, a heavy travelling gantry crane — on early ships capable of lifting over 500 tonnes — straddles the stern, picks the lighter from the water, travels forward along the ship and lowers it into a cell in the hold. Loading and unloading can proceed at anchor in an open roadstead, so the mother ship need not berth, and individual lighters can be dropped at successive ports or pushed upriver by tug while the ship sails on.

History

LASH vessels were built in the United States, Europe and Japan and used on routes to West Africa, South Asia, the Caribbean and the Mississippi river system. A related but distinct system, the Seabee (sea-barge) carrier, used a submersible stern elevator and larger barges. LASH shipping declined as container terminals spread worldwide and as barge-handling costs rose; by the 2000s only a handful of barge carriers remained in commercial or military sealift service.

Comparison with other systems

Unlike container ships, LASH vessels need no shore cranes but pay for it in complex shipboard gear and low space utilisation by container-ship standards. Unlike roll-on/roll-off ships, cargo stays sealed in the lighter from origin to final inland destination.
