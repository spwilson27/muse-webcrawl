French poetry is a central branch of French literature, with a recorded history from the medieval chansons de geste and troubadour lyric to the experiments of modernism. Classical French verse is built on syllabic metre — above all the twelve-syllable alexandrine — strict rhyme schemes, and fixed forms such as the ballade, rondeau and sonnet.

Medieval and Renaissance poetry

The earliest French poetry includes epic chansons de geste such as The Song of Roland (c. 1100) and the courtly lyrics of the troubadours and trouvères. In the 15th century François Villon gave the ballade a voice of unrivalled intensity and irony. The Renaissance Pléiade, led by Pierre de Ronsard and Joachim du Bellay — whose Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549) was a manifesto for French verse — naturalised the sonnet and the ode.

Classicism to Romanticism

Under the discipline of Malherbe and the classical rules codified by Boileau, the alexandrine became the vehicle of Racine's tragedies. Romanticism broke the mould: Lamartine's Méditations (1820), the lyric and epic verse of Victor Hugo, and the work of Alfred de Musset and Gérard de Nerval made poetry the era's dominant literary form.

Modern poetry

Charles Baudelaire's Les Fleurs du mal (1857) inaugurated modern poetry, mixing classical form with urban, decadent themes. The Symbolists — Verlaine, Rimbaud, Mallarmé — pursued musicality and suggestion; Rimbaud's visionary prose poems transformed the genre. In the 20th century Apollinaire's Calligrammes, the Surrealists (Breton, Éluard, Aragon), and later poets such as Saint-John Perse, René Char and Yves Bonnefoy kept French poetry at the avant-garde of European letters.
