Cooper pair

In condensed matter physics, a Cooper pair is a pair of electrons (or other fermions) bound together at low temperatures in a certain manner first described in 1956 by American physicist Leon Cooper. Cooper showed that an arbitrarily small attraction between electrons in a metal can cause a paired state of electrons to have lower energy than the Fermi energy, which implies that the pair is bound.

[Image: Schematic of a Cooper pair: two electrons weakly bound above the Fermi sea]
A Cooper pair consists of two electrons with opposite momenta and spins.

The Cooper problem

Cooper considered two electrons added to a filled Fermi sea in the presence of a weak attractive interaction, such as that arising from exchange of virtual phonons. He found that a bound state exists for any attraction, however weak — in sharp contrast to ordinary two-body quantum mechanics in three dimensions, where a minimum strength is required. This instability of the Fermi sea is the seed of BCS theory and of Superconductivity generally.

Properties

The two electrons in a pair have opposite momenta and (in conventional superconductors) opposite spins, forming a spin singlet with total spin zero. The pairs are large and heavily overlapping: a typical pair size (coherence length) is of the order of 100 nm, encompassing many thousands of other electrons. Because the pairs behave approximately as bosons, they can condense into a single macroscopic quantum state, which underlies zero electrical resistance and the Meissner effect.
