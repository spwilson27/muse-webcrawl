Claude Lévi-Strauss

Claude Lévi-Strauss (28 November 1908 – 30 October 2009) was a French anthropologist and ethnologist, the central founder of structural anthropology and one of the most influential thinkers of the 20th century.

Life

Born in Brussels to French Jewish parents and raised in Paris, Lévi-Strauss studied law and philosophy at the Sorbonne. In 1935 he went to Brazil as a professor at the University of São Paulo and conducted fieldwork among the Caduveo, Bororo, Nambikwara and Tupi-Kawahib peoples. After wartime exile in New York, he returned to France, took a chair at the Collège de France (1959–1982), and was elected to the Académie française in 1973.

Work

The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) treated marriage rules as systems of exchange. Tristes Tropiques (1955), part memoir and part ethnography, brought him wide fame. In The Savage Mind (1962) and the four-volume Mythologiques (1964–1971), he argued that myths across cultures share underlying structures — binary oppositions such as raw/cooked and nature/culture — revealing universal patterns of human thought.

Influence

Structuralism shaped linguistics, literary theory and philosophy, even as later critics charged it with neglecting history and human agency. Lévi-Strauss died in Paris a month before his 101st birthday.
