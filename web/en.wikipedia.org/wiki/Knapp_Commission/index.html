The Knapp Commission, officially the Commission to Investigate Alleged Police Corruption, was a five-member panel formed in May 1970 by New York City Mayor John V. Lindsay to investigate corruption within the New York City Police Department (NYPD). It was named after its chairman, federal judge Whitman Knapp.

Background

The commission was created after police officers Frank Serpico and David Durk, frustrated by the department's indifference to their reports of widespread graft, brought their evidence to the press. A New York Times investigation published in April 1970 forced the mayor to act.

Hearings and findings

The commission's public hearings in 1971, featuring testimony by Serpico and others, were televised and shocked the public. Its final report, issued in December 1972, found that corruption in the NYPD was widespread and systematic. It distinguished between "grass-eaters", officers who passively accepted small bribes offered to them, and "meat-eaters", who aggressively sought out corrupt payments. The report recommended stronger accountability of commanders, improved internal affairs investigation, and permanent external oversight mechanisms.

Legacy

The Knapp Commission reshaped public expectations of police oversight in New York and inspired similar inquiries, including the Mollen Commission of the 1990s. Serpico's story was later dramatised in the 1973 film Serpico.
