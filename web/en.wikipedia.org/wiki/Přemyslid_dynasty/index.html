The Přemyslid dynasty (Czech: Přemyslovci) was the royal dynasty that ruled Bohemia — and for periods Moravia, Poland, Hungary and Austria — from the 9th century until 1306. It took its name from the legendary Přemysl the Ploughman, whom the prophetess Libuše was said to have raised from the plough to marry her and found Prague; the first historically attested Přemyslid is Duke Bořivoj I, baptised in the 880s. For more than four centuries the dynasty built the Bohemian state, obtained a hereditary royal crown, and made Prague a major Central European capital, before the line ended with the murder of the young king Wenceslaus III in 1306.

Rise of the Bohemian state

From their stronghold at Prague Castle the Přemyslids subdued rival Slavic tribes of Bohemia. Under Boleslaus I and Boleslaus II the duchy extended over Moravia and parts of Silesia and Slovakia; the bishopric of Prague was founded in 973. Saint Wenceslaus (Václav), murdered by his brother Boleslaus in 935 (or 929), became Bohemia's patron saint. Břetislav I recovered Moravia definitively in the 11th century, and Vratislaus II received a personal royal crown from Emperor Henry IV in 1085.

The royal age

The crown became hereditary under Přemysl Ottokar I, confirmed by the Golden Bull of Sicily (1212), which also fixed Bohemia's privileged status within the Holy Roman Empire. His successors — Wenceslaus I, Přemysl Ottokar II and Wenceslaus II — presided over a silver-mining boom (Kutná Hora, the Prague groschen), German town settlement, and territorial expansion: Ottokar II ruled from the Baltic approaches to the Adriatic before falling at the Battle on the Marchfeld (1278) against Rudolf of Habsburg. Wenceslaus II added the Polish crown (1300) and placed his son on the Hungarian throne.

Extinction and legacy

Wenceslaus III, succeeding in 1305, was murdered at Olomouc on 4 August 1306 while preparing a campaign to defend his Polish crown; with him the male line ended after more than 400 years. The Bohemian crown passed, through election and marriage to Přemyslid princesses, to the House of Luxembourg (1310), under whom Charles IV made Prague an imperial capital. The Přemyslids remain foundational figures of Czech national identity, from the cult of St Wenceslaus to the Libuše legend retold in Smetana's Má vlast.
