Spiritual (music)

Spirituals are a genre of religious folk song created by enslaved African people in the United States. Rooted in African musical traditions, Protestant hymnody, and the experience of slavery, spirituals express Christian faith, sorrow, hope, and longing for freedom, and they form one of the largest and most significant bodies of American folk song.

Origins

Spirituals emerged in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in the slave communities of the American South, where enslaved people combined African practices — call-and-response singing, ring shouts, rhythmic hand-clapping — with the hymns and psalms they encountered in white churches and camp meetings. Songs such as "Steal Away", "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot", "Go Down Moses", and "Deep River" used biblical imagery, especially the Exodus story, that resonated with the singers' own bondage. Some spirituals are traditionally said to have carried coded references to escape via the Underground Railroad, though the extent of such coding is debated by historians.

From folk song to concert stage

After emancipation, the Fisk Jubilee Singers of Fisk University in Nashville brought arranged spirituals to concert audiences in the United States and Europe beginning in 1871, raising money for their university and preserving the repertoire in print. Composers such as Harry T. Burleigh later produced celebrated concert arrangements, and Antonín Dvořák famously drew on spiritual-style melodies in his "New World" Symphony, predicting that Black folk music would be the foundation of an American school of composition.

Legacy

Spirituals directly influenced gospel music, blues, jazz, and soul, and they played a central role in the civil rights movement, where adapted freedom songs were sung at mass meetings and marches. Performers including Marian Anderson, Paul Robeson, Mahalia Jackson, and Jessye Norman carried the concert spiritual around the world.
