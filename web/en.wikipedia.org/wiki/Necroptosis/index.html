Necroptosis

Necroptosis
TypeRegulated (programmed) cell death
Key moleculesRIPK1, RIPK3, MLKL
Coined2005 (Degterev et al.)

Necroptosis is a programmed form of necrosis, or inflammatory cell death. Conventionally, necrosis is associated with unprogrammed cell death resulting from cellular damage or infiltration by pathogens, in contrast to orderly, programmed cell death via apoptosis. The discovery of necroptosis showed that cells can execute necrosis in a programmed fashion and that apoptosis is not always the preferred form of cell death.

Mechanism

Necroptosis is typically triggered when death receptors such as TNFR1 are engaged while caspase-8 is inhibited or absent. The kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3 then form a complex called the necrosome; RIPK3 phosphorylates the pseudokinase MLKL, which oligomerizes, translocates to the plasma membrane and ruptures it, releasing intracellular contents and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that provoke inflammation.

Function and disease

Necroptosis is thought to serve as a backup defence against viruses that encode caspase inhibitors, and it contributes to pathology in ischemia-reperfusion injury, neurodegenerative disease, inflammatory bowel disease and other conditions. Inhibitors such as necrostatin-1 (targeting RIPK1) are research tools and drug candidates.
