Songhai Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Mud-brick scholarly quarter of Timbuktu at its Songhai-era height]

The Songhai Empire (c. 1464–1591) was the largest empire in West African history, centred on the Niger River bend with its capital at Gao. Built by Sonni Ali from the Songhai kingdom that succeeded Mali's eastern provinces, and expanded by Askia Muhammad I, it controlled the trans-Saharan trade in gold, salt, kola nuts and slaves, and made Timbuktu — seized by Sonni Ali in 1468 — famous for its Sankore university and book trade. In 1591 a Moroccan expeditionary force with muskets and cannon destroyed Songhai power at the Battle of Tondibi, ending the age of the great Sudanese empires.

Sonni Ali and Askia the Great

Sonni Ali (r. 1464–1492) created the empire by conquering Timbuktu and Djenné and defeating the Mossi and Tuareg, ruling through river fleets and cavalry. His successor Askia Muhammad (r. 1493–1529), a devout Muslim, made his own hajj, reorganised administration, taxation and the judiciary, standardised weights and measures, and patronised the scholars of Timbuktu, whose libraries and copyists made books among the city's most valuable commodities.

Fall

Succession struggles among the Askias weakened the empire in the later 16th century. Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur of Morocco, coveting the Saharan salt mines and Sudanese gold, sent an expedition under Judar Pasha across the desert in 1590–91; its arquebusiers routed the far larger Songhai army at Tondibi (March 1591). Gao and Timbuktu were occupied, their scholars deported to Marrakesh, and the empire shattered into successor states (the Dendi kingdom resisted in the south-east) — though trans-Saharan trade never recovered its former scale.
