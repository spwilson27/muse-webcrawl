Alfred McCoy Tyner (December 11, 1938 – March 6, 2020) was an American jazz pianist and composer known for his work with the John Coltrane Quartet (from 1960 to 1965) and his long solo career. He was one of the most influential pianists in jazz history, and his style influenced virtually every pianist who followed him.

Early life

Tyner was born in Philadelphia, the eldest of three children. Encouraged by his mother, he began piano lessons at age 13 and took up the instrument seriously at 14. His early influences included Bud Powell and Thelonious Monk. As a teenager he played at the local Blue Note club and in neighborhood groups; pianist Bud Powell was a neighbor and mentor. At age 17 he converted to Islam through the Ahmadiyya movement and changed his first name to Sulieman Saud, though he performed under his birth name professionally.

Career

In 1959 Tyner joined the Benny Golson–Art Farmer Jazztet, appearing on the album Meet the Jazztet (1960). That same year he left to join saxophonist John Coltrane's quartet, alongside bassist Jimmy Garrison and drummer Elvin Jones. With this group Tyner recorded classic albums including My Favorite Things (1961), Ballads (1963), Crescent (1964), and A Love Supreme (1964). His powerful, percussive playing—with quartal (fourth-based) chord voicings, pentatonic runs, and a thunderous left hand—was central to the quartet's modal sound.

Tyner left the quartet in 1965 as Coltrane's music became more atonal, saying he could no longer hear himself in the music. He then pursued a career as a leader, recording extensively for Blue Note Records, including The Real McCoy (1967). In the 1970s he recorded for Milestone Records with large ensembles and his own groups, producing albums such as Sahara (1972), Enlightenment (1973), and Fly with the Wind (1976). He continued touring and recording for the rest of his life and was named an NEA Jazz Master in 2002.

Style

Tyner's style is characterized by a heavy left hand providing deep, resonant bass structures; voicings built in fourths rather than thirds; and dynamic, orchestral right-hand lines. His approach reshaped modern jazz harmony and influenced pianists including Chick Corea, Herbie Hancock, and countless others.
