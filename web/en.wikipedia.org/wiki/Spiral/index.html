Spiral

In mathematics, a spiral is a curve which emanates from a central point, getting progressively farther away as it revolves around the point. Spirals occur throughout nature — in shells, hurricanes, galaxies, and the arrangement of seeds in a sunflower head — and have been studied since antiquity.

Two-dimensional spirals

A spiral is typically described in polar coordinates by a radius r that is a monotonic continuous function of the angle θ. Famous examples include:

  • The Archimedean spiral, r = a + bθ, in which successive turns are equally spaced; described by Archimedes around 225 BC.
  • The logarithmic spiral, r = aebθ, which grows geometrically and is self-similar; studied by Descartes and Bernoulli, who called it the spira mirabilis.
  • The Fermat spiral, r2 = a2θ, related to the distribution of florets in a flower head.
  • The hyperbolic spiral and the lituus, in which the radius decreases as the angle increases.

Three-dimensional spirals

The most common three-dimensional spiral is the helix, in which the radius stays constant while the point advances along an axis, as in a corkscrew or a coiled spring. Conical helices wind around a cone, and spherical spirals (loxodromes) wind around a sphere at a constant bearing.

In nature and culture

Spirals appear in nautilus shells, spiral galaxies, whirlpools, the horns of some animals, and weather systems. They are also ancient decorative motifs, found in Neolithic carvings such as the triple spiral at Newgrange, and they symbolise growth, cycles, and eternity in many cultures.
