Constitution of Ethiopia

[Hero image placeholder: Federal Parliamentary Assembly building in Addis Ababa]

The Constitution of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, adopted on 8 December 1994 by the Transitional Government's Constituent Assembly and entering into force on 21 August 1995, is the supreme law of Ethiopia. It replaced the 1987 constitution of the People's Democratic Republic and established Ethiopia as a federal parliamentary republic composed of ethnically based regional states.

History

Ethiopia's earlier constitutions include the 1931 constitution promulgated by Emperor Haile Selassie — the country's first written constitution — its 1955 revision, the 1974 provisional arrangements under the Derg, and the 1987 constitution of the People's Democratic Republic of Ethiopia. After the fall of the Derg in 1991, the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front convened a national conference and drafting process leading to the 1994 constitution.

Structure and key provisions

The constitution has 106 articles in eleven chapters. Article 1 establishes the Federal Democratic Republic. Article 8 vests sovereignty in "the Nations, Nationalities and Peoples of Ethiopia". Article 39 famously grants every nation, nationality and people the unconditional right to self-determination, including secession. Article 40 vests ownership of land in the state and the peoples. The constitution creates a bicameral federal parliament — the House of Peoples' Representatives and the House of Federation — with a ceremonial president and an executive prime minister. The House of Federation, representing nations and nationalities, holds constitutional interpretation powers, exercised with the Council of Constitutional Inquiry.

Criticism and debate

The ethnic federalism of the 1995 constitution is both credited with recognising Ethiopia's diversity and blamed for institutionalising ethnic politics and inter-regional disputes. Debates over Articles 39 and 40 remain central to Ethiopian politics.
