The Tetragrammaton (from Greek τετραγράμματον, "consisting of four letters") is the four-letter Hebrew theonym יהוה, the personal name of God in the Hebrew Bible. Transliterated as YHWH, it is conventionally vocalised as Yahweh in scholarship, while the rendering "Jehovah" arose from a later combination of the consonants with the vowels of a substitute reading.

The name appears about 6,800 times in the Masoretic Text. Because of reverence for the name, Jewish tradition ceased pronouncing it aloud by the Second Temple period, substituting Adonai ("the Lord") in reading; this is reflected in most English translations, which render the name as "the LORD" in small capitals.

Pronunciation and vocalisation

The original pronunciation was not recorded because Hebrew writing originally represented consonants only. Most scholars reconstruct it as "Yahweh", supported by short forms such as "Yah" (as in Hallelujah, "praise Yah"), theophoric name elements, and Greek transcriptions in early Christian writers. Medieval Masoretes added vowel points belonging to Adonai (or, occasionally, Elohim) to signal the substitute reading, giving rise to the hybrid form "Jehovah" in some later Christian usage.

In the Hebrew Bible

The name is revealed to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3, where God glosses it with the phrase Ehyeh asher ehyeh, "I am that I am". It is the name used in the Ten Commandments and throughout the covenant texts, and its misuse is prohibited by the commandment against taking the name in vain.

Usage in Judaism and Christianity

In Judaism the name is written but not pronounced; the euphemism HaShem ("the Name") is used in ordinary speech. Scribes copy the letters with special care in Torah scrolls. In Christianity, most translations follow the Septuagint in substituting "Lord" (Greek Kyrios), though a minority of versions use Yahweh or Jehovah throughout.
