The Silent Generation, also called the Traditionalist Generation, is the demographic cohort following the Greatest Generation and preceding the Baby Boomers. The Pew Research Center uses 1928 to 1945 as the generation's birth years. The term "Silent Generation" was popularised by a 1951 Time magazine cover essay, which characterised the young people of the early 1950s—who had come of age amid the Great Depression and the Second World War—as cautious, withdrawn and conformist in comparison with their elders.

Characteristics and debates

Demographers such as Neil Howe and William Strauss (who called the cohort the "Artist" generation in their generational theory) describe a relatively small generation—birth rates fell during the Depression and war—that reached adulthood in the prosperous but conformist 1950s. Its members were too young to fight in the Second World War but many served in the Korean War. In the United States the generation produced both pillars of the post-war establishment and, paradoxically, many leaders of the 1960s protest movements: Martin Luther King Jr., Malcolm X, and much of the civil-rights and early feminist leadership were born in this cohort, as were figures of the Beat Generation and the rock-and-roll era—leading some commentators to dispute the "silent" label.

Notable members

Members of the generation include Martin Luther King Jr. (1929), Neil Armstrong (1930), Warren Buffett (1930), Clint Eastwood (1930), Sandra Day O'Connor (1930), Václav Havel (1936), Bernie Sanders (1941) and Bob Dylan (1941). In Britain the cohort overlaps with those who built the post-war settlement and staffed the professions during the 1960s–80s.

Demographics

Because the birth years were years of low fertility, the Silent Generation is smaller than both the preceding and following generations, a fact with consequences for labour markets and politics. In the United States its members have been noted for high rates of home ownership and pension wealth; by the 2020s the oldest members are in their late nineties, and the generation's share of elected office has declined, though it long included senior national leaders.
