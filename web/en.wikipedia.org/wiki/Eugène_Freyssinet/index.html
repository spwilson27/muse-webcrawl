Eugène Freyssinet

Eugène Freyssinet (13 July 1879 – 8 June 1962) was a French structural and civil engineer. He is best known as the inventor of prestressed concrete, a technique that transformed twentieth-century construction and made possible long-span bridges and thin-shell structures of unprecedented elegance.

Life and work

Born at Objat in the Corrèze, Freyssinet studied at the École Polytechnique and the École des Ponts et Chaussées. Working as a government engineer in the Allier region, he designed a series of daring reinforced-concrete arch bridges, including the Pont du Veurdre and the Pont de Saint-Pierre-du-Vauvray (1923), then the longest concrete arch in the world. At Plougastel (1930) he built three 180-metre arches, using the construction process to study the creep of concrete — the slow deformation under sustained load that he identified as the central obstacle to prestressing.

Prestressed concrete

In 1928 Freyssinet patented his system of prestressing: high-strength steel wires are tensioned and anchored against the concrete so that the member is pre-compressed, closing cracks and greatly increasing load capacity. He understood that only high-strength steel, tensioned hard enough to survive losses from creep and shrinkage, would work. After early work on prestressed pipes and poles, the technique found worldwide application after the Second World War in bridges, hangars, reservoirs and containment structures. His huge parabolic hangars at Orly (1921–1923) and the thin-shell Basilica of St. Pius X at Lourdes (1958) remain landmarks of structural art.

Legacy

Freyssinet founded a specialist firm and a professional society devoted to prestressing, and the international federation for prestressing (FIP) awarded its first Freyssinet Medal in his honour. He is widely regarded as the father of prestressed concrete.
