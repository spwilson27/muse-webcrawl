Planned obsolescence

Planned obsolescence
Also calledBuilt-in obsolescence
ConceptDesigning products with an artificially limited useful life
Term promoted byBernard London (1932); popularised by Brooks Stevens (1954)

Planned obsolescence, or built-in obsolescence, is a policy of planning or designing a product with an artificially limited useful life or a purposely frail design, so that it becomes obsolete after a certain pre-determined period and consumers are induced to buy replacements. The rationale is to generate repeat sales and long-term volume.

History

The best-documented early case is the Phoebus cartel (1924–1939), in which major lightbulb manufacturers including Osram, Philips and General Electric standardised bulb lifetimes at 1,000 hours and fined members whose bulbs lasted too long — while a hand-made bulb in a Livermore, California fire station has burned since 1901. In 1932 Bernard London proposed planned obsolescence as a remedy for the Great Depression. General Motors' annual model changes under Alfred P. Sloan — "dynamic obsolescence" — made style rather than failure the driver. Industrial designer Brooks Stevens popularised the phrase in the 1950s, and Vance Packard's The Waste Makers (1960) attacked the practice.

Types

Strategies include contrived durability (weak parts, glued-in batteries, unavailable spares), software obsolescence (updates that drop support or slow older devices), perceived or psychological obsolescence (fashion cycles), and systemic obsolescence (changing formats and connectors). Cases such as printer ink cartridges with page-counting chips and the 2017 "batterygate" affair — in which Apple paid settlements after throttling older iPhones without clear disclosure — have drawn litigation and fines.

Criticism and responses

Critics cite waste, e-waste burdens, resource depletion and consumer cost; defenders argue shorter design lives can lower prices and spur innovation. Regulatory responses include France's 2015 law making planned obsolescence an offence, the EU's repairability indexes and ecodesign rules, and right-to-repair legislation in several jurisdictions.
