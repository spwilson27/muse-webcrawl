Cyril and Methodius, "Apostles to the Slavs", honoured as co-patrons of Europe.

Cyril (born Constantine, 826/827–869) and Methodius (815/820–885) were Byzantine Greek brothers from Thessalonica who became missionaries to the Slavic peoples. Known as the "Apostles to the Slavs", they devised the Glagolitic alphabet, the first alphabet for a Slavic language, translated the Bible and liturgy into Old Church Slavonic, and thereby laid the foundations of Slavic literacy and culture. Pope John Paul II named them co-patrons of Europe in 1980.

The Moravian mission

In 862 Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia asked the Byzantine emperor Michael III for missionaries who could teach in Slavonic rather than Latin, to counter Frankish influence. The emperor sent the brothers, both fluent in the Slavic dialect of Thessalonica. Constantine created the Glagolitic script and translated the Gospels, beginning with the words "In the beginning was the Word".

Rome and later missions

Attacked by Frankish clergy who held that only Hebrew, Greek and Latin could be used in worship, the brothers travelled to Rome in 867, where Pope Adrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy and ordained their disciples. Constantine, taking the monastic name Cyril, died in Rome in 869. Methodius returned as archbishop of Sirmium and papal legate, survived imprisonment by Frankish bishops, and continued the mission until his death in 885. His disciples, expelled from Moravia, carried Slavonic letters to Bulgaria, where the Preslav and Ohrid schools developed the Cyrillic script named in Cyril's honour.

Legacy

Their feast is a national holiday in Bulgaria, North Macedonia, the Czech Republic, Slovakia and Russia (as Slavic Literature and Culture Day). Cyrillic-derived alphabets serve more than 250 million people today.
