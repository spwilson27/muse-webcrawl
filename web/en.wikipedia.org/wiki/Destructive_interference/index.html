Destructive interference

Two waves of equal amplitude and opposite phase cancel completely

In physics, destructive interference occurs when two waves combine such that they cancel each other out, wholly or in part, resulting in a wave of reduced or zero amplitude. It is the opposite of constructive interference, in which waves reinforce one another.

Principle

Interference follows from the superposition principle: where two waves overlap, the resulting displacement is the sum of the individual displacements. Destructive interference occurs when the waves are out of phase — when the crest of one wave coincides with the trough of the other. For two waves of the same frequency, complete cancellation requires equal amplitudes and a phase difference of π radians (180 degrees), or equivalently a path difference of an odd multiple of half a wavelength.

Examples

Destructive interference explains the dark fringes in Young's double-slit experiment, the colours of thin films and soap bubbles, and the operation of anti-reflective coatings, whose reflected waves are arranged to cancel. In acoustics it produces the "dead spots" in rooms and is the working principle of noise-cancelling headphones, which generate an inverted copy of ambient sound so that the two cancel at the ear. In water waves and radio engineering, interference nulls similarly shape directional antenna patterns.
