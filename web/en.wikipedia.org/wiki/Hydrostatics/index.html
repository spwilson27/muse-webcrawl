Hydrostatics

Hydrostatics
Branch ofFluid mechanics
ConcernsFluids at rest, pressure distribution, buoyancy
Key figuresArchimedes, Pascal, Stevin

Hydrostatics, or fluid statics, is the branch of fluid mechanics that studies fluids at rest and the pressure they exert on immersed bodies and container walls. It underlies the design of dams, ships, barometers, and hydraulic machinery.

Pressure in a static fluid

In a fluid at rest there are no shear stresses, so pressure acts equally in all directions at a point (Pascal's law). Pressure increases linearly with depth: p = p0 + ρgh, where ρ is the fluid density, g the acceleration due to gravity, and h the depth below the surface. This relation is sometimes called the hydrostatic equation.

Buoyancy

Archimedes' principle states that a body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid. Whether a body floats, sinks, or remains neutrally buoyant depends on the comparison between its average density and that of the fluid. The stability of floating bodies depends on the relative positions of the centre of gravity and the metacentre.

Applications

Hydrostatic principles are used in manometers and barometers for pressure measurement, in hydraulic presses that multiply force, in the hydrostatic test of pressure vessels, and in calculating loads on dams and ship hulls. Simon Stevin and Blaise Pascal developed much of the classical theory in the 16th and 17th centuries; Stevin also noted the "hydrostatic paradox" that pressure at a depth is independent of the container's shape.
