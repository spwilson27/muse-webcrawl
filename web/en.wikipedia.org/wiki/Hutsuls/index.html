Hutsuls

The Hutsuls (Ukrainian: гуцули, hutsuly; Romanian: huțuli) are an ethnic group of highlanders inhabiting the Hutsul region of the Carpathian Mountains in western Ukraine and northern Romania, chiefly in the oblasts of Ivano-Frankivsk, Chernivtsi, and Zakarpattia. They speak a distinctive dialect of Ukrainian strongly influenced by Romanian, Polish, and other neighbouring languages, and are traditionally regarded as one of the subgroups of Rusyns/Ukrainians.

Culture

Hutsul culture is centred on mountain pastoralism — sheep and cattle herding on high pastures (polonyna) — and on forestry and woodcraft. It is famous for its carved woodwork, inlaid metalwork, pottery, woven blankets (lizhnyky), and richly embroidered clothing with geometric patterns. Distinctive instruments include the trembita, a very long alpine horn used for signalling in the mountains, and the floyara flute. Hutsul music and the fast couple-dance arkan feature prominently in Ukrainian folk culture; the region was popularised in literature by Mykhailo Kotsiubynsky's novella Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors (1911), filmed by Sergei Parajanov in 1965.

History

Hutsul origins are debated: theories derive them from Slavic tribes, Vlach (Romanian) shepherds who migrated along the Carpathians, or admixtures of both. First mentions of the group date to the 17th–18th centuries, when the region lay between Polish, Moldavian, and later Austrian rule; the legendary outlaw-hero Oleksa Dovbush (the "Carpathian Robin Hood") remains a folk figure. In the 2001 Ukrainian census more than 21,000 people identified as Hutsuls. The breed of sturdy mountain horse known as the Hutsul pony takes its name from the group.
