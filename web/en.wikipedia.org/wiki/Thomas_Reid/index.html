Thomas Reid (26 April 1710 – 7 October 1796) was a Scottish philosopher and founder of the Scottish school of common sense. A contemporary and critic of David Hume, Reid argued that perception gives direct, reliable access to the external world, against the skeptical conclusions drawn from the "theory of ideas" by Locke, Berkeley, and Hume.

Life

Reid was educated at Marischal College, Aberdeen, and served as a Presbyterian minister before becoming a professor at King's College, Aberdeen. In 1764 he published his Inquiry into the Human Mind and succeeded Adam Smith as Professor of Moral Philosophy at the University of Glasgow, a post he held until 1781.

Philosophy

Reid held that certain "first principles" of common sense — the existence of the external world, the reliability of memory and perception, and the existence of other minds — are self-evident foundations of all reasoning and cannot coherently be doubted. His direct realism held that in perception the mind is directly aware of external objects, not of mental copies or "ideas." His later works, Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man (1785) and Essays on the Active Powers of Man (1788), developed his accounts of judgment, agency, and moral philosophy.

Influence

Reid's common-sense philosophy dominated Scottish universities in the early nineteenth century and later influenced the American pragmatists and the twentieth-century philosopher G. E. Moore.
