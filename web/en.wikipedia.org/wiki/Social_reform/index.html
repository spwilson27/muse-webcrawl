Social reform

Social reform is the organised effort to improve conditions in society — such as poverty, education, public health, working conditions, or the legal status of disadvantaged groups — through gradual change within existing political and economic institutions. It is conventionally contrasted with revolution, which seeks to replace those institutions wholesale, although in practice reform movements have sometimes produced revolutionary consequences.

Nineteenth-century reform movements

The Industrial Revolution generated a wave of reform campaigns in Britain, the United States and continental Europe. Abolitionists campaigned against slavery and the slave trade; factory reformers won limits on working hours and child labour, such as the British Factory Acts; public-health reformers like Edwin Chadwick drove the building of sewers and clean water systems; and prison and asylum reformers, including Elizabeth Fry and Dorothea Dix, publicised brutal institutional conditions. The temperance movement sought to restrict alcohol, culminating in Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933), later widely judged a failure and repealed.

Suffrage and civil rights

Women's suffrage movements won the vote in New Zealand (1893), Britain (1918/1928) and the United States (1920), among many other countries. In the twentieth century, reform broadened to include the labour movement's campaigns for collective bargaining and social insurance, the creation of welfare states after 1945, and the civil rights movements that dismantled legal racial segregation and discrimination in the United States and apartheid in South Africa.

Methods and organisations

Social reform is typically pursued through journalism and exposés (the "muckrakers" of the Progressive Era), voluntary associations and charities, settlement houses such as Jane Addams's Hull House in Chicago, professional expertise, test-case litigation, and political parties. Progressive Era reforms in the United States included food and drug regulation, antitrust law, direct election of senators and initiative and referendum procedures.

Criticism

Reform has been criticised from both directions: by conservatives as intrusive social engineering, and by radicals as piecemeal change that stabilises unjust systems by relieving their worst pressures. Historians continue to debate how far reforms such as the welfare state represented genuine redistribution of power versus new forms of social control.
