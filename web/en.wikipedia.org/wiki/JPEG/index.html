JPEG (pronounced "jay-peg") is a commonly used method of lossy compression for digital images, particularly for those produced by digital photography. The name stands for the Joint Photographic Experts Group, the committee that created the standard in 1992 (ISO/IEC 10918-1 / ITU-T T.81).

Compression

JPEG compression converts the image to a luminance–chrominance colour space, optionally downsamples the chroma channels, divides the image into 8×8 blocks and applies the discrete cosine transform (DCT) to each block. The resulting coefficients are quantised — the lossy step, discarding high-frequency detail to which the eye is less sensitive — and then entropy-coded. Compression ratios of 10:1 are typical with little perceptible loss; adjustable quality settings trade file size against visible artefacts such as blockiness and ringing.

File formats

The compression standard is usually embodied in the JFIF file format, with files carrying .jpg or .jpeg extensions; the Exif variant is written by digital cameras, and JPEG data may be embedded in TIFF and other containers. JPEG became the dominant photographic format on the World Wide Web and in consumer imaging. Later formats derived from the same group's work include JPEG 2000 (wavelet-based), JPEG XR and JPEG XL.

Patents and licensing

The baseline JPEG standard was designed to be implemented royalty-free; a claimed patent by Forgent Networks in the 2000s was challenged and narrowed on re-examination.
