Public Order Emergency Commission

Established: 25 April 2022
Commissioner: Paul Rouleau
Report tabled: 17 February 2023
Location: Ottawa, Canada

The Public Order Emergency Commission (French: Commission sur l'état d'urgence), also known as the Rouleau Commission, was a public inquiry in Canada established on 25 April 2022 to examine the circumstances that led the federal government to declare a public order emergency on 14 February 2022 under the Emergencies Act — the first invocation of that statute since it replaced the War Measures Act in 1988.

Background

The declaration followed the Canada convoy protests, in which truck drivers and supporters protesting COVID-19 vaccine mandates occupied downtown Ottawa for about three weeks and blockaded border crossings, including the Ambassador Bridge at Windsor, Ontario, and the crossing at Coutts, Alberta. The emergency declaration gave authorities extraordinary powers, including to compel tow trucks, freeze bank accounts of participants and prohibit assemblies in designated areas; the occupation was cleared by police between 18 and 20 February 2022, and the declaration was revoked on 23 February.

Under section 63 of the Emergencies Act, the government was required to establish an inquiry into the declaration. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau appointed Ontario Court of Appeal justice Paul Rouleau as commissioner.

Hearings and report

The commission held public hearings in Ottawa from October to November 2022, hearing from protesters, residents, police chiefs, municipal and provincial officials, intelligence officers, and cabinet ministers including Trudeau himself — the first sitting Canadian prime minister to testify at a public inquiry. It also examined the flow of information between police services and governments and the role of misinformation and foreign funding claims.

Rouleau's five-volume report, tabled in the House of Commons on 17 February 2023, concluded, "with reluctance", that the federal government had met the "very high" legal threshold for invoking the Emergencies Act, while documenting failures in policing, intelligence sharing and intergovernmental cooperation, and making 56 recommendations. In January 2024, in a separate judicial review, the Federal Court of Canada held that the invocation had been unreasonable and infringed Charter rights — a judgment the government said it would appeal — leaving the legal legacy of the episode contested.
