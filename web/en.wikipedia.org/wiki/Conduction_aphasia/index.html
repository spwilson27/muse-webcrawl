Conduction aphasia, also called associative aphasia, is an acquired language disorder characterised by intact auditory comprehension, fluent (yet paraphasic) speech production, but poor speech repetition. People with conduction aphasia are typically aware of their errors and show significant difficulty in repeating phrases, particularly as length and complexity increase.

Classical model

The disorder was predicted by Carl Wernicke and elaborated by Ludwig Lichtheim in the nineteenth century: a lesion disconnecting Wernicke's area (comprehension) from Broca's area (production) via the arcuate fasciculus should leave both functions largely intact while blocking the transfer needed for repetition. Norman Geschwind revived this disconnection account in the 1960s.

Clinical features

Speech is fluent but littered with phonemic paraphasias — sound substitutions such as "tadle" for "table". A characteristic behaviour is conduite d'approche, successive attempts that home in on the target word. Reading aloud and writing may also be impaired; comprehension of spoken language is relatively preserved. Modern lesion studies implicate the posterior superior temporal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and insula, and some researchers question whether the arcuate fasciculus alone explains the syndrome.

Causes and treatment

The usual cause is stroke in the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, though tumours and trauma can produce it. Treatment is speech-language therapy aimed at repetition and phonological processing; prognosis for recovery is often comparatively good.
