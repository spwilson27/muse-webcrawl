Lampriformes is an order of ray-finned fishes that includes the opahs, oarfishes, ribbonfishes, crestfishes, velifers, and related deep-sea species. The order contains about 46 living species in roughly 20 genera and seven families. Lampriforms are mostly pelagic fishes of the open ocean, many living in deep waters, and they show an extraordinary range of body shapes, from the disc-shaped opah to the immensely elongated oarfish.

Characteristics

Members of the order share features such as a protrusible upper jaw — the premaxilla excludes the maxilla from the gape, an arrangement unique among fishes — and the absence of fin spines in most forms. Body forms range from deep-bodied and compressed (opahs, velifers) to ribbon-like and extremely long (oarfishes and ribbonfishes). The giant oarfish (Regalecus glesne) is the longest bony fish alive, reliably reported to reach 8 metres (26 ft) and possibly longer; oarfish strandings are thought to lie behind some sea serpent legends.

The opah (Lampris guttatus and relatives) is notable as the first fish known to be fully endothermic: it maintains its whole body above ambient water temperature by generating heat in its pectoral muscles and conserving it with countercurrent exchangers (retia mirabilia) in the gills.

Classification

Lampriformes is usually divided into two suborders: the Bathysomi, containing the velifers, opahs, and crestfishes, and the Taeniosomi, containing the elongate ribbonfishes, oarfishes, and tube-eye. Fossil lampriforms are known from the Late Cretaceous onward. Molecular studies place the order among the early-diverging lineages of the Acanthomorpha (spiny-rayed fishes).
