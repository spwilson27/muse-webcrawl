A biogeochemical cycle, or nutrient cycle, is the pathway by which a chemical element or molecule moves through both the living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) compartments of Earth. The term combines bio (life), geo (Earth), and chemical, reflecting that cycles are driven jointly by biological, geological, and chemical processes. Elements essential to life — above all carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, oxygen, and hydrogen — circulate continuously between the atmosphere, oceans, soils, rocks, and organisms, changing chemical form as they go.

The carbon cycle: fluxes between atmosphere, biosphere, oceans, and sediments.

Reservoirs and fluxes

Each cycle is described in terms of reservoirs (pools) — stores such as atmospheric CO2, ocean water, limestone, and living biomass — and fluxes, the rates of transfer between them. Residence times vary enormously: a carbon atom may spend days in a leaf, centuries in deep ocean water, or hundreds of millions of years locked in sedimentary rock before uplift and weathering release it again.

Major cycles

The carbon cycle links photosynthesis and respiration with ocean exchange, fossil-fuel combustion, and geological storage. The nitrogen cycle depends on microbial fixation, nitrification, and denitrification, and has been profoundly altered by the Haber–Bosch process. The water cycle moves H2O by evaporation, condensation, and runoff. The phosphorus cycle, lacking a significant atmospheric phase, proceeds mainly through rock weathering, uptake, and sedimentation, making phosphorus a frequent limiting nutrient. Sulfur and oxygen cycles are tightly coupled to carbon through redox chemistry and life.

Human impact

Human activity now rivals or exceeds natural fluxes in several cycles: burning fossil fuels adds roughly ten billion tonnes of carbon to the atmosphere annually; synthetic fertilizer production fixes more nitrogen than all terrestrial natural processes combined; and mining mobilizes phosphorus and sulfur at unprecedented rates. These perturbations drive climate change, ocean acidification, eutrophication, and shifts in ecosystem productivity, making biogeochemical cycles a central subject of Earth system science and of the "planetary boundaries" framework.
