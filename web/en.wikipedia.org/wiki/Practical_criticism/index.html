Practical criticism

Practical criticism is an approach to literary study, developed at Cambridge in the 1920s by I. A. Richards, in which readers analyse poems and passages of prose presented without their authors' names, dates or other contextual information. The method forces attention onto the words on the page — their literal meaning, feeling, tone and intention — and away from reputation, biography and literary history. Richards set out the experiment in Practical Criticism: A Study of Literary Judgment (1929), one of the founding texts of modern literary criticism.

The experiment

For several years Richards gave his Cambridge students printed sheets of poems — by authors ranging from Shakespeare and Donne to minor contemporary versifiers — with all identifying information removed, and asked for written judgments. The resulting "protocols" revealed systematic misreadings: stock responses, doctrinal adhesions, technical presuppositions and sentimental or inhibited reactions that distorted judgment. Richards catalogued these difficulties and argued that trained, attentive reading could be taught.

Influence

Practical criticism became a staple of the Cambridge English Tripos and, through unseen-text examination questions, of British school examinations, where it remains common in A-level English literature. Together with Richards's earlier work with C. K. Ogden (The Meaning of Meaning, 1923) and William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930), it laid the groundwork for the American New Criticism and its doctrine of close reading. Later movements — historicist, Marxist and post-structuralist — criticised the method for isolating the text from its social and historical contexts, but the unseen-passage exercise survives as a core pedagogical technique.
