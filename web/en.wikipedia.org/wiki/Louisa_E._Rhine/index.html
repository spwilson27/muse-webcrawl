Louisa Ella Rhine (née Weckesser; November 9, 1891 – March 17, 1983) was an American parapsychologist best known for her work in the collection and analysis of spontaneous cases of extrasensory perception (ESP), and for her collaboration with her husband, J. B. Rhine, at the Duke University Parapsychology Laboratory.

Life and career

Born on a farm in Seneca County, Ohio, Rhine studied plant physiology at Ohio Northern University and took her doctorate in botany at the University of Chicago in 1928, completing research on plant ecology. She married Joseph Banks Rhine in 1920; the couple moved to Duke University in 1927 to work with William McDougall.

While J. B. Rhine concentrated on experimental card-guessing tests, Louisa Rhine devoted herself to spontaneous cases — the unsolicited reports of telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis sent in by members of the public. Over decades she assembled and classified more than 15,000 case reports, one of the largest collections of its kind, analysing them for patterns such as the predominance of experiences concerning family members and serious events such as deaths.

Works

Her books include Hidden Channels of the Mind (1961), The Invisible Picture (1981), and Something Hidden, a memoir of her life with J. B. Rhine. She argued that spontaneous experiences, though not laboratory evidence, were the natural phenomena that experimental parapsychology sought to explain. She continued research after her husband's retirement, associated with the Institute for Parapsychology in Durham, North Carolina.
