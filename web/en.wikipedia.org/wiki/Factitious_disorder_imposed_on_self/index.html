Factitious disorder imposed on self

Factitious disorder imposed on self, also known as Munchausen syndrome, is a factitious disorder in which a person, without a clear motive such as financial gain or avoiding work, repeatedly acts as if they have a physical or mental illness when they are not really sick. Sufferers may feign, exaggerate, or deliberately induce symptoms in themselves in order to assume the sick role and receive medical attention and care.

People with the disorder often present repeatedly to hospitals with dramatic but inconsistent histories.

Signs and symptoms

Typical features include a dramatic but vague medical history, symptoms that do not match examination findings, eagerness for invasive procedures and surgery, extensive knowledge of medical terminology, numerous scars from past operations, and wandering from hospital to hospital (sometimes called "hospital hopper" behaviour). Symptoms may be produced by self-injury, tampering with samples or thermometers, or taking substances that mimic disease.

History and terminology

The term "Munchausen syndrome" was coined by British physician Richard Asher in 1951, after Baron Munchausen, the fictional character famous for telling fantastic and impossible stories about himself. The DSM-5 uses the diagnostic name "factitious disorder imposed on self", distinguishing it from factitious disorder imposed on another and from malingering, in which symptoms are feigned for obvious external incentives.

Treatment

There is no well-established specific treatment; management focuses on avoiding unnecessary procedures, coordinated care among providers, and psychotherapy addressing underlying psychological needs.
