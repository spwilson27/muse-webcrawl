Huai River

The Huai River (Chinese: 淮河; pinyin: Huái Hé) is a major river in China, approximately 1,110 kilometres long with a drainage basin of about 187,000 km². It flows from west to east between the Yellow River to the north and the Yangtze to the south, and the line of the Huai together with the Qinling Mountains forms the traditional geographical divide between northern and southern China — roughly the January 0 °C isotherm and the 800 mm rainfall line, separating wheat-growing from rice-growing country.

Course

The Huai rises in the Tongbai Mountains on the border of Henan and Hubei and flows east through southern Henan, northern Anhui and northern Jiangsu. Since the catastrophic course changes of the Yellow River in 1194, which captured the Huai's lower channel, the river has lacked a direct outlet to the sea: its waters pass through Lake Hongze and then south by the Grand Canal and diversion channels to the Yangtze, or east via the Subei irrigation channels.

Flooding and control

The Huai basin has a long history of destructive floods, aggravated by its flat gradient and by the Yellow River's silt-laden incursions. After the severe floods of 1949–1950 the Chinese government launched large-scale water conservancy works on the Huai — among the first major river-control programmes of the People's Republic — building reservoirs, dykes and the Sanhe sluice that regulates Lake Hongze. Pollution from industry along the river became a serious concern from the 1990s and has prompted repeated clean-up campaigns.
