Splash (fluid mechanics)

In fluid mechanics, a splash is the disturbed flow produced when a solid object or a droplet impacts the surface of a liquid, or when a moving liquid strikes a stationary surface. Splashes involve the rapid ejection of sheets, crowns, jets, and secondary droplets, and they are governed by the balance of inertia, surface tension, viscosity, and gravity.

Drop impact

When a liquid drop strikes a deep pool, it opens a hemispherical cavity; the displaced liquid rises around the impact point as a crown-shaped sheet whose rim destabilises into fingers and secondary droplets. The collapse of the cavity can drive a fast upward central jet (the Rayleigh jet), which may itself break into droplets. On a solid surface, the drop instead spreads into a thin lamella; whether it splashes depends on the impact speed, drop size, viscosity and surface tension (summarised by the Weber and Reynolds numbers), the roughness of the surface, and the surrounding gas pressure — lowering air pressure can suppress splashing entirely.

Historical study

The physicist A. M. Worthington pioneered the study of splashes in the late nineteenth century, using spark photography to freeze millisecond events; his book A Study of Splashes (1908) remains a classic. Harold Edgerton's stroboscopic photographs of a milk-drop coronet became iconic images of high-speed photography, and modern research uses cameras recording millions of frames per second together with numerical simulation.

Importance

Splashing controls many practical processes: raindrops splashing on soil disperse plant pathogens and erode surfaces; splashing in fuel injection and spray coating affects efficiency and uniformity; inkjet printing depends on avoiding splash on impact; and droplet impacts are central to forensic bloodstain pattern analysis. In medicine and hygiene, splashes generate aerosols that can spread infection.
