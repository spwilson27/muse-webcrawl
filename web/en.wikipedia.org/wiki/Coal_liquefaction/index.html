Coal liquefaction

Coal liquefaction is a process of converting coal into liquid hydrocarbons: liquid fuels and petrochemicals. This process is often referred to as "coal to liquid" (CTL). Both direct and indirect liquefaction processes have been developed. The process involves dissolving or gasifying coal and then adding hydrogen, because coal is richer in carbon and poorer in hydrogen than petroleum-derived liquids.

Direct liquefaction

The Bergius process, developed by Friedrich Bergius in the years before the First World War, hydrogenates coal directly at high temperature and pressure in the presence of catalysts and a solvent oil. Hydrogenation plants supplied a large share of Nazi Germany's aviation fuel during the Second World War. Later single-stage and two-stage processes (such as the H-Coal and NEDOL processes) improved yields and product quality.

Indirect liquefaction

In indirect liquefaction the coal is first completely gasified to syngas, a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen. The gas is cleaned and its composition adjusted, then converted to liquid hydrocarbons by Fischer–Tropsch synthesis over cobalt- or iron-based catalysts. The South African company Sasol has operated this route commercially at Secunda since the 1950s, historically motivated by oil sanctions against the apartheid regime.

Economics and environment

Liquefaction plants are capital-intensive and generally competitive only where coal is cheap and oil expensive or unavailable. Without carbon capture, coal-to-liquid fuels produce substantially more carbon dioxide per unit of energy than conventional petroleum fuels, which has limited deployment outside China and South Africa.
