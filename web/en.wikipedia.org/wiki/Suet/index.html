Suet is the raw, hard fat of beef, lamb or mutton found around the loins and kidneys. It has a high smoke point and a melting point between 45 °C and 50 °C (113–122 °F), which makes it useful in cooking, where it is valued for producing flaky, light pastry. Suet is a key ingredient in traditional British puddings, both sweet and savoury, such as Christmas pudding, steak and kidney pudding, spotted dick and jam roly-poly, as well as in mincemeat and some dumplings.

Characteristics

Unlike the softer fat deposits elsewhere in the animal, suet is firm and crumbly at room temperature because of its high proportion of saturated fatty acids, particularly stearic acid in the form of triglycerides. Fresh suet is usually grated or shredded before use; commercial preparations are often sold pre-shredded and lightly dusted with flour to prevent clumping. Because suet melts relatively late in cooking, it leaves small air pockets in pastry and steamed puddings, giving them a characteristic light texture.

Uses

In British and Irish cooking, suet is used in suet pastry for steak and kidney pie and pudding, in dumplings served with stews, and in sweet steamed puddings. It is a traditional ingredient of mincemeat for mince pies. Suet is also rendered to make tallow, historically used for candles and soap, and is sold as a high-energy food for wild birds, especially in winter.

Vegetarian substitutes

Vegetarian suet substitutes are made from hydrogenated vegetable oils, such as palm and sunflower oil, mixed with rice flour. They are marketed for cooks who avoid animal products while still wanting the texture of suet pastry.
