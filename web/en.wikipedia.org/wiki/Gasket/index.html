Gasket

A selection of gaskets of various shapes and materials.

A gasket is a mechanical seal which fills the space between two or more mating surfaces, generally to prevent leakage from or into the joined objects while under compression. Gaskets allow for imperfect mating surfaces on machine parts where they can fill irregularities. They are commonly produced by cutting from sheet materials, and are used throughout industry in pipe flanges, engines, pressure vessels, pumps, and compressors.

Materials and types

Gasket materials are selected according to pressure, temperature, and the chemical nature of the fluid being sealed. Common types include:

  • Sheet gaskets punched from rubber, cork, paper or fibre sheet, compressed non-asbestos fibre, or PTFE.
  • Spiral-wound gaskets, made of a metal strip wound together with a soft filler, used for high-pressure flanges.
  • Metallic gaskets, including ring-joint gaskets machined from solid metal for extreme pressure and temperature service.
  • O-rings, elastomeric torus seals seated in a groove.
  • Head gaskets, special multi-layer seals between an engine block and cylinder head, which must withstand combustion pressures and temperatures.

Design considerations

A gasket must compress enough to conform to flange surface imperfections yet resist extrusion and creep under load and temperature cycling. Engineers specify bolt torque to achieve an adequate "gasket stress" without crushing the seal; the balance between seating stress and operating stress, together with thermal expansion differences, determines whether a joint stays leak-tight over its service life. Chemical compatibility, permeation, and fire safety also govern material choice.

History

Simple gaskets of leather, hemp, and tallow served early steam engines and pumps; the industrial nineteenth century brought rubber and asbestos-fibre sheets, the latter later phased out for health reasons and replaced by aramid and other fibres bound in elastomer.
