Illiberal democracy

An illiberal democracy is a governing system in which, although elections take place, citizens are cut off from knowledge of the activities of those who exercise real power because of the lack of civil liberties and institutional checks; it is therefore not an "open society". The term was popularized by journalist Fareed Zakaria in a 1997 Foreign Affairs article and subsequent book, The Future of Freedom (2003), which argued that democratization without constitutional liberalism — the rule of law, separation of powers, and protection of basic rights — produces regimes that are elected yet illiberal.

Illiberal democracies hold elections but weaken courts, media, and civil liberties.

Characteristics

Scholars associate illiberal democracies with a set of recurring features: executive aggrandizement at the expense of legislatures and courts; manipulation of electoral rules and media ownership to entrench incumbents; restrictions on opposition parties, independent media, and non-governmental organizations; the packing or intimidation of judiciaries; and majoritarian rhetoric that treats the ruling party's electoral mandate as overriding legal constraints. Elections may remain competitive in form, but the playing field is systematically tilted.

Debates and usage

Hungarian prime minister Viktor Orbán embraced the label in a 2014 speech, announcing his intention to build an "illiberal state" modeled on successful non-Western systems — a rare instance of a leader adopting the term positively. Political scientists debate whether "illiberal democracy" is a stable regime type or a transitional stage toward competitive authoritarianism, and some prefer terms such as "democratic backsliding" to describe the erosion of liberal institutions within formally democratic states.

Critics also note a double standard: Zakaria and others pointed out that Western democracies themselves developed liberal institutions before mass suffrage in many cases, and that the concept can understate how contested the boundary between liberal and illiberal practice remains in established democracies.
