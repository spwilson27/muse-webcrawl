Haemophilia A

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Haemophilia A is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern.

Haemophilia A (spelled hemophilia A in American English) is a blood disorder caused by deficiency of clotting factor VIII, which impairs the body's ability to form blood clots. It is the most common type of haemophilia, occurring in roughly 1 in 5,000 male births. The condition is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner through mutations in the F8 gene; most patients are male, while female carriers may have mild symptoms. About a third of cases arise from new mutations with no family history.

Signs and symptoms

The severity depends on residual factor VIII activity: severe (<1% of normal), moderate (1–5%), or mild (5–40%). Bleeding is characteristically deep rather than superficial: spontaneous haemorrhages into joints (haemarthroses), especially knees, elbows, and ankles, cause pain and, if recurrent, destructive joint disease; bleeding into muscles and soft tissues, prolonged bleeding after injury, surgery, or dental extraction; and, most dangerously, intracranial haemorrhage. Easy bruising is common, but platelet-type surface bleeding (petechiae) is not typical.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) with normal prothrombin time and platelet count, confirmed by low factor VIII activity on specific assay, and by genetic testing of F8. A common cause of severe disease is the intron 22 inversion of the gene. Haemophilia A must be distinguished from haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency), von Willebrand disease, and acquired haemophilia due to autoantibodies.

Treatment

Treatment replaces the missing factor: plasma-derived or recombinant factor VIII concentrates, traditionally given on demand for bleeds and increasingly as regular prophylaxis. The bispecific antibody emicizumab, which mimics factor VIII's cofactor function, is used for prophylaxis, including in patients who develop neutralising "inhibitor" antibodies against factor VIII — the most serious complication of replacement therapy. Mild cases may respond to desmopressin (DDAVP). Gene therapy (valoctocogene roxaparvovec) has been approved in some jurisdictions, providing endogenous factor VIII production after a single infusion. In the 1980s, many patients were infected with HIV and hepatitis C through contaminated blood products, a major medical scandal that transformed blood-product safety.

History

Haemophilia became famous as the "royal disease" because Queen Victoria was a carrier and passed the mutation to royal families across Europe, including the Russian imperial family — her great-grandson Tsarevich Alexei suffered from the disease (now believed, in the Romanov case, to have been haemophilia B). John Conrad Otto gave an early account in 1803; the term "haemophilia" dates to the 1820s.
