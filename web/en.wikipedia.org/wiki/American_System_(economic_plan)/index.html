American System (economic plan)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The American System was an economic plan that played an important role in American policy during the first half of the 19th century. Rooted in the ideas of Alexander Hamilton and his Report on Manufactures, the System consisted of three mutually reinforcing parts: a protective tariff to support American industry, a national bank to foster commerce and issue a stable currency, and federal subsidies for internal improvements such as roads, canals, and other infrastructure.

The plan was most famously championed by Kentucky congressman and Secretary of State Henry Clay, who argued that federal support would economically unite the regions of the country and promote balanced growth among agriculture, commerce, and manufacturing.

Main points

  • Protective tariffs: duties such as the Tariff of 1816 and the Tariff of 1828 ("Tariff of Abominations") designed to shield infant American industries from British competition.
  • National bank: the Second Bank of the United States, chartered in 1816, provided a stable national currency and credit.
  • Internal improvements: federally funded transportation projects, including the National Road, to link markets across the growing nation.

History

Clay first used the term "American System" in an 1824 speech defending a new tariff. The system became a central plank of the National Republican and later Whig Party platforms. President Andrew Jackson's veto of the Maysville Road bill in 1830 and his destruction of the Second Bank in the Bank War of 1832 dealt heavy blows to the program, and by the 1850s much of the System had been dismantled, though its ideas influenced later Republican economic policy.
