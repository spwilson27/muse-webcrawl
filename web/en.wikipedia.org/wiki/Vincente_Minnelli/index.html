Vincente Minnelli (born Lester Anthony Minnelli; 28 February 1903 – 25 July 1986) was an American stage and film director, considered one of the great stylists of the Hollywood Golden Age. Working principally in the Arthur Freed unit at Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, he directed some of the most acclaimed musicals of the 1940s and 1950s, as well as admired melodramas. He won the Academy Award for Best Director for Gigi (1958), which won nine Oscars including Best Picture.

Early life and theatre

Born in Chicago, Minnelli grew up in his family's travelling tent theatre troupe, the Minnelli Brothers Dramatic Tent Show, and worked as a window dresser and costume and set designer before moving to New York. As art director and then director at Radio City Music Hall and on Broadway (including the Ziegfeld Follies of 1936 and Very Warm for May), he developed the lush, colour-conscious visual style that defined his films.

MGM and the musical

Brought to Hollywood by producer Arthur Freed in 1940, Minnelli made his directing debut with Cabin in the Sky (1943) and achieved a landmark success with Meet Me in St. Louis (1944), starring Judy Garland, whom he married in 1945; their daughter is Liza Minnelli. His musicals include The Pirate (1948), An American in Paris (1951) — Best Picture Oscar winner, famous for its 17-minute closing ballet — The Band Wagon (1953), Brigadoon (1954), Kismet (1955), and Gigi (1958).

Melodramas and later work

Minnelli's non-musicals are equally esteemed: Madame Bovary (1949), Father of the Bride (1950), The Bad and the Beautiful (1952), Lust for Life (1956) with Kirk Douglas as Vincent van Gogh, Tea and Sympathy (1956), Designing Woman (1957), and Some Came Running (1958). His final film was A Matter of Time (1976), starring his daughter Liza. His autobiography, I Remember It Well, was published in 1974.

Style and legacy

Minnelli was famed for fluid long takes, expressive camera movement, painterly colour design, and dream sequences (often surrealist-influenced ballet interludes). French critics of Cahiers du Cinéma championed him as an archetypal auteur. The musical sequence "That's Entertainment!" from The Band Wagon and the ballet of An American in Paris remain canonical.
