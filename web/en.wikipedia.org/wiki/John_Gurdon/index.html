John Gurdon

Sir John Bertrand Gurdon (born 2 October 1933) is a British developmental biologist. He is best known for his pioneering research in nuclear transfer and cloning: in 1962 he showed that the nucleus of a differentiated intestinal cell of the frog Xenopus laevis could direct the development of a whole new frog, proving that mature cells retain the full genetic information needed for development. In 2012 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Shinya Yamanaka "for the discovery that mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent".

Early life

Gurdon was educated at Eton, where a school report famously ranked him last of 250 boys in biology and judged his ambition to become a scientist "quite ridiculous". He read zoology at Christ Church, Oxford, and switched to embryology for his doctorate under Michael Fischberg.

Nuclear transfer experiments

Building on Briggs and King's work in the leopard frog, Gurdon transplanted nuclei from the intestinal epithelium of feeding Xenopus tadpoles into egg cells whose own nuclei had been destroyed by ultraviolet light. Some developed into normal tadpoles and, in later work, fertile adult frogs — the first cloning of an animal from a differentiated cell by nuclear transfer. The experiments refuted the idea that development involves permanent loss of genes and laid conceptual foundations for Dolly the sheep (1996) and for Yamanaka's induced pluripotent stem cells (2006).

Later career

Gurdon became a professor at Cambridge, co-founded the institute in Cambridge that now bears his name (the Gurdon Institute, for developmental and cancer biology), and served as Master of Magdalene College (1995–2002). He was knighted in 1995.
