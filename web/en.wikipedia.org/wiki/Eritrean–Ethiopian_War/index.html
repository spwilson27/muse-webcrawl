The Eritrean–Ethiopian War, also known as the Badme War, was fought between Eritrea and Ethiopia from May 1998 to June 2000 over disputed territory along their common border, including the town of Badme. It was one of the largest conventional wars in Africa in the late 20th century, fought with massed infantry, artillery and front-line trenches reminiscent of the First World War, and cost an estimated 70,000 to 100,000 lives.

Background

Eritrea had won independence from Ethiopia in 1991 (formalised by referendum in 1993) after a thirty-year liberation war; the two ruling fronts, the EPLF in Eritrea and the TPLF-led EPRDF in Ethiopia, had been allies against the Derg regime. Relations soured over economic policy — including Eritrea's introduction of its own currency, the nakfa, in 1997 — and over the imprecisely demarcated colonial-era border. Skirmishes around Badme in May 1998 escalated rapidly into full-scale war.

Course of the war

Fighting proceeded in three major offensives. Eritrea initially occupied the Badme area; Ethiopia's February 1999 offensive retook Badme; and in May–June 2000 a large Ethiopian offensive broke Eritrean lines on the western and central fronts and advanced toward Asmara's approaches, prompting Eritrea to accept the OAU peace framework. Both sides bought advanced weaponry, including supersonic fighters, and expelled tens of thousands of each other's nationals.

Aftermath

The Algiers Agreement of December 2000 ended the war and created a boundary commission, which in 2002 awarded Badme to Eritrea. Ethiopia's refusal to accept the physical demarcation froze relations in a "no war, no peace" standoff for eighteen years, with a UN peacekeeping mission (UNMEE) deployed until 2008. In 2018 Ethiopian prime minister Abiy Ahmed accepted the commission's ruling, and the two countries restored relations in a dramatic rapprochement.
