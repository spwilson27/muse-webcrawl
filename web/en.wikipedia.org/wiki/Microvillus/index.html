Microvillus

Microvilli (singular: microvillus) are microscopic cellular membrane protrusions that increase the surface area for diffusion and minimize any increase in volume, and are involved in a wide variety of functions, including absorption, secretion, cellular adhesion, and mechanotransduction. Thousands of microvilli form the brush border found on the apical surface of some epithelial cells, such as those of the small intestine and the proximal tubules of the kidney.

Structure

Each microvillus is about 0.5–1.5 μm long and roughly 0.1 μm in diameter, containing a core bundle of cross-linked actin filaments (typically 20–30 filaments) anchored at the tip by proteins including fimbrin (plastin), villin, and espin, and linked to the membrane by myosin-1a bridges. At the base, the actin bundles extend into the terminal web of the cell. Microvilli are covered by the glycocalyx, a layer of glycoproteins and glycolipids that in the intestine hosts digestive enzymes such as disaccharidases and peptidases.

Function

The brush border of intestinal enterocytes multiplies the absorptive surface area of the gut; together with villi and the folds of Kerckring it contributes to an overall surface-area amplification of several hundredfold. In the kidney, microvilli of proximal tubule cells support reabsorption. Microvilli are also found on cells of the immune system and on ova, where they interact with sperm during fertilization. Modified microvilli — stereocillia — form the hair bundles of auditory and vestibular hair cells.

Clinical significance

Microvillus inclusion disease is a rare congenital disorder caused by mutations in the MYO5B gene, in which enterocytes fail to form proper microvilli, causing intractable diarrhea in newborns. Celiac disease and infections can cause microvillus atrophy and malabsorption.
