Enuresis

Enuresis is the repeated, involuntary voiding of urine. When it occurs during sleep it is termed nocturnal enuresis or bedwetting. The term usually refers to children aged five years and older who wet the bed or their clothing after the age at which bladder control is expected, although enuresis can also occur in adults.

Classification

Primary enuresis describes a child who has never achieved a sustained period of dryness, while secondary enuresis begins after at least six months of dry nights, often following a stressful event or a medical change. Enuresis is further divided into nocturnal (during sleep), diurnal (during waking hours), and mixed forms. Nocturnal enuresis may be monosymptomatic, or accompanied by daytime lower urinary tract symptoms.

Causes

Bedwetting is usually attributed to a combination of factors: delayed maturation of bladder control, a small functional bladder capacity, production of large volumes of urine at night related to the pattern of vasopressin secretion, difficulty arousing from sleep, and genetics — a child with two parents who wet the bed has a substantially higher chance of doing so. Secondary enuresis may be associated with urinary tract infection, constipation, diabetes, or psychological stress.

Prevalence

Nocturnal enuresis is common: roughly 15–20% of five-year-olds wet the bed at least occasionally, with the rate declining by about 15% per year thereafter. By adolescence around 1–2% still have episodes, and a small proportion of adults are affected. It is more common in boys than in girls.

Treatment

Most children outgrow enuresis without treatment. Management begins with reassurance, regular voiding habits, treatment of constipation, and limiting evening fluids. The two best-supported interventions for persistent nocturnal enuresis are the enuresis alarm, which wakes the child at the onset of wetting and conditions arousal to bladder fullness, and desmopressin, a synthetic analogue of vasopressin that reduces night-time urine production. Punishment is ineffective and discouraged.
