Eustachian tube

Also called: auditory tube, pharyngotympanic tube

Length: approx. 35 mm in adults

Named after: Bartolomeo Eustachi

The Eustachian tube, also called the auditory tube or pharyngotympanic tube, is a canal that connects the middle ear cavity to the nasopharynx, the upper part of the throat behind the nose. It is named after the Italian anatomist Bartolomeo Eustachi, who described it in the sixteenth century. In adults it is about 35 mm long; the third nearest the middle ear is bony, while the remainder is cartilaginous and lined with mucous membrane continuous with that of the tympanic cavity.

Function

The tube is normally closed and opens briefly during swallowing, yawning or chewing, through the action of muscles including the tensor veli palatini. Its principal function is to equalise air pressure on both sides of the eardrum so that the membrane can vibrate freely; it also drains mucus from the middle ear into the throat and protects the ear from nasopharyngeal secretions and sounds. The familiar "popping" sensation during aircraft descent or diving is the tube opening to equalise pressure.

Clinical significance

Eustachian tube dysfunction — failure of the tube to open or close properly — causes ear fullness, muffled hearing and pain with pressure changes (barotrauma). In children the tube is shorter, wider and more horizontal, allowing infection to spread from the throat to the middle ear, a major reason otitis media is so common in early childhood. A tube that remains abnormally open (patulous Eustachian tube) lets patients hear their own breathing and voice abnormally loudly.
