Sanitation

Sanitation refers to public health conditions related to clean drinking water and the adequate treatment and disposal of human excreta and sewage. It encompasses the facilities and services for the safe management of human urine and faeces, solid waste, and wastewater, together with associated hygiene behaviours such as hand washing. Preventing human contact with faeces is central: inadequate sanitation is a major cause of diarrhoeal disease, cholera, typhoid, and parasitic infections worldwide.

Components

A complete sanitation chain includes containment (toilets and latrines), emptying, conveyance (sewers or pit-emptying services), treatment, and safe disposal or reuse. Systems range from centralised sewerage with wastewater treatment plants in cities to on-site solutions such as septic tanks, ventilated improved pit latrines, and container-based sanitation in informal settlements. Sanitation policy also covers menstrual hygiene management, school and healthcare-facility sanitation, and the safety of sanitation workers.

Global status

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6.2 commits countries to adequate and equitable sanitation and hygiene for all by 2030. WHO/UNICEF monitoring estimates that billions of people still lack safely managed sanitation services and that hundreds of millions practise open defecation, principally in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. India's Swachh Bharat Mission (2014–2019) built over 100 million household toilets and declared the country open-defecation free, a claim debated by researchers. Investments in sanitation yield large economic returns through reduced disease, improved school attendance — especially for girls — and dignity and safety benefits.

History

Advanced urban sanitation existed in the Indus Valley civilisation (c. 2600 BCE) and Roman aqueduct and sewer systems (the Cloaca Maxima). Nineteenth-century sanitary reform in industrial cities — driven by figures such as Edwin Chadwick and engineers like Joseph Bazalgette, who built London's sewers after the Great Stink of 1858 — founded modern public-health engineering.
