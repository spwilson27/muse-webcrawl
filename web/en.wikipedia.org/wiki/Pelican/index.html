Pelicans (genus Pelecanus) are a genus of large water birds of the family Pelecanidae. They are characterised by a long beak and a large throat pouch used for catching prey and draining water from the scooped-up contents before swallowing. Eight living species are recognised, found on coasts and inland waters on all continents except Antarctica. The Dalmatian pelican is among the largest flying birds, with a wingspan exceeding three metres.

Description and feeding

Pelicans have large bills with a hooked upper mandible and a flexible pouch suspended from the lower jaw. The white pelicans of the Americas and the Old World typically fish cooperatively, herding fish into shallow water and scooping them up, while brown and Peruvian pelicans plunge-dive from the air. Contrary to popular legend, pelicans do not store food in their pouches; the pouch is a catching net, and prey is swallowed promptly. They also do not feed their young by tearing at their own breast — a medieval legend that made the "pelican in her piety" a symbol of self-sacrifice and the Eucharist in Christian iconography.

Breeding

Pelicans are colonial breeders, nesting on the ground or in trees near water. Both parents incubate the eggs with their feet and feed the young by regurgitation. Young pelicans gather in crèches as they grow.

Conservation and culture

The brown pelican famously declined in the mid-20th century because of DDT thinning its eggshells, and recovered after the pesticide was banned; it was removed from the U.S. endangered species list in 2009. Pelicans feature in heraldry and as symbols: the brown pelican is the state bird of Louisiana, and the great white pelican is the national bird of Romania.
