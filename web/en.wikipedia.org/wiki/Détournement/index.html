Détournement (French: [detuʁnəmɑ̃], roughly "rerouting" or "hijacking") is an artistic and political technique developed by the Letterist International and later the Situationist International, in which existing cultural material — advertisements, films, comics, famous paintings, literature — is appropriated and altered so that its original meaning is subverted and a new, opposing meaning is created.

In their 1956 text "A User's Guide to Détournement", Guy Debord and Gil J. Wolman defined it as the reuse of "preexisting artistic elements in a new ensemble". Because the raw material is drawn from the culture of the spectacle itself, détournement was intended as a weapon against that culture: the hijacked work turns the language of the dominant order against it.

Practice

Classic examples include Asger Jorn's Modifications (1959), in which he painted over flea-market landscape paintings; the détourned comic strips published in situationist journals, where speech balloons were replaced with revolutionary texts; and Debord's films, which re-edited footage from Hollywood movies, newsreels and advertisements. The situationists distinguished "minor détournement" of insignificant elements from "deceptive détournement" of intrinsically significant works.

Recuperation

The situationists paired détournement with its opposite, recuperation: the process by which the spectacle absorbs subversive ideas and images and sells them back as commodities. They argued that any détourned work could itself be recuperated, a fate they saw befalling avant-garde art generally.

Influence

Détournement anticipated and influenced later practices including pop art appropriation, punk's cut-and-paste graphics, culture jamming and adbusting, sampling in music, and internet meme culture, in which familiar images are recaptioned to invert their meaning. The concept remains a reference point in media studies and critical theory.
