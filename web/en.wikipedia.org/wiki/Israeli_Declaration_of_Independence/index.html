The Israeli Declaration of Independence, formally the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel (Hebrew: הכרזה על הקמת מדינת ישראל), was proclaimed on 14 May 1948 (5 Iyar 5708) by David Ben-Gurion, the Executive Head of the World Zionist Organization and Chairman of the Jewish Agency for Palestine. It declared the establishment of a Jewish state in Eretz-Israel, to be known as the State of Israel, which would come into effect at termination of the British Mandate for Palestine at midnight that day. The event is celebrated annually in Israel as Independence Day, Yom HaAtzmaut.

Background

The possibility of a Jewish homeland in Palestine had been a goal of Zionist organisations since the late 19th century. The Balfour Declaration of 1917 expressed British support for such a homeland, and the League of Nations incorporated it into the Mandate for Palestine in 1922. After the Second World War and the Holocaust, pressure for a Jewish state intensified. On 29 November 1947, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, recommending the partition of Palestine into Jewish and Arab states. The Jewish leadership accepted the plan; the Arab leadership rejected it, and civil war broke out.

The ceremony

The declaration ceremony was held at the Tel Aviv Museum (now Independence Hall) on Rothschild Boulevard. It began at 4 p.m., eight hours before the Mandate was due to end, because the following day was the Sabbath. Ben-Gurion struck the table with a gavel and read the declaration, which took about sixteen minutes. The ceremony was broadcast live as the first transmission of the new state's radio service, Kol Yisrael.

Content

The declaration consists of several parts. It recounts the historical connection of the Jewish people to the Land of Israel, describes the catastrophe of the Holocaust, and asserts the legal basis for the state in the Balfour Declaration, the Mandate, and the UN partition resolution. It proclaims the establishment of the state, announces that the People's Council will serve as a Provisional Council of State, and pledges that the state "will ensure complete equality of social and political rights to all its inhabitants irrespective of religion, race or sex" and "guarantee freedom of religion, conscience, language, education and culture". It also appeals to the Arab inhabitants of the state to preserve peace and participate in its development, and to the Jewish diaspora to rally round the Jews of Eretz-Israel.

Signatories and aftermath

Twenty-five members of the People's Council signed at the ceremony; eleven more signed later, and one, Aharon Zisling, added his signature afterwards — 37 in total. Within hours the United States extended de facto recognition (President Harry S. Truman issued a statement eleven minutes after midnight), followed by the Soviet Union and others. The following day, the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq invaded, beginning the 1948 Arab–Israeli War.

Legal status

The declaration is not a constitution, and Israel's Basic Laws have largely filled that role. Nevertheless, Israel's Supreme Court has held that the declaration expresses the state's fundamental values, and the 1992 Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty directs that fundamental rights be interpreted "in the spirit of the principles set forth in the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel". The original scroll is held by the Israel State Archives.
