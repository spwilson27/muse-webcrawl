Gamelan

Gamelan is the traditional ensemble music of the Javanese, Sundanese and Balinese peoples of Indonesia, made up predominantly of percussive instruments — metallophones, gongs, xylophones, drums and flutes — that are built and tuned as a single inseparable set. It is the best known of Indonesia's musical traditions and is inscribed by UNESCO on its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

Instruments

The core of a Javanese gamelan consists of the saron family of metallophones, the kettle-gongs bonang and kenong, the large hanging gongs gong ageng and kempul that mark the largest structural divisions, the soft gender, the two-headed drum kendang that leads the ensemble, and often the gambang xylophone, suling flute, rebab spike fiddle and voices. Balinese gamelan uses similar instruments but with paired tuning — two instruments tuned slightly apart to produce a shimmering beating effect — and a much faster, more explosive playing style.

Tuning and structure

Gamelan uses two tuning systems: the five-tone sléndro, approximating equal pentatonic steps, and the seven-tone pélog. Each gamelan set has its own unique tuning, so instruments cannot be exchanged between sets. Music is organised in colotomic cycles: a repeating melodic framework (balungan) is punctuated by gongs at nested levels, while elaborating instruments improvise around the core melody, anchored by one of the modal frameworks called pathet.

Cultural role

Gamelan accompanies court ritual, wayang shadow-puppet theatre, dance and village ceremonies. Introduced to the West at the 1889 Paris Exposition, where it deeply impressed Debussy, it later influenced composers from Britten and Messiaen to Steve Reich and the American minimalists, and gamelan ensembles are now maintained by universities and communities worldwide.
