Adhesive bandage

An adhesive bandage, also called a sticking plaster, medical plaster, or simply a plaster in British English, is a small medical dressing used for injuries not serious enough to require a full-size bandage. They are often known by the genericized trademarks Band-Aid (in North America) and Elastoplast (in the UK).

Function

The adhesive bandage protects the wound from friction, bacteria, damage, and dirt. It also holds the two cut ends of the skin together to speed healing, and the absorbent pad (usually medicated with an antiseptic) absorbs exudate while cushioning the injury. Some dressings are designed to keep the wound moist ("occlusive" dressings) and adhere only to dry skin at the edges. Because the pad does not stick to the wound, removal is much less painful than with gauze-and-tape dressings.

History

The adhesive bandage was invented in 1920 by Thomas Anderson and Johnson & Johnson employee Earle Dickson of Highland Park, New Jersey, whose wife Josephine frequently cut and burned herself while cooking. Dickson combined a piece of surgical tape with a small gauze pad and crinoline covering so his wife could dress her own wounds. Johnson & Johnson commercialized the product under the Band-Aid name in 1921; it initially sold poorly until the company gave free bandages to Boy Scout troops and butchers, popularizing the product. Elastoplast was introduced in the UK in 1928 by Smith & Nephew. During the Second World War, millions were shipped overseas with Red Cross parcels.

Materials and forms

The backing material may be woven fabric, plastic (such as PVC, polyethylene, or latex-free polyurethane), or latex rubber, coated with an adhesive such as acrylate or zinc oxide. The absorbent pad is typically cotton gauze, sometimes impregnated with antiseptics such as benzalkonium chloride. Modern variants include transparent film bandages, liquid bandages, hydrocolloid dressings, fingertip and knuckle shapes, and bandages decorated for children. Water-resistant versions protect wounds during swimming or washing, though prolonged moisture can loosen the adhesive.
