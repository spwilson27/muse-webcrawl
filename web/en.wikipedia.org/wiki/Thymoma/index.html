Thymoma

Thymoma
SpecialtyOncology, thoracic surgery
SymptomsOften none; chest pain, cough, breathlessness; symptoms of myasthenia gravis
Associated conditionsMyasthenia gravis, pure red cell aplasia, hypogammaglobulinaemia (Good syndrome)
TreatmentSurgical resection; radiotherapy and chemotherapy for advanced disease

A thymoma is a tumour originating from the epithelial cells of the thymus, a lymphoid organ situated in the anterior mediastinum. Thymomas are uncommon — an incidence of roughly 0.1–0.2 per 100,000 people per year — but they are the most common primary tumour of the anterior mediastinum in adults. They usually occur between the ages of 40 and 60 and affect men and women equally.

Signs and symptoms

A third to a half of patients are asymptomatic, the tumour being found incidentally on imaging. Others present with local symptoms — chest pain, cough, dyspnoea, or superior vena cava obstruction. Thymomas are notable for their association with paraneoplastic autoimmune disorders: about 30–40% of patients have myasthenia gravis, and smaller proportions develop pure red cell aplasia or the immunodeficiency of Good syndrome.

Classification and staging

The World Health Organization classifies thymomas by histology into types A, AB, B1, B2 and B3, according to the shape of the epithelial cells and the proportion of lymphocytes; thymic carcinoma is a separate, more aggressive entity. Clinical staging traditionally uses the Masaoka–Koga system, from stage I (fully encapsulated) to stage IV (metastatic disease), which together with completeness of resection is the principal determinant of prognosis.

Treatment and prognosis

Complete surgical resection (thymectomy), increasingly performed by minimally invasive approaches, is the mainstay of treatment for localised disease. Radiotherapy is used for incompletely resected or advanced tumours, and platinum-based chemotherapy for unresectable or metastatic disease. Prognosis is generally favourable for early-stage tumours, with 10-year survival exceeding 80% for stage I–II disease, though late recurrences occur and long follow-up is advised.
