The corrido (Spanish pronunciation: [koˈriðo]) is a traditional Mexican narrative ballad, sung in verse, that recounts historical events, heroic deeds, romances, tragedies and the lives of outlaws and revolutionaries. Descended from the Spanish romance tradition and shaped by European dance rhythms such as the polka and waltz, the corrido flourished along the Mexico–United States border from the nineteenth century onward and remains one of the most durable forms of Mexican popular music, performed in norteño, banda, mariachi and modern regional Mexican styles.

Structure

A classic corrido follows a formulaic frame: an opening greeting and statement of the date and place (saludo), the unfolding of the story in quatrains of eight-syllable lines, often a moral or quoted speech by the protagonist, and a farewell (despedida). The narrative stance is that of a communal chronicler — "Voy a cantar, señores" ("I am going to sing, gentlemen") — and corridos historically functioned as sung newspapers for largely non-literate audiences, carrying news of battles, disasters and border conflicts.

History

Corridos reached a golden age during the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), when hundreds of ballads celebrated figures such as Emiliano Zapata and Pancho Villa and reported battles and betrayals. Earlier border corridos, such as "El Corrido de Gregorio Cortez" — immortalised in Américo Paredes's 1958 study With His Pistol in His Hand — articulated Mexican-American resistance to Anglo authority. The corrido tradition continued into celebrations of migrants, labour struggles and local heroes, and classic performers such as Lydia Mendoza and Los Alegres de Terán carried it onto records and radio.

Narcocorridos and modern developments

From the 1970s, narcocorridos began chronicling drug traffickers in the heroic corrido mould; Los Tigres del Norte became the genre's most famous interpreters, while Chalino Sánchez's raw recordings made him a posthumous legend. Because some songs are commissioned by cartel figures, narcocorridos have been banned from radio and public performance in several Mexican states, fuelling debate about censorship and glorification of crime. In the late 2010s a new generation blended the form with trap and hip-hop as corridos tumbados, popularised by artists such as Natanael Cano and Peso Pluma, taking the ballad tradition to global streaming audiences.
