Nociceptor

A nociceptor ("pain receptor") is a sensory neuron whose free nerve endings initiate the sensation of pain by responding to damaging or potentially damaging stimuli and sending signals to the spinal cord and brain — the process of nociception. The term was coined by Charles Scott Sherrington in 1906. Unlike most sensory receptors, nociceptors respond only to stimuli strong enough to threaten tissue integrity, and they characteristically fail to adapt to a maintained stimulus.

Types

Nociceptors are classified by the fibre that carries their signal and the stimuli they detect:

  • Aδ fibres — thinly myelinated, fast (5–30 m/s); mediate sharp, well-localised "first pain". They include mechanical and mechanothermal nociceptors.
  • C fibres — unmyelinated, slow (0.5–2 m/s); mediate dull, burning "second pain". Most are polymodal, responding to mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli.
  • Silent (sleeping) nociceptors — normally unresponsive afferents in viscera and joints that are recruited during inflammation.

Transduction molecules

Nociceptor endings express specialised ion channels that convert stimuli into action potentials: TRPV1 for heat (above ~43 °C), acid, and capsaicin; TRPM8 for cold and menthol; TRPA1 for pungent chemicals such as mustard oil; ASICs for acidity; P2X3 for ATP released from damaged cells; and the voltage-gated sodium channels Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Nav1.9, which shape excitability. Loss-of-function mutations in Nav1.7 (SCN9A) cause congenital insensitivity to pain, while gain-of-function mutations cause erythromelalgia and other pain disorders.

Sensitisation

Inflammatory mediators — prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine, serotonin, cytokines, and nerve growth factor — lower the activation threshold of nociceptors, an effect called peripheral sensitisation that produces tenderness at injured sites and is the target of NSAID analgesics. Nociceptor activation can also trigger the axon reflex, releasing substance P and CGRP from peripheral terminals and causing neurogenic inflammation.

Locations

Nociceptors are distributed through the skin, muscles, joints, periosteum, and most viscera; the brain parenchyma itself lacks nociceptors, which is why awake brain surgery is possible, although the meninges are pain-sensitive.
