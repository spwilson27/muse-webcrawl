The Sultanate of Rum was a medieval Seljuk Turkic state in Anatolia, established after the Byzantine defeat at the Battle of Manzikert (1071) opened the peninsula to Turkic settlement. Its name derives from Rūm, the Arabic and Persian term for the Romans (Byzantines) whose lands the Turks now ruled.

History

Suleiman ibn Qutulmish founded the sultanate in 1077 with its capital at Nicaea. The First Crusade captured Nicaea in 1097, after which the capital moved inland to Konya (Iconium). Under sultans such as Kilij Arslan II, who defeated the Byzantines at Myriokephalon (1176), and especially Ala al-Din Kayqubad I (r. 1220–1237), the sultanate reached its height: it controlled ports on both the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts, built a network of caravanserais along the trade routes, and patronised Persianate court culture and architecture.

The Mongol victory at the Battle of Köse Dağ (1243) reduced the sultanate to a Mongol vassal. Thereafter power drained to the Ilkhanate and to the semi-independent Anatolian beyliks on the frontier, among them the small beylik of Osman — the future Ottoman Empire. The sultanate effectively ended in 1308 with the death of the last sultan, Mesud II.

Culture

The Seljuks of Rum built distinctive mosques, madrasas, tombs, and caravanserais decorated with carved stone and tilework. Persian was the language of administration and literature — the mystic poet Jalal al-Din Rumi, after whom the sultanate is partly named in popular memory, lived and wrote in Konya — while the population remained religiously and linguistically mixed.
