Peace of Thorn (1466)

Signed19 October 1466
LocationThorn (Toruń), Prussia
PartiesKingdom of Poland; the Teutonic Order
EffectEnds the Thirteen Years' War; western Prussia ceded to Poland
Also calledSecond Peace of Toruń

The Peace of Thorn of 1466 — known as the Second Peace of Toruń to distinguish it from the treaty of 1411 — ended the Thirteen Years' War between the Kingdom of Poland and the Teutonic Order. Signed at Thorn (Polish: Toruń) on 19 October 1466, it transferred the western part of the Order's Prussian state to the Polish Crown and reduced the Grand Master to a Polish vassal for the remainder, decisively shifting the balance of power in the Baltic.

Background

In 1454 the Prussian Confederation — an alliance of Prussian cities and nobles resisting the Teutonic Order's taxation and rule — renounced allegiance to the Order and offered Prussia to King Casimir IV of Poland, who incorporated it into his kingdom. The ensuing war lasted thirteen years. The Order, though victorious in some early battles, was ground down financially: dependent on mercenaries it could not pay, it watched its castles, including its headquarters at Marienburg, fall to Polish and Confederation forces — Marienburg itself was handed over by unpaid Bohemian mercenaries in 1457. The Polish victory at the Battle of Żarnowiec (1462) and the naval Battle of the Vistula Lagoon (1463) left the Order exhausted and ready to negotiate under papal and imperial mediation.

Terms

Under the peace, the Teutonic Order ceded to Poland in full sovereignty the western part of Prussia — Pomerelia with Danzig (Gdańsk), the Chełmno and Michałów lands, and the territories of Elbing (Elbląg), Marienburg (Malbork) and Stuhm — which became the Polish province of Royal Prussia, together with the bishopric of Warmia, whose prince-bishops came under Polish protection. The Order retained eastern Prussia (the future Ducal Prussia), but the Grand Master held it as a fief of the Polish Crown, swore fealty to the King of Poland, and became a senator of the kingdom; the Order was to provide military aid to Poland, and its membership was opened to Poles. Toruń itself, birthplace a few years later of Nicolaus Copernicus, became one of the great cities of Royal Prussia.

Consequences

The peace ended the Teutonic Order's status as a great power and gave Poland secure access to the Baltic and the Vistula trade, underpinning the prosperity of Gdańsk and Toruń. The arrangement endured until 1525, when the last Grand Master, Albert of Hohenzollern, secularised the Order's remaining state as the Duchy of Prussia and paid homage to the Polish king in Kraków. Disputes over the treaty's interpretation — particularly Polish claims to the fief — echoed in Polish–Prussian relations until the partitions of Poland. The first Peace of Thorn (1411), which had closed the Polish–Lithuanian–Teutonic War after Grunwald with only minor territorial changes, makes the 1466 settlement plainly the more consequential of the two.
