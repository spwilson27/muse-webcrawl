The East Germanic languages are a branch of the Germanic languages, now entirely extinct, spoken by the East Germanic peoples who migrated from Scandinavia — according to tradition, from the island of Gotland and the coast opposite — to the region between the Oder and Vistula rivers, and later across much of Europe. The only East Germanic language attested in substantial texts is Gothic.

Languages

The branch is conventionally held to comprise:

  • Gothic — the language of the Visigoths and Ostrogoths, attested in Bishop Ulfilas's fourth-century translation of the Bible, preserved above all in the Codex Argenteus. A descendant, Crimean Gothic, survived in the Crimea into the 16th–18th centuries and is known from a word list recorded by the Flemish diplomat Ogier de Busbecq.
  • Vandalic — the language of the Vandals, known only from a handful of words and names.
  • Burgundian — the language of the Burgundians, likewise almost unattested beyond personal names and a few words.

Characteristics

East Germanic is distinguished from North and West Germanic by a number of shared innovations and retentions. Unlike North and West Germanic, it did not undergo the change of final -z to -r (rhotacisation): Gothic retains -s where Old Norse has -r. Gothic also shows no trace of the i-umlaut that reshaped the other branches. A characteristic East Germanic development is the sharpening (gemination) of certain consonants, and the treatment of Proto-Germanic long vowels differs from that in West Germanic.

History

East Germanic peoples — Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, Rugii, Heruli and others — pressed south and east from the Baltic coast in the early centuries AD. Gothic-speaking kingdoms were founded in Italy, southern France, Spain and North Africa during the Migration Period, but their languages were everywhere displaced by Latin and its Romance descendants; Crimean Gothic, isolated in the Black Sea region, was the branch's last survivor.
