New school hip hop

Stylistic originsOld-school hip hop, electro-funk, drum machines
Cultural originsMid-1980s, New York City
Typical instrumentsDrum machine, sampler, turntables, vocals
Other namesNew school rap

The new school of hip hop was the second wave of hip hop music, originating in the mid-1980s in New York City. It succeeded the disco- and party-oriented old school with a harder, minimalist sound built on drum machines, sparse beats, and assertive, often socially conscious or boastful rhyming.

Characteristics and history

The new school is usually dated from 1983–84, with Run-D.M.C.'s self-titled debut album (1984) and LL Cool J's Radio (1985), both produced with heavy involvement from Rick Rubin and released on Def Jam Recordings. Drum machines such as the Oberheim DMX and Roland TR-808, aggressive scratching, and stripped-down arrangements replaced the live disco bands and long party jams of the old school. Rapping became denser and more rhythmic, and the album replaced the 12-inch single as the genre's artistic unit.

Key new school artists include Run-D.M.C., LL Cool J, the Beastie Boys, Eric B. & Rakim, Boogie Down Productions, Big Daddy Kane, Public Enemy, and the Ultramagnetic MCs. The era marks hip hop's commercial breakthrough — Run-D.M.C.'s collaboration with Aerosmith on "Walk This Way" (1986) crossed rap into the rock mainstream — and its growing political voice, as in Public Enemy's It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back (1988). By about 1989–90, denser sample-based production and the advent of the "golden age" and gangsta rap currents brought the new school period to a close.
