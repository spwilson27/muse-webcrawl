North–South divide
Also calledGlobal North and Global South

Overview

The North–South divide is a socio-economic and political categorisation of countries that distinguishes the wealthier, industrialised states of the "Global North" from the poorer, developing states of the "Global South". The divide is not strictly geographical: most Northern countries lie in the northern hemisphere, but Australia and New Zealand, in the south, are classed with the North, while many Southern countries lie north of the equator.

Origins

The terminology gained currency with the 1980 report of the Independent Commission on International Development Issues, chaired by Willy Brandt. The "Brandt Line" drawn on world maps separated rich from poor countries roughly at a latitude of 30 degrees north, looping around Australia and New Zealand.

The concept reflects differences in income, industrialisation, life expectancy, education, and political influence in international institutions.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics argue that the binary division oversimplifies a diverse world, obscures rapid development in countries such as China and India, and has been partly superseded by other frameworks. Nevertheless, the terms "Global North" and "Global South" remain common in development studies and international diplomacy.
