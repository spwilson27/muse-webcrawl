In macroeconomics, a multiplier measures how much an endogenous variable changes in response to a change in some exogenous variable — most famously, how much national income rises following an initial increase in spending. The concept was introduced by Richard Kahn in 1931 as an employment multiplier and was placed at the heart of John Maynard Keynes's General Theory (1936), where the investment multiplier explains how an initial injection of investment or government spending propagates through the economy in successive rounds of consumption.

Mechanism

Suppose the government spends an additional unit of currency. The recipients gain income and, according to their marginal propensity to consume (MPC), spend a fraction of it; the next recipients do the same, and so on, until the increments fade to nothing. The total increase in income is the initial spending divided by the marginal propensity to save:

k = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS

With an MPC of 0.8, the multiplier is 5. In practice, leakages into taxation and imports reduce the multiplier — an effect formalised in the fiscal multiplier used to assess stimulus programmes — and estimates of real-world multipliers vary widely with economic conditions, tending to be larger in recessions and smaller near full capacity. Variants include the money multiplier of fractional-reserve banking and the balanced-budget multiplier, which Keynesian analysis finds equal to one.

Criticism

Critics from monetarist and new classical schools argue that multiplier effects are offset by "crowding out" — higher interest rates or inflation reducing private spending — and by households saving in anticipation of future taxes (Ricardian equivalence). The size of the fiscal multiplier remains one of the most contested empirical questions in macroeconomics, central to debates over austerity after the 2008 financial crisis.
