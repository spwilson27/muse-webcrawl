The Fairchild Republic A-10 Thunderbolt II is a single-seat, twin-turbofan, straight-wing, subsonic attack aircraft developed by Fairchild Republic for the United States Air Force (USAF). In service since 1977, it is named after the Republic P-47 Thunderbolt of World War II, but is commonly referred to by the nickname "Warthog" or simply "Hog".

The A-10 was designed for close air support (CAS) of friendly ground troops, attacking armoured vehicles and tanks, and providing quick-action support against enemy ground forces. Its airframe was designed for durability, with measures such as 1,200 pounds (540 kg) of titanium armour to protect the cockpit and aircraft systems, enabling it to absorb a significant amount of damage and continue flying.

Development

The A-10 arose from the USAF's A-X programme of the late 1960s, prompted by the poor performance of fast jets in the close-support role in Vietnam and the threat of massed Warsaw Pact armour in Europe. Fairchild Republic's YA-10 won the 1973 fly-off against Northrop's YA-9. Production ran from 1975 to 1984, with 716 aircraft built.

Design

The aircraft is built around the enormous GAU-8 Avenger 30 mm rotary cannon, which fires depleted-uranium armour-piercing shells at up to 3,900 rounds per minute — the gun is so large that the nose landing gear is offset to accommodate it. The straight wing carries 11 hardpoints for up to 16,000 lb (7,260 kg) of ordnance including AGM-65 Maverick missiles, cluster bombs and precision-guided munitions. Twin General Electric TF34 engines are mounted high on the rear fuselage to reduce vulnerability to ground fire. Redundant flight controls, self-sealing fuel tanks and a titanium "bathtub" around the pilot prioritise survivability.

Operational history

The A-10 first saw combat in the 1991 Gulf War, destroying more than 900 Iraqi tanks and thousands of other vehicles. It has since served in Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya and Syria. Repeated Air Force attempts to retire the type early were resisted by Congress; upgraded A-10C models with precision weapons and new wings are planned to serve into the 2030s, though divestment of older airframes has begun.
