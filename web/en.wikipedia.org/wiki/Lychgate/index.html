Lychgate

A lychgate (also spelled lichgate or lych gate) is a roofed gateway at the entrance to a traditional English churchyard, under which a corpse was rested on a bier at the start of a funeral service. The name comes from the Old English līc, meaning a corpse.

Function

In medieval and early modern England, mourners bringing a body for burial would stop at the lychgate, where the priest met the corpse and began the burial service, reciting part of the office before the procession entered consecrated ground. Some lychgates incorporate a stone slab or bench — a "lych stone" — for resting the coffin. In parts of England, churchwardens were once obliged to maintain the lychgate, as the parish was responsible for the corpse from the churchyard gate.

Architecture and survival

Lychgates are typically built of timber framing or stone with a pitched roof of tile or slate, sometimes with a small chamber above (used historically as a mortuary or schoolroom). Few genuinely medieval examples survive; many English lychgates date from the Victorian Gothic Revival. After the First World War, numerous lychgates were dedicated as war memorials, often bearing the names of the parish's fallen.

Spread

The form was carried by British settlers and church tradition to Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and elsewhere in the Anglophone world, where lychgates remain a common feature of Anglican churchyards.
