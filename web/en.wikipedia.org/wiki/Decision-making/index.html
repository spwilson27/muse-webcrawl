Decision-making

Decision-making
FieldsPsychology, management, economics, cognitive science
Key conceptsBounded rationality, heuristics, bias, expected utility
Key figuresHerbert A. Simon, Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky

In psychology, decision-making is the cognitive process resulting in the selection of a belief or a course of action among several possible alternative options. Every decision-making process produces a final choice, which may or may not prompt action. Research about decision-making is published under many labels, including problem solving, judgment and choice, and is studied by psychologists, economists, and management scientists.

Overview

Decision-making can be regarded as a problem-solving activity yielding a solution deemed to be optimal, or at least satisfactory. It is therefore a process which can be more or less rational or irrational and can be based on explicit or tacit knowledge and beliefs. Herbert A. Simon proposed the notion of bounded rationality: decision-makers aim to satisfice — to choose a solution that is "good enough" — because they cannot process all available information.

Heuristics and biases

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people rely on cognitive shortcuts, or heuristics, which usually work well but can produce systematic errors called cognitive biases. Examples include the availability heuristic, anchoring, loss aversion, and the framing effect. Kahneman later popularised a two-system account: fast, intuitive "System 1" thinking and slow, deliberative "System 2" thinking.

Group decision-making

Groups can pool knowledge and correct individual errors, but they are vulnerable to groupthink, social loafing, and polarisation. Structured techniques such as the Delphi method, nominal group technique, and devil's advocacy are used to improve group decisions in organisations and government.

Decision-making in management

Managers classify decisions as programmed (routine, structured) or non-programmed (novel, unstructured). Frameworks such as the Vroom–Yetton model advise when to decide autocratically and when to involve subordinates. In naturalistic settings, Gary Klein's recognition-primed decision model describes how experts — firefighters, pilots, nurses — make rapid decisions by pattern-matching to prior experience.
