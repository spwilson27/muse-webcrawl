Sea lions are marine mammals of the family Otariidae, the "eared seals", which they share with the fur seals. Six species survive: the Steller (or northern) sea lion, Australian sea lion, South American sea lion, New Zealand sea lion, California sea lion and Galápagos sea lion; the Japanese sea lion was driven to extinction in the 20th century. Sea lions are found from subarctic to tropical waters of both hemispheres, except the northern Atlantic.

Characteristics

Sea lions differ from true (earless) seals in having small external ear flaps, long hairless foreflippers used for propulsion in swimming, and hind flippers that rotate forward so the animal can move quadrupedally on land. They are strongly sexually dimorphic: males may weigh three to four times as much as females and develop thickened necks and sagittal crests — the male South American sea lion's mane-like neck gives the group its name. Sea lions feed on fish, squid and occasionally crustaceans and even penguins, and are preyed on by killer whales and large sharks.

Behaviour

Sea lions are social, hauling out in large groups on beaches and rocks and breeding in rookeries where territorial males defend harems. The California sea lion (Zalophus californianus) is the species most often seen in zoos and aquariums, noted for its intelligence and trainability; it has famously colonised San Francisco's Pier 39 since 1989.

Conservation

Several species are threatened: the New Zealand and Australian sea lions are endangered, and the western population of Steller sea lions declined sharply in the late 20th century for reasons debated among scientists (fishing competition, predation and regime shifts are all proposed). Sea lions also interact with fisheries, raiding nets and aquaculture, which brings them into conflict with people.
