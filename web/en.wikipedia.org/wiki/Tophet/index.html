Tophet

MeaningBiblical place in the Valley of Hinnom; also Punic child burial precincts
Key sitesCarthage, Motya, Sulcis, Nora
Periodc. 8th–2nd century BCE (Punic sites)

Tophet (Hebrew: תֹּפֶת, Tōpheth) is a location in Jerusalem in the Valley of Hinnom (Gehenna) where, according to the Hebrew Bible, Israelites sacrificed children by fire to the gods Moloch and Baal — a practice condemned by the prophets and said to have been defiled by King Josiah. Jeremiah describes the Israelites building "the high places of Tophet" to burn their sons and daughters in the fire.

Punic tophets

The term was adopted by archaeologists for the open-air precincts found in Carthage and other Phoenician-Punic settlements in Sicily, Sardinia and North Africa, containing thousands of urns with the cremated remains of infants and small animals, many surmounted by carved stelae with dedications to Baal Hammon and Tanit. The Carthage tophet, in use from about 760 BCE until the city's destruction in 146 BCE, held an estimated 20,000 urns.

Debate

Greek and Roman authors such as Diodorus Siculus and Plutarch described Carthaginian child sacrifice in times of crisis — Diodorus tells of 500 children offered in 310 BCE — but the scale and nature of the rite remain among archaeology's fiercest debates. Some scholars argue the tophets were simply cemeteries for stillborn infants and children who died naturally; others hold that osteological analysis of cremated bones, the animal substitutions in urns, and the inscriptions' vow language support genuine sacrifice of some children as the molk offering known from Punic texts.
