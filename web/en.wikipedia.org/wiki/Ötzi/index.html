Ötzi, also called the Iceman, is the natural mummy of a man who lived around 3300 BCE in the Copper Age. His remarkably preserved body, found in a glacier in the Ötztal Alps on the Austria–Italy border, is the oldest known natural human mummy in Europe and has yielded an extraordinary record of Neolithic life.

Discovery

German tourists Helmut and Erika Simon found the body on 19 September 1991 at about 3,210 m near the Tisenjoch pass, initially assuming it was a deceased mountaineer. Recovered over the following days, the corpse — technically just inside Italy — was eventually housed at the South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology in Bolzano, where it is kept in a refrigerated chamber and viewed through a small window.

Equipment and body

Ötzi carried a nearly pure copper axe with a yew haft — evidence that pushed back the chronology of European copper metallurgy — a flint dagger, a bow and arrows, a backpack frame, and clothing of goat, deer, and bear skin, including a woven grass cape and shoes stuffed with grass. His body bears 61 tattoos, the oldest known, made by rubbing charcoal into small cuts. Analysis of his stomach revealed last meals of ibex and red deer meat, einkorn wheat, and fat.

Death and genome

CT scans revealed a flint arrowhead lodged in his left shoulder: Ötzi bled to death after being shot, and also suffered a blow to the head. His genome, sequenced in 2012, showed ancestry closest to modern Sardinians and Corsicans, lactose intolerance, a predisposition to cardiovascular disease, and traces of the Lyme-disease bacterium Borrelia. The circumstances of his death — and the "curse" folklore that grew around early deaths among researchers — remain subjects of fascination.
