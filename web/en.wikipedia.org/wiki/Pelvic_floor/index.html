Pelvic floor

Muscles of the pelvic floor, inferior view.

The pelvic floor or pelvic diaphragm is a layer of muscles and connective tissue spanning the bottom of the pelvic cavity. It supports the pelvic organs—the bladder, intestines, and in females the uterus and vagina—and plays a key role in urinary and faecal continence, sexual function, and childbirth.

Structure

The principal muscles are the levator ani group—puborectalis, pubococcygeus, and iliococcygeus—together with the coccygeus (ischiococcygeus) posteriorly. These muscles form a funnel-shaped sling attached to the pubic bones, ischial spines, and the arcus tendineus. Structures passing through the pelvic floor include the urethra, the rectum and anal canal, and in females the vagina, passing through the urogenital hiatus. The perineum lies below the pelvic floor.

Function

The pelvic floor maintains continence by supporting the bladder and rectum and assisting the urethral and anal sphincters; it resists increases in intra-abdominal pressure during coughing, lifting, and exertion; and in females it stretches substantially during vaginal delivery. Its muscles also contribute to core stability and sexual function.

Clinical significance

Weakness or injury of the pelvic floor—from childbirth, ageing, chronic straining, or surgery—can cause pelvic organ prolapse, urinary or faecal incontinence, and chronic pelvic pain. Excessive tension can also produce dysfunction. Treatment includes pelvic floor muscle training (Kegel exercises), physiotherapy, pessaries, and reconstructive surgery.
