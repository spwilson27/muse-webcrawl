Gertrude Jekyll (29 November 1843 – 8 December 1932) was a British horticulturist, garden designer, craftswoman, photographer and writer. She designed or advised on some 400 gardens in the United Kingdom, Europe and the United States, and wrote more than 1,000 articles and a series of influential books. Her work with the architect Edwin Lutyens — in which his strong formal layouts were softened by her painterly planting — helped define the Arts and Crafts garden.

Life and career

Jekyll trained as an artist and was deeply influenced by the Arts and Crafts movement and by J. M. W. Turner's use of colour. Failing eyesight ended her ambitions as a painter and turned her to gardening at her home, Munstead Wood in Surrey, where she experimented with hardy herbaceous borders planted in drifts of graduated colour. She bred and selected plant varieties and ran a nursery supplying her designs.

Design philosophy

Jekyll treated the garden as a series of pictures, applying colour theory to planting: cool blues and greys at a border's ends, hot reds and oranges at its centre, with careful attention to foliage texture and succession through the seasons. Her books, including Wood and Garden (1899) and Colour Schemes for the Flower Garden (1914), made her principles available to ordinary gardeners, and she contributed extensively to Country Life magazine. Gardens such as Hestercombe in Somerset and Lindisfarne Castle have been restored following her surviving plans.
