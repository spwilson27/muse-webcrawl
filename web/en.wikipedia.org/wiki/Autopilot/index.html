Autopilot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An airliner cockpit with glowing flight displays in cruise flight]
A modern glass cockpit.

An autopilot is a system used to control the path of a vehicle without constant manual input from a human operator, most developed in aircraft, where it can fly entire routes and even land automatically (autoland). Lawrence Sperry demonstrated the first successful aircraft autopilot in 1914; Wiley Post used one on his 1933 solo round-the-world flight, and modern flight-management systems integrate autopilot, autothrottle and navigation.

Operation

Autopilots sense attitude, heading and altitude and move control surfaces via servos to follow selected modes (heading, altitude hold, navigation tracking, ILS approach). Transport aircraft typically have two or three redundant autopilots; autoland requires fail-operational systems and specially approved runways and crews (CAT III operations in fog).

Use and limits

Pilots engage autopilot for most of cruise, reducing workload but risking skill fade and mode confusion — factors in accidents such as Air France 447 (2009, after autopilot disconnection) and the 2013 Asiana 214 crash. Regulations require pilots to remain able to fly manually.

Beyond aviation

The term extends to ships (autohelms, dynamic positioning), spacecraft (fully autonomous rendezvous), and cars, where \'Autopilot\' is Tesla\'s brand for its Level 2 driver-assistance suite — a naming choice regulators have criticised as overstating capability.
