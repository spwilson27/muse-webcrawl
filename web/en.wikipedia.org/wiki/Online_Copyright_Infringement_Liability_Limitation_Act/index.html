Online Copyright Infringement Liability Limitation Act

The Online Copyright Infringement Liability Limitation Act (OCILLA) is Title II of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), enacted in 1998 and codified at 17 U.S.C. § 512. It creates a conditional "safe harbor" limiting the liability of online service providers for copyright infringement by their users.

Safe harbors

Section 512 sets out four categories of activity protected when providers meet statutory conditions: transitory digital network communications, system caching, information residing on systems at the direction of users, and information location tools such as search engines and links. The hosting safe harbor is the most consequential: to qualify, a provider must lack actual or "red flag" knowledge of infringement, not benefit financially from infringement it can control, adopt a policy for terminating repeat infringers, and accommodate standard technical measures.

Notice and takedown

OCILLA established the notice-and-takedown system: copyright owners send notices identifying infringing material, and providers must act expeditiously to remove it to preserve their safe harbor. Users may send counter-notices asserting their material was wrongly removed, after which the provider generally restores it in 10–14 business days unless the owner sues. Knowingly misrepresenting material as infringing can create liability under § 512(f).

Impact

The system underpins user-generated content platforms such as YouTube and social media, and has been the subject of major litigation (for example Viacom v. YouTube) and continuing debate about over-removal, automated filters, and the balance between creators and intermediaries. Similar regimes were adopted in the European Union's E-Commerce Directive and later the Digital Services Act.
