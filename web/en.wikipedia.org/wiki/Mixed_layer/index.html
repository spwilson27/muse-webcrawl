Mixed layer


Depth profile of temperature and density through the ocean mixed layer.

The mixed layer is a layer of the ocean (or, less commonly, a lake or the atmosphere) in which active turbulence has homogenised some range of depths. In the ocean, the surface mixed layer is the uppermost layer, mixed by wind stress, waves and surface cooling, so that temperature, salinity and density are nearly uniform with depth.

The depth of the oceanic mixed layer varies from a few metres in calm summer conditions to several hundred metres in high-latitude winter convection. Below it lies the thermocline, where temperature changes rapidly with depth.

Properties

The mixed layer is characterised by:

  • Nearly constant temperature and salinity with depth
  • Nearly constant density (the pycnocline lies at its base)
  • High levels of turbulence generated by wind and buoyancy fluxes

Biologically, the mixed layer is important because it controls the light and nutrient environment of phytoplankton: deep mixing can carry cells below the euphotic zone, while shallow stratification retains them in sunlit water. Mixed layer depth is also a key variable in climate models, because the layer acts as a thermal flywheel moderating the ocean's response to seasonal heating.

Formation

Mixing is driven by wind stirring, surface wave action (including Langmuir circulation), and convective overturning when surface cooling or evaporation makes surface water denser than the water below. The Ekman layer in the atmosphere and the planetary boundary layer are analogous concepts.
