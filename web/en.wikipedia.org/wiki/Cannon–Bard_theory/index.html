Cannon–Bard theory

The Cannon–Bard theory of emotion is a physiological theory developed by Walter Bradford Cannon and his doctoral student Philip Bard in the 1920s. It holds that emotional experience and physiological arousal occur simultaneously and independently: an emotional stimulus is relayed by the thalamus at once to the cortex, producing the feeling of emotion, and to the sympathetic nervous system, producing bodily arousal.

Background

Cannon set out his critique of the reigning James–Lange theory in a 1927 paper, "The James–Lange Theory of Emotions: A Critical Examination and an Alternative Theory", in the American Journal of Psychology. Bard's subsequent experiments on decorticate cats in the late 1920s, showing coordinated "sham rage" responses organized below the cortex, supplied supporting evidence and pointed to the diencephalon as the seat of emotional expression.

Cannon's criticisms of James–Lange

Cannon argued that the James–Lange view — that emotion is the perception of bodily change — failed on several grounds: animals whose viscera were surgically disconnected from the brain still displayed emotion; the same visceral changes occur in very different emotional and non-emotional states; the viscera are relatively insensitive; visceral changes are too slow to account for the immediacy of feeling; and artificially inducing visceral changes, for example by injecting adrenaline, does not produce genuine emotion.

The theory

In the Cannon–Bard account, sensory input reaches the thalamus, which simultaneously signals the cerebral cortex (yielding the subjective experience of emotion) and the hypothalamus and sympathetic nervous system (yielding arousal and action readiness). Feeling afraid and trembling therefore happen at the same time, rather than one causing the other.

Legacy

The theory redirected emotion research toward the brain, anticipating later work on the limbic system and Papez circuit. It was itself challenged by the Schachter–Singer two-factor theory (1962), which reintroduced cognitive appraisal and bodily arousal as joint determinants of emotion, and by appraisal theories generally.
