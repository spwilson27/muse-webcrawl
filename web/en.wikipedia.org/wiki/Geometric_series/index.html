Geometric series

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a geometric series is the sum of an infinite number of terms that have a constant ratio between successive terms. For example, the series

1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + ⋯

is geometric, because each successive term can be obtained by multiplying the previous term by 1/2. In general, a geometric series is written as

a + ar + ar2 + ar3 + ⋯,

where a is the first term (the coefficient of each term) and r is the common ratio between adjacent terms.

Sum

The sum of a finite geometric series with n terms is

Sn = a (1 − rn) / (1 − r),   for r ≠ 1.

When |r| < 1, the infinite series converges and its sum is

S = a / (1 − r).

When |r| ≥ 1, the series diverges (except for the trivial case a = 0). The partial-sum formula can be derived by multiplying Sn by r and subtracting from Sn, which telescopes the sum.

Examples

  • 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ⋯ = 2 (a = 1, r = 1/2).
  • The repeating decimal 0.777… equals 7/9, treating it as a geometric series with a = 7/10 and r = 1/10.
  • The series 1 − 1/2 + 1/4 − 1/8 + ⋯ sums to 2/3 (a = 1, r = −1/2).

History

Geometric series appear already in ancient mathematics. Euclid's Elements (Book IX, Proposition 35) gives a version of the partial-sum formula. Archimedes used a geometric series with ratio 1/4 to compute the area under a parabola in his quadrature of the parabola. In the 17th century, the convergence of infinite geometric series was a key stepping stone toward the development of calculus, and Nicole Oresme had earlier given elegant proofs of divergence for the harmonic series by comparison arguments.

Applications

Geometric series arise in economics (present value of annuities and perpetuities), physics (damped oscillations, radioactive decay chains), computer science (analysis of divide-and-conquer algorithms), and in Zeno's paradoxes of motion, where the dichotomy paradox is resolved by noting that infinitely many halved distances sum to a finite total.
