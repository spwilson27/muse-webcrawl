Kol Nidre

Chanting Kol Nidre at the opening of Yom Kippur (image placeholder)

Kol Nidre (Aramaic: כָּל נִדְרֵי, "all vows") is a declaration recited in synagogue at the opening of the evening service on Yom Kippur, the Jewish Day of Atonement. Strictly speaking it is not a prayer but a legal formula in Aramaic that annuls vows — traditionally understood as vows made rashly or under duress between the individual and God, not obligations toward other people. Sung before sunset, while the congregation stands and Torah scrolls are held at the ark, it is among the most solemn and best-known moments of the Jewish liturgical year.

The declaration

The formula asks that vows, oaths, prohibitions and obligations assumed during the year — in some versions those of the past year, in others those of the coming year — be declared null and void if made inadvertently or rashly. Maimonides and other authorities stressed that Kol Nidre cannot release a person from vows or obligations owed to another person, debts, or solemn court oaths; its scope is limited to vows between the individual and God, and its recitation is intended to begin the Day of Atonement with a clean slate. The declaration is traditionally chanted three times, growing in volume.

History and controversy

The formula originated in the post-Talmudic (Geonic) period in Babylonia, likely in the early medieval centuries, and several Geonim opposed it as an unlawful attempt to dissolve vows wholesale. It nevertheless spread through the Jewish world and became fixed at the head of the Yom Kippur service. Because of its wording it was sometimes misrepresented in antisemitic polemic as licence for Jews to break promises to non-Jews — a charge rabbinic authorities have always denied, pointing to the formula's restriction to vows between a person and God. Some Reform prayer books in the 19th century omitted Kol Nidre, but its emotional and musical power led to its restoration, and it is retained in most contemporary liturgies of all movements.

Music

The traditional Ashkenazi melody for Kol Nidre is one of the most famous pieces of Jewish liturgical music, part of the body of "MiSinai" tunes said to date from medieval German Jewry. In 1914 the cellist and composer Max Bruch's Kol Nidrei, Op. 47 (1880–81), an adagio for cello and orchestra based on the chant, had already made the melody famous in concert halls; it remains a staple of the cello repertoire. Settings and adaptations have also been made by many cantors and by composers such as Arnold Schoenberg (Kol Nidre, Op. 39, 1938).
