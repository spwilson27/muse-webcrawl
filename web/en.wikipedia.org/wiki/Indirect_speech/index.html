Indirect speech

Indirect speech, also called reported speech or indirect discourse, is a grammatical mechanism for reporting the content of another person's utterance without quoting it verbatim. For example, the direct statement "I am leaving tomorrow" becomes, in indirect speech, "She said that she was leaving the next day."

Changes involved

Converting direct to indirect speech typically involves changes in pronouns (first and second person become third person), verb tense (often shifted back, e.g. present to past, when the reporting verb is past — known as backshift), and deictic expressions of time and place ("now" → "then", "here" → "there", "tomorrow" → "the next day"). Questions are reported with structures such as "He asked whether…" or "She asked where…", and commands with infinitives, as in "He told her to sit down."

Related forms

Free indirect speech (free indirect discourse) is a narrative technique in fiction that blends third-person narration with the voice and perspective of a character, used prominently by authors such as Jane Austen, Gustave Flaubert, and Virginia Woolf.
