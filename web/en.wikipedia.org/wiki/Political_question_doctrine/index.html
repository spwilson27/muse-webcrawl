Political question doctrine

The Supreme Court of the United States, originator of the political question doctrine

In United States constitutional law, the political question doctrine holds that certain issues are constitutionally assigned to the legislative or executive branches of government, or are otherwise unsuited to judicial resolution, and therefore are not justiciable by the federal courts. The doctrine is rooted in the separation of powers and in concerns about the judiciary's institutional competence and legitimacy.

Origins

The doctrine traces to Chief Justice John Marshall's opinion in Marbury v. Madison (1803), which distinguished between acts of executive discretion, reviewable only politically, and legal duties that courts could enforce. Early applications included Luther v. Borden (1849), in which the Supreme Court declined to decide which of two rival governments was the legitimate government of Rhode Island, treating the matter as committed to Congress under the Guarantee Clause.

The Baker factors

The modern framework comes from Baker v. Carr (1962). Although the Court held that legislative apportionment was justiciable, Justice Brennan's opinion identified six factors that may mark an issue as a nonjusticiable political question: a textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department; a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving it; the impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination of a kind clearly for nonjudicial discretion; the impossibility of a court's undertaking independent resolution without expressing lack of respect due coordinate branches; an unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made; and the potentiality of embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements by various departments on one question.

Applications

The doctrine has been applied in areas such as foreign affairs, the impeachment process (Nixon v. United States, 1993), and partisan gerrymandering, which the Court held nonjusticiable in federal court in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019). It remains one of the principal doctrines—alongside standing, ripeness, and mootness—by which federal courts limit the scope of judicial review.
