Defilade

Defilade is a military term describing a unit or position that is protected from direct enemy fire by natural or artificial obstacles, such as a hill, ridge or embankment. A position in defilade is shielded from flat-trajectory weapons and from direct observation, although it may remain exposed to indirect fire such as mortars and artillery firing over the obstacle.

A vehicle in a hull-down position exploits defilade offered by rising ground

Use in fortification

In the design of fortifications from the sixteenth century onward, defilade — in French, défilement — was a central problem: the trace of a fortress had to be arranged so that no part of its interior could be seen or struck by an attacker occupying surrounding ground. Engineers achieved defilade by lowering works, exploiting reverse slopes, and raising parapets and traverses. The term is related to enfilade, its opposite, in which a position is exposed to fire along its length.

Armoured warfare

In armoured warfare, a tank positioned behind a crest with only its turret exposed is said to be hull-down; if no part of the vehicle is exposed but its commander can still observe, it may be described as turret-down. Modern fighting vehicles and their crews use micro-terrain, folds in the ground and prepared scrapes to obtain defilade, and fire-control systems may allow engagement from defilade positions using indirect or mast-mounted sensors.
