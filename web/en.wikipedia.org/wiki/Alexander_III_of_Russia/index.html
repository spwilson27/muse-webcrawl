Alexander III of Russia

Alexander III (Russian: Алекса́ндр III Алекса́ндрович, romanized: Aleksandr III Aleksandrovich; 10 March 1845 – 1 November 1894) was Emperor of Russia, King of Congress Poland and Grand Duke of Finland from 1881 until his death. Coming to the throne after the assassination of his father, Alexander II, he reversed many of his father's liberalising reforms, championing "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality" and a programme of Russification, for which he is remembered by the epithet "the Peacemaker" (Миротво́рец) because no major wars were fought during his reign.

Reign

Alexander had become heir only in 1865, after the death of his elder brother Nicholas. Influenced by his tutor, the conservative Konstantin Pobedonostsev, he dismantled plans for consultative assemblies, strengthened police powers through the Okhrana, increased censorship, restored controls over universities and local self-government (zemstvos and municipal dumas), and imposed discriminatory May Laws and quotas on Jews while pressing Russian language and Orthodoxy in Poland, Finland and the Baltic provinces. In 1888 he and his family survived the Borki train disaster, an event later commemorated by a cathedral in Kharkiv.

Abroad he kept Russia at peace, signed the Reinsurance Treaty with Germany (1887) and, when that lapsed, concluded the Franco-Russian Alliance (1892–94) that became one of the alignments of the First World War. Under finance minister Sergei Witte the empire began rapid industrialisation and construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway (begun 1891). Alexander died of kidney disease at Livadia in 1894 and was succeeded by his son Nicholas II, the last tsar.
