Trypanosoma is a genus of kinetoplastids (class Trypanosomatidae), a monophyletic group of unicellular parasitic flagellate protozoa. Trypanosoma parasites infect a variety of hosts and cause various diseases, including the fatal human diseases sleeping sickness, caused by Trypanosoma brucei, and Chagas disease, caused by Trypanosoma cruzi.

Description

Trypanosomes are elongated, motile cells, typically 15–30 micrometres long, with a single flagellum that runs along the body attached by an undulating membrane. They possess a characteristic large mass of mitochondrial DNA, the kinetoplast, near the base of the flagellum. Most species require two hosts in their life cycle: a vertebrate, in whose blood or tissues they live, and an invertebrate vector, usually a blood-feeding insect.

Important species

  • Trypanosoma brucei gambiense and T. b. rhodesiense cause African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) and are transmitted by tsetse flies (Glossina).
  • Trypanosoma cruzi causes Chagas disease in the Americas and is transmitted by triatomine ("kissing") bugs, as well as congenitally and by transfusion.
  • Trypanosoma congolense, T. vivax and T. brucei brucei cause nagana, a wasting disease of livestock in Africa.
  • Trypanosoma evansi causes surra in animals in Asia, Africa and South America.
  • Trypanosoma equiperdum causes dourine in horses, transmitted venereally.

Immune evasion

T. brucei evades the host immune system by antigenic variation: the parasite's surface is covered by a dense coat of variant surface glycoprotein (VSG), and it periodically switches which of a large archive of VSG genes it expresses, staying ahead of the antibody response. T. cruzi, in contrast, lives intracellularly within host cells for much of its life cycle.
