Logia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Logia (Greek: λόγια, "sayings, oracles") is the plural of logion (λόγιον), a term used in New Testament scholarship for individual sayings attributed to Jesus of Nazareth, and for collections of such sayings. Unlike the narrative Gospels, a logia collection consists of sayings, parables, and pronouncements arranged with little or no narrative framework.

Usage in scholarship

The term became prominent through the writings of Papias of Hierapolis (c. 60–130 AD), who, as quoted by Eusebius, said that the apostle Matthew compiled "the logia" in a Hebrew dialect. Scholars have long debated whether Papias meant a sayings collection or a Gospel. The hypothesis of a lost sayings source—the Q source—behind the material shared by the Gospels of Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark is often described as a logia collection. The idea gained force with the discovery at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt, in 1897 and 1903, of Greek papyrus fragments of a sayings gospel, each saying beginning "Jesus says…"; these were later identified as parts of the Gospel of Thomas, a complete Coptic copy of which was found at Nag Hammadi in 1945. The Gospel of Thomas consists of 114 logia with almost no narrative, demonstrating that pure sayings collections circulated in early Christianity.

Form critics classify individual logia into types such as wisdom sayings, prophetic sayings, parables, and "I" sayings, and study how they were transmitted and edited before being incorporated into the written Gospels. The English word is also sometimes used outside biblical studies for collected oracles or utterances of a religious figure.
