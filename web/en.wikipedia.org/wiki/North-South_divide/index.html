North-South divide

[Image: The Brandt line]

The North–South divide is a socio-economic and political categorisation of countries, broadly separating the wealthier, industrialised nations of the northern hemisphere (the "Global North") from the poorer, developing nations concentrated in the tropics and southern hemisphere (the "Global South"). The division was popularised by the 1980 Brandt Report, whose proposed "Brandt line" looped around the globe at roughly 30 degrees north latitude, with notable exceptions such as Australia and New Zealand, placed in the North despite their geography.

Characteristics

The divide is associated with disparities in income, life expectancy, literacy, industrialisation, and political influence. Most states of the Global North are high-income democracies, while the Global South includes much of Africa, Latin America, and Asia. The terms are used by the United Nations, the World Bank, and development scholars, though the rise of economies such as China, India, and the Gulf states has blurred the boundary.

Criticisms

Critics note that the model is geographically imprecise, masks inequality within countries, and has been overtaken by concepts such as emerging markets and the "Global South" as a political identity expressed through groupings like the G77 and BRICS. Related divides are described within individual countries, such as the north–south divide in England and in Italy.
