Phanariotes

Phanariotes (Greek: Φαναριώτες, Fanariótes; Romanian: Fanarioți; Turkish: Fenerliler) were members of prominent Greek families in Phanar (Fener), the chief Greek quarter of Constantinople in the Ottoman Empire, where the Ecumenical Patriarchate is located. Between the late 17th century and 1821, Phanariotes dominated the administration of the Patriarchate, served the Sublime Porte as dragomans (chief interpreters), and were appointed hospodars (princes) of the Danubian Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia. The spelling Phanariotes is one of two common renderings; the subject is also treated under the variant Phanariots.

Rise to power

After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the surviving Byzantine aristocracy and wealthy Greek merchants regrouped around the Patriarchate in Phanar. Their knowledge of European languages made them indispensable as interpreters: from 1661 the office of Grand Dragoman was held by Greeks, beginning with Panagiotis Nikousios, and Alexander Mavrocordatos ("the Exaporite") negotiated the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) for the Porte. In 1711 (Moldavia) and 1716 (Wallachia), after the native princes proved unreliable, the Sultan began appointing Phanariote hospodars, who bought their thrones, ruled for short terms, and farmed taxes — a system remembered in Romania as an era of corruption and heavy fiscal pressure.[1]

Culture and downfall

Phanariote courts in Bucharest and Iași were centres of Greek Enlightenment culture, science, and intrigue alike; the families intermarried and patronised schools and printing. Their ascendancy ended abruptly with the Greek War of Independence in 1821: the Porte executed Patriarch Gregory V and many Phanariotes, expelled Greeks from the dragomanate, and thereafter appointed native princes in the Principalities. In independent Greece and Romania, "Phanariote" became a byword for Byzantine intrigue, though modern historians stress the families' role as cultural intermediaries between the Ottoman world and Europe.
