Death Valley is a desert valley in the northern Mojave Desert of eastern California, one of the hottest and driest places on Earth. It is a long, narrow graben between the Panamint Range and the Amargosa Range, created as tectonic forces pulled the crust apart. Nearly surrounded by mountains that wring moisture from passing air — a quadruple rain shadow — the valley floor receives less than 60 mm of rain a year.

Extremes and features

At Furnace Creek on 10 July 1913 the temperature reached 56.7 °C (134 °F), still the World Meteorological Organization's recognised world record for air temperature, and summer days above 50 °C remain routine; ground temperatures are hotter still. Badwater Basin, a vast salt flat, lies 86 metres below sea level, the lowest point in North America — within sight, remarkably, of Mount Whitney, the highest point in the contiguous United States. Sights include the coloured hills of Artists Palette, the dunes of Mesquite Flat, the sailing stones of Racetrack Playa (rocks that move across the playa leaving tracks, explained by thin ice sheets in winter winds), and occasional wildflower "superblooms" after wet winters. The valley got its English name in 1849 when a party of gold-seekers barely escaped it — "Goodbye, Death Valley". It became a national monument in 1933 and a national park in 1994.
