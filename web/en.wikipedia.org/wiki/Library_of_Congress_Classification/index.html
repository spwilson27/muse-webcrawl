Library of Congress Classification

The Library of Congress Classification (LCC) is a system of library classification developed by the Library of Congress in the United States, beginning in 1897 under the direction of Herbert Putnam and his chief cataloguer Charles Ammi Cutter's successors. It is used by most research and academic libraries in the US and several other countries. The system divides all knowledge into twenty-one basic classes, each identified by a single letter of the alphabet, which are further subdivided by additional letters and numbers.

History and use

The classification was created to organise the Library of Congress's own rapidly growing collections after the library moved into its new building in 1897. Schedules for each class were published separately over the following decades and are continually revised. Unlike the Dewey Decimal Classification, LCC was designed pragmatically for a specific large collection rather than from a philosophical scheme of knowledge, and it provides detailed provision for topics such as law, medicine, and literature by country and period.
