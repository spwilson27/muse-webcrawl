Tree squirrels are the members of the squirrel family (Sciuridae) commonly known as squirrels. They include more than 100 arboreal species that are native to all continents except Antarctica and Oceania. Tree squirrels are rodents characterised by their long, bushy tails, agile climbing, and their habit of nesting in trees, in contrast to the ground squirrels and chipmunks that burrow underground.

Description

Tree squirrels vary widely in size. The smallest, the African pygmy squirrel (Myosciurus pumilio), is only about 10 cm (3.9 in) long, while the largest, the Indian giant squirrel (Ratufa indica), can reach 1 m (3 ft) in length including the tail. Their long tails help with balance when moving through the canopy and can also be used for communication and as a parachute of sorts during leaps.

Their hind feet are double-jointed, allowing them to descend trees headfirst, and their sharp claws give excellent grip on bark. Tree squirrels have excellent vision and a keen sense of smell, which helps them recover cached food.

Distribution and habitat

Tree squirrels are found in a huge variety of wooded habitats, from the boreal forests of North America and Eurasia to tropical rainforests in South America, Africa, and Asia. Well-known genera include Sciurus (the Eurasian red squirrel and the eastern gray squirrel), Tamiasciurus (the American red squirrels), and Ratufa (the Oriental giant squirrels). Flying squirrels (tribe Pteromyini) are specialised tree squirrels capable of gliding between trees using a patagium, a membrane stretched between their limbs.

Diet and behaviour

Tree squirrels are mainly herbivorous, feeding on seeds, nuts, fruits, buds, and bark, though many will opportunistically eat insects, eggs, and small vertebrates. They are famous for scatter-hoarding: burying nuts individually in the ground, a behaviour that aids the dispersal of trees such as oaks and hickories, since forgotten caches may germinate. Many species build dreys — nests of twigs and leaves — in tree forks, or occupy tree cavities.

Relationship with humans

Some tree squirrels, notably the eastern gray squirrel, have been introduced outside their native range, where they may outcompete native species; in Britain and Italy the introduced gray squirrel is blamed for declines of the native red squirrel, partly through competition and partly through the squirrelpox virus, which grays carry without ill effect. In urban parks worldwide, tree squirrels are among the most familiar wild mammals to people.
