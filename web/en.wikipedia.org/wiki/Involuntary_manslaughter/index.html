Involuntary manslaughter

Involuntary manslaughter is the unlawful killing of another human being without intent to kill or to cause serious bodily harm. It is distinguished from voluntary manslaughter — a killing in the heat of passion — and from murder, which requires malice aforethought. The offence typically arises where a death results from negligence, recklessness, or an unlawful act not amounting to a felony.

Forms

Criminally negligent manslaughter (in England and Wales, gross negligence manslaughter) occurs where the defendant's conduct fell so far below the standard of care expected that it merits criminal punishment, and caused death. The modern English test, from R v Adomako (1995), requires a duty of care, breach of that duty, causation, and negligence so "gross" as to be criminal. Unlawful act manslaughter (constructive manslaughter) requires an intentional unlawful act that a reasonable person would recognise as exposing another to some risk of harm. In many United States jurisdictions, the misdemeanour–manslaughter rule similarly makes a killing during a misdemeanour involuntary manslaughter.

Vehicular homicide

Many jurisdictions single out deaths caused by driving — through offences such as vehicular manslaughter or causing death by dangerous driving — with penalties scaled to the degree of culpability and aggravating factors such as intoxication.

Sentencing

Penalties vary widely. In England and Wales, gross negligence manslaughter and unlawful act manslaughter carry a maximum of life imprisonment, with guideline sentences commonly in the range of a few years to over a decade depending on culpability. In the United States, penalties are set state by state, often from one to ten or more years' imprisonment.
