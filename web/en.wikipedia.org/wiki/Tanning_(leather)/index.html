Tanning (leather)

Tanning is the process of treating the skins and hides of animals with chemical agents to produce leather, which is durable and resistant to decomposition. Untreated hide dries out into a hard, inflexible material or, if kept wet, rots; tanning permanently alters the protein structure of the skin — principally by cross-linking its collagen fibres — so that it remains flexible and stable. The word derives from the Latin tanare, in turn associated with oak bark (tannum), a traditional source of tannin.

Process

Before tanning, hides are cured (often by salting), soaked, limed to loosen hair and epidermis, dehaired, fleshed, and delimed; an enzymatic "bate" softens the grain. The prepared pelt is then pickled in acid and salt and introduced to the tanning agent. In chrome tanning — by far the most common modern method — chromium(III) sulphate cross-links the collagen, producing soft, pale blue-grey leather ("wet blue") in a day or less. In vegetable tanning, plant tannins from bark, wood and leaves (oak, chestnut, quebracho, mimosa) are used in pits over weeks or months, yielding firm, richly coloured leather used for soles, saddlery and belts.

History and environment

Leather tanning is among humanity's oldest technologies, with evidence of tawing and oil-curing dating to prehistory. Traditional tanneries were notorious for their smell and were often confined to town outskirts. Modern tanning is a major industry concentrated in countries such as China, Italy, India and Brazil, but chrome tanning effluent and the risk of hexavalent chromium contamination make it a significant source of industrial pollution when poorly regulated.
