Appeal to tradition

The appeal to tradition (Latin: argumentum ad antiquitatem) is a common logical fallacy in which a thesis is deemed correct solely on the basis that it has been held or practised for a long time. The arguer claims that because a practice or belief is traditional, it should be continued or accepted as true.

Form

The general form is: "X has been done or believed for a long time; therefore X is right, good, or true." The antiquity of a practice is not by itself evidence of its correctness — traditions have supported both beneficial customs and practices later judged harmful, from bloodletting in medicine to hereditary legal privileges. The opposite error, assuming something is better merely because it is new, is the appeal to novelty (argumentum ad novitatem).

Discussion

Defenders of tradition sometimes argue that long-standing practices embody accumulated practical wisdom — an idea associated with thinkers such as Edmund Burke and, in a different register, G. K. Chesterton's "fence": one should not remove a practice without understanding why it was established. Logicians reply that such considerations can give a tradition a defeasible presumption, but cannot settle a question on their own; the reasons a tradition arose may no longer apply, or may never have been sound.
