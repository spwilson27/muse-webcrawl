Auxins are a class of plant hormones (or plant-growth regulators) with some morphogen-like characteristics. Auxins play a cardinal role in coordination of many growth and behavioral processes in plant life cycles and are essential for plant body development. The Dutch biologist Frits Warmolt Went first described auxins and their role in plant growth in 1927.

The most important member of the auxin family is indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), which generates the majority of auxin effects in intact plants, and is the most potent native auxin.

Discovery

In 1881, Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied phototropism in canary grass coleoptiles and hypothesized that a signal produced in the tip traveled to the growth zone. In the 1920s Frits Went isolated the growth-promoting substance from oat coleoptiles using agar blocks, demonstrating that the chemical messenger—which he named auxin, from the Greek auxein, "to grow"—diffused from the tip and stimulated elongation on the shaded side. Kenneth V. Thimann later isolated and characterized IAA.

Biological effects

  • Cell elongation: auxin promotes elongation of shoots via the acid growth mechanism.
  • Apical dominance: auxin produced in the shoot apex suppresses the outgrowth of lateral buds.
  • Root initiation: auxin promotes adventitious root formation, used commercially in cuttings.
  • Tropisms: lateral redistribution of auxin causes differential growth in phototropism and gravitropism.
  • Fruit development: auxin regulates fruit set and delays abscission.

Synthetic auxins

Synthetic auxin analogs such as 2,4-D and dicamba are widely used as herbicides; naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) are used as rooting compounds in horticulture.
