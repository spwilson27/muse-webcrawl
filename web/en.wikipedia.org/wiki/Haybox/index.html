Haybox

A haybox, also called a retained-heat cooker, straw box or fireless cooker, is an insulated container in which food that has been brought to the boil continues to cook slowly in its own retained heat, without further fuel. Traditionally the pot was nestled in hay, straw, feathers or blankets inside a wooden box; modern versions use foam, fabric insulation or purpose-made thermal cookers.

Use

The cook heats a covered pot of food — typically soups, stews, rice or porridge — on a stove until boiling, simmers it briefly, then seals it inside the insulated box for several hours. Because the temperature falls only gradually, the food cooks through while using far less energy than continued stovetop cooking, though care is needed to keep food hot enough to remain safe.

History

Retained-heat cooking is centuries old, but the haybox was widely promoted during the First and Second World Wars as a fuel-saving measure, and featured in wartime cookery advice in Britain and elsewhere. It has since been promoted in development work as a low-cost, fuel-efficient technology for households dependent on firewood, and is related to similar devices such as the Wonderbag.
