The Charmat method is a sparkling wine production technique in which the secondary fermentation that creates the bubbles takes place in large sealed stainless steel pressure tanks rather than in the individual bottle, as in the traditional method used for Champagne. After fermentation, the wine is bottled under pressure to preserve its carbonation.

History

The process was first patented in 1895 by the Italian oenologist Federico Martinotti, director of the Experimental Institute of Oenology at Asti, which is why it is also called the metodo Martinotti in Italy. The French winemaker Eugène Charmat refined and patented an improved tank design in 1907 at the University of Montpellier, and his name became attached to the process internationally.

Process

A still base wine is placed in a pressurised tank (an autoclave) together with a mixture of sugar and yeast known as the liqueur de tirage. The secondary fermentation produces carbon dioxide, which, unable to escape, dissolves into the wine under pressure. The process typically takes from a few weeks to a few months. The wine is then chilled, filtered under pressure to remove the yeast lees, dosed with a sugar solution (liqueur d'expédition) if required, and bottled under counter-pressure so that the bubbles are retained.

Comparison with the traditional method

Because the wine spends relatively little time in contact with the yeast lees, Charmat-method wines tend to emphasise fresh, primary fruit and floral aromas rather than the toasty, autolytic flavours characteristic of bottle-fermented wines aged on lees. The method is also considerably faster and cheaper, making it well suited to aromatic grape varieties such as Glera (used for Prosecco) and Muscat (used for Asti), whose character would be masked by long lees ageing.

Wines produced

  • Prosecco (Italy)
  • Asti and Moscato d'Asti (Italy)
  • Lambrusco (Italy)
  • Many German Sekt wines and inexpensive New World sparkling wines
