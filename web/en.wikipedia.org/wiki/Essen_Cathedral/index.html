Essen Cathedral (German: Essener Münster), dedicated to Saints Cosmas and Damian and the Blessed Virgin Mary, is the seat of the Bishop of Essen in western Germany. The church stands on the Burgplatz in the centre of the city of Essen and was formerly the church of Essen Abbey, a community of secular canonesses founded around 845.

History

The first church on the site was built for the abbey in the ninth century. It was repeatedly rebuilt after fires, most notably after the fire of 946 and in the eleventh century, when Abbess Theophanu added the crypt, which survives today and contains the tombs of several abbesses and of the founder Altfrid. Following a further fire in 1275, the church was rebuilt as a Gothic hall church, though the massive Ottonian-influenced westwork was retained.

With the secularisation of the abbey in 1803 the church became a parish church. When the Diocese of Essen was erected in 1958, often called the "Ruhr bishopric" (Ruhrbistum), the minster became its cathedral. The building had suffered severe damage in the Second World War and was restored in the 1950s.

Treasury

The Essen Cathedral Treasury (Domschatz Essen), housed in a building adjoining the cathedral, is one of the most important church treasuries in Germany. Because the abbey's collection was never dispersed, it preserves an unusually complete ensemble of Ottonian goldsmith work: the Golden Madonna (c. 980), the Cross of Otto and Mathilde, the Cross of Theophanu, the Cross of Mathilde, the so-called crown of Otto III, a seventh-century Anglo-Saxon reliquary casket, and richly decorated liturgical manuscripts such as the Theophanu Gospels.
