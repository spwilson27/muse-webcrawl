Stock market bubble

Stock market bubble
TypeEconomic bubble
CauseExcessive speculation, herd behaviour
Famous examplesTulip mania (1637), South Sea Bubble (1720), 1929 crash, dot-com bubble (2000)

A stock market bubble is a type of economic bubble taking place in stock markets when market participants drive stock prices above their value in relation to some system of stock valuation. Behavioural finance theory attributes stock market bubbles to cognitive biases that lead to groupthink and herd behaviour. Bubbles occur not only in real-world markets but also in highly controlled experimental markets, which has puzzled some researchers, since the participants' assets have well-defined fundamental values.

Phases

Economist Hyman Minsky described five stages of a bubble: displacement (a new technology or low interest rates attract investors), boom (prices rise and attract wider attention), euphoria (caution is abandoned and prices detach from fundamentals), profit-taking (informed investors begin to sell), and panic (prices fall sharply as everyone rushes to exit).

Historical examples

Early examples include the Dutch tulip mania of 1637 and the South Sea Bubble of 1720. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 ended a speculative boom of the Roaring Twenties and preceded the Great Depression. Japan's asset price bubble burst in the early 1990s, leading to the "Lost Decade." The dot-com bubble peaked in March 2000, when the Nasdaq Composite lost nearly 80% of its value over the following two and a half years. The United States housing bubble contributed to the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Identification

Bubbles are notoriously difficult to identify in real time; high valuations may reflect genuine expectations of future growth. Economists use measures such as the price–earnings ratio and the cyclically adjusted CAPE ratio to assess whether markets appear overvalued relative to history.
