Eric Clapton

Eric Patrick Clapton (born 30 March 1945) is an English rock and blues guitarist, singer, and songwriter, widely regarded as one of the most influential guitarists of all time.

Eric Patrick Clapton (born 30 March 1945) is an English rock and blues guitarist, singer, and songwriter. He is regarded as one of the most successful and influential guitarists in rock music, ranked second in Rolling Stone's list of the "100 Greatest Guitarists of All Time". He is the only artist to be inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame three times: once as a solo artist and separately as a member of the Yardbirds and of Cream.

Early life

Clapton was born in Ripley, Surrey, the son of 16-year-old Patricia Molly Clapton and Edward Walter Fryer, a 25-year-old Canadian soldier who returned to Canada before Clapton was born. He was raised believing his grandparents were his parents and his mother was his older sister. He received his first guitar, a German Hoyer, for his thirteenth birthday, and was drawn to the blues of Muddy Waters, Freddie King, B.B. King and Robert Johnson.

Career

Clapton first rose to fame with the Yardbirds (1963–65), leaving as the band turned toward pop. With John Mayall & the Bluesbreakers he recorded the landmark Blues Breakers album (1966), after which graffiti declaring "Clapton is God" appeared in London. He then formed the power trio Cream with Jack Bruce and Ginger Baker, whose albums Disraeli Gears and Wheels of Fire and songs such as "Sunshine of Your Love" and "White Room" made them one of the first supergroups. After the short-lived Blind Faith, he formed Derek and the Dominos, whose Layla and Other Assorted Love Songs (1970), featuring Duane Allman, is considered a masterpiece; the title track was inspired by his unrequited love for Pattie Boyd, wife of his friend George Harrison.

Solo career

After battling heroin addiction in the early 1970s, Clapton returned with 461 Ocean Boulevard (1974), including his hit cover of "I Shot the Sheriff". Albums such as Slowhand (1977, with "Wonderful Tonight" and "Cocaine") and Journeyman (1989) sustained his success. Following the death of his four-year-old son Conor in a fall from a New York apartment in 1991, he wrote "Tears in Heaven", which, with his acoustic Unplugged album (1992), earned six Grammy Awards. He founded the Crossroads Centre addiction rehabilitation facility in Antigua in 1998 and held periodic Crossroads Guitar Festivals to fund it.

Legacy

Clapton's lyrical, vibrato-rich playing on Gibson and later Fender Stratocaster guitars — notably "Blackie" and "Brownie" — shaped generations of rock guitarists. He has sold over 100 million records worldwide and received 18 Grammy Awards.
