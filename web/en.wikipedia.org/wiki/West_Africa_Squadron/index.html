The West Africa Squadron, also known as the Preventive Squadron, was a squadron of the Royal Navy whose mission, after the British abolition of the slave trade in 1807, was to suppress the Atlantic slave trade by patrolling the coast of West Africa. It was formed in 1808 and remained in service, with varying strength, until 1870.

Operations

Operating from Freetown in Sierra Leone, the squadron seized slave ships and brought them before the Vice-Admiralty court there; liberated Africans were settled in Sierra Leone as "recaptives". Its work was initially hampered by the weakness of Britain's treaty rights, which permitted seizure only of British vessels or of ships actually carrying slaves. Later treaties with Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands, Brazil and others, and the Palmerston Act of 1839 and Aberdeen Act of 1845, broadened its powers. Famous actions include the captured slave brig Black Joke, taken into service as a tender, which out-sailed and captured larger slavers in 1828–29.

Impact and costs

Between 1808 and 1860 the squadron captured around 1,600 slave ships and freed about 150,000 Africans. Historians debate its share in the overall decline of the trade relative to diplomacy and changing economics, but its interception rate rose substantially by the 1840s–50s, particularly in the Bight of Benin under commanders such as William Tucker. Service was unpopular and dangerous: disease, especially malaria and yellow fever, killed a significant share of its crews. The squadron's role has been commemorated as an early example of humanitarian naval intervention, though it also formed part of Britain's expanding informal influence on the West African coast.
