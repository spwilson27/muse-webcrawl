Aircraft noise

Aircraft noise is the sound produced by aircraft in flight, during takeoff and landing, and on the ground during engine testing and taxiing. It is a significant environmental concern around airports, being linked to sleep disturbance, cardiovascular disease, impaired learning in children, and annoyance, and it is a factor in airport planning conflicts and flight-path disputes worldwide.

Sources

Noise comes from the engines — jet exhaust mixing, fan and compressor tones in turbofans — and from the airframe, including airflow over deployed landing gear, flaps, and slats, which dominates on approach. Modern high-bypass turbofans, chevron nozzles, and acoustic liners have greatly reduced engine noise since the 1960s, making airframe noise and operations the focus of further reductions. Helicopters produce distinctive rotor noise, and supersonic aircraft generate sonic booms.

Regulation and mitigation

International noise standards are set through ICAO's Annex 16 "Chapter" limits, progressively tightened (Chapter 14 applies to new types from 2017). Mitigation includes quieter aircraft certification, noise-abatement departure and approach procedures, continuous-descent approaches, preferential runways and flight paths, curfews, noise quotas (such as London airports' Quota Count system), land-use zoning, and home insulation schemes. Noise is commonly measured in decibels using metrics such as Lden and day–night average sound level (DNL).

Health effects

Epidemiological studies associate chronic exposure with hypertension and heart disease; the WHO's 2018 environmental noise guidelines recommend limiting aircraft noise averages to 45 dB Lden. Annoyance responses have grown even as individual aircraft became quieter, partly due to rising flight numbers.
