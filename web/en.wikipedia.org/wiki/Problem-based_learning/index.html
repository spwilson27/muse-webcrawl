Problem-based learning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
In problem-based learning, small groups of students learn by working through realistic problems.

Problem-based learning (PBL) is a student-centered pedagogy in which students learn about a subject through the experience of solving an open-ended problem found in trigger material. The PBL tutorial process involves working in small groups of learners. Each student identifies their own learning needs and takes responsibility for their own learning; the teacher acts as a facilitator rather than as a lecturer delivering content.

Origins

PBL was pioneered in the medical school programme at McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada, in the late 1960s by Howard Barrows and colleagues, who sought to make medical education better resemble clinical practice. The approach spread to other medical schools—including Maastricht University in the Netherlands, which adopted PBL across the university—and into fields such as law, economics, engineering, business, and school education.

Process

A typical PBL cycle begins when a group of about five to eight students, guided by a tutor, is presented with a problem scenario. The group clarifies unfamiliar terms, defines the problem, brainstorms explanations using prior knowledge, and formulates learning objectives. Students then pursue these objectives through individual self-study before reconvening to report, compare findings, and apply their new knowledge to the problem. The Maastricht "seven-step" model formalises this sequence.

Evidence and criticism

Research comparing PBL with conventional curricula generally finds that PBL students perform similarly on tests of factual knowledge and better on measures of application, clinical performance, and satisfaction, though effects vary with implementation quality. Critics argue that PBL is resource-intensive, that novices may lack the prior knowledge to direct their own learning efficiently (the "minimal guidance" critique), and that coverage of a systematic body of knowledge can suffer if problems are poorly chosen.
