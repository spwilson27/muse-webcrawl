A mansard or mansard roof (also called a French roof or curb roof) is a four-sided gambrel-style hip roof characterised by two slopes on each of its sides, with the lower slope pitched steeply — steeper than the upper, and nearly vertical or sometimes slightly convex or concave. The steep lower slope, usually pierced by dormer windows, creates an additional, fully usable storey of attic space within the roof.

History

The design was popularised by the French architect François Mansart (1598–1666), who used it extensively, most famously at the Château de Blois and the Château de Maisons, though the Louvre architect Pierre Lescot had employed a form of it a century earlier. The style is named after Mansart (with the "d" arising from later usage).

The mansard became ubiquitous in Paris during Napoleon III's Second Empire (1852–1870), when Baron Haussmann's rebuilding filled the city with mansard-topped blocks; a common explanation is that French property taxes assessed by floor below the roofline made an extra attic storey economical. In North America the mansard is the signature of the Second Empire style of the 1860s–1880s and appears on buildings from the Parliament of Canada to the "Addams Family"-style Victorian house of popular imagination.

Variations

Profiles may be straight, convex ("bell-cast"), concave, or S-curved (ogee). A mansard with slopes on only two sides, or on the front façade alone (common in Georgian terraces in London, where the roof hides behind a parapet), is a partial form of the type. Modern zoning codes in several cities still permit extra mansard storeys as a bonus above height limits.
