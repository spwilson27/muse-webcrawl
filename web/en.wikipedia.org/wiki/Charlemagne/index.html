Charlemagne

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Equestrian statuette believed to depict Charlemagne, in bronze]

Charlemagne (c. 747 – 28 January 814), also called Charles the Great, was King of the Franks from 768, King of the Lombards from 774, and the first emperor in western Europe since the fall of Rome, crowned by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800. Ruling from Aachen, he united much of western and central Europe for the first time since the Roman Empire, conquered Saxony, Lombard Italy and the Spanish March, and sponsored the Carolingian Renaissance — a revival of learning, manuscript copying and standardised writing that preserved much of Latin literature. He is often called the 'Father of Europe'.

Conquests and empire

Succeeding jointly with his brother Carloman (sole ruler from 771), Charlemagne fought more than fifty campaigns: against the Lombards in Italy, the Saxons in a brutal thirty-year war involving forced conversion and massacres such as Verden (782), the Avars (whose treasure he seized), Bretons, Slavs and Muslim Spain, where his rearguard's destruction at Roncevaux (778) became the epic Song of Roland. His coronation in 800 — whose significance he may not have sought — was recognised, after friction, even by Byzantium.

Government and legacy

Charlemagne governed through counts, itinerant inspectors (missi dominici), written capitularies and the church; his court scholars — Alcuin of York, Einhard (his biographer), Paul the Deacon — reformed education and script. Forced to divide the empire among his sons by Frankish custom, he died in 814 leaving it to his only surviving son, Louis the Pious; within a generation it split into the kingdoms that became France and Germany.
