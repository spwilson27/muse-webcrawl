Umm El Qa'ab

Location: Abydos, Sohag Governorate, Egypt
Type: Royal necropolis
Period: Predynastic to Second Dynasty (c. 3300–2700 BC)

Umm El Qa'ab (Arabic: أم القعاب, also transliterated Umm el-Qa'ab, "Mother of Pots") is a royal necropolis of ancient Egypt at Abydos, in Upper Egypt. It takes its modern Arabic name from the millions of pottery sherds left by later pilgrims that litter the site.

Burials

The cemetery contains the tombs of predynastic rulers of the Naqada III period and the pharaohs of the First Dynasty, including Narmer, Aha, Djer, Djet, Den, Anedjib, Semerkhet and Qa'a, together with two tombs of the Second Dynasty kings Peribsen and Khasekhemwy. The tombs are mudbrick-lined subterranean chambers; around the First Dynasty burials lie rows of subsidiary graves for retainers, in some cases evidently sacrificed at the king's funeral.

Significance

Excavations by Émile Amélineau in the 1890s, Flinders Petrie around 1900, and the German Archaeological Institute from the 1970s onwards recovered crucial evidence for the origins of the Egyptian state, including the earliest known examples of Egyptian writing on labels and seal impressions, and the famous ivory label of Narmer from the tomb complex. Egyptians of later periods identified the tomb of Djer as the burial place of the god Osiris, making Umm El Qa'ab a major pilgrimage destination.
