Beamforming

Beamforming is a signal processing technique used with arrays of transducers — antennas, microphones or hydrophones — to achieve spatial selectivity. By combining the elements of a phased array so that signals at particular angles experience constructive interference while others experience destructive interference, the array can transmit a beam in a chosen direction, or, on reception, listen preferentially from one direction without physically moving the array.

Technique

In the simplest case, identical signals are fed to each element with progressive time delays (or phase shifts), so the wavefronts reinforce along the desired steering angle. On reception, applying the corresponding delays and summing the element outputs yields a signal estimate for that direction. Weighting the elements (apodisation) trades main-lobe width against side-lobe level. Adaptive beamforming adjusts the weights from the received data itself, placing nulls on interferers; algorithms such as minimum-variance distortionless response (MVDR/Capon) are widely used. Beamforming may be done in analogue hardware, digitally after sampling each element, or in hybrid form.

Applications

Beamforming underpins radar and sonar (where it began, with Second World War sonar and radar arrays), radio astronomy, medical ultrasound imaging, seismology, and microphone arrays for speech capture and hearing aids. In wireless communications it is central to modern standards: Wi-Fi (802.11ac and later) and 5G mobile networks use MIMO beamforming to direct energy toward each user, improving capacity and range; satellite and point-to-point links use it for spatial reuse. In audio, microphone-array beamforming enables direction-aware recording, conference systems and smart speakers.
