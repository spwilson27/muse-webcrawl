Transit map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transit map is a topological map, presented as a schematic diagram, used to illustrate the routes and stations in a public transport system — most often a city's metro, tram or commuter rail network. Unlike a geographical map, a transit map sacrifices geographical accuracy for clarity: lines are typically drawn straight or at fixed angles (usually 45° and 90°), distances between stations are evened out, and the centre is enlarged relative to the suburbs, so that riders can readily trace routes and find interchanges.

History

Early railway and underground maps followed geography. The revolutionary step came with the map of the London Underground designed by electrical draughtsman Harry Beck, first published in 1933. Beck reasoned that because passengers travel underground, the exact surface geography was irrelevant; only the sequence of stations and the connections between lines mattered. Modelling his design on an electrical circuit diagram, he straightened lines to horizontals, verticals and 45° diagonals and spaced stations evenly. London Underground initially rejected the design as too radical, but a trial printing proved hugely popular, and it became the template for transit maps worldwide.

Design principles

  • Schematic layout: lines restricted to a small set of angles; bends minimised.
  • Colour coding: each line in a distinct colour, consistently used on trains, signage and timetables.
  • Interchange symbols: connected ticks or linked circles showing where passengers can change lines.
  • Simplified geography: only key landmarks (a river, for example) retained, often in simplified form, to orient the user.
  • Legibility: station names placed to avoid collisions; fare zones often shown as concentric shading.

Variants

Some systems take different approaches: the Moscow Metro map became famous for its circular ring line design; the New York City Subway map has alternated between schematic (Massimo Vignelli's 1972 diagram) and quasi-geographical styles; spider maps show bus routes radiating from a single hub. Digital journey planners have reduced dependence on printed diagrams, but the schematic map remains the standard at-a-glance representation of a network and a celebrated genre of information design.
