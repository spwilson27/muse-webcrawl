Piano accordion

A piano accordion is an accordion equipped with a right-hand keyboard similar to a piano or organ. In recent decades, the term piano accordion has sometimes been used to distinguish it from button accordions, especially in North America, although both types are simply accordions. The player's left hand operates a set of bass buttons, most commonly arranged in the Stradella bass system, while the bellows are worked between the two hands to supply air to the instrument's free reeds.

Description

The piano accordion consists of a treble casing containing the keyboard and reed blocks, a bass casing containing the bass and chord mechanism, and pleated bellows joining the two. Pressing a key or button opens a pallet that lets air flow over a reed when the bellows are moved. Instruments range from small 12-bass student models to full-size professional accordions with 41 treble keys and 120 bass buttons, often with multiple reed ranks and register switches ("chin switches" and palm switches) that change the timbre.

The Stradella bass system provides single counter-bass and fundamental bass notes plus preset major, minor, dominant seventh and diminished chords, allowing a full harmonic accompaniment with the left hand. Larger instruments may have free-bass converters for melodic bass playing.

History

The accordion was patented in Vienna in 1829 by Cyrill Demian. Piano-keyboard accordions emerged in the later 19th century as makers sought a familiar interface for keyboard players, and the type became dominant in the United States and much of Europe during the early-to-mid 20th century. It became a staple of popular, dance, folk and polka music, and of variety entertainment; performers such as Lawrence Welk popularised it on American television.

Repertoire and use

The piano accordion is used across folk traditions (French musette, Italian, German, and Mexican norteño-influenced styles, among others), tango, jazz, Cajun and zydeco (alongside button accordions), rock and popular music, and a growing classical repertoire of original works and transcriptions. Competitions and conservatoires in several countries now treat it as a serious concert instrument.
