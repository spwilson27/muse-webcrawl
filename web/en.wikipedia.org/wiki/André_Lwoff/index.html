André Michel Lwoff (8 May 1902 – 30 September 1994) was a French microbiologist who shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1965 with François Jacob and Jacques Monod "for their discoveries concerning genetic control of enzyme and virus synthesis". Working at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, Lwoff demonstrated that bacteriophages can persist harmlessly within host bacteria as prophages and be induced to multiply and destroy the cell — the phenomenon of lysogeny — a discovery fundamental to virology and to understanding how viral genes are regulated.

Life and career

Lwoff was born in Ainay-le-Château, the son of a Russian-Jewish psychiatrist and a sculptor. He joined the Pasteur Institute at the age of 19, supported by Édouard Chatton, and began his career studying ciliated protozoa, discovering with his wife Marguerite Lwoff the growth-factor requirements ("haematin") of micro-organisms. During the Second World War he took part in the French Resistance. After the war he directed the Institute's department of microbial physiology, building a group that made the attic laboratory of the Pasteur Institute famous in the history of molecular biology. From 1959 he also held a chair of microbiology at the Sorbonne.

Scientific work

Lwoff's classic experiments with Louis Siminovitch and Niels Kjeldgaard (1950) showed that ultraviolet irradiation of lysogenic bacteria triggered the whole population to produce phage and lyse — proving that the prophage was a genetic element carried in every cell, capable of switching from silent coexistence to viral production. He proposed the concept of the provirus, classified viruses by their structure and nucleic-acid type, and chaired the first international committee on viral taxonomy. With Jacob and Monod, his group articulated the repressor model of gene regulation that emerged from the operon work recognised by the Nobel Committee.

Later life

Lwoff became a wide-ranging essayist on the philosophy and history of biology (his book L'Ordre biologique, 1969) and campaigned against capital punishment. He received many honours, including the Leeuwenhoek Medal and the Royal Society's Copley Medal, and was a foreign member of the Royal Society and the U.S. National Academy of Sciences.
