A high-IQ society is an organization for people who have achieved a score at or above a specified percentile on a standardized intelligence quotient (IQ) test or other standardized test of intelligence. The best known is Mensa International, which admits candidates who score in the top two percent of the general population. More selective societies set progressively higher thresholds.

History

Mensa was founded in Oxford, England, in 1946 by the Australian barrister Roland Berrill and the British scientist and lawyer Lancelot Ware. Their aim was a non-political society of highly intelligent people whose only qualification for membership would be a high IQ. The idea of still more selective societies arose as members sought groups restricted to narrower bands of the upper tail; the Triple Nine Society (top 0.1 percent) was founded in 1978, and a proliferation of smaller societies followed, many of them online.

Thresholds

Entry requirements are conventionally expressed as percentiles of the general population. Common levels include the top 2 percent (Mensa, approximately IQ 130 on a test with a standard deviation of 15), the top 1 percent (Intertel), the top 0.1 percent (Triple Nine Society, approximately IQ 146), the top 0.01 percent (Prometheus Society), and the top 0.0001 percent (Mega Society, approximately one in a million). Because tests differ in their ceilings and norming, societies maintain lists of accepted tests and minimum scores; very high-range tests are controversial because they cannot be reliably normed on large samples.

Activities

Societies typically publish journals and newsletters, hold local and annual gatherings, and maintain special interest groups. Mensa additionally administers scholarship programmes and, through affiliated foundations, supports research into intelligence and gifted children. Critics note that a high test score measures only certain cognitive abilities and question the value of organizing socially around such a score; members generally describe the appeal as the opportunity for conversation with intellectual peers.
