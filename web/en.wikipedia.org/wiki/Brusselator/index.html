Brusselator

The Brusselator is a theoretical model for a type of autocatalytic reaction. The Brusselator model was proposed by Ilya Prigogine and his collaborators at the Université Libre de Bruxelles. The name is a portmanteau of "Brussels" and "oscillator".

It is characterized by the reactions:

A → X

2X + Y → 3X

B + X → Y + D

X → E

with rate constants conveniently set to 1, leading to the system of ordinary differential equations:

dx/dt = A + x²y − Bx − x

dy/dt = Bx − x²y

Behaviour

The Brusselator has a fixed point at x = A and y = B/A. The fixed point becomes unstable when B > 1 + A², undergoing a Hopf bifurcation and leading to a limit cycle oscillation. This behaviour made the model a landmark example in the study of dissipative structures and far-from-equilibrium thermodynamics, showing how sustained temporal order (oscillation) can emerge spontaneously in a chemical system kept away from equilibrium.

When diffusion terms are added, the reaction–diffusion system can exhibit spatial pattern formation analogous to Turing patterns.

Significance

The Brusselator was developed in the late 1960s as a minimal model of the kind of oscillatory chemistry demonstrated experimentally by the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction. Although the trimolecular step (2X + Y → 3X) is not physically realistic as an elementary reaction, the model proved highly influential as a pedagogical and theoretical example of nonlinear dynamics, limit cycles and self-organization. Prigogine received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1977 for his work on dissipative structures.
