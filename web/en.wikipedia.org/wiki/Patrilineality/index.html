Patrilineality

Patrilineality, also known as the male line or agnatic kinship, is the general tendency in kinship systems to trace group membership, descent, inheritance, and succession through the male line — from father to son to grandson. A society organised patrilineally counts a person as belonging to their father's lineage (patrilineage), and children typically take their father's family name, clan, and social group.

In a patrilineal system, descent and group membership pass through fathers.

Characteristics

Patrilineal systems are the most common form of unilineal descent worldwide, found historically in much of Europe, the Middle East, South and East Asia, and many African and pastoralist societies. Typical features include:

  • Descent and clan membership reckoned through males; a married woman often joins, or remains affiliated with, her husband's lineage depending on local custom.
  • Inheritance of land, office, and titles passing from father to son, sometimes under primogeniture.
  • Surnames transmitted from father to children, as in the traditional European and Chinese naming systems.
  • Patrilocal residence, in which a married couple lives with or near the husband's family, frequently accompanying patrilineal descent.

Examples

Traditional Chinese kinship, with its patrilineal clans (zu) and ancestor worship focused on the male line, is a classic example, as is the agnatic succession of Roman law, from which the adjective "agnatic" derives. Many Nuer, Igbo, and other African lineage systems are patrilineal, as are Arab tribal systems in which the nasab (genealogy) runs through fathers. Royal succession in much of Europe historically followed male-preference or strictly agnatic (Salic law) principles.

Contrast with other systems

Patrilineality contrasts with matrilineality, in which descent is traced through women, and with bilateral (cognatic) kinship, in which both parents' families are recognised symmetrically, as in most modern Western societies. Anthropologists note that kinship reckoning need not be consistent across domains: a society may be patrilineal for clan membership but bilateral for inheritance.
