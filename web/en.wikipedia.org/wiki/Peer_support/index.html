Peer support

DefinitionSupport from people with similar lived experience
Common contextsMental health, addiction recovery, chronic illness, veterans, bereavement
Key ideaExperiential knowledge and mutual aid

Peer support groups bring together people who share a common experience or condition.

Peer support occurs when people provide knowledge, experience, emotional, social or practical help to each other. It commonly refers to an initiative consisting of trained supporters, and can take a number of forms such as peer mentoring, listening, or counselling. Peer support is also used for people whose problems (such as addiction or mental ill-health) have the approval of their peers, or "others like me".

History

Mutual aid among peers long predates professional services. Modern peer support is often traced to 18th-century France, where the reformer Jean-Baptiste Pussin employed recovered patients at the Bicêtre hospital. In the 20th century, Alcoholics Anonymous (founded 1935) popularised the twelve-step peer model, and the mental-health consumer/survivor movement of the 1970s promoted peer-run alternatives to psychiatric services.

In mental health services

Many health systems now employ certified peer support workers or peer specialists—people with lived experience of mental illness who support others in recovery. Research associates peer support with improved engagement, hope and quality of life, and it is a recognised component of recovery-oriented care.

Other settings

Peer support programmes exist in schools (anti-bullying and mentoring), workplaces, the military, prisons, and online communities for chronic illness and bereavement.
