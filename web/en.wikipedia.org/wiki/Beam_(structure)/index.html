Beam (structure)

A beam is a structural element that primarily resists loads applied laterally across its axis (an element designed to carry primarily axial load would be a strut or column). Its mode of deflection is primarily by bending. The loads applied to the beam result in reaction forces at the beam's support points; the total effect of all the forces acting on the beam is to produce shear forces and bending moments within the beam, which in turn induce internal stresses, strains and deflections. Beams are characterised by their manner of support, profile (cross-section shape), equilibrium conditions, length, and material.

Overview

Historically beams were squared timbers in wooden construction; modern beams may be steel I-sections, reinforced or prestressed concrete, aluminium, or composite materials. In building frames, beams transfer loads from floors and roofs to columns and walls; in bridges, girders carry the deck across spans.

Classification

By support conditions, beams may be simply supported (pinned at one end, on a roller at the other), cantilevered (fixed at one end only), continuous over several supports, or fixed at both ends. Internally, the bending moment at any section is balanced by compression on one face of the beam and tension on the opposite face, separated by the neutral axis. The theory of beam bending — the Euler–Bernoulli beam theory — relates curvature to bending moment and flexural rigidity, and underpins structural analysis together with Timoshenko theory for short, deep beams where shear deformation matters.
