Cultural psychology

Independent vs. interdependent models of the self are a classic topic in cultural psychology.

Cultural psychology is a field of psychology that studies how culture and mind mutually constitute each other — how cultural practices, institutions and meanings shape psychological processes, and how those processes in turn sustain culture. Its guiding idea, associated with Richard Shweder, is "one mind, many mentalities."

Major themes

Research compares how people in different societies perceive, think, feel and relate. Well-known findings include contrasts between analytic cognition, emphasized in many Western settings, and holistic cognition, more common in East Asian settings (Richard Nisbett); between independent self-construals, common in North America and Western Europe, and interdependent self-construals, common in much of Asia, Africa and Latin America (Hazel Markus and Shinobu Kitayama); and cross-cultural variation in emotion, morality, motivation and child-rearing.

Methods and critique

Cultural psychologists use experiments, surveys, ethnography and historical analysis. The field has criticized mainstream psychology for building general theories on samples drawn overwhelmingly from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic ("WEIRD") populations, arguing that many supposedly universal findings are culturally specific.

History

The field draws on Wilhelm Wundt's Völkerpsychologie, on Lev Vygotsky's cultural-historical psychology, and on mid-20th-century culture-and-personality anthropology, before crystallizing as a named discipline in the 1990s.
