Coming out

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coming out (of the closet), often shortened to "coming out", is a metaphor used to describe LGBT people's self-disclosure of their sexual orientation, romantic orientation, or gender identity. The phrase originates in the expression "coming out of the closet", referring to the concealment of a stigmatised identity, and in the earlier debutante tradition of a young woman "coming out" into society.

History

The term was used in gay subculture before the Stonewall riots of 1969, when a "coming-out ball" echoed debutante balls. After Stonewall and the rise of the gay liberation movement, coming out acquired a political dimension: activists such as Harvey Milk urged gay people to come out to friends and family as a strategy for achieving social acceptance, reasoning that prejudice thrives on the invisibility of gay people.

Process and psychology

Coming out is generally described as a process rather than a single event. Stage models, such as Vivienne Cass's 1979 identity model, describe movement from identity confusion through comparison, tolerance, acceptance, and pride to synthesis. Individuals may come out selectively — to friends before family, or in some contexts but not others. Research associates being out, in supportive environments, with lower psychological distress, though disclosure in hostile environments can carry risks including rejection, homelessness among youth, and discrimination.

National Coming Out Day

National Coming Out Day is observed annually on 11 October, first celebrated in 1988, marking the anniversary of the 1987 National March on Washington for Lesbian and Gay Rights.

Outing

"Outing" refers to the disclosure of another person's LGBT identity without their consent, and is generally regarded as unethical, though it has been used controversially as a political tactic against public figures perceived as hypocritical.
