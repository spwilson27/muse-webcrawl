Tahdig (Persian: ته‌دیگ, from tah "bottom" + dīg "pot") is the crispy, golden-brown layer of rice — or of another starch placed at the bottom of the pot — formed when Persian steamed rice (chelow) is cooked. Prized for its crunchy texture and rich flavour, it is considered the delicacy of the meal and is traditionally broken into pieces and served alongside the fluffy rice above it.

Preparation

In the classic method, parboiled long-grain rice such as basmati is drained, then returned to a pot whose base has been coated with oil, butter, or a mixture of yogurt and saffron. The rice is mounded into a pyramid, pierced with steam holes, covered with a cloth-wrapped lid, and steamed over low heat so the bottom layer fries gently into an even, amber crust while the grains above remain separate and tender. The pot is often inverted onto a platter so the tahdig emerges as a single golden disc.

Variations

Tahdig can be made with a layer of flatbread (such as lavash or sangak), thinly sliced potatoes, or pasta — the crispy bottom of Persian-style spaghetti (makaroni) is especially popular. Regional and family versions add saffron, turmeric, or a yogurt-and-egg mixture for colour and flavour. Similar scorched-rice preparations appear in other cuisines, including Korean nurungji, Chinese guoba, and the socarrat of Spanish paella.

Cultural significance

Because only one pot-bottom exists per meal, tahdig is often portioned out ceremonially, offered to honoured guests first, and is famously contested at the table. It features widely in Iranian cookbooks and diaspora food writing as an emblem of Persian home cooking.
