Internment


Type: Imprisonment without trial
Typical targets: Enemy aliens, political groups, ethnic minorities

Internment is the imprisonment of people, commonly in large groups, without charges or intent to file charges. The term is especially used for the confinement of "enemy citizens in wartime or of terrorism suspects". Although internment is usually imposed during armed conflict, it has also been applied in civil and political contexts, and is distinct from imprisonment after conviction and from the confinement of prisoners of war.

History

Mass internment was widely practised during the First and Second World Wars, when belligerent states confined enemy aliens. During the Second World War the United States interned some 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, following Executive Order 9066 in 1942 — a policy for which the US government formally apologised and paid reparations under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. Britain interned enemy aliens on the Isle of Man, Canada and Australia operated camps for enemy aliens and prisoners, and Nazi Germany's concentration camps represented an extreme and murderous form of confinement.

Legal framework

The Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949 permits the internment of civilians in occupied territory only for imperative reasons of security, with rights to humane treatment, review of the decision, and release when the reasons cease. Internment without such safeguards is widely regarded as a violation of international human rights law, including the prohibition on arbitrary detention.

Modern usage

The term continues to be used for wartime and counter-terrorism detention, and in debates over facilities such as the Guantanamo Bay detention camp and the camps reported in Xinjiang, China, which the Chinese government describes as vocational education and training centres.
