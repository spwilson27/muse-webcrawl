English subjunctive

The English subjunctive is a set of verb forms used to express wishes, demands, suggestions, hypotheses and situations contrary to fact. Unlike languages such as Spanish or German, English has no fully distinct subjunctive conjugation: the subjunctive is visible mainly in the use of the plain (base) form of the verb in certain subordinate clauses, and in the special form were for all persons of the verb be in irrealis constructions.

The mandative subjunctive

In clauses following verbs and adjectives of demand, recommendation or necessity (demand, suggest, essential, important), English — especially American English — uses the base form: "I insist that he be present"; "It is vital that she arrive on time." Here the verb takes no third-person -s and negation is by bare not without do-support ("that he not be told"). British English often substitutes should ("that he should be present") or the indicative, though the mandative subjunctive has been reviving in Britain since the mid-twentieth century.

The irrealis "were"

In hypothetical and counterfactual clauses, were is used with first- and third-person singular subjects: "If I were you"; "I wish it were over." Many grammarians analyse this not as a true past subjunctive but as a unique irrealis form of be. The construction survives also in set phrases such as "if need be", "be that as it may", "far be it from me" and "God save the King" — fossilised subjunctives preserving older, once-general uses.

History

Old English had a fuller subjunctive paradigm, but inflectional levelling in Middle English eroded most distinctions, leaving the modern remnants. Usage of the mandative subjunctive declined in British English in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries before its recent revival, partly under American influence; it remains in vigorous health in formal American prose.
