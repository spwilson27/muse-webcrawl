A blasting cap is a small, sensitive explosive device used to detonate a larger, less sensitive charge of high explosive. Because most commercial and military high explosives are deliberately stable, a blasting cap provides the sharp shock needed to initiate them reliably. Caps are among the most hazardous items in blasting work and must be stored and transported separately from main charges.

Types

Fuse caps are crimped onto safety fuse and lit by the fuse's spit of flame. Electric blasting caps contain a small heating element — a bridge wire or semiconductor bridge — embedded in the initiating composition, fired by a current from a blasting machine or battery. Electronic detonators, increasingly common in mining, contain a programmable timing chip, allowing very precise delay sequences that improve fragmentation and reduce vibration. Caps are made in instantaneous and in short- and long-period delay variants.

Composition

A typical cap is a thin metal shell (aluminium or copper) containing a small quantity of a primary explosive such as lead azide pressed over a base charge of a secondary explosive such as PETN. Historical caps used mercury fulminate, the explosive Alfred Nobel combined with a metal shell in his 1865 detonator — the invention that made dynamite practical and safe to use.

Safety

Blasting caps can be initiated by impact, friction, heat, static electricity, and stray radio-frequency energy, and accidents have historically caused many injuries, especially to children who found lost caps. Modern practice emphasises strict accounting, separate magazines, and keeping caps out of the reach of unauthorised persons.
