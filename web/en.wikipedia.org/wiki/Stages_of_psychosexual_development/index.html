Stages of psychosexual development

In psychoanalysis, the stages of psychosexual development are the phases through which, according to Sigmund Freud, the libido (sexual energy) of a child focuses on different erogenous zones. Freud proposed five stages—oral, anal, phallic, latency, and genital—first set out in his Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality (1905). He argued that if a person becomes fixated at a stage, because of trauma or overindulgence, traits associated with that stage persist into adult personality. The theory was foundational to psychoanalysis but is regarded by modern psychology as lacking empirical support and testability.

Key ideas

During the phallic stage, Freud claimed, boys experience the Oedipus complex—desire for the mother and rivalry with the father—resolved by identification with the father, forming the superego. Karl Abraham and other early analysts elaborated sub-stages and character types, such as the "oral" (dependent, optimistic or pessimistic) and "anal" (orderly, obstinate, parsimonious) characters. Erik Erikson later transformed Freud's scheme into his eight stages of psychosocial development, emphasising social rather than sexual crises.

Criticism

The theory has been criticised on several grounds: it was derived from retrospective reconstructions with adult patients rather than observation of children; its claims are difficult to falsify, a point pressed by philosopher Karl Popper; longitudinal studies have found little evidence linking early experiences such as weaning or toilet training to the predicted adult traits; and feminist critics have challenged its accounts of female development. Nevertheless, the model remains historically influential in the humanities and in the history of psychology, and some of its broad notions—early experience matters, development proceeds in stages—persist in altered forms.
