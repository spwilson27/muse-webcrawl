The barasingha (Rucervus duvaucelii), also known as the swamp deer, is a deer species distributed in the Indian subcontinent. Populations in northern and central India are fragmented, and two isolated populations occur in southwestern Nepal. Its name, from Hindi barah ("twelve") and singh ("horns"), refers to the many-tined antlers of adults, which commonly have 10 to 14 points and sometimes more than 20.

Description

The barasingha is a large deer with a shoulder height of 110 to 120 cm and a head-to-body length of nearly 180 cm. The coat is somewhat woolly and dark brown with lighter spots; the belly and the insides of the legs are paler. The stags' antlers are distinctive, borne on long brow tines and characteristically curved, with numerous small points branching from the main beams. The hooves are broad and splayed, an adaptation to walking on the soft ground of swamps and wet grasslands.

Distribution and habitat

Historically the barasingha ranged widely across the floodplains of the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra rivers. Today it survives mainly in protected areas such as Kanha National Park in Madhya Pradesh, Dudhwa National Park in Uttar Pradesh, and Kaziranga in Assam. Three subspecies are recognised: the wetland form R. d. duvaucelii of the Terai, the hard-ground form R. d. branderi of Madhya Pradesh, and the eastern form R. d. ranjitsinhi of Assam.

Behaviour and ecology

Barasingha are grazers, feeding almost entirely on grasses and aquatic vegetation. Herds may number several dozen animals, and during the rut stags gather harems and bellow to challenge rivals. Females usually give birth to a single fawn after a gestation of about 240 to 250 days.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Habitat loss through conversion of grasslands to agriculture, poaching, and disease transmitted from livestock caused steep declines in the twentieth century. Intensive management at Kanha National Park, including grassland restoration and reintroduction, raised the hard-ground population from only a few dozen animals to several hundred, and is regarded as a notable conservation success.
