Circular dichroism

Circular dichroism (CD) is the differential absorption of left- and right-circularly polarised light by an optically active (chiral) molecule. Because the two enantiomers of a chiral molecule interact differently with circularly polarised light, CD spectroscopy is a standard tool for determining the absolute configuration, conformation and secondary structure of proteins, nucleic acids and other biomolecules.

Principle

When plane-polarised light passes through an absorbing chiral sample, the components of left- and right-circular polarisation are absorbed unequally (Δε = εL − εR). This produces elliptically polarised transmitted light. The effect is strongest in absorption bands of the molecule's chromophores, so a CD spectrum is measured as a function of wavelength, typically in the ultraviolet for proteins.

Applications

Far-UV CD (190–250 nm) reports on protein secondary structure: α-helices, β-sheets and random coils each give characteristic spectral signatures, allowing quantitative estimation of structural composition. Near-UV CD probes tertiary structure. CD is also used to monitor protein folding kinetics, to determine the handedness of DNA structures such as G-quadruplexes, and to characterise enantiomeric excess of small molecules.
