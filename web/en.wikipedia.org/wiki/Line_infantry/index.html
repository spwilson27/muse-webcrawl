Line infantry

TypeInfantry
RoleShock action, volley fire
Erac. 1650 – mid-19th century
EquipmentSmoothbore musket, bayonet

Line infantry was the type of infantry that composed the basis of European land armies from the late 17th century to the mid-19th century. Maurice of Nassau and Gustavus Adolphus are generally regarded as its pioneers, while in England its adoption is attributed to the New Model Army. These soldiers were trained to fight in rigid formations — the line, the column and the square — and were armed with smoothbore muskets fitted with bayonets.

History

In the late 17th and 18th centuries, line infantry advanced toward the enemy in long lines, usually two or three ranks deep, delivering volley fire by platoon or by rank. Because the smoothbore musket was inaccurate beyond about 100 metres, massed fire and disciplined manoeuvre mattered more than individual marksmanship. The bayonet charge remained the decisive action: after exchanging volleys, a regiment would fix bayonets and advance to break the opposing line by shock.

Tactics

The line maximized the number of muskets that could bear on the enemy but was slow to manoeuvre and vulnerable on the flanks. The column, popularised by the armies of Revolutionary and Napoleonic France, traded firepower for mobility and shock. The infantry square was the standard defence against cavalry. Line infantry was distinguished from light infantry and skirmishers (such as jägers and riflemen), who fought in open order, and from grenadiers, originally assault troops selected for size and strength.

Decline

The spread of rifled muskets in the mid-19th century, and then of breech-loading rifles and machine guns, made dense linear formations prohibitively costly, as seen in the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War. By the end of the century, line infantry in the old sense had disappeared, replaced by infantry fighting in extended order using cover.
