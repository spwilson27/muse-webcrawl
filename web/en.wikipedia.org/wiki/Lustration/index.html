Lustration

Lustration is the process of making public and restricting the participation of officials and collaborators of a former repressive regime — especially the secret police of communist governments in Central and Eastern Europe — in the political and administrative life of a successor state. The term derives from the Latin lustratio, a ritual purification.

After 1989

Following the fall of communism, Czechoslovakia adopted one of the first and strictest lustration laws in 1991, screening hundreds of thousands of people for collaboration with the StB secret police. Poland's process was longer and more contentious, culminating in the Institute of National Remembrance (IPN, established 1998) and expanded vetting laws in 2006–2007. Germany took a different route, opening the Stasi files to citizens through the agency headed by Joachim Gauck under the Stasi Records Act of 1991. Hungary, Bulgaria, Romania, and the Baltic states adopted varying and generally weaker schemes.

Debates

Supporters argue lustration is necessary for transitional justice, public trust, and preventing blackmail of officials by those holding secret files. Critics contend that secret-police files are unreliable, that screening can punish the innocent, violate due process, and be abused for political revenge — concerns partly upheld by constitutional courts and the Council of Europe, which issued guidelines on lustration in 1996.

Later uses

The concept has been applied beyond post-communist Europe, notably to Ukraine's 2014 "Government Cleansing" law after the Euromaidan, which barred many officials of the Yanukovych era from public office.
