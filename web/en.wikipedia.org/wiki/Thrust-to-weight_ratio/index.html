Thrust-to-weight ratio

Thrust-to-weight ratio is a dimensionless ratio of thrust to weight of a rocket, jet engine, or propeller engine, or of a vehicle powered by such an engine. It is an indicator of the performance of the engine or vehicle: an instantaneous ratio that varies over the course of a flight as fuel is consumed and thrust varies with altitude and speed.

Significance

For a vehicle to lift off vertically from rest, its thrust-to-weight ratio must exceed 1 (in Earth gravity). Fighter aircraft with ratios above 1 can accelerate in a vertical climb; modern fighters such as the F-15 and F-22 exceed a ratio of 1 at typical combat weights, whereas airliners have ratios of roughly 0.25–0.35 and rely on wings for lift. Launch vehicles typically lift off with ratios of about 1.15–1.3, trading acceleration against gravity losses and structural load limits.

Calculation

The ratio is computed as thrust divided by the product of the vehicle's mass and standard gravity. For engines alone, "thrust-to-weight ratio" compares the engine's maximum thrust with the engine's own weight and is a standard quality metric: modern turbofans reach roughly 5–6:1, some military engines exceed 8:1, and rocket engines such as the RS-25 and Merlin achieve much higher figures. The ratio must be quoted under specified conditions (sea level or vacuum, with or without afterburner) to be meaningful.
