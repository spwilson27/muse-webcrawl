Diary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A traditional paper diary]

A diary is a record (originally in handwritten format) with discrete entries arranged by date, reporting on what has happened over the course of a day or another period. A personal diary may include a person's experiences, thoughts, and feelings, and is generally written not for publication but for the writer's private use. The word comes from the Latin diarium, "daily allowance" or "daybook".

History

Day-by-day records are ancient — the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius's Meditations and the Japanese ladies' diaries (nikki) of the Heian period are early examples — but the modern personal diary flourished from the Renaissance onward with the spread of paper and literacy. The diary of Samuel Pepys (1660–1669) is the most celebrated in English, offering an unrivalled record of Restoration London. Later famous diaries include those of Anaïs Nin, Franz Kafka, Virginia Woolf, and Anne Frank, whose wartime journal The Diary of a Young Girl became one of the most widely read books in the world.

Types

Beyond the private journal, diaries serve many specialised purposes: travel journals and ships' logbooks, war diaries kept officially by military units, diet and exercise diaries used in medicine and self-monitoring, dream diaries, appointment diaries (desk diaries), and business diaries kept for legal and tax records. An institutional diary may be intended for publication, as with the published diaries of politicians.

Digital diaries

From the 1990s, diary writing moved online: early "online diaries" and services such as LiveJournal developed into blogs, and private journaling is now also done in dedicated apps. The line between diary, blog, and social-media feed is defined largely by the intended audience.
