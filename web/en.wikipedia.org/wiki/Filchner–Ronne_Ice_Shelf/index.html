The Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf (also known as the Ronne–Filchner Ice Shelf) is an Antarctic ice shelf bordering the Weddell Sea. It is the second largest ice shelf in Antarctica (and on Earth) by area after the Ross Ice Shelf, covering roughly 410,000 to 430,000 square kilometres. The seaward side is divided by Berkner Island into the eastern Filchner Ice Shelf and the larger western Ronne Ice Shelf.

Geography

The shelf is fed by several large ice streams draining the East and West Antarctic ice sheets, including Foundation Ice Stream, Rutford Ice Stream, Evans Ice Stream and the Slessor, Recovery and Support Force glaciers. Ice thickness ranges from several hundred metres at the grounding line to around 200 metres at the calving front. In 1998 the enormous iceberg A-38, about 150 by 50 kilometres, calved from the Ronne front — one of the largest icebergs ever recorded; further large calvings have followed.

Exploration

The Filchner shelf was discovered in January 1912 by Wilhelm Filchner's German Antarctic Expedition aboard the Deutschland. The Ronne shelf was sighted from the air and mapped by Finn Ronne's expedition of 1947–48, which named it for Ronne's wife, Edith Ronne. Research stations including Halley (nearby on the Brunt Ice Shelf) and seasonal camps have studied the region; the Filchner-Ronne Programme investigated its role in bottom-water formation in the Weddell Sea.

Climate significance

Cold, dense water produced beneath the shelf spills into the Weddell Sea and drives global ocean circulation. Modelling studies warn that redirection of warm ocean water into the Filchner Trough later this century could greatly increase melting and sea-level contribution — the so-called "Filchner-Ronne tipping point" scenario.
