Gestus

Gestus is a theoretical concept in the theatre theory of the German playwright Bertolt Brecht, denoting a specific bodily attitude, gesture or combination of gesture, posture and speech that expresses a character's social position and relationships. A Gestus is not merely a gesture illustrating emotion; it is a physically embodied statement about how people behave towards one another within a particular social situation — "the gist" of an attitude made visible.

An actor demonstrating a socially expressive Gestus.

Definition

Brecht developed the term (German: Gestus, related adjective gestisch) from the 1920s onwards as part of his theory of epic theatre. The "basic Gestus" (Grundgestus) of a scene is the fundamental social attitude it demonstrates — for example, one person deceiving another, ordering another about, or selling something to another. Every line and action in performance should be shaped so that this underlying social relation becomes clear to the audience.

Social Gestus and the theatre

The social Gestus is the gesture relevant to society, "the gest which allows conclusions to be drawn about the social circumstances." Brecht's examples include the way a worker tips his cap to a foreman, or how salesmen display goods. In performance, actors demonstrate characters rather than identifying with them, consistent with the alienation effect (Verfremdungseffekt): the actor shows the character's behaviour as something observed and quotable, inviting the audience to judge it critically and historically rather than empathise uncritically.

Gestus in music and staging

Brecht also applied the term to music: a piece of music has a Gestus when it sets a text in a way that clarifies its social attitude rather than merely intensifying emotion. His collaborations with composers such as Kurt Weill and Hanns Eisler sought a "gestic" music. The concept influenced later theatre practitioners and theorists of physical theatre, and remains a standard term in Brecht scholarship and actor training.
