Portrait medal

A portrait medal is a relatively large medal, usually cast rather than struck, bearing the portrait of a person on one side (the obverse) and an emblematic or allegorical design, often with an inscription, on the reverse. Unlike coins, portrait medals were not currency but commemorative or honorific objects.

History

The art form originated in Renaissance Italy. The medal of John VIII Palaiologos, made by Pisanello in 1438–39 during the Byzantine emperor's visit to Italy, is generally considered the first portrait medal and established the genre: a profile portrait with a Latin inscription obverse and a personal device reverse. Pisanello and contemporaries such as Matteo de' Pasti produced medals for the courts of Ferrara, Mantua, and Rimini. In the 16th century the art spread to Germany and France (including the work of Leone Leoni and Germain Pilon), and later medals were increasingly struck by machine. Medals of rulers and worthies remained popular collectables through the Baroque period and beyond.

Characteristics

Renaissance portrait medals were typically cast in bronze (sometimes silver or gold for presentation), 5–10 cm in diameter, with the sitter shown in strict profile in imitation of Roman imperial coins, reflecting humanist ideals of fame and individual reputation.
