Electrophilic aromatic substitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electrophilic aromatic substitution (SEAr) is an organic reaction in which an atom attached to an aromatic system, usually hydrogen, is replaced by an electrophile. It is the most characteristic reaction of aromatic compounds and a cornerstone of industrial and laboratory synthesis of substituted arenes.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in two steps. First, the π system of the aromatic ring attacks the electrophile, forming a resonance-stabilised carbocation known as an arenium ion (or sigma complex); this step temporarily breaks aromaticity and is usually rate-determining. Second, a base removes the proton from the carbon bearing the new substituent, restoring the aromatic ring.

Important reactions

  • Nitration — using the nitronium ion generated from concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids.
  • Halogenation — bromination and chlorination, usually with a Lewis acid catalyst such as FeBr3.
  • Sulfonation — with fuming sulfuric acid; reversible, so the sulfonic acid group can serve as a blocking group.
  • Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation — with alkyl or acyl chlorides and AlCl3.

Directing effects

Substituents already on the ring influence both the rate of further substitution and the position attacked. Electron-donating groups activate the ring and direct ortho/para; most electron-withdrawing groups deactivate it and direct meta, halogens being the notable deactivating but ortho/para-directing exception.
