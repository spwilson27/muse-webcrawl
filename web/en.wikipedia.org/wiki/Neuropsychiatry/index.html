Neuropsychiatry

Neuropsychiatry is a branch of medicine that deals with mental disorders attributable to diseases of the nervous system. It overlaps with both neurology and psychiatry, and its practitioners — neuropsychiatrists — are concerned with the psychiatric and behavioural consequences of brain disorders such as dementia, epilepsy, stroke, traumatic brain injury, Parkinson's disease, and multiple sclerosis, as well as with neurodevelopmental and functional disorders that sit at the boundary between the two parent specialties.

History

Before the 20th century, neurology and psychiatry were a single discipline: figures such as Jean-Martin Charcot, Emil Kraepelin, and Alois Alzheimer worked across what are now two fields. The fields diverged in the early 1900s as psychiatry turned toward psychoanalysis and neurology toward lesions and laboratory science. Interest in reintegration grew from the 1970s with the rise of brain imaging, neuropsychology, and the recognition that "neurological" diseases frequently cause psychiatric symptoms and vice versa. Professional bodies such as the American Neuropsychiatric Association (founded 1988) and combined training programmes in behavioural neurology and neuropsychiatry followed.

Scope

  • Behavioural and cognitive changes in dementia and other neurodegenerative diseases.
  • Psychosis, depression, and personality change following stroke, head injury, or brain tumour.
  • Psychiatric aspects of epilepsy, including postictal and interictal psychosis.
  • Functional neurological disorder and other conditions where symptoms lack a clear structural cause.
  • Neurodevelopmental disorders across the lifespan, including Tourette syndrome and attention deficit disorders.
  • Catatonia, delirium, and the neuropsychiatric effects of drugs and systemic disease.

Approach

Neuropsychiatric assessment combines the mental state examination with neurological examination, cognitive testing, neuroimaging, electroencephalography, and laboratory studies. Treatment is correspondingly integrative, combining pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, cognitive rehabilitation, and neuromodulation where appropriate. Proponents argue that the specialty challenges mind–brain dualism by grounding psychiatric symptoms in brain function while retaining psychiatry's attention to the patient's subjective experience.
