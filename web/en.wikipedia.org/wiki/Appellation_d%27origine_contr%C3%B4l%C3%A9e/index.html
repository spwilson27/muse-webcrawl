The appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC; French for "controlled designation of origin") is a French certification granted to certain geographical indications for wines, cheeses, butters and other agricultural products, administered by the Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité (INAO).

Principles

An AOC guarantees that a product originates from a defined area and was produced according to strict rules covering such factors as grape varieties or animal breeds, maximum yields, production methods and minimum ripening. The system rests on the concept of terroir — the idea that the character of a food or wine derives from its place of origin, including soil, climate and local know-how.

History

Early protections date to the 15th century (Roquefort cheese) and to 18th-century delimitations such as Chianti and Tokaj. The modern French system was created in 1935, largely through the efforts of Baron Pierre Le Roy, to combat fraud and protect winegrowers after the phylloxera crisis. The AOC framework became the model for the European Union's Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) system and for appellation regimes worldwide; since 2012 the label has formally been harmonised with the European AOP designation.
