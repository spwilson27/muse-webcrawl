Reno v. Condon

Reno v. Condon, 528 U.S. 141 (2000), was a case in which the Supreme Court of the United States upheld the Driver's Privacy Protection Act of 1994 (DPPA) against a Tenth Amendment challenge. The Court held that the Act, which restricts the disclosure and resale of personal information from state motor vehicle records, is a valid exercise of Congress's power under the Commerce Clause and does not violate the principles of federalism established in New York v. United States and Printz v. United States.

Background

Congress passed the DPPA after widely publicised incidents in which personal information obtained from motor vehicle records was used for stalking and murder, including the killing of actress Rebecca Schaeffer. The Act regulates state departments of motor vehicles as owners of databases and restricts their sale of drivers' personal information, subject to exceptions. South Carolina, whose law permitted such sales, sued, arguing that the DPPA commandeered state officials in violation of the Tenth Amendment.

Decision

In a unanimous opinion by Chief Justice William Rehnquist, the Court held that the DPPA regulates states as participants in the market for driver information — an article of interstate commerce — rather than dictating how states regulate their own citizens. Because the Act applies equally to private resellers and does not require states to enact laws or administer a federal programme, it does not commandeer state legislatures or executive officials.

Significance

The case is a key precedent limiting the anti-commandeering doctrine: Congress may regulate state activities that are also regulated in the private sector, even where compliance imposes costs on states.
