Domestic rabbit

The domestic rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus domesticus) is the domesticated form of the European rabbit, a member of the lagomorph order. Humans have bred rabbits for meat, fur, wool and as pets, producing more than 300 recognised breeds worldwide.

History

Rabbits were first kept in semi-domestic conditions in Roman warrens (leporaria), and true domestication is generally attributed to monks in southern France in the early Middle Ages, who kept rabbits as a source of meat permitted during Lent. Selective breeding in the 18th and 19th centuries produced distinct breeds for meat, fur (such as the Rex) and long wool (Angora).

As pets and livestock

Rabbits are the third most popular mammal pet in several countries; they are social, can be litter-trained, and live 8–12 years. As livestock they are raised commercially for meat in many countries and for laboratory research. Feral populations descended from domestic rabbits cause ecological damage in places such as Australia.
