Flux (metabolism)

Metabolic flux
Flux describes the rate of turnover of molecules through a metabolic pathway.

Flux, or metabolic flux is the rate of turnover of molecules through a metabolic pathway. Flux is regulated by the enzymes involved in a pathway. Within cells, regulation of flux is vital for all metabolic pathways to regulate the pathway's activity in different conditions. Flux is therefore of great interest in metabolic pathway modelling, where it is often studied through flux balance analysis.

In this manner, flux is analogous to the current in an electrical circuit or the flow of water through a network of pipes: it is a rate, typically expressed in units such as mmol per gram dry weight per hour, and at a metabolic steady state the fluxes into and out of each internal metabolite pool balance. Although a metabolite concentration can be high or low, what matters physiologically is how fast material moves through the network.

Measuring flux

Fluxes cannot usually be measured directly for every reaction inside a cell. They are commonly inferred using isotopic labeling: cells are fed a labeled substrate, commonly glucose labeled with the stable isotope 13C, and the resulting labeling patterns in metabolic intermediates and products are measured by mass spectrometry or NMR. These patterns constrain the possible flux distributions, an approach known as 13C metabolic flux analysis (MFA).

Flux balance analysis

Flux balance analysis (FBA) is a mathematical method for analyzing metabolism that does not require kinetic parameters. It uses the stoichiometry of a reconstructed metabolic network, encoded in a stoichiometric matrix, and assumes a steady state in which all fluxes sum to zero for each metabolite. Combined with constraints on individual fluxes (capacities, uptake rates) and an objective function — commonly maximization of biomass production — linear programming then predicts a flux distribution. FBA is widely used in systems biology and metabolic engineering to predict growth phenotypes and gene-knockout effects, and genome-scale models exist for organisms from E. coli to humans.

Control of flux

Metabolic control analysis quantifies how much each enzyme in a pathway controls the overall flux, using flux control coefficients: the fractional change in pathway flux resulting from a fractional change in enzyme activity. A key insight of the theory is that control is usually distributed among many steps rather than residing in a single "rate-limiting step," and the flux control coefficients of a pathway sum to one (the summation theorem). Flux is regulated over different timescales by allosteric regulation of enzymes, covalent modification such as phosphorylation, and changes in gene expression.

Applications

  • Metabolic engineering of microbes for production of fuels, chemicals, and pharmaceuticals
  • Understanding altered metabolism in disease, including the Warburg effect in cancer
  • Studying photosynthesis and carbon fixation in plants
  • Drug target identification by predicting essential reactions
