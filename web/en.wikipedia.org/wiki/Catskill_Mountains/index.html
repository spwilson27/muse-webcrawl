The Catskill Mountains, also known simply as the Catskills, are a mountainous region in southeastern New York State, west of the Hudson River. Geologically a dissected plateau rather than true mountains, they form the northeastern end of the Allegheny Plateau. The range's highest summit is Slide Mountain at about 1,270 metres (4,180 ft).

History and culture

The name derives from the Dutch Kaaterskill, "wildcat creek". The Catskills were made famous in American culture by Washington Irving's tale of Rip Van Winkle, set in their foothills, and by the Hudson River School painters such as Thomas Cole, who celebrated their wilderness scenery. In the twentieth century the region became a famous resort area — the "Borscht Belt" — of grand hotels serving chiefly Jewish vacationers from New York City, a milieu that nurtured generations of American stand-up comedians.

Environment

Much of the range lies within Catskill Park, a patchwork of public Forest Preserve land (protected as "forever wild" under the New York State constitution) and private land. The mountains also contain reservoirs of the New York City water supply system, including the Ashokan and Pepacton reservoirs, and their streams are celebrated for fly fishing — the Beaver Kill and Willowemoc Creek are often called the birthplace of American dry-fly fishing.
