Akrasia

Akrasia (Greek: ἀκρασία, "lacking command" or "incontinence") is the state of acting against one's better judgment: performing an action while knowing that a different action would be better. One classic example is procrastinating, eating a food one knows to be unhealthy, or continuing a habit one believes one should stop. The problem of akrasia — how it is possible to act freely and deliberately contrary to one's own judgment — has been a central topic in moral philosophy since antiquity.

In ancient philosophy

In Plato's Protagoras, Socrates argues that akrasia is impossible: no one goes willingly toward what they believe to be bad, so apparent cases of weakness of will must really be cases of ignorance or miscalculation of pleasures and pains. Aristotle, in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, takes the phenomenon more seriously, distinguishing the incontinent person (akratēs), who knows the good but is overcome by appetite, from the self-controlled person (enkratēs) and the vicious person, who does not even judge rightly.

Modern debates

The issue was revived in the 20th century by R. M. Hare and especially Donald Davidson, whose essay "How is Weakness of the Will Possible?" (1969) framed the puzzle: an akratic act seems to be both intentional and yet contrary to the agent's all-things-considered judgment. Philosophers have since debated whether akrasia requires a partitioned mind, whether it is a form of irrationality, and how it relates to compulsion, addiction, and free will.
