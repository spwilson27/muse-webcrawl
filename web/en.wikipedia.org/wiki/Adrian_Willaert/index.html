Adrian Willaert (c. 1490–1562)

Adrian Willaert (c. 1490 – 7 December 1562) was a Flemish composer of the Renaissance and founder of the Venetian School. From 1527 until his death he was maestro di cappella at St Mark's Basilica in Venice, one of the most prestigious musical posts in Europe, and he was among the most influential teachers and composers of his generation.

Life

Willaert was born in Flanders, probably at Roeselare, and went to Paris to study law before turning to music under Jean Mouton, a composer of the French royal chapel. He travelled to Italy, serving Cardinal Ippolito I d'Este in Rome and Ferrara, and possibly spending time in Hungary. In 1527 he was appointed maestro di cappella at St Mark's, Venice, succeeding Petrus de Fossis, and he remained in the post for 35 years, transforming the cappella into a leading European musical institution.

Music

Willaert composed masses, motets, hymns, psalms, chansons, villanelle, and madrigals. Exploiting the two opposing choir lofts of St Mark's, he developed writing for divided choirs (cori spezzati), most famously in his eight-voice psalm settings published in 1550, laying the foundation for the Venetian polychoral style later brought to fame by the Gabrielis. His large collection Musica nova (1559) contains some of the most ambitious madrigals and motets of the century. His pupils included Cipriano de Rore, Andrea Gabrieli, and the theorist Gioseffo Zarlino.

Influence

Willaert's synthesis of Netherlandish contrapuntal technique with Italian taste shaped the course of sixteenth-century music; contemporaries hailed him as a teacher of an entire generation of composers across Europe.
