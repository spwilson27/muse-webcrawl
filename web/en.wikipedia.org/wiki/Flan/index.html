Flan is a name shared by two distinct dishes. In much of the English-speaking world influenced by French and British cooking, a flan is an open pastry or sponge case filled with a sweet or savoury filling. In Spanish-speaking countries and in the Philippines, flan refers to a caramel custard dessert — the dish known in French as crème caramel — made with eggs, condensed or fresh milk, sugar, and a layer of caramelised sugar syrup.

History

The word derives from the Old French flaon, in turn from the Medieval Latin fladonem, a flat cake. Custard-based dishes date back to Roman cookery, where eggs were commonly used as a binding agent in both sweet and savoury preparations. The caramel custard form developed in Europe and was spread through colonisation to Latin America and the Philippines, where it became a staple festive dessert, often made with condensed milk and, in the Philippines, served as leche flan — a richer version traditionally made with egg yolks and carabao's milk.

Pastry flan

The pastry flan is an open tart consisting of a shortcrust or puff pastry base with a filling. Sweet fillings include fruit and custard; savoury versions include the quiche of Lorraine. In British usage, "flan" largely replaced "tart" for shallow open pies during the twentieth century.

Caramel custard flan

To make the custard version, sugar is caramelised and poured into a mould, followed by a mixture of eggs, milk, sugar and vanilla. The mould is baked in a water bath (bain-marie) until set, chilled, and inverted so the caramel forms a sauce over the custard. Variants include coconut flan, coffee flan, and the Peruvian crema volteada.
