Drip irrigation or trickle irrigation is a type of micro-irrigation system that saves water and nutrients by allowing water to drip slowly to the roots of plants, either onto the soil surface or directly into the root zone, through a network of valves, pipes, tubing and emitters. It is done using narrow tubes that deliver water directly to the base of the plant.

History

Primitive drip methods are ancient: clay pots buried in the soil were used in China more than two thousand years ago, and subsurface clay-pipe irrigation was practised in Roman-era North Africa. The modern system emerged in the 1930s–1960s through experiments with perforated pipe, and was commercialised in Israel by engineer Simcha Blass and Kibbutz Hatzerim, who founded the company Netafim in 1965. The arrival of inexpensive plastics made large-scale drip systems practical, and the technique spread worldwide, especially in arid regions.

Components and operation

A typical system comprises a pump unit, filtration equipment (essential to prevent clogging), a main line and sub-main pipes, lateral drip lines, and emitters or drippers that discharge a few litres per hour at each plant. Fertilisers can be injected into the water stream — a practice called fertigation. Modern systems may be automated with soil-moisture sensors and timers.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because water is applied precisely where it is needed, evaporation, runoff and deep percolation are minimised, and application efficiencies above 90 per cent are achievable, compared with roughly 60–70 per cent for sprinkler and flood methods. Dry foliage reduces fungal disease, and dry inter-row soil suppresses weeds. Disadvantages include higher initial cost, the risk of emitter clogging, salt accumulation at the wetting front, and vulnerability of surface tubing to damage.
