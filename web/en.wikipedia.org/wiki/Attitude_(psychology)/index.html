Attitude (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An attitude is a psychological construct, a mental and emotional entity that inheres in, or characterizes, a person. Attitudes are complex and are acquired through experiences. They are a person's predisposed state of mind regarding a value and are precipitated through a responsive expression towards oneself, a person, place, thing, or event (the attitude object), which in turn influences the individual's thought and action.

The ABC model: attitudes comprise affective, behavioural and cognitive components.

Structure

The classic, tripartite view of attitudes holds that an attitude is a summary evaluation of an object of thought that can be broken down into three components: the affective component (feelings about the object), the behavioural component (the tendency to act toward it), and the cognitive component (beliefs and knowledge about it). This is known as the ABC model of attitudes.

Formation and change

Attitudes are formed through experience, socialisation, conditioning and observation of others. Psychologists study attitude formation and change through persuasion, including routes described by the elaboration likelihood model. A major topic in attitude research is cognitive dissonance: the discomfort experienced when attitudes and behaviour conflict, which motivates people to restore consistency.

Attitudes and behaviour

Early research suggested attitudes predicted behaviour only weakly, but later work showed that prediction improves when attitudes are specific, accessible and measured at the same level of generality as the behaviour. Models such as the theory of planned behaviour incorporate attitudes together with subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.

Measurement

Attitudes are commonly measured with self-report scales (such as Likert scales), and indirectly with implicit measures such as the Implicit Association Test, which aims to uncover evaluations people may not consciously endorse.
