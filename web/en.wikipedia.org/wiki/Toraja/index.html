Toraja

The Toraja are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the mountainous region of Tana Toraja in South Sulawesi, Indonesia, numbering roughly 1.1 million, of whom about half a million live in the regencies of Tana Toraja and North Toraja. They are renowned for their elaborate funeral rites, cliff burials and great peaked-roof houses called tongkonan.

Culture and religion

Before the 20th century the Toraja practised an animist religion, Aluk To Dolo ("the way of the ancestors"), centred on a cosmos divided between the upper world, earth and underworld, and on reciprocal obligations between the living, the dead and the rice crop. Dutch colonial rule and missionisation made most Toraja nominally Christian (about 65% Protestant or Catholic, with Muslim and Hindu minorities), but Aluk rituals — officially recognised as a branch of Hinduism in Indonesia — persist within funerary practice.

Funeral rites

The Rambu Solo funeral is the most expensive and socially significant Toraja ceremony, sometimes delayed months or years while funds are raised; the deceased is meanwhile treated as "sick" and kept in the house. Buffalo and pigs are sacrificed — the buffalo escorting the soul to Puya, the afterlife — and effigies (tau-tau) and cliff graves guard the dead. The festivities draw large numbers of tourists and returning migrants (merantau), whose earnings finance the rituals.
