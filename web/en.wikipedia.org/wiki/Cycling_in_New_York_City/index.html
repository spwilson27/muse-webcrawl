Cycling in New York City

Cycling in New York City is associated with mixed cycling conditions that include dense urban proximities, relatively flat terrain, and a wide range of weather. The city has built an extensive network of bike lanes and greenways, and since 2013 has operated Citi Bike, one of the largest bicycle-sharing systems in the world. Cycling is used for commuting, recreation, racing and delivery work, and the number of daily cycling trips grew rapidly in the 2010s and 2020s.

History

Cycling boomed in New York during the bicycle craze of the 1890s, when paths such as the Coney Island cycle path were built. Car-oriented planning dominated the mid-20th century, and cycling was marginalised. Advocacy groups such as Transportation Alternatives (founded 1973) and events like the Five Boro Bike Tour (begun 1977) kept cycling visible. Under Mayor Michael Bloomberg and DOT commissioner Janette Sadik-Khan (2007–2013), the city built hundreds of miles of bike lanes, including protected lanes on avenues in Manhattan, pedestrianised parts of Broadway, and launched Citi Bike in May 2013.

Infrastructure

The city has more than a thousand miles of bike lanes, including parking-protected lanes, and off-street greenways such as the Hudson River Greenway on Manhattan's west side and the Brooklyn Waterfront Greenway. Major bridges — Brooklyn, Manhattan, Williamsburg and Queensboro — have dedicated cycling paths. Cycling across the George Washington Bridge connects the city to New Jersey.

Safety and policy

Cyclist fatalities and injuries remain a major policy issue; the city adopted a Vision Zero programme in 2014 aiming to eliminate traffic deaths. Protected lanes, a 25 mph default speed limit and enforcement changes followed. The rapid growth of food-delivery riding, often on e-bikes, has raised further questions about battery fires, rider pay and street safety.
