A collateralized debt obligation (CDO) is a type of structured asset-backed security. A CDO pools together cash-flow-generating assets — such as mortgages, bonds and loans — and repackages them into discrete tranches that are sold to investors with different levels of risk and return. Payments to investors flow in a priority order known as a waterfall: senior tranches are paid first and carry the lowest risk and yield, while the equity tranche absorbs losses first in exchange for the highest potential return.

Structure

A special purpose vehicle buys a portfolio of debt assets and issues the tranched securities. Rating agencies assigned investment-grade ratings to many senior tranches on the assumption that defaults in the underlying pool would not be strongly correlated. CDO-squared products pooled tranches of other CDOs, adding further layers of complexity, while synthetic CDOs used credit default swaps instead of physical assets to take positions on reference obligations.

Role in the financial crisis

CDOs backed by subprime residential mortgages expanded rapidly in the early 2000s. When US house prices fell and mortgage defaults rose together, the assumed diversification failed: supposedly safe tranches suffered heavy losses, valuations collapsed, and institutions holding CDOs faced enormous write-downs. CDOs thus became a central transmission mechanism of the 2007–2008 financial crisis, and issuance dropped sharply afterwards amid regulatory reform.
