Bordeaux wine (Occitan: vin de Bordèu, French: vin de Bordeaux) is produced in the Bordeaux region of southwest France, centred on the city of Bordeaux on the Garonne and the Gironde estuary. It is one of the largest and most prestigious wine-growing areas in the world, producing red, dry white, and sweet wines from roughly 120,000 hectares of vineyards and some 8,500 producers.

Styles and grapes

Red Bordeaux — called "claret" in Britain — accounts for the great majority of production and is almost always a blend. On the gravel soils of the Left Bank (Médoc and Graves), Cabernet Sauvignon predominates; on the clay and limestone of the Right Bank (Saint-Émilion and Pomerol), Merlot and Cabernet Franc lead. Dry whites are typically blends of Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, and the sweet wines of Sauternes and Barsac are made from grapes affected by the noble rot Botrytis cinerea.

Classification

The most famous ranking is the Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855, drawn up for the Exposition Universelle in Paris, which graded 61 Médoc and Graves châteaux into five crus classes; Château Lafite Rothschild, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion, and (from 1973) Mouton Rothschild form the premiers crus. Saint-Émilion maintains its own periodically revised classification, while Pomerol has none. Wines are sold under the appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) system, with some 60 appellations in the region.

History

Viticulture was established by the Romans, flourished under English rule from 1152 to 1453, and was transformed in the 17th century by Dutch drainage of the Médoc marshes. The trade through the port of Bordeaux made its wines a global benchmark, and the Bordeaux blend style has been imitated in nearly every wine region in the world.
