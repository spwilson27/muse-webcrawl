The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) is an astronomical observatory under construction by the European Southern Observatory (ESO). When completed, it is planned to be the world's largest ground-based optical and near-infrared telescope, with a segmented primary mirror 39.3 metres (129 ft) in diameter. The telescope is located on top of Cerro Armazones in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile, close to ESO's Paranal Observatory and the Very Large Telescope.

The ELT's light-gathering power and angular resolution are expected to allow direct imaging of Earth-like exoplanets, studies of the first galaxies, and precision tests of fundamental physics, including whether the fundamental constants of nature have varied over cosmic time.

History

ESO's Council approved the ELT programme in 2012, and construction formally began with a groundbreaking ceremony in 2014. Work on the access road and the levelling of the mountaintop preceded the start of foundation works in 2017; in June 2017 the top of Cerro Armazones was blasted to create a level platform. The contract for the giant dome and telescope structure was awarded to the Italian-led ACe consortium. First light, originally hoped for in the mid-2020s, has slipped with construction delays; ESO projected technical first light towards the end of the decade.

Design

The primary mirror, designated M1, consists of 798 hexagonal segments, each about 1.4 m across, with an additional set of spares, giving a total of 931 segments produced. The segments are made of the glass-ceramic Zerodur and are actively controlled to maintain the mirror's figure. A 4.2 m convex secondary mirror (M2) and a 3.75 m tertiary mirror (M3) feed two Nasmyth platforms. The flat quaternary mirror M4, nearly 2.4 m across and supported by more than 5,000 actuators, forms part of the telescope's adaptive-optics system, working with a flexible fifth mirror (M5) to correct atmospheric turbulence in real time.

Instruments

First-generation instruments planned for the ELT include the MICADO near-infrared camera, the HARMONI integral-field spectrograph, and the METIS mid-infrared imager and spectrograph. The ANDES high-resolution spectrograph (formerly HIRES) is planned for a later phase, targeting the characterisation of exoplanet atmospheres and the search for variations in fundamental constants.
