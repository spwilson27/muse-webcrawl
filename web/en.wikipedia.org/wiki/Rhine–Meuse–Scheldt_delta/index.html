Rhine–Meuse–Scheldt delta

The Rhine–Meuse–Scheldt delta is a river delta in the Netherlands and Belgium formed by the confluence of the Rhine, the Meuse and the Scheldt rivers. In some cases, the Scheldt delta is considered a separate delta to the Rhine–Meuse delta. The result is a multitude of islands, branches and branch names that may at first sight look bewildering, especially as a waterway that appears to be one continuous stream may change names as many as seven times.

Geography

The Rhine, entering the Netherlands near Lobith, splits into distributaries including the Waal, Nederrijn and IJssel. The Meuse joins the Waal system near the former island of Bommelerwaard, and the combined waters reach the North Sea through a network of estuaries around Rotterdam and Zeeland, including the Nieuwe Waterweg, the Haringvliet and the Oosterschelde.

Formation and flooding

The delta has been shaped for thousands of years by river sedimentation, peat growth, sea-level rise and human intervention — diking, reclamation and channel works. Catastrophic events such as the St. Elizabeth's flood of 1421, which created the Biesbosch wetlands, repeatedly reshaped it. After the North Sea flood of 1953 killed more than 1,800 people in Zeeland and South Holland, the Netherlands built the Delta Works, one of the largest flood-protection systems in the world, including the Maeslantkering storm surge barrier.

Economy

The delta is one of the most densely populated and economically important regions of Europe. The Port of Rotterdam, at the mouth of the Rhine–Meuse system, is Europe's largest seaport, and the Scheldt gives access to the Port of Antwerp, one of the world's leading ports.
