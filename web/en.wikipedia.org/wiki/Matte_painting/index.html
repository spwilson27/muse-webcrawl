Matte painting

A matte painting is a painted representation of a landscape, set, or distant location that allows filmmakers to create the illusion of an environment that is not present at the filming location. Historically, matte painters and film technicians combined several elements into an image with a portion of the image masked, then optically combined with live action. In modern films, the term "digital matte painting" describes computer-generated or digitally painted environments combined with digital footage.

History

The technique descends from the glass shots used since the earliest days of cinema; Norman Dawn used a glass shot in Missions of California (1907). Matte paintings were extensively used throughout classical Hollywood, notably by artists such as Albert Whitlock, whose work appeared in many Hitchcock films, and at studios' special effects departments for epics and science fiction films. Landmark examples include the vistas of The Wizard of Oz (1939), the original Star Wars trilogy, Raiders of the Lost Ark (including the famous warehouse ending), and Blade Runner.

The last major blockbuster to use traditional glass matte paintings extensively is often cited as Titanic (1997), by which time digital compositing had taken over. Today, digital matte painters work with Photoshop, 3D projection techniques ("matte painting in 2.5D"), and game engines to create entire film environments.

Technique

In traditional practice, the artist painted the scene on a sheet of glass, leaving a clear area for the live action, which was either photographed through the glass in-camera or combined later on an optical printer. Camera movement was restricted, since parallax would betray the trick; later motion-control cameras and digital techniques removed these limits.
