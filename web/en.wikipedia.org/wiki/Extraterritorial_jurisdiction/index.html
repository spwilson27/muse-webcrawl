Extraterritorial jurisdiction

Extraterritorial jurisdiction (ETJ) is the legal power of a government to exercise authority beyond its normal territorial boundaries. It may take legislative form (laws purporting to govern conduct abroad), judicial form (courts hearing cases arising abroad) or enforcement form (agents acting on foreign territory, the most restricted category). Extraterritorial claims frequently generate friction between states, since they sit uneasily with the principle of non-interference in internal affairs.

Bases of extraterritorial jurisdiction

International law recognises several principles under which a state may prescribe law for conduct outside its territory: the nationality principle (over its own citizens abroad), the protective principle (conduct threatening state security, such as espionage or counterfeiting its currency), the passive personality principle (offences against its nationals), the effects doctrine (foreign conduct producing substantial effects within the territory, widely used in antitrust and securities law), and universal jurisdiction over a small class of crimes — piracy, genocide, war crimes and torture — that any state may prosecute.

Examples

Well-known extraterritorial statutes include the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, which prohibits US persons and issuers from bribing foreign officials anywhere in the world; US sanctions legislation applied to foreign companies; the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, which reaches organisations outside the EU that process EU residents' data; and child-protection laws in many countries criminalising sexual offences by their nationals abroad. Historically, the capitulations and consular courts of the Ottoman Empire and nineteenth-century China subjected foreigners to their own national courts rather than local law — an arrangement remembered as a symbol of unequal treaties.

Diplomatic and military arrangements

Diplomatic premises are inviolable under the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, though they remain the territory of the host state. Status of forces agreements govern the criminal jurisdiction of the sending state over its military personnel stationed abroad, allocating cases between host and sending-state courts.

Controversies

Other states sometimes respond to aggressive extraterritorial assertions with blocking statutes that prohibit compliance, or clawback statutes allowing recovery of penalties paid. Disputes over extraterritorial sanctions, tax enforcement and data regulation remain recurring irritants in international relations, and enforcement jurisdiction — arrests or searches on foreign soil without consent — is generally treated as a violation of sovereignty regardless of the validity of the underlying claim.
