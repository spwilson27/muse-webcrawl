Attempt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An attempt, in criminal law, is an inchoate offense: conduct that falls short of completing a target crime but is undertaken with the intent to commit that crime. A person who tries to commit a crime and fails — for example, firing at a victim and missing, or being interrupted by police — may be guilty of attempt even though the intended harm never occurred.

Elements

Attempt generally requires two elements: (1) the mens rea of intent to commit the target offense, and (2) an actus reus consisting of an overt act beyond mere preparation. Jurisdictions disagree about how close the act must come to completion. Common-law tests have included the "last act" test, the "physical proximity" test, and the "indispensable element" test; the American Model Penal Code instead asks whether the defendant took a "substantial step" strongly corroborative of criminal purpose, a test adopted in many U.S. jurisdictions.

Defenses and impossibility

Abandonment or renunciation may be a defense where the defendant voluntarily and completely gives up the criminal purpose. "Impossibility" is treated differently by type: factual impossibility (the defendant fails because of circumstances, such as picking an empty pocket) is generally no defense, while pure legal impossibility (the intended act is not actually a crime) generally is.

Punishment

Attempts are usually punished less severely than the completed offense, though some jurisdictions punish attempted murder and certain other attempts very heavily. In English law, attempt is governed by the Criminal Attempts Act 1981; the similar inchoate offenses are conspiracy and solicitation (incitement).
