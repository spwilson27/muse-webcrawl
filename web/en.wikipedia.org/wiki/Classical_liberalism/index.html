Classical liberalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical liberalism is a political tradition and a branch of liberalism that advocates free markets and limited government under the rule of law, emphasising individual rights, civil liberties and economic freedom. It developed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries from the ideas of Locke, Adam Smith, Montesquieu and Mill, in opposition to absolutism, mercantilism and inherited privilege.

Its economic programme — low taxes, balanced budgets, free trade, sound money and minimal regulation (the Laissez-faire of the Manchester School) — dominated British policy for much of the nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, as “liberalism” in Britain and America came to include a larger welfare and regulatory role for the state (social liberalism), the label “classical” was revived by thinkers such as Friedrich Hayek and the Mont Pelerin Society to defend the older doctrine; in continental European usage “liberalism” often still means the classical doctrine.

Principles

Classical liberals hold that individuals have natural or fundamental rights to life, liberty and property that governments exist to protect; that legitimate government rests on consent and is constitutionally limited; and that spontaneous order — in markets, language and law — usually coordinates society better than central direction. They differ among themselves on the proper extent of the state, from minarchist night-watchman conceptions to Mill's acceptance of public education and some intervention. Critics charge that formal liberty without material means is hollow; defenders reply that markets and civil society, not state allocation, best secure both prosperity and freedom.
