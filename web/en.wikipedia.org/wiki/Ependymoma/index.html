Ependymoma

An ependymoma is a tumor that arises from the ependyma, the tissue lining the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. Ependymomas occur in both children and adults and account for roughly 2–3% of all primary brain tumors; in children they are the third most common central nervous system tumor. They may occur supratentorially, in the posterior fossa, or in the spine.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on location and may include headaches, nausea and vomiting from raised intracranial pressure, hydrocephalus, ataxia, and, for spinal tumors, back pain and weakness. Posterior fossa ependymomas in young children often present with vomiting and lethargy.

Diagnosis and classification

Diagnosis is by MRI and histopathological examination of resected tissue. Under the microscope, ependymomas characteristically form perivascular pseudorosettes and true ependymal rosettes. Modern WHO classification distinguishes molecular groups, including supratentorial tumors with ZFTA (formerly RELA) fusions, posterior fossa groups A and B, and spinal subtypes such as myxopapillary ependymoma.

Treatment and prognosis

The mainstay of treatment is maximal safe surgical resection, often followed by focal radiotherapy. Chemotherapy has a limited role, mainly in very young children to delay radiation. Prognosis depends strongly on the extent of resection, tumor location, and molecular group; posterior fossa group A tumors in young children have a poorer outcome.
