Source (journalism)

In journalism, a source is a person, publication or other record or document that gives timely information. Outside journalism, sources are the materials upon which research is based in other fields. Journalistic sources may include officials, eyewitnesses, documents, data and anonymous informants.

Attribution and ground rules

Information from sources is used under varying terms of attribution. Material given on the record may be quoted and attributed by name. Material on background may be used with a general description of the source but not their name. Material on deep background may be used but not attributed at all, while material off the record is given in confidence and not for publication. These conventions vary somewhat between countries and news organizations.

Anonymous sources

Journalists rely on anonymous sources for sensitive stories, including investigations of government and corporate wrongdoing; the most famous example is "Deep Throat" (later revealed as FBI official Mark Felt), who guided Washington Post reporting on the Watergate scandal. Most news organizations require editors to know an anonymous source's identity and seek corroboration, because anonymity can also allow sources to plant self-serving or false claims.

Source protection

Many democracies recognize some form of reporter's privilege or shield law allowing journalists to protect confidential sources, grounded in press-freedom principles, though its strength varies. Reporters have occasionally gone to jail for contempt rather than reveal a source. Sources who disclose information in the public interest are often called whistleblowers.

Primary and secondary sources

Journalists and scholars distinguish primary sources — original documents, data and firsthand accounts — from secondary sources that interpret or summarize them, and tertiary sources such as encyclopedias that compile secondary material.
