Thiosulfate

FormulaS2O32−
StructureTetrahedral central sulfur bonded to three oxygens and one terminal sulfur
Common saltSodium thiosulfate, Na2S2O3·5H2O
RolesReducing agent, ligand, photographic fixer component, antidote adjunct

Thiosulfate (also spelled thiosulphate in British usage) is an oxyanion of sulfur with formula S2O32−. It can be viewed as sulfate in which one oxygen atom is replaced by sulfur. The two sulfur atoms are not equivalent: a central sulfur is bonded tetrahedrally to three oxygen atoms and to a terminal sulfane sulfur, giving thiosulfate distinctive redox and coordination chemistry.

Preparation and properties

Sodium thiosulfate, the most familiar salt, is manufactured by reacting sulfur with sodium sulfite solution or from industrial sulfur streams. The pentahydrate forms clear crystals once called “hypo” by photographers. Thiosulfate salts are generally soluble in water and act as mild reducing agents. In acid, thiosulfate decomposes to sulfur dioxide and elemental sulfur, a cloudy reaction used in school kinetics experiments to illustrate rates and orders of reaction.

Redox and analytical chemistry

Thiosulfate reduces iodine to iodide while being oxidized to tetrathionate: I2 + 2 S2O32− → 2 I− + S4O62−. This stoichiometric reaction underpins iodometric titrations for oxidants such as chlorine, copper(II) and dissolved oxygen methods. Stronger oxidants or different conditions can carry sulfur further toward sulfate. Thiosulfate also removes excess chlorine from water and is used to dechlorinate samples and aquaria, though dosage and products depend on the chlorine species present.

Coordination and applications

Thiosulfate binds metal ions through sulfur (and sometimes oxygen), forming soluble complexes with silver and gold. In photography, fixer dissolves unexposed silver halide by complexing silver ions, preventing later darkening of prints and films. In gold processing, thiosulfate leaching has been studied and used as an alternative to cyanidation in some ores; it can be less toxic but is sensitive to copper, ammonia, pH and reagent consumption.

Medically, sodium thiosulfate is an antidote component for cyanide poisoning, providing sulfur for conversion of cyanide to thiocyanate, and is used in some contexts for calciphylaxis and cisplatin toxicity under specialist care. Geochemically and biologically, thiosulfate is an intermediate in sulfur cycling; sulfur-oxidizing bacteria can use it as an energy source, linking reduced sulfur compounds to sulfate production.

Stability and handling

Solutions are best kept away from acids and strong oxidants unless a reaction is intended. Acidification releases sulfur dioxide and precipitates sulfur, while bacterial action can also alter stored process solutions. Despite its reputation as a benign laboratory staple, concentrated reagents and medical preparations require normal chemical and clinical controls.
