Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres (29 August 1780 – 14 January 1867) was a French Neoclassical painter. Ingres was profoundly influenced by past artistic traditions and aspired to become the guardian of academic orthodoxy against the ascendant Romantic style. Although he considered himself a painter of history in the tradition of Nicolas Poussin and Jacques-Louis David, it is his portraits, both painted and drawn, that are recognised as his greatest legacy.

Early life and training

Ingres was born in Montauban, the son of a painter and musician. He studied at the Académie Royale of Toulouse before entering the Paris studio of Jacques-Louis David in 1797. In 1801 he won the Prix de Rome with The Ambassadors of Agamemnon in the Tent of Achilles, though financial difficulties delayed his departure for Rome until 1806.

Career

In Rome and Florence Ingres painted portraits of French officials and their families and developed his lifelong fascination with the female nude, beginning with The Valpinçon Bather (1808). La Grande Odalisque (1814), painted for Caroline Murat, queen of Naples, drew criticism for its elongated, distorted anatomy — the figure was said to have "three vertebrae too many" — yet it exemplifies his pursuit of idealised, sinuous line over strict anatomical accuracy.

Returning to Paris in 1824, Ingres was triumphantly received for The Vow of Louis XIII, exhibited at the Salon, and became the standard-bearer of classicism against the Romanticism of Eugène Delacroix. He directed the French Academy in Rome from 1835 to 1841. His late masterpiece, The Turkish Bath (1862–63), painted when he was over eighty, gathers his lifelong study of the nude into a tondo composition.

Style and legacy

Ingres prized drawing above all — "drawing is the probity of art," he declared. His portraits, such as those of Monsieur Bertin (1832) and the Comtesse d'Haussonville (1845), combine precise linear description with a sculptural sense of volume. Though seen in his lifetime as a conservative, his distortions of form and flattening of space profoundly influenced later artists including Degas, Renoir and Picasso.
