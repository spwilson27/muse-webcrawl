Apotheosis

Apotheosis (from Ancient Greek ἀποθέωσις, apothéōsis, "deification", from ἀπό + θεός, "god"), also called divinisation or deification, is the glorification of a subject to divine levels and, commonly, the treatment of a human being, any living thing, or an abstract idea in the likeness of a deity. The term has meanings in theology, where it refers to a belief, and in art, where it refers to a genre.

Baroque ceiling frescoes commonly depicted the apotheosis of heroes and saints ascending to heaven.

In religion and mythology

In Greek mythology, several mortals were raised to divine status after death, most famously Heracles, whose mortal part burned away on his funeral pyre while his divine part ascended to Olympus, where he married the goddess Hebe. Asclepius, the healer struck down by Zeus, was likewise deified. The Roman imperial cult institutionalised apotheosis: beginning with the deification of Julius Caesar (recognised by the Senate in 42 BC, marked by the comet interpreted as his soul), deceased emperors judged worthy were formally enrolled among the gods, receiving temples, priests, and sacrifices. The practice drew on Hellenistic ruler cults, in which kings such as Alexander the Great and the Ptolemies were worshipped as gods during their lifetimes or after death.

In ancient Egypt, pharaohs were regarded as divine in life and identified with Osiris after death. In imperial China, notable figures could be deified and worshipped, such as the war god Guan Yu, a historical general of the third century AD. In Japan, the emperor was traditionally regarded as a descendant of the goddess Amaterasu, and certain historical figures were enshrined as kami. In some Christian traditions, the related concept of theosis describes the transformation of believers by divine grace, though this is carefully distinguished from becoming God by nature. In Hinduism, devotees may be regarded as saints and, in some cases, avatars or deified after death.

In art

Apotheosis became a major genre of Renaissance and Baroque art, especially in ceiling frescoes and court painting, where rulers and heroes were shown ascending into heaven amid clouds and gods. Notable examples include The Apotheosis of the Pisani Family by Tiepolo and Constantino Brumidi's The Apotheosis of Washington (1865), the fresco in the dome of the United States Capitol, which depicts George Washington seated among allegorical figures. The genre served political as well as religious purposes, legitimising earthly power by associating it with divine sanction.

Figurative use

By extension, "apotheosis" is used figuratively for the highest point of development or the supreme exemplar of something — "the apotheosis of Romantic poetry" — and in narrative analysis for the stage of the hero's journey in which the hero gains ultimate knowledge or a divine boon before returning home.
