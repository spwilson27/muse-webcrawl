C. L. R. James

Cyril Lionel Robert James (4 January 1901 – 31 May 1989), better known as C. L. R. James, was a Trinidadian historian, journalist and Marxist thinker. His works, including The Black Jacobins (1938), a history of the Haitian Revolution, and Beyond a Boundary (1963), a study of cricket and colonial society, are widely regarded as classics of 20th-century political and cultural writing.

Life

Born in Tunapuna, Trinidad, James won a scholarship to Queen's Royal College in Port of Spain. He played cricket, taught, and published early fiction before moving to England in 1932, where he wrote cricket reports for the Manchester Guardian and became active in Trotskyist and Pan-Africanist circles, working with George Padmore. From 1938 to 1953 he lived in the United States, where he co-founded the Johnson–Forest Tendency before being deported during the McCarthy era. He returned to Trinidad during the independence period, then settled in London, where he died in 1989.

Works

The Black Jacobins (1938) recounted the slave revolution led by Toussaint Louverture that created Haiti, and reshaped the historiography of slavery and revolution. Beyond a Boundary (1963), part memoir and part analysis of cricket, asked "What do they know of cricket who only cricket know?" and became a landmark of sports writing.

Legacy

James influenced generations of Caribbean, African and British intellectuals, and his emphasis on the self-activity of ordinary people remains central to autonomist and postcolonial thought.
