Bretwalda

[Image placeholder: map of Anglo-Saxon kingdoms]

Bretwalda (also brytenwalda and bretenanwealda) is an Old English word first used in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, applied to some Anglo-Saxon kings from the 5th through 9th centuries who had achieved overlordship over some or all of the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. The term is generally understood to mean "ruler of Britain" or "wide ruler".

The list

The Chronicle's entry for 827 (829) lists eight bretwaldas, expanding on the seven kings named by Bede in his Ecclesiastical History as having held imperium over the kingdoms south of the Humber:

  1. Ælle of Sussex (c. 477–514)
  2. Ceawlin of Wessex (560–592)
  3. Æthelberht of Kent (590–616)
  4. Rædwald of East Anglia (c. 600–624)
  5. Edwin of Northumbria (616–633)
  6. Oswald of Northumbria (634–642)
  7. Oswiu of Northumbria (642–670)
  8. Ecgberht of Wessex (829–839), added by the Chronicle

Interpretation

Historians debate whether "bretwalda" was a formal title used in the kings' lifetimes or a retrospective label applied by the Chronicle's compilers. Some read it as evidence of a recognised office of overlordship in early Anglo-Saxon England; others argue the individual overlordships were personal, temporary, and varied greatly in extent, and that the Chronicle's list reflects 9th-century West Saxon perspectives. The related term brytenwalda, "ruler of Britain", has been interpreted as a claim to authority over the whole island, including its British (Welsh) peoples.
