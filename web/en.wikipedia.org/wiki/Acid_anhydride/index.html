Acid anhydride

An acid anhydride is a compound that formally derives from an acid by removal of water. In organic chemistry, the term most often denotes a carboxylic acid anhydride, with the functional group R–C(=O)–O–C(=O)–R′, formed by the dehydration of two carboxylic acid groups. In inorganic chemistry it refers to an acidic oxide that reacts with water to form an oxoacid.

Examples

Acetic anhydride is the most important organic acid anhydride, produced on a large scale for acetylation reactions and the manufacture of cellulose acetate. Maleic anhydride and phthalic anhydride are key industrial intermediates for resins and plasticisers. Sulfur trioxide and phosphorus pentoxide are inorganic anhydrides of sulfuric and phosphoric acid respectively.

Reactions

Anhydrides react with water to regenerate the parent acids, and with alcohols and amines to give esters and amides, making them useful acylating agents, generally less reactive than acyl chlorides but more reactive than esters.
