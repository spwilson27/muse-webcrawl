Gladius

Gladius (Latin: [ˈɡɫadiʊs]) is a Latin word meaning "sword" (of any type), but in its narrowest sense, it refers to the primary sword of ancient Roman foot soldiers. Early ancient Roman swords were similar to those of the Greeks, called xiphe (plural). However, during the third war with Carthage, Roman armies adopted the short, double-edged gladius Hispaniensis — the "Spanish sword" — which became the standard sidearm of the legionary for centuries.

A fully equipped Roman legionary after the reforms of Gaius Marius was armed with a shield (scutum), one or two javelins (pila), a sword (gladius), often a dagger (pugio), and perhaps a number of darts (plumbatae). The gladius was typically used with the scutum in close combat, thrusting from behind the cover of the large curved shield, though it could also be used for cutting.

Types

Several patterns are recognized by modern scholars, including the Mainz type, the Fulham type, and the Pompeii type. Typical gladii were about 60–85 cm long overall, with a blade of 45–68 cm and a width of about 5 cm; they weighed roughly 0.7–1 kg. Later, in the second and third centuries AD, the longer spatha gradually replaced the gladius as the infantry sword of the Roman army.

Etymology and legacy

The word gives rise to terms such as "gladiator" (swordsman) and the genus name of the swordfish, Xiphias gladius. The gladius has become an iconic symbol of ancient Rome and its military power.
