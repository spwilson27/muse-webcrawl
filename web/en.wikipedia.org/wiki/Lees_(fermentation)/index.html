Lees (fermentation)

[Image: Wine lees in a barrel]

Lees are deposits of dead yeast (and other particles) that form at the bottom of a fermentation vessel after fermentation and ageing of alcoholic drinks such as wine and beer. When the yeast cells die, they sink to the bottom; these residual deposits may be left in contact with the wine for a period of time, a practice known as sur lie ("on the lees"), or removed by racking.

In winemaking

In winemaking, lees consist of dead yeast cells (both Saccharomyces and other wild yeasts), fragments of grape skins and seeds, tartrates and other insoluble matter that settles after fermentation. Gross lees are the coarse deposits that fall soon after fermentation; fine lees are the lighter, finer particles that settle more slowly during ageing.

Ageing wine on its fine lees, common for white wines such as Muscadet (which carries the appellation sur lie) and for traditional-method sparkling wines such as Champagne, contributes texture, body, and complex flavours — often described as bready, yeasty or creamy — through the process of yeast autolysis, in which dead cells break down and release mannoproteins and amino acids into the wine. Stirring the lees, called bâtonnage, increases this contact and effect. Extended lees contact also helps protect the wine from oxidation.

In brewing

In beer brewing, the corresponding sediment of dead yeast, proteins and hop debris at the bottom of the fermenter is more commonly called trub. Bottle-conditioned ales are fermented with a small dose of yeast in the bottle, leaving a thin layer of lees in the finished product, which is usually left behind when pouring.

Other uses

The phrase "to drink to the dregs" derives from consuming the last of a drink including its sediment. Lees and spent yeast are also the raw material for yeast extracts such as Marmite and Vegemite, produced by autolysis of surplus brewer's yeast.
