Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of music composition that utilises recorded sounds as raw material. It was developed in Paris from 1948 by the French composer and broadcaster Pierre Schaeffer, who contrasted its "concrete" starting point — actual sounds collected from the world — with the "abstract" route of conventional composition, which begins with written notes.

Origins

At the Studio d'Essai of French radio (RTF), Schaeffer began experimenting with disc recordings: playing phonograph records backwards, at altered speeds, isolating fragments and locking grooves into loops. His first results, the Cinq études de bruits ("Five Studies of Noises," 1948), included the Étude aux chemins de fer, composed entirely from train sounds. With the arrival of magnetic tape in the early 1950s came splicing, looping and layering, and collaborators including Pierre Henry, with whom Schaeffer made the Symphonie pour un homme seul (1950). Their studio — formalised as the Groupe de Recherches Musicales (GRM) in 1958 — became an institution of French electroacoustic music.

Theory

Schaeffer argued for "reduced listening" — hearing a sound for its own qualities, detached from its source — and classified all sounds in his Traité des objets musicaux (1966) by their morphology. Sounds treated this way he called objets sonores (sound objects). Works were designed for loudspeaker performance ("acousmatic" music, heard without seeing the source) rather than for notation and performers.

Influence

Musique concrète's techniques underpin modern sampling, hip-hop production, sound collage and film sound design. Concrete methods fed into the BBC Radiophonic Workshop's work, the tape music of Stockhausen (who worked briefly in Schaeffer's studio), and the Beatles' tape experiments such as "Tomorrow Never Knows" and "Revolution 9." Its friendly rivalry with the purely electronic elektronische Musik of Cologne shaped the early geography of electroacoustic music.
