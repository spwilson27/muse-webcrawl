Portcullis

Type: Fortified gate grille
Material: Wood and iron
Used in: Medieval castles and gatehouses

A portcullis (from the French porte coulissante, "sliding door") is a heavy, vertically closing gate, typically made of wood, metal, or a combination, that slides in grooves in the jambs of a gateway. Portcullises fortified the entrances of many medieval castles, securely closing off the castle during a siege.

Description

The grille was constructed of vertical and horizontal bars, often ending in spikes at the bottom, and was suspended by chains or ropes from a windlass in a chamber above the gate. It could be dropped rapidly to seal the passage, or lowered and raised slowly as needed. Gatehouses often had two portcullises, so that attackers who passed the first could be trapped in the space between them — sometimes called a killing zone — and attacked from openings above such as murder holes.

Use

Unlike hinged doors, a portcullis could be dropped in seconds during a surprise attack and could not easily be battered or burned, since a fire set against it would not prevent it from being dropped. Iron-shod examples were resistant to axes. Portcullises remained in use in fortifications into the early modern period and survive in many castles, including the Tower of London.

Heraldry

The portcullis is also a well-known heraldic badge. It was a badge of the Beaufort family and appears in the arms and iconography of the Palace of Westminster; the crowned portcullis is the emblem of the UK Parliament.
