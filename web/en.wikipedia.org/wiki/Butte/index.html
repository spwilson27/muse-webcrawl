In geomorphology, a butte (/bjuːt/) is an isolated hill with steep, often vertical sides and a small, relatively flat top. Buttes are smaller landforms than mesas, plateaus, and tablelands: a common rule of thumb is that a butte is taller than it is wide, while a mesa is wider than it is tall. The word comes from French butte, meaning a small hill or mound.

Formation

Buttes form where a cap of hard, resistant rock (such as sandstone, lava, or dolomite) overlies softer strata. Erosion and weathering strip away the surrounding softer rock, leaving an isolated remnant protected by its caprock. Over time, continued erosion of the cap eventually undercuts it, and a butte may be whittled down into a slender pinnacle or spire before disappearing entirely. They are characteristic of arid and semi-arid regions, including the Colorado Plateau and the badlands of the western United States.

Famous examples

  • The Mittens and Merrick Butte in Monument Valley, Arizona–Utah
  • Devils Tower in Wyoming (an igneous intrusion sometimes described as butte-like)
  • Courthouse Butte and Bell Rock near Sedona, Arizona
  • Chimney Rock, Nebraska

In culture

Buttes are iconic features of the landscape of the American West and appear frequently in Western films, photography, and art. In mythology of some Native American peoples, buttes are said to be the petrified remains of giants or beings from ancient times.
