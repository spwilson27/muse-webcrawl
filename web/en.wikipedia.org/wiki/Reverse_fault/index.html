Reverse fault (thrust fault)

Reverse fault
Stress regimeCompressional (horizontal shortening)
Hanging wall motionUp, relative to footwall
Thrust faultReverse fault with dip of 45° or less

A reverse fault is a type of dip-slip fault in which the block above the fault plane—the hanging wall—moves up relative to the block below, the footwall. Reverse faults result from horizontal compressional stresses that shorten the Earth's crust. A reverse fault with a low dip angle (typically 45 degrees or less) is called a thrust fault; very large, low-angle thrusts along which the hanging wall has travelled many kilometres are sometimes termed overthrust faults.

Mechanics

Reverse faulting occurs where the greatest principal stress is horizontal, squeezing the crust. Because the hanging wall moves up against the force of gravity, such movement requires large tectonic forces and is characteristic of convergent plate boundaries and collisional mountain belts. Movement on reverse faults shortens and thickens the crust, and repeated slip can stack slices of rock (thrust sheets or nappes) on top of one another, placing older rocks above younger ones.

Structures

  • Thrust faults — low-angle reverse faults; the fundamental building blocks of fold-and-thrust belts in ranges such as the Alps, Himalayas, Rockies and Zagros.
  • Blind thrusts — thrusts that do not reach the surface, often expressed above by folding; the 1994 Northridge earthquake in California occurred on a blind thrust.
  • Duplexes — stacked, lens-shaped thrust slices bounded above and below by thrust surfaces.
  • Fault-propagation folds — folds that form at the tip of a propagating thrust.

Earthquakes

Megarhrust faults at subduction zones—very large thrusts forming the plate boundary itself—produce Earth's largest earthquakes, including the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile (magnitude 9.5), the 1964 Alaska earthquake, the 2004 Sumatra earthquake and tsunami, and the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan.

Distinction from normal faults

A reverse fault is the opposite of a normal fault, in which the hanging wall moves down under extensional stress. The two can be distinguished in the field by which block has moved upward and by the offset of marker beds.
