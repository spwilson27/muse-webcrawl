Apocope

Apocope
FieldPhonology / historical linguistics
DefinitionLoss of one or more sounds at the end of a word
FromGreek apokopḗ, "a cutting off"

In phonology, apocope is the loss of one or more sounds from the end of a word, most commonly an unstressed final vowel. The word derives from Greek ἀποκοπή, "a cutting off". Apocope is one of the regular sound changes studied in historical linguistics and has shaped the form of many modern languages.

Examples

English lost most final unstressed vowels (written final -e) during the Middle English period, so Old English nama became name, pronounced without the final vowel. In French, apocope removed most final vowels other than schwa, turning Latin lupu(m) into loup. In Spanish, apocope produces shortened forms such as buen from bueno before masculine nouns, and in colloquial speech affects forms like pa' for para.

Related processes

Apocope is the final counterpart of aphaeresis (loss at the beginning of a word) and syncope (loss in the middle). In rhetoric and poetry, apocope is also treated as a figure of speech in which a letter or syllable is omitted from the end of a word for effect or metre.
