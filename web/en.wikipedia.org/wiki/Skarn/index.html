Skarn

Skarns or tactites are hard, coarse-grained metamorphic rocks formed by metasomatism — the chemical alteration of a host rock by hot, chemically active fluids, typically adjacent to an igneous intrusion. Skarns are usually rich in calcium-magnesium-iron-manganese silicate minerals such as garnet (especially grossular and andradite) and pyroxene (diopside and hedenbergite), together with wollastonite, vesuvianite, epidote, and scapolite.

The word skarn comes from an old Swedish mining term for the silicate gangue associated with iron ore deposits in the Bergslagen district. Most skarns form when carbonate rocks such as limestone or dolomite are intruded by magma; fluids released from the cooling intrusion react with the carbonate to produce calc-silicate minerals. Skarns derived from carbonate protoliths are called exoskarns; those formed within the intrusion itself are endoskarns.

Economic importance

Skarns are important hosts of ore deposits. They are mined for tungsten (scheelite), copper, iron, tin, zinc, lead, gold, and molybdenum. Famous skarn districts include the tungsten deposits of the King Island mine in Tasmania, the copper skarns of Morenci, Arizona, and the iron skarns of Kiruna-type districts in Sweden.

Classification

Skarns are classified by their dominant metal (e.g., Fe skarn, Cu skarn, W skarn, Zn-Pb skarn) and by their calcic or magnesian mineralogy, reflecting whether the protolith was limestone or dolomite.
