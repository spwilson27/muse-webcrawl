Wilhelma was a German Templer colony in Palestine, founded in 1902 on land purchased near the village of Yehudiyya, northeast of Jaffa and close to modern Ben Gurion Airport. Named in honour of Kaiser Wilhelm II — recalling the Kaiser's 1898 visit to Palestine — it was one of the agricultural settlements established in the Holy Land by the Temple Society (Tempelgesellschaft), a pietist movement from Württemberg that had founded colonies at Haifa, Jaffa, Sarona and Jerusalem from 1868 onwards.

The colony

Wilhelma was planned as a farming village along a central street on the German model, with stone houses, a community hall and church, and prosperous mixed farming: dairy cattle, orchards, vineyards and grain. The colony maintained close ties with the neighbouring Templer settlements and benefited from the railway between Jaffa and Jerusalem and the road to Lydda. Its inhabitants held German citizenship, and during the First World War the men served with Ottoman-German forces; after the British conquest of Palestine in 1917–18, many Templers were deported to Egypt as enemy aliens, though most later returned under the British Mandate.

Internment and end

During the Second World War the British authorities interned the Templers of Wilhelma — where Nazi party organisation among younger settlers had been notable — converting the colony into an internment camp. German residents were progressively deported to Australia, many to the camp at Tatura, and exchanges and emigration emptied the colony. In April 1948, as the British Mandate ended, the remaining internees were evacuated, and Wilhelma ceased to exist.

Bnei Atarot

After the 1948 Arab–Israeli War the site was settled by Jewish refugees and veterans, who founded the moshav of Bnei Atarot in December 1948, partly named for the destroyed moshav Atarot north of Jerusalem. Many of Wilhelma's Templer houses survive in Bnei Atarot today and are protected as heritage buildings, making the moshav a visible remnant of the German colonies in Israel.
