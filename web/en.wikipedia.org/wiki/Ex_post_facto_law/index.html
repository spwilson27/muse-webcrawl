MeaningA law applied retroactively

An ex post facto law (Latin: "after the fact") is a law that applies retroactively — typically one that criminalises an act that was legal when committed, increases the punishment for an offence after it occurred, or otherwise alters the legal consequences of past acts to the disadvantage of the accused. The prohibition of such laws is a bedrock principle of the rule of law, expressed in the maxim nulla poena sine lege (no punishment without a pre-existing law), and is found in most modern constitutions and in Article 7 of the European Convention on Human Rights and Article 15 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.

United States

The Constitution of the United States prohibits ex post facto laws twice: Congress is barred from passing them (Article I, Section 9) and the states are barred as well (Article I, Section 10), reflecting the framers' experience of British and Confederation-era abuses. In Calder v. Bull (1798) Justice Samuel Chase defined four categories of prohibited law: laws criminalising previously innocent acts; laws aggravating a crime beyond its grade when committed; laws increasing the punishment; and laws changing the rules of evidence to make conviction easier. American courts apply the prohibition to laws that are criminal or punitive in effect, but not to merely civil or regulatory retroactivity — a distinction tested in cases over retroactive tax laws, deportation statutes and sex-offender registration schemes (upheld as regulatory in Smith v. Doe, 2003). Justice Chase's counterpart opinion by Justice Iredell in Calder is also a locus classicus of the debate between natural law and legal positivism.

Other systems and exceptions

Most legal systems distinguish retroactive criminalisation (prohibited) from retroactive changes in procedure or civil law (often permitted, subject to fairness and legitimate-expectation doctrines). The most famous exception is the Nuremberg trials after the Second World War, where Nazi leaders were prosecuted for "crimes against humanity" and wars of aggression not clearly codified in prior international law; the tribunal and later Article 7(2) of the European Convention justified this on the ground that the acts were criminal under the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations. Bills of attainder — legislative acts declaring a person guilty without trial — are closely related and are separately prohibited in the United States. The instinct against retroactivity also underlies protective devices such as the grandfather clause.
