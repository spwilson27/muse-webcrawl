Bessemer process

The Bessemer process was the first inexpensive industrial process for the mass production of steel from molten pig iron before the development of the open hearth furnace. The key principle is the removal of impurities by oxidation with air blown through the molten iron.

History

English inventor Henry Bessemer patented the process in 1856; William Kelly had developed a similar process in the United States. The process is carried out in a large tilting pear-shaped vessel, the Bessemer converter.

Process

Air blown through holes at the bottom oxidises silicon, manganese and carbon; the oxidation raises the temperature and keeps the iron molten. The blow lasts about 20 minutes and ends when the flame drops. Desired carbon and alloying elements are then added.

Impact and decline

Cheap steel enabled railways, skyscrapers, bridges and ships. The process struggled with phosphorus-rich ores until the Gilchrist–Thomas modification. It was largely superseded by the open-hearth and then basic oxygen steelmaking in the 20th century.
