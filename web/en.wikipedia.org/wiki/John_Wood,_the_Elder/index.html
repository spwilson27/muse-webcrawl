John Wood, the Elder (25 August 1704 – 23 May 1754) was an English architect, known as John Wood of Bath, who transformed the city of Bath from a modest spa town into one of the masterpieces of Georgian town planning. His designs for Queen Square, Gay Street and the Circus — built in the honey-coloured local stone — created the model of the elegant classical terrace that defined the Georgian city. He was the father of John Wood the Younger, who completed his work with the Royal Crescent.

Career

Wood trained in London, working on speculative developments such as Cavendish Square, and returned to Bath in the 1720s with ambitious plans for the expanding spa. Working with the entrepreneur Ralph Allen, whose quarries supplied the stone, and the Beau Nash social scene that drew fashionable visitors, Wood laid out Queen Square (1728–36), the first great set-piece of the new Bath, designed as a palace front in the Palladian manner.

The Circus and druidic design

Wood was fascinated by British antiquity, writing a speculative history of Stonehenge and designing with sacred geometry. His masterpiece, the Circus (begun 1754, the year of his death), is a circle of thirty-three houses whose diameter matches that of Stonehenge, its facades stacked with Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders and carved with emblems. He also designed the Exchange in Bristol and Liverpool Town Hall.

Legacy

Wood's Bath — completed by his son — is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the best-preserved ensembles of eighteenth-century urban design in Europe. Wood's writings on the origins of building and on Bath stone influenced the speculative builders who Georgianised towns across Britain.
