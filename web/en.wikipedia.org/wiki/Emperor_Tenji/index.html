Emperor Tenji (天智天皇, Tenji Tennō, 626 – 7 January 672), also rendered Tenchi, was the 38th emperor of Japan according to the traditional order of succession. As Prince Naka no Ōe he was the driving force of the Taika Reforms, which reshaped the Japanese state on the Chinese model.

The Isshi Incident and Taika Reforms

In 645, together with Nakatomi no Kamatari (founder of the Fujiwara), Prince Naka no Ōe assassinated Soga no Iruka at court in the Isshi Incident, destroying the Soga clan's dominance. The ensuing Taika Reforms proclaimed imperial ownership of land and subjects, instituted household registers and taxation, and centralised government along Tang lines — a programme Tenji continued for the rest of his life.

Reign

After his mother Empress Saimei's death in 661, Tenji ruled as crown prince without formally taking the throne for seven years, preoccupied with the defence of Japan after the catastrophic defeat of the Japanese-Baekje restoration army by Tang and Silla forces at the Battle of Baekgang in 663. He built frontier fortifications in Kyushu and moved the capital inland to Ōmi (Ōtsu), where he was finally enthroned in 668. His government issued the Ōmi Code (668), among Japan's earliest law codes, compiled household registers (the Kōgo registers of 670), and sponsored the construction of water clocks; Tenji is credited with introducing a clepsydra at the capital in 660.

Tenji was also a noted poet: the opening poem of the Ogura Hyakunin Isshu anthology is attributed to him ("In a hut in the autumn fields…").

Death and succession

Tenji died in late 671/672. A disputed succession between his son Prince Ōtomo (Emperor Kōbun) and his brother Prince Ōama erupted into the Jinshin War of 672, won by Ōama, who reigned as Emperor Tenmu.
