Gibson Girl

The Gibson Girl was the personification of the feminine ideal of physical attractiveness as portrayed by the satirical pen-and-ink illustrations of artist Charles Dana Gibson during a 20-year period spanning the late 19th and early 20th centuries in the United States and elsewhere. The artist saw his creation as representing the composite of "thousands of American girls."

Description

The Gibson Girl was tall and slender yet with the voluptuous figure made possible by a swan-bill corset. Her neck was slender and her hair piled high upon her head in the contemporary bouffant, pompadour, or chignon styles. She was portrayed as an equal — and sometimes a teasing antagonist — to men, confident, witty, and at ease in society. Gibson's illustrations depicted her as independent and self-assured: she rode bicycles, played golf and tennis, attended college, and smoked, even if she rarely worked for a living and remained, in Gibson's telling, ultimately in search of a husband.

History

Gibson's illustrations appeared in Life magazine and other major publications from the early 1890s. The image became a merchandising phenomenon, appearing on saucers, ashtrays, tablecloths, pillow covers, chair covers, souvenir spoons, screens, and fans. The Gibson Girl look influenced early film stars, such as Florence Lawrence and Mary Pickford, and was arguably the first national beauty standard in the United States.

Models for the image included Gibson's wife Irene Langhorne and her sisters, among them Nancy Astor, as well as the actress and model Evelyn Nesbit.

Decline

The Gibson Girl's popularity waned with the First World War. Changing fashions and women's roles — including suffrage activism and wartime work — made her image seem dated, and she was succeeded in the 1920s by the more daring figure of the flapper.
