Duration (finance)

Duration
Introduced by: Frederick Macaulay (1938)
Measures: interest-rate sensitivity

In finance, duration is a measure of the sensitivity of the price of a bond or other debt instrument to a change in interest rates. In more detail, it is the weighted average of the times until a bond's fixed cash flows are received, with the weights proportional to the present value of each cash flow. Duration is expressed in years.

Macaulay and modified duration

Macaulay duration, proposed by Frederick Macaulay in 1938, is the present-value-weighted average time to receipt of cash flows. Modified duration adjusts this by the bond's yield, giving an approximate percentage price change for a 1% change in yield: a bond with a modified duration of 7 will fall roughly 7% in price if yields rise by one percentage point. For larger rate moves, convexity provides a second-order correction.

Uses

Duration is central to immunization strategies, in which a portfolio's duration is matched to an investment horizon so that price and reinvestment effects of rate changes offset; to asset–liability management by banks and insurers; and to comparing interest-rate risk across bonds, where longer maturities and lower coupons generally raise duration.
