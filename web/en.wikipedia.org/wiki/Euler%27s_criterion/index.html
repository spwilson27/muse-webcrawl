Euler's criterion

Euler's criterion is a theorem in number theory, formulated by Leonhard Euler in 1748, that determines whether an integer is a quadratic residue modulo an odd prime. It states that for an odd prime p and an integer a not divisible by p, a is a quadratic residue modulo p if and only if a(p−1)/2 ≡ 1 (mod p), and a non-residue if and only if the same power is congruent to −1 (mod p).

Statement

In terms of the Legendre symbol, the criterion reads: (a/p) ≡ a(p−1)/2 (mod p). This gives an effective way to evaluate Legendre symbols and is a step toward the law of quadratic reciprocity.

Proof idea

By Fermat's little theorem, ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p), so a(p−1)/2 ≡ ±1. If a is a residue, a ≡ x², and raising to (p−1)/2 gives xp−1 ≡ 1. Conversely, the polynomial X(p−1)/2 − 1 has at most (p−1)/2 roots modulo p, exactly matching the (p−1)/2 residues, so only residues satisfy the congruence.

Applications

Euler's criterion is used in primality testing, algorithms for computing square roots modulo primes, and generalizations to the Euler–Jacobi pseudoprime test.
