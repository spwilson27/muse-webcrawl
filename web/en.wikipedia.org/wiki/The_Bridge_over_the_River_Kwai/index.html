The Bridge over the River Kwai


The bridge on the River Kwai at Kanchanaburi, Thailand

The Bridge over the River Kwai (French: Le Pont de la rivière Kwaï) is a 1952 novel by the French author Pierre Boulle, and the basis of the celebrated 1957 film The Bridge on the River Kwai directed by David Lean. Both works fictionalise the construction of the Burma Railway by Allied prisoners of war held by the Japanese during the Second World War.

Plot

British prisoners under Lieutenant Colonel Nicholson are ordered by their captor, Colonel Saito, to build a railway bridge over the River Kwai. Nicholson, after winning a confrontation over the treatment of officers, becomes obsessed with completing a bridge that will stand as a monument to British discipline and engineering — even though it serves the enemy's war effort. Meanwhile a commando team is dispatched to destroy the bridge, setting up the story's tragic climax in which Nicholson, realising what he has done, falls upon the detonator.

The film

Lean's film, starring Alec Guinness as Nicholson and Sessue Hayakawa as Saito, won seven Academy Awards including Best Picture, Best Director and Best Actor. Its whistling rendition of the "Colonel Bogey March" became iconic. The screenplay, credited to Pierre Boulle, was secretly written by the blacklisted Carl Foreman and Michael Wilson, whose authorship was only officially acknowledged decades later.

Historical background

The real Burma Railway cost the lives of an estimated 13,000 Allied POWs and perhaps 90,000 Asian labourers. The actual bridge at Kanchanaburi (over the Mae Klong, renamed the Khwae Yai) survived the war and remains a major memorial and tourist site; Boulle, himself a former prisoner in Indochina, drew on his experiences while stressing that Nicholson was a fictional character.
