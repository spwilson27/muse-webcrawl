Nucleoside

A nucleoside is a molecule consisting of a nitrogenous base — a purine or pyrimidine — joined to a five-carbon sugar, either ribose or deoxyribose, by a β-glycosidic bond. A nucleoside plus one or more phosphate groups is a nucleotide, the building block of nucleic acids.

Common nucleosides

DeoxyribonucleosidesDeoxyadenosine, deoxyguanosine, deoxycytidine, thymidine

Naming

Nucleosides are named after their base with characteristic suffixes: the purine nucleosides end in -osine (adenosine, guanosine, inosine) and the pyrimidine nucleosides in -idine (cytidine, uridine, thymidine). The prefix deoxy- denotes the deoxyribose forms found in DNA.

Function and medicine

Within cells, nucleosides are phosphorylated by kinases to nucleotides such as ATP, the cell's principal energy currency. Modified nucleosides occur naturally in transfer RNA (for example pseudouridine and inosine) and are important in medicine: many antiviral and anticancer drugs are nucleoside analogues — including zidovudine (AZT), acyclovir, and remdesivir — which are converted to nucleotides in the cell and interfere with viral or cellular polymerases. Pseudouridine modification of mRNA, notably N1-methylpseudouridine, underlies the mRNA vaccines against COVID-19.
