Carbon emission trading

Carbon emission trading (also called cap and trade) is a market-based approach to controlling pollution by providing economic incentives for reducing emissions of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases. A central authority sets a cap on total emissions and issues a corresponding number of allowances, each permitting the holder to emit one tonne of CO2 equivalent. Firms that emit less than their allocation can sell surplus allowances to firms that would find abatement more expensive, so reductions occur where they are cheapest.

Under cap and trade, allowances flow from firms that cut emissions cheaply to firms facing higher abatement costs.

How it works

The regulator fixes the cap — which typically declines over time — and distributes allowances by auction, by free allocation (grandfathering or benchmarking), or a mixture. Covered installations must monitor and report their emissions and surrender one allowance per tonne emitted. Because allowances are tradable, a market price for carbon emerges; firms abate whenever their marginal abatement cost is below the price and buy allowances otherwise. Banking of allowances for future years, and sometimes limited borrowing or the use of offset credits, add flexibility.

History

The economics of tradable permits were developed by Ronald Coase, J. H. Dales and W. David Montgomery. The first large-scale application was not to carbon but to sulphur dioxide under the U.S. Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, whose acid-rain programme cut emissions faster and more cheaply than projected. The 1997 Kyoto Protocol established international emissions trading among developed countries, and the EU Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005, became the flagship carbon market. China launched the world's largest scheme by covered emissions in 2021, initially covering the power sector.

Economics and debate

Supporters argue that cap and trade guarantees a quantity of emissions reductions while minimising total cost, and that auctioning can raise revenue for climate policy or household compensation. Critics point to price volatility, the windfall profits some firms earned from free allocation in early EU phases, overallocation that collapsed prices in 2007–2013, carbon leakage to unregulated jurisdictions, and the patchy environmental integrity of offset credits. Economists often compare trading favourably or unfavourably with a carbon tax, which fixes the price instead of the quantity; hybrid designs with price floors and ceilings attempt to combine both advantages.

Major schemes

  • EU ETS — covers power, industry and intra-European aviation in the EU, EEA and (linked) Switzerland; the cap declines by a linear reduction factor each year.
  • China national ETS — intensity-based allocation for power generators, covering over 4 billion tonnes of CO2 annually.
  • California / Western Climate Initiative — linked with Québec; covers fuels and industry.
  • RGGI — the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative among northeastern U.S. states, covering power plants.
  • UK ETS and New Zealand ETS — national schemes launched in 2021 and 2008 respectively.
