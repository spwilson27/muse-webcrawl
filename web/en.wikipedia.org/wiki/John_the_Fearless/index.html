John I (French: Jean sans Peur; 28 May 1371 – 10 September 1419) was a scion of the French royal family who ruled the Burgundian State from 1404 until his assassination in 1419. He played a key role in French national affairs during the early 15th century, particularly in the struggles to place the kingdom under the regency of a mentally ill king, Charles VI, during the Hundred Years' War with England, and the Armagnac–Burgundian Civil War that his policies ignited.

Early life and Nicopolis

John was the eldest son of Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, and Margaret of Flanders. In 1396 he led the Franco-Burgundian contingent on the crusade that ended in disaster at the Battle of Nicopolis against Sultan Bayezid I. Captured by the Ottomans, he was ransomed for an enormous sum paid by his father; his conduct in battle earned him the epithet "the Fearless." He succeeded his father as duke in 1404.

Rivalry with Orléans and civil war

In Paris John competed for control of the royal government with his cousin Louis, Duke of Orléans, brother of the intermittently insane Charles VI. In November 1407, John had Louis assassinated in a Paris street — the first murder of a French prince of the blood in the civil war era — and boldly justified it as tyrannicide in a public defense by the theologian Jean Petit. The ensuing Armagnac–Burgundian Civil War divided France: John's "Burgundians" drew support from Paris and the northern towns, while the "Armagnacs" rallied around the Dauphin and the Orléanist princes. John pursued popular reforms (the Cabochien ordinance of 1413) and cultivated the Parisian butchers' guild and the university.

Hundred Years' War and assassination

During Henry V's invasion of 1415, John kept his distance from the French army destroyed at Agincourt, maneuvering between the crown and the English. In 1418 his forces seized Paris and massacred Armagnac prisoners; the Dauphin Charles fled to Bourges. Seeking a joint front against the English, John met the Dauphin on the bridge at Montereau on 10 September 1419 — and was cut down by the Dauphin's men, in apparent revenge for the Orléans murder.

Legacy

John's assassination drove his son and successor, Philip the Good, into the English alliance sealed by the Treaty of Troyes (1420), which disinherited the Dauphin and prolonged the war. John was buried in the Chartreuse de Champmol in Dijon. His emblem, a carpenter's plane, symbolized his intention to "plane down" his Orléanist enemies.
