Latin literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A manuscript page of Virgil's Aeneid in Carolingian minuscule]
A manuscript page of Virgil's Aeneid in Carolingian minuscule

Latin literature is the body of writing in the Latin language from the Roman Republic through the Empire and, in continued forms, the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Its classical canon — the 'Golden Age' of Cicero, Virgil, Horace, Ovid and Livy, and the 'Silver Age' of Seneca, Tacitus and Juvenal — shaped European literature, rhetoric and education for two thousand years.

Republic and Golden Age

Latin writing began with translations from Greek: Livius Andronicus's Odyssey (c. 240 BC). Comedy flowered with Plautus and Terence; Lucretius wrote Epicurean philosophy as epic verse (De rerum natura); Catullus remade lyric poetry. The Augustan age produced Virgil's Aeneid and Georgics, Horace's odes and Ars Poetica, Ovid's Metamorphoses, the elegists and Livy's history — while Cicero's speeches and treatises fixed Latin prose style.

Silver Age and late antiquity

Under the emperors, Tacitus anatomised tyranny in the Annals; Juvenal and Martial sharpened satire and epigram; Seneca's tragedies and essays, Pliny the Elder's encyclopaedia, Apuleius's novel The Golden Ass and Petronius's fragmentary Satyricon extended the range. Christian Latin — Augustine's Confessions, Jerome's Vulgate Bible — carried the language into the medieval world, where Latin remained Europe's scholarly lingua franca into the eighteenth century.

Legacy

Latin literature supplied the forms (epic, ode, satire, epistle, novel), the rhetoric curriculum and much of the mythology through which Europe read the ancient world; neologisms, quotations ('carpe diem', 'arma virumque cano') and genres from Renaissance humanism to the modern historical novel descend from it.
