Traprain Law is a hill and hill fort about 221 m (725 ft) in elevation, located approximately 6 km (4 miles) east of Haddington in East Lothian, Scotland. A distinctive volcanic intrusion rising sharply from the surrounding plain, it was a major settlement of the Votadini tribe and is famous as the findspot of the Traprain Law treasure, one of the largest hoards of late Roman silver known from beyond the imperial frontier.

History

The hill was occupied from the Bronze Age, and by the Iron Age it had become a fortified oppidum enclosed by massive ramparts. At its greatest extent the fort covered about 16 hectares (40 acres). During the Roman period the Votadini appear to have been on friendly terms with Rome, and Traprain flourished as a trading centre, receiving Roman goods over several centuries. Occupation continued into the early fifth century AD, after the Roman withdrawal from Britain.

The Traprain Law treasure

In May 1919, excavations directed by Alexander Curle uncovered a hoard of Roman silver plate buried in a pit within the fort. The treasure comprised over 23 kg (53 lb) of silver — flagons, bowls, spoons, strainers and other vessels, many Christian in character and most deliberately cut up into pieces ("hacksilver"), probably so that it could be divided as bullion or payment. The hoard, dated to the early fifth century AD, is now displayed in the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh. It is interpreted as loot, diplomatic gifts, or payment to frontier allies.

Later associations

Traprain Law has been identified in tradition with the court of King Lot of Lothian in Arthurian legend, and the nearby village of Athelstaneford claims association with the origin of the Scottish saltire flag.
