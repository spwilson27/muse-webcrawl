Autarky

A self-sufficient closed economy

Autarky is the characteristic of self-sufficiency, usually applied to societies, communities, states, and their economic systems. In an autarkic economy, international trade is absent or heavily curtailed: the entity can survive and continue its activities without external assistance or trade, producing everything it consumes.

History of the concept

The ideal of self-sufficiency was praised in ancient Greek philosophy, notably by Aristotle, who regarded a self-sufficient city-state as the proper end of the polis. Mercantilist writers later favoured maximising exports and minimising dependence on imports, though true autarky in the modern sense emerged as a policy of states in the twentieth century.

Twentieth-century examples

Fascist Italy promoted autarchia under Mussolini, particularly after League of Nations sanctions over the invasion of Ethiopia in 1935; Francoist Spain pursued autarkic policies from 1939 until the liberalising Stabilization Plan of 1959. Nazi Germany's Four Year Plan of 1936 sought to make the German economy war-ready through synthetic substitutes and domestic production. Albania under Enver Hoxha and North Korea under the Juche ideology are frequently cited as the closest modern approximations of national autarky, though neither was completely closed.

Economic analysis

Economists generally argue that autarky forgoes the gains from comparative advantage and specialisation, lowering real incomes; the "autarky price" — the domestic price ratio that would prevail without trade — is a standard benchmark in trade theory. Partial self-sufficiency may nevertheless be pursued for strategic reasons, such as food and energy security.
