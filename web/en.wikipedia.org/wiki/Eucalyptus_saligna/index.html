Eucalyptus saligna

KingdomPlantae
FamilyMyrtaceae
GenusEucalyptus
SpeciesE. saligna Sm.
Common namesSydney blue gum, blue gum

Eucalyptus saligna, commonly known as the Sydney blue gum or blue gum, is a species of tall forest tree of the family Myrtaceae, native to eastern Australia. It is a straight-trunked tree growing to 30–55 metres, occasionally over 60 metres, with smooth pale grey or white bark that often has a rough, fibrous "stocking" at the base, and a dense green crown of lance-shaped leaves.

Description and habitat

The species was formally described by James Edward Smith in 1797. Its white flowers appear in summer and are an important nectar source, and its foliage is eaten by koalas. E. saligna occurs in tall open forest on fertile soils along the coast and ranges from near Batemans Bay in southern New South Wales north to southeastern Queensland, often in association with flooded gum (Eucalyptus grandis), with which it is sometimes confused and can hybridise.

Uses

Sydney blue gum is one of Australia's most important plantation and timber trees. Its wood is moderately durable, pinkish to dark red-brown, and is used for general construction, flooring, poles and paper pulp. Because of its fast growth it has been planted extensively outside Australia — in South Africa, South America, Hawaii and elsewhere — though, like other eucalypts planted abroad, it can naturalise and become invasive in some regions.
