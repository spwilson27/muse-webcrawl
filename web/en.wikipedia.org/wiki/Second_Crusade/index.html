The Second Crusade (1147–1150) was the second major crusade launched from Europe, announced by Pope Eugene III in response to the fall of the County of Edessa to the forces of Imad ad-Din Zengi in 1144. It was the first crusade led by kings — Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany — and was preached across Europe, most famously by Bernard of Clairvaux. Despite its scale, the eastern expedition ended in failure.

Prelude

Edessa, the first crusader state founded, fell on 24 December 1144. Pope Eugene III issued the bull Quantum praedecessores in December 1145, and Bernard's preaching at Vézelay in 1146 inspired Louis VII and thousands of others to take the cross; Conrad III was persuaded later that year.

The eastern expedition

The German army was shattered by the Seljuk Turks at the Battle of Dorylaeum in October 1147 while crossing Anatolia; the French were mauled in the same region at Mount Cadmus in January 1148. The kings reached the Holy Land by sea with their remnants. At the Council of Acre (June 1148) the crusaders, with the leaders of Jerusalem, chose to attack Damascus — then nominally an ally of Jerusalem against Zengi — rather than Edessa. The siege of Damascus (24–28 July 1148) collapsed within days amid recriminations, and the crusade dissolved. The failure was a scandal in Europe and damaged Outremer's position, easing the path of Nur ad-Din and later Saladin.

Iberian and Baltic fronts

The crusade had greater success elsewhere: an Anglo-Flemish-German fleet helped Afonso I of Portugal capture Lisbon from the Moors in 1147, and Spanish forces took Almería and Tortosa. In the north, the Wendish Crusade was preached against the pagan Slavs east of the Elbe.

Legacy

The Second Crusade's failure in the east deepened Muslim unity under Nur ad-Din and set the stage for the loss of Jerusalem in 1187 and the Third Crusade. In Iberia, by contrast, it contributed significantly to the Reconquista.
