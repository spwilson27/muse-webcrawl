Sail plan
RelatedRig, sail, mast

A sail plan is a drawing of a sailing craft, viewed from the side, depicting its sails, the spars that carry them, and some of the rigging. By extension, "sail plan" also describes the configuration of sails themselves — which sails a vessel carries, their sizes, shapes and arrangement.

A wide variety of sail plans have been used on craft over the centuries, adapted to local materials, weather conditions, the cargo or purpose of the vessel, and the state of rigging technology. The sail plan determines a vessel's sailing characteristics: how close to the wind it can sail, how fast it is on each point of sail, and how much crew is needed to handle it.

Types of rig

Common sail plans include:

  • Sloop — a single mast with a mainsail aft and one headsail forward; the most common plan for modern yachts.
  • Cutter — similar to a sloop but with two or more headsails and the mast set further aft.
  • Ketch and yawl — two-masted plans with a smaller mizzen mast aft of the main mast.
  • Schooner — two or more masts with the aft mast tallest, traditionally fore-and-aft rigged.
  • Cat rig — a single sail on a mast stepped near the bow.
  • Lateen — a triangular sail on a long diagonal yard, used from the Mediterranean to the Indian Ocean.
  • Junk rig — fully battened sails used on Chinese and Southeast Asian vessels.
  • Square rig — sails set on horizontal yards across the masts, characteristic of large ocean-going sailing ships.

Considerations

Designers balance sail area against stability: the centre of effort of the sail plan must relate correctly to the hull's centre of lateral resistance to give the vessel a balanced helm. Dividing the sail area among several smaller sails makes them easier to handle with a small crew, a principle behind sail training vessels and cruising yachts alike.
