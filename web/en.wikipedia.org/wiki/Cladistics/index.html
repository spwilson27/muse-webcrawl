Cladistics

A cladogram showing relationships among groups.

Cladistics is an approach to biological classification in which organisms are grouped into clades—each consisting of a common ancestor and all of its descendants—based on shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies). The method was founded by the German entomologist Willi Hennig, whose 1950 work, translated in 1966 as Phylogenetic Systematics, laid out the principles of reconstructing evolutionary trees, or cladograms, from character data.

Principles

Cladistic analysis distinguishes ancestral traits (plesiomorphies) from derived ones (apomorphies). Only shared derived traits are evidence of common descent. Groups that include an ancestor and all its descendants are monophyletic and are the only groups cladistics accepts as natural; groups that exclude some descendants are paraphyletic (such as "reptiles" when birds are excluded), and groupings based on convergent traits are polyphyletic. Competing cladograms are typically evaluated by parsimony—the preferred tree minimizes the number of evolutionary changes required.

Methods

Characters may be morphological or molecular (DNA or protein sequences). Modern analyses use computer algorithms to search vast numbers of possible trees, with statistical support assessed by methods such as bootstrapping and Bayesian inference. Matrix-based analyses combine many characters scored across taxa.

Impact

Cladistics transformed taxonomy from the mid-twentieth century, replacing classifications based on overall similarity with ones reflecting evolutionary history. It underlies modern phylogenetic nomenclature and has been applied beyond biology, for instance in linguistics and textual criticism, where manuscripts or languages descend with modification.
