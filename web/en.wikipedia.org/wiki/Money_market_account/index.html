Money market account

A money market account (MMA), also called a money market deposit account (MMDA), is a deposit account offered by banks and credit unions in the United States that pays interest based on current rates in the money markets, typically higher than a regular savings account, while offering limited check-writing or debit transaction privileges. Money market accounts are insured by the FDIC (for banks) or the NCUA (for credit unions) up to the standard limits.

Money market accounts generally pay higher interest than ordinary savings accounts, in exchange for higher minimum balances.

History

Money market accounts were authorised by the Garn–St Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982, which allowed banks and savings institutions to offer accounts competitive with the money market mutual funds that had grown rapidly in the late 1970s when market interest rates rose far above the Regulation Q ceilings on bank deposits. The accounts became a standard retail product during the 1980s.

Features

  • Interest: variable rates, often tiered by balance, usually higher than passbook savings but lower than certificates of deposit of comparable term.
  • Minimum balance: frequently $1,000 or more, with fees if the balance falls below the minimum.
  • Transactions: historically limited by Regulation D to six convenient withdrawals or transfers per month; the Federal Reserve suspended that limit in April 2020, though many banks retain restrictions.
  • Access: may include checks, a debit card, and online transfers, making the account a hybrid between a savings and a checking account.

Distinction from money market funds

Despite the similar name, a money market account is a bank deposit and is federally insured, whereas a money market fund is a mutual fund investing in short-term debt securities; it is not insured, although it seeks to maintain a stable value (historically $1 per share). Confusion between the two has been a recurring consumer-protection issue.
