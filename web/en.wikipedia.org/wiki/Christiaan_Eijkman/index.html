Christiaan Eijkman (11 August 1858 – 5 November 1930) was a Dutch physician and professor of physiology whose demonstration that beriberi is caused by poor diet led to the discovery of antineuritic vitamins (thiamine). Together with Sir Frederick Hopkins, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1929.

[Hero image: Christiaan Eijkman in his laboratory in Batavia — image placeholder]

Work on beriberi

Eijkman was sent to the Dutch East Indies in 1886 as part of a commission investigating beriberi, which was then widely believed to be infectious, and became director of the medical laboratory at Batavia (Jakarta). He noticed that chickens fed polished white rice developed polyneuritis resembling beriberi, while those fed unpolished brown rice remained healthy. He concluded that the rice bran contained a substance essential to health — or that polished rice lacked a vital protective factor — though he initially interpreted the result in terms of an antidote to a toxin. His colleague Gerrit Grijns developed the deficiency explanation further.

Later career

Eijkman returned to the Netherlands in 1898 and became professor of hygiene at Utrecht University. The nutrient he had postulated was isolated as thiamine (vitamin B1) in 1926 by Jansen and Donath, working in Eijkman's former laboratory.
