Bundle theory

An object as a mere bundle of properties

Bundle theory, in ontology, is the theory that an object consists of nothing more than its properties; there is no underlying "substance" or "substratum" in which the properties inhere. The term comes from the objection that, on the rival substratum or "bare particular" theory, objects are pictured as pin-cushions (substrata) into which pins (properties) are stuck, whereas the bundle theorist holds the object is simply the bundle of pins.

History

The view is associated with David Hume, who described the self as "a bundle or collection of different perceptions" and extended the analysis to physical objects. Similar ideas appear in early Buddhism, in the doctrine of no-self (anatta) and the analysis of a person into the five aggregates (skandhas). Bertrand Russell defended a form of bundle theory in his middle period, treating objects as bundles of compresent qualities.

The tying problem

The central problem for bundle theory is explaining what ties properties into a single object — why this red, this round and this heavy are one apple rather than a scattered heap. Replies include treating compresence itself as a primitive relation, or taking the bundle's members to be tropes (particularised properties) that are mutually dependent. Bundle theory also faces the problems of change (an object survives losing a property, though its bundle changes) and of the identity of indiscernibles: two objects with exactly the same properties would, on a bundle theory using universals, collapse into one.
