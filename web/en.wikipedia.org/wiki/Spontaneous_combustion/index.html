Spontaneous combustion or spontaneous ignition is a type of combustion that occurs by self-heating (an increase in temperature due to exothermic internal reactions), followed by thermal runaway and finally ignition — without the application of an external flame or spark. Materials that can ignite spontaneously are called pyrophoric in the extreme case, but ordinary spontaneous combustion most often involves bulk storage of organic materials such as hay, coal, compost, and oily rags.

Causes

Self-heating arises from several processes:

  • Biological heating: In damp hay, compost, and manure piles, microbial activity can raise the temperature to about 70 °C. If the pile is large and well insulated, chemical oxidation then takes over and can carry temperatures to ignition — a long-recognized cause of barn fires.
  • Oxidation: Drying oils such as linseed oil polymerize exothermically. Rags soaked with such oils, crumpled into a pile that traps the heat, can smoulder and ignite within hours — a notorious hazard in workshops and a standard warning in fire safety.
  • Coal and pyrite oxidation: Coal seams and coal stockpiles self-heat through oxidation, accelerated by oxidation of iron pyrite; underground coal fires can burn for decades or centuries.
  • Adsorption and other reactions: Freshly made charcoal and certain chemicals can heat on exposure to air or moisture.

The common thread is a race between heat generation, which increases steeply with temperature, and heat loss to the surroundings. Above a critical size and ambient temperature, generation wins and the material ignites.

Spontaneous human combustion

Spontaneous human combustion (SHC) is the alleged phenomenon of a human body bursting into flame without an identifiable external source. Reported cases share a characteristic pattern — the torso largely consumed, extremities often intact, and little fire damage to surroundings — which investigators generally explain by the wick effect: an external source (such as a cigarette) ignites clothing, and rendered body fat then fuels a slow, localized burn like the wick of a candle. No scientifically verified case of a body igniting without any external source is known. The idea entered popular culture through 19th-century literature, including a famous episode in Charles Dickens's Bleak House.

Prevention

Fire-safety practice addresses spontaneous combustion by curing hay before storage, monitoring the temperature of large piles, storing oily rags in sealed metal containers, ventilating coal stocks, and limiting pile sizes so heat can dissipate.
