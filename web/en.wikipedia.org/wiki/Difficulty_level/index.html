Difficulty level

In video games, a difficulty level is a setting that determines how challenging the game is to play, typically selected by the player before or during play. Common gradations run from "easy" through "normal" to "hard", with many games adding extremes such as "story mode" at the low end and "nightmare", "insane" or "permadeath" modes at the high end.

Implementation

Changing difficulty may alter many parameters at once: enemy health, damage and accuracy; the player's own health and resources; the number and placement of enemies; the generosity of checkpoints, ammunition and hints; and sometimes the behaviour of the artificial intelligence itself. Some games instead scale difficulty invisibly, a technique called dynamic difficulty adjustment or "rubber-banding", in which the game quietly helps struggling players or strengthens opponents who are winning too easily.

History

Early arcade games adjusted challenge within a single credit, growing harder as the player advanced, while home games of the 1980s began offering explicit choices. Series such as Doom (with its joking level names from "I'm too young to die" to "Nightmare!") helped popularise selectable difficulty as a standard menu feature. Later, games like the Souls series became known for a single, uncompromising difficulty, sparking recurring debate about accessibility options and whether difficulty settings are integral to a work's design.

Design considerations

Designers balance difficulty against the psychological state of "flow", in which challenge roughly matches player skill. Difficulty selection lets a single game serve casual and expert audiences, and completion on higher settings is often recognised with achievements, alternate endings or unlockable content. In educational software and adaptive testing, analogous graded difficulty is used to keep tasks within the learner's zone of proximal development.
