Seed accelerator

A seed accelerator is a fixed-term, cohort-based programme that helps early-stage startup companies grow in exchange for equity. Accelerators provide a small seed investment, mentorship, workspace, structured programming, and access to a network of investors, culminating in a "demo day" at which founders pitch to venture capitalists and angel investors.

Model and history

The modern accelerator model was pioneered by Y Combinator, founded by Paul Graham and partners in Cambridge, Massachusetts in 2005, followed by Techstars (2006) and hundreds of imitators worldwide. Programmes typically run about three months, accept batches of ten or more startups, invest a standard amount (often on a post-money SAFE or convertible note) for roughly 5–10% equity, and rely on intensive mentor feedback — "office hours", weekly dinners, and rehearsals — to compress a young company's learning curve.

Distinguishing features

Accelerators differ from incubators, which usually offer longer, open-ended tenancy and services without a fixed cohort or demo day, and from angel investors, who invest individually without a programme. Competition for places at top accelerators is fierce; acceptance signals quality to later investors and correlates with faster fundraising, though most accelerated companies, like most startups, ultimately fail.

Criticism

Critics note the proliferation of low-quality programmes, the equity cost to founders, and evidence that accelerator effects vary enormously by programme. Corporate and university accelerators, often taking no equity, have become common variants.
