Dual monarchy

Dual monarchy is a political arrangement in which two distinct states are governed by a single monarch, while each retains its own government, laws, and institutions. It is a form of personal union strengthened by common institutions for shared affairs — typically foreign policy, defence, and the finances needed for them — and differs from a federation (a single state) and from a mere personal union (where the ruler is shared but policies are not coordinated).

Austria-Hungary

The archetypal example is Austria-Hungary, created by the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 (the Ausgleich). The Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary became two constitutional states under one sovereign, Franz Joseph I, who was Emperor of Austria and (Apostolic) King of Hungary. Common ministers handled foreign affairs, war, and joint finance; each half had its own parliament, government, citizenship, and budget, and the economic compromise between them was renegotiated every ten years. The union lasted until its dissolution at the end of the First World War in 1918.

Other examples

  • The Kalmar Union and, more precisely, Denmark–Norway (1537–1814), where Norway was a separate kingdom under the Danish crown.
  • Sweden–Norway (1814–1905), a personal union with a shared monarch and foreign service but separate parliaments and armies.
  • The Iberian Union (1580–1640), joining Portugal and Spain under the Spanish Habsburgs while Portuguese institutions remained distinct.
  • The term is also applied to the ancient condominium of Sparta's two kings (a diarchy rather than a dual monarchy in the modern sense).
