Liu Zhi (scholar)

Nanjing, where Liu Zhi spent much of his life in study

Liu Zhi (Chinese: 劉智; pinyin: Liú Zhì; c. 1660 – c. 1730s), courtesy name Jielian, was a Chinese Muslim (Hui) scholar of the Qing dynasty. He is regarded as the most prominent of the Han Kitab authors — scholars who explained Islamic doctrine in classical Chinese, drawing heavily on Neo-Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist vocabulary to present Islam within a Chinese intellectual framework.

Life

Almost nothing certain is known of Liu Zhi's personal life. He was born in Nanjing around 1660 into a family that claimed descent from an earlier Muslim lineage. He studied Arabic and Persian and the Chinese classics, reportedly spending decades mastering the Confucian canon alongside the Qur'an and works of Sufism, before beginning to write around the turn of the eighteenth century. He declined official position and lived as a teacher and writer.

Works

Liu Zhi's major work is the Tianfang Xingli ("The Nature and Principle of Islam", c. 1704, published 1760 through the efforts of his followers), a systematic exposition of Islamic cosmology, metaphysics, and ethics heavily influenced by Sufi thought, particularly Ibn Arabi's school, recast in Neo-Confucian terminology such as li (principle) and qi. His other principal work, the Tianfang Dianli ("The Rites and Ceremonies of Islam", 1710), describes Islamic ritual law and the life of the Prophet Muhammad, again presented respectfully in Chinese scholarly form. He also wrote the Tianfang Zhisheng Shilu, a biography of Muhammad completed by his students from his drafts.

Significance

Liu Zhi's writings completed the project, begun by scholars such as Wang Daiyu and Ma Zhu, of creating a Chinese-language Islamic theology. His works were reprinted many times, studied in mosque schools across China, and remain the most sophisticated expression of the Han Kitab tradition, which mediated between Islam and Confucian orthodoxy for Chinese-speaking Muslims.
