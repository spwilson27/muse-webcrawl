Cantonese cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bamboo steamers of dim sum being opened at a yum cha table]

Cantonese cuisine — the cooking of Guangdong province, Guangzhou above all — is one of China's Four Great Traditions and the Chinese cuisine the world met first, carried by emigration from the Pearl River Delta to Chinatowns everywhere. Its hallmarks are freshness, delicacy and restraint: clear flavours, minimal masking spice, and techniques that showcase the ingredient — steaming, quick stir-frying over fierce heat for wok hei, white-cut poaching, slow double-boiled soups. Its two great public rituals are dim sum at morning yum cha and the roast-meat shop (siu mei) with its lacquered char siu and crackling-skinned pork.

Dim sum and yum cha

Dim sum — "touch the heart" — grew from Silk Road tea houses into Guangzhou's grand tea restaurants: dozens of small dishes in bamboo steamers, har gow (crystal shrimp dumplings) and siu mai as the twin tests of a kitchen, chosen from trolleys or menus while tea is poured, tapped in thanks (a gesture traced, as legend has it, to the Qianlong emperor). In Hong Kong the ritual sharpened into an art and a daily habit, exported wherever Cantonese people settled.

Emigration and adaptation

"To eat in Guangzhou" is the Chinese food lover's proverb, and Guangzhou's cooks took the saying global. In 19th- and 20th-century America, Cantonese kitchens invented chop suey and much of the Chinese-American canon to local tastes; in Britain, Cantonese restaurants defined Chinese food for generations. Hong Kong added its own layers — dai pai dong stalls and cha chaan teng cafés marrying Cantonese technique to colonial ingredients (baked pork-chop rice, French toast, milk tea). Through it all the core stayed: live seafood in tanks, greens blanched at the second, and the conviction that a fresh ingredient needs little done to it.
