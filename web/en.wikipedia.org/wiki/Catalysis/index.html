Catalysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catalysis is the process of increasing the rate of a chemical reaction with a substance — a catalyst — that participates in the reaction but is not consumed by it. Catalysts work by providing an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so more molecular collisions succeed.

Types

In homogeneous catalysis the catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (often a dissolved metal complex). In heterogeneous catalysis the catalyst is a solid surface on which gases or liquids react — the basis of the Haber–Bosch process for ammonia (iron catalyst), catalytic cracking in oil refining, and the catalytic converters that clean car exhausts (platinum, palladium, rhodium). Enzymes are nature's catalysts: protein (and occasionally RNA — see ribozyme) molecules that accelerate the reactions of metabolism with extraordinary specificity.

Importance

Most industrial chemical production involves catalysis at some step; catalysts are crucial to fertilisers, fuels, plastics and pharmaceuticals, and to green chemistry goals of lower energy use and less waste. The 2001, 2005 and 2010 Nobel Prizes in Chemistry honoured advances in catalytic synthesis and cross-coupling.
