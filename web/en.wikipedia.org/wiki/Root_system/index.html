Root system

In mathematics, a root system is a configuration of vectors in a Euclidean space satisfying certain geometrical properties. The concept is fundamental in the theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras, especially the classification and representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras. Since Lie groups (and some analogues such as algebraic groups) became important in many branches of mathematics during the twentieth century, the apparently special nature of root systems belies the number of areas in which they are applied. Further, the classification scheme for root systems, by Dynkin diagrams, occurs in parts of mathematics with no overt connection to Lie theory, such as singularity theory. Finally, root systems are important for their own sake, as in spectral graph theory and the study of reflection groups.

The root system A2, consisting of six vectors in a two-dimensional subspace.

Definitions

Let V be a finite-dimensional Euclidean vector space with the standard Euclidean inner product denoted by (·, ·). A root system in V is a finite set Φ of non-zero vectors (called roots) such that:

  • The roots span V.
  • The only scalar multiples of a root α ∈ Φ that belong to Φ are α itself and −α.
  • For every root α ∈ Φ, the set Φ is closed under reflection through the hyperplane perpendicular to α; that is, for all α, β ∈ Φ, the vector σα(β) = β − 2(α, β)/(α, α) · α belongs to Φ.
  • (Integrality condition) If α and β are roots in Φ, then the projection of β onto the line through α is a half-integer multiple of α; equivalently, 2(α, β)/(α, α) is an integer.

The rank of the root system is the dimension of V. Two root systems are combined if they arise as the union of the root systems in two orthogonal subspaces.

Examples

In a one-dimensional space the only root system (up to scaling) consists of a pair of opposite vectors {α, −α}, denoted A1. In two dimensions there are four root systems, conventionally denoted A2, B2, G2 and A1 × A1, with 6, 8, 12 and 4 roots respectively. The root system G2, for instance, consists of 12 vectors forming the vertices of a hexagram-like configuration with roots of two different lengths.

Classification

Irreducible root systems — those that cannot be partitioned into orthogonal subsets — are classified by their Dynkin diagrams. There are four infinite families, the classical root systems An, Bn, Cn and Dn, associated with the classical Lie groups, and five exceptional cases: E6, E7, E8, F4 and G2. This classification mirrors exactly the classification of finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over the complex numbers, each simple Lie algebra having an associated root system unique up to isomorphism.

Simple roots and the Weyl group

A subset Δ of Φ is a set of simple roots if it is a basis of V consisting of roots such that every root in Φ can be written as a linear combination of elements of Δ with coefficients all non-negative or all non-positive. The reflections in the hyperplanes perpendicular to the roots generate a finite reflection group called the Weyl group of the root system, a central object in the representation theory of Lie algebras.

Closely related notions include weight lattices, coroots, and the Cartan matrix, whose entries encode the integers 2(α, β)/(β, β) for pairs of simple roots and which determines the root system up to isomorphism.
