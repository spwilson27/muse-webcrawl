Spirituals

Spirituals are religious folk songs created by enslaved African people in the United States. Rooted in African musical traditions, Protestant hymnody, and the experience of slavery, they expressed Christian faith, hope of deliverance, and commentary — sometimes coded — on the singers' condition. Famous examples include "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot", "Go Down Moses", "Steal Away", "Wade in the Water", and "Deep River".

Origins

Spirituals emerged during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in the ring shouts, prayer meetings, and camp meetings of enslaved communities, particularly in the American South. They were sung a cappella in call-and-response form, blending African rhythms, scales, and performance practices with the hymns and psalmody of white evangelical worship. Biblical imagery — above all the Exodus story of the Israelites' escape from bondage in Egypt — carried a double meaning: spiritual salvation and earthly freedom. Some songs are traditionally said to have served as signals or guides for fugitives on the Underground Railroad, though historians debate the extent of such coded use.

Publication and concert tradition

The first major collection, Slave Songs of the United States (1867), was compiled by Northern abolitionists who had worked among freedpeople in the Sea Islands. In 1871 the Fisk Jubilee Singers, students from Fisk University in Nashville, began touring to raise money for their school, presenting arranged spirituals as concert music to acclaim in the United States and Europe, including before Queen Victoria. Collegiate ensembles from Hampton and Tuskegee followed, and composers such as Harry T. Burleigh later arranged spirituals as art songs, influencing figures like Antonín Dvořák, whose "New World" Symphony drew on their melodic character.

Legacy

Spirituals stand at the head of the African American musical tradition, feeding into gospel music, the blues, jazz, and ultimately much of American popular music. W. E. B. Du Bois, in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), called them "sorrow songs" and the most beautiful expression of human experience to come from American soil.
