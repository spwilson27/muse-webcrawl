Ballast water discharge

SourceShips' ballast tanks
Main concernIntroduction of invasive aquatic species
RegulationIMO Ballast Water Management Convention (2004)

Ballast water discharge is the release of water carried in a ship's ballast tanks, taken up in one port or marine region and discharged in another. Ballast water is essential for the stability and trim of vessels travelling without a full cargo, but its discharge is a major pathway for the global spread of invasive aquatic species, as well as pathogens such as the bacteria that cause cholera.

Environmental impact

A single ballast tank can contain thousands of aquatic organisms — plankton, larvae, algae, and small invertebrates — which may survive the voyage and establish populations where they have no natural predators. Notorious ballast-mediated invasions include the zebra mussel and round goby in the North American Great Lakes, the comb jelly Mnemiopsis leidyi in the Black and Caspian seas, and toxic dinoflagellates in Australian waters. The economic costs of such invasions, to fisheries, aquaculture, and infrastructure, run to billions of dollars.

Regulation

The International Maritime Organization adopted the Ballast Water Management Convention in 2004; it entered into force in September 2017. The convention requires ships to exchange ballast water in the open ocean (the D-1 standard) and, ultimately, to install treatment systems meeting a discharge performance standard (the D-2 standard) limiting the number of viable organisms per unit volume. Treatment technologies include filtration, ultraviolet irradiation, chlorination, and deoxygenation. Some states, notably the United States, impose parallel requirements through national law.
