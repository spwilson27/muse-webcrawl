Stakeholder capitalism

Stakeholder capitalism is a model of corporate governance in which a company is oriented toward serving the interests of all its stakeholders — customers, employees, suppliers, investors, communities and the environment — rather than shareholders alone. The term is associated with Klaus Schwab, founder of the World Economic Forum, who first set it out in 1971 and restated it in the Davos Manifesto of 2020, and with R. Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory of 1984.

Background

The model contrasts with the shareholder primacy associated with Milton Friedman's 1970 essay arguing that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. Stakeholder theorists argue that a firm's long-term success depends on maintaining the trust and support of the groups affected by its operations, and that value creation should be measured against broader outcomes, often framed as environmental, social and governance (ESG) criteria.

Developments

In August 2019 the Business Roundtable, representing nearly 200 major US corporations, issued a statement redefining the purpose of a corporation to promote "an economy that serves all Americans", endorsing commitments to customers, employees, suppliers and communities alongside shareholders. Legal vehicles such as the benefit corporation (B Corp) embed stakeholder duties in company charters.

Criticism

Critics from the right argue that stakeholder capitalism dilutes managerial accountability — a manager answerable to everyone is answerable to no one — and invites corporate involvement in politics. Critics from the left dismiss much of it as public relations ("greenwashing") that changes little in practice. Empirical studies of its effects on firm value remain contested.
