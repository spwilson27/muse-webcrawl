Stamp Act 1765

The Stamp Act 1765 (short title: the Duties in American Colonies Act 1765) was an act of the Parliament of Great Britain that imposed a direct tax on the British colonies in America. It required that many printed materials in the colonies — legal documents, licences, newspapers, pamphlets, playing cards and almanacs — be produced on stamped paper purchased from government agents and bearing an embossed revenue stamp.

Background

Britain emerged from the Seven Years' War with a greatly enlarged national debt and the cost of maintaining troops in North America. Prime Minister George Grenville's ministry sought revenue from the colonies, which had long been taxed by their own assemblies but rarely by Parliament. The Sugar Act of 1764 had already tightened customs enforcement; the Stamp Act, passed in March 1765 and effective from 1 November 1765, was the first significant internal tax levied directly on the colonists.

Colonial resistance

The act provoked widespread resistance. Colonial assemblies passed resolutions asserting that only they could tax their constituents, popularising the slogan "no taxation without representation." In October 1765, delegates from nine colonies met in the Stamp Act Congress in New York and petitioned for repeal. Crowds intimidated stamp distributors into resigning, stamped paper was destroyed or locked away, and merchants organised non-importation agreements boycotting British goods. Violence included the destruction of the home of Massachusetts lieutenant governor Thomas Hutchinson.

Repeal

Hurt by the boycott and pressure from London merchants, Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766 — but simultaneously passed the Declaratory Act, asserting its authority to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The cycle of taxation and resistance continued with the Townshend Acts and contributed directly to the outbreak of the American Revolutionary War.
