The killing of Cecil the lion in July 2015 provoked an international outcry against trophy hunting. Cecil was a male African lion who lived primarily in Hwange National Park in Zimbabwe and was a major attraction for tourists and the subject of an Oxford University research project. On the night of 1–2 July 2015 he was lured from the park, wounded with an arrow by American dentist and recreational hunter Walter Palmer, tracked for many hours, and finally killed with a second shot; his head and skin were taken as trophies.

The lion and the hunt

Cecil, recognisable by his black-fringed mane and a GPS collar fitted by the Wildlife Conservation Research Unit (WildCRU) of Oxford University, had been studied since 2008 and led two prides in Hwange. Palmer had paid about US$50,000 for a hunting package arranged by professional hunter Theo Bronkhorst on farmland adjoining the park, where lion hunting was not authorised for that area; the carcass of an elephant was reportedly used as bait to draw Cecil out of the protected area. After the kill, the collar was allegedly tampered with and the carcass skinned and beheaded.

Reaction

When the story broke internationally at the end of July 2015, it went viral: Palmer's dental practice in Minnesota was besieged by protesters and closed temporarily; he received death threats and went into hiding. Conservation groups, politicians and celebrities condemned the killing, while Zimbabwean opinion was more mixed, some noting that a lion's death drew more Western attention than the hardships of Zimbabwean people. Late-night host Jimmy Kimmel's emotional appeal raised well over $100,000 for WildCRU in a day. The affair also prompted airlines to ban carriage of hunting trophies and renewed legislative efforts in the United States and Europe to restrict trophy imports.

Legal outcome

Bronkhorst was charged with failing to prevent an illegal hunt, but the case against him was eventually dropped by Zimbabwean courts in 2016; the landowner's charges were also dismissed. Zimbabwe declined to seek Palmer's extradition, stating his papers were in order. In Hwange, Cecil's brother Jericho assumed protection of the pride's cubs. The episode significantly shifted public debate on trophy hunting, and researchers later noted both a "Cecil effect" surge in conservation donations and increased scrutiny of the scientific case for regulated hunting.
