Counter-Reformation

The Counter-Reformation, also called the Catholic Reformation or the Catholic Revival, was the period of Catholic resurgence that was initiated in response to the Protestant Reformation, beginning with the Council of Trent (1545–1563) and ending with the conclusion of the European wars of religion in 1648.

During this period, the Catholic Church undertook comprehensive reforms: clarifying doctrine, founding seminaries to train priests, disciplining abuses such as the sale of indulgences, and launching new religious orders — above all the Jesuits (Society of Jesus, founded 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola) — devoted to education, missions and the defence of Catholicism.

Council of Trent

The Council of Trent, meeting in 25 sessions between 1545 and 1563, issued decrees reaffirming transubstantiation, the seven sacraments, the authority of tradition alongside scripture, and justification by faith combined with works, while reforming clerical discipline: bishops were required to reside in their dioceses, and each diocese was to establish a seminary. Its decrees shaped Catholic doctrine for four centuries, until the Second Vatican Council.

Institutions and instruments

The revived Roman Inquisition (1542), the Index of Forbidden Books (1559), and the Roman Catechism (1566) policed orthodoxy. Catholic powers — Spain under Philip II, the Habsburg emperors, and later France — opposed Protestant expansion, producing conflicts including the French Wars of Religion and the Thirty Years' War. Missionaries such as Francis Xavier carried Catholicism to Asia and the Americas alongside European colonisation.

Art and culture

The Counter-Reformation profoundly shaped Baroque art and architecture, favouring emotional, dramatic and accessible religious imagery to inspire devotion — seen in Bernini's sculptures, the church of Il Gesù in Rome, and the paintings of Caravaggio and Rubens.
