Friedrich August von Hayek (8 May 1899 – 23 March 1992) was an Austrian-British economist and philosopher, a leading figure of the Austrian school and of 20th-century classical liberalism. He shared the 1974 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for work on money, fluctuations and the interdependence of economic, social and institutional phenomena.

Ideas and career

After serving in the First World War, Hayek studied at Vienna, directed the Austrian Institute for Business Cycle Research, and moved to the London School of Economics in 1931, where his debate with John Maynard Keynes defined inter-war macroeconomics. In The Road to Serfdom (1944) he warned that central economic planning tends toward authoritarianism — a political bestseller that influenced generations of conservatives. His 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society" argued that the knowledge needed to run an economy is dispersed and local, and that prices are a signalling system no planner can replicate — the core of the socialist calculation debate against Lange and others.

Hayek later taught at Chicago and Freiburg, wrote The Constitution of Liberty (1960) and Law, Legislation and Liberty, and in 1947 founded the Mont Pelerin Society. His ideas strongly influenced Margaret Thatcher and the market-oriented turn of the 1980s.
