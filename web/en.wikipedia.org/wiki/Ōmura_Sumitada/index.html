Ōmura Sumitada

Ōmura Sumitada (大村 純忠, 1533 – June 23, 1587) was a Japanese daimyō lord of the Sengoku period. He achieved historical renown as the first Japanese lord to convert to Christianity, taking the baptismal name Dom Bartolomeu. He is remembered for opening the port of Nagasaki to Portuguese trade in 1571, a decision that shaped the city's development as Japan's principal gateway to the outside world.

Early life

Sumitada was born in 1533, the second son of Arima Haruzumi, lord of Shimabara. He was adopted into the Ōmura clan, a family of daimyō in Hizen Province (present-day Nagasaki Prefecture), and succeeded as head of the clan, ruling from his seat connected with the port of Yokoseura.

Contact with the Portuguese

Seeking to strengthen his domain's trade and his position against rival lords, Sumitada welcomed Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries to his lands. In 1562 he opened the port of Yokoseura to Portuguese shipping. The following year, in 1563, he was baptised by the Jesuit missionary Luís de Almeida, becoming the first Japanese daimyō to embrace Christianity. Many of his retainers and subjects followed him into the new faith, and Buddhist temples and Shinto shrines in his domain were destroyed in episodes of religious zeal.

Opening of Nagasaki

After Yokoseura was burned during conflicts with rival clans, Sumitada sought a new harbour for the Portuguese. In 1571 he granted them the use of Nagasaki, then a small fishing village, which rapidly grew into a flourishing international port. In 1580, facing pressure from the powerful Ryūzōji clan, Sumitada ceded Nagasaki and Mogi to the Society of Jesus, an extraordinary arrangement under which the Jesuits administered the town. Following Toyotomi Hideyoshi's unification campaigns, Nagasaki was taken under direct central control in 1587, shortly before Sumitada's death.

Death and legacy

Sumitada died on June 23, 1587. His decision to welcome the Portuguese and to convert to Christianity left a lasting mark on the history of Nagasaki, which became the centre of Japan's trade with Europe and of the Japanese Christian (Kirishitan) community until the faith was suppressed in the following century.
