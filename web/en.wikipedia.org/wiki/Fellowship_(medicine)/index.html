Fellowship (medicine)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fellowship in medicine is a period of subspecialty training that a physician undertakes after completing a residency programme. Physicians in fellowship are called fellows. During fellowship, the physician practises under the supervision of fully qualified specialists ("attendings") while gaining advanced expertise in a narrower field, such as interventional cardiology within cardiology, or hand surgery within orthopaedic surgery.

Training pathway

In the United States, the usual pathway is: undergraduate degree, four years of medical school, residency (typically three to seven years depending on specialty), then fellowship (typically one to three years). Completion of an accredited fellowship, often followed by a subspecialty board examination, allows certification in the subspecialty. Fellowships are accredited, in the US, by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), and many positions are filled through the National Resident Matching Program's Specialties Matching Service.

Types of fellowship

  • Clinical fellowships — focused on direct patient care in a subspecialty (e.g. gastroenterology, nephrology, critical care).
  • Surgical fellowships — advanced operative training (e.g. surgical oncology, transplant surgery, minimally invasive surgery).
  • Research fellowships — devoted primarily to laboratory or clinical research, sometimes combined with clinical duties.
  • Combined and non-accredited fellowships — institution-specific programmes that may not lead to board certification but provide focused skills, such as global health or medical education.

Role and status

Fellows hold a medical licence and have completed residency, so they carry substantial clinical responsibility, but they remain trainees: their work is supervised, their salary is generally that of a postgraduate trainee rather than an attending, and the period is undertaken to qualify for subspecialty practice and academic posts.
