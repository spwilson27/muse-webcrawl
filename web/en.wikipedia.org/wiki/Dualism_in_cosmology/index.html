Dualism in cosmology

Dualism in cosmology or dualistic cosmology is the moral or spiritual belief that two fundamental concepts exist, which often oppose each other. It is an umbrella term that covers a diversity of views from various religions, including both traditional religions and scriptural religions.

Overview

Moral dualism is the belief of the great complement or conflict between the benevolent and the malevolent. It simply implies that there are two moral opposites at work, independent of any interpretation of what might be "moral" and independent of how these may be represented. The moral opposites might, for example, exist in a world view which has one god, more than one god, or none.

In religions

In Zoroastrianism, the world is shaped by the struggle between Ahura Mazda, the wise lord of truth (asha), and Angra Mainyu, the destructive spirit of falsehood (druj). Manichaeism, founded by the prophet Mani in the 3rd century CE, taught an elaborate cosmology of a Kingdom of Light and a Kingdom of Darkness whose mingling produced the material world. Gnostic traditions likewise contrasted a transcendent God with a flawed creator, the demiurge. In Chinese thought, yin and yang describe complementary rather than warring opposites whose balance sustains the cosmos. In Christianity, Satan is generally subordinate to God rather than an equal rival, so theologians debate whether it qualifies as true dualism.

Ontological dualism

Related but distinct is ontological dualism, the view that the cosmos consists of two kinds of substance or realm — for example spirit and matter, or the sacred and the profane. This contrasts with monism, which holds that all reality is fundamentally one.
