Immune tolerance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: immune tolerance diagram]

Immune tolerance is the state in which the immune system does not mount a destructive response against a particular antigen — most importantly, against the body's own tissues ("self"). Failure of tolerance leads to autoimmune disease; tolerance is also the goal in organ transplantation and allergy treatment.

Central tolerance

Central tolerance is established as lymphocytes develop. In the thymus, developing T cells that react strongly to self-antigens presented by medullary epithelial cells (expressing many tissue antigens under the transcription factor AIRE) are deleted by apoptosis — negative selection. B cells in the bone marrow similarly undergo deletion, receptor editing or anergy when they bind self-antigen strongly.

Peripheral tolerance

Self-reactive cells that escape to the periphery are controlled by several mechanisms: anergy (functional inactivation without co-stimulation), deletion, suppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs, dependent on the transcription factor FOXP3), and immunological ignorance of antigens in immune-privileged sites such as the eye and testes.

Acquired tolerance

Tolerance can be induced: Ray Owen's observations in cattle twins and Peter Medawar's experiments on skin grafts in the 1940s–50s showed that exposure to foreign antigen in fetal or neonatal life could produce lifelong tolerance — work that earned Medawar a Nobel Prize and underpins transplantation immunology. Oral tolerance to food antigens and tolerance in pregnancy are natural examples.
