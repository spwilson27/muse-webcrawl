High Priest of Israel

The High Priest of Israel (Hebrew: כֹּהֵן גָּדוֹל, Kohen Gadol, "Great Priest") was the head of the Israelite priesthood, presiding over the cult at the Tabernacle and later the Temple in Jerusalem. According to the Hebrew Bible the office was hereditary in the line of Aaron, brother of Moses, the first high priest.

Duties and vestments

The high priest alone could enter the Holy of Holies, and only once a year on Yom Kippur (the Day of Atonement), to offer sacrifices for the sins of the whole nation. His consecration involved anointing with holy oil, and he wore special garments: the ephod, the breastplate (hoshen) set with twelve gemstones representing the Twelve Tribes and containing the Urim and Thummim, a blue robe with golden bells and pomegranates, and a golden plate on his turban inscribed "Holiness to the LORD". Special purity laws applied to him, including marriage restrictions.

History

During the Second Temple period the high priesthood became a powerful political as well as religious office. Under foreign domination (Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman), appointment to the office was often controlled by the ruling power; the Hasmoneans combined the high priesthood with kingship. The office effectively ended with the Roman destruction of the Temple in AD 70. In rabbinic Judaism many of its functions were replaced by prayer and synagogue worship, though priestly descendants (kohanim) retain certain roles.
