Paulet Affair (1843)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Paulet Affair was a five-month occupation of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1843 by British naval officer Captain Lord George Paulet of HMS Carysfort. Acting on the complaints of the British consul Richard Charlton, Paulet seized control of the islands on 25 February 1843 and forced King Kamehameha III to cede them provisionally to Britain. The occupation ended on 31 July 1843 when Rear Admiral Richard Darton Thomas, Paulet's commanding officer, arrived, repudiated the seizure and restored Hawaiian sovereignty — an event still commemorated in Hawaii as Hawaiian Restoration Day (Lā Hoʻihoʻi Ea).

Background

In the early 1840s the Hawaiian Kingdom was asserting its independence while British, French and American traders disputed land claims and legal privileges in Honolulu. Consul Charlton pressed a contested land claim and complained of discrimination against British subjects. When his demands were refused, Charlton sailed to Mexico to fetch Paulet, whose frigate arrived in Honolulu in February 1843 with a list of ultimatums and a threat of attack.

The occupation

Under protest and to avoid bloodshed, Kamehameha III signed a provisional cession on 25 February 1843, expressly subject to the decision of the British government. Paulet's administration raised the Union Jack, dissolved the Hawaiian government, imposed British law, destroyed Hawaiian flags and even renamed streets with English names. The king's protest was carried to London and Washington by emissaries; the United States and France were meanwhile moving to recognise Hawaiian independence, which Britain and France jointly did on 28 November 1843.

Restoration

Admiral Thomas, commander of the Pacific Station, reached Honolulu on 26 July 1843, declared Paulet's acts unauthorised, and on 31 July formally restored sovereignty to Kamehameha III in a ceremony on what is now Thomas Square in Honolulu — one of the oldest parks in Hawaii. At a thanksgiving service the king uttered the phrase Ua Mau ke Ea o ka ʻĀina i ka Pono ("The sovereignty of the land is perpetuated in righteousness"), which became the motto of Hawaii. Paulet was recalled and criticised, though not severely punished; his health broke and he resigned from the navy soon after.

Legacy

The affair confirmed the importance of international recognition of Hawaiian independence and is remembered each 31 July as Restoration Day, one of the national holidays of the old kingdom. Thomas Square, named for the admiral, remains a symbolic site in modern Hawaiian sovereignty movements.
