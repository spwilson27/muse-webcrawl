Hacker ethic

The hacker ethic is a philosophy and set of moral values that emerged within the early hacker culture centred on the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory and similar communities in the 1960s and 1970s. It was articulated most famously by Steven Levy in his 1984 book Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution, and is closely associated with the development of free and open-source software, information freedom, and decentralised collaboration.

The hacker ethic grew out of the hands-on computing culture at MIT and other early labs.

Levy's principles

Levy summarised the hacker ethic in a set of tenets, including:

  • Access to computers — and anything which might teach you something about the way the world works — should be unlimited and total. Always yield to the Hands-On Imperative!
  • All information should be free.
  • Mistrust authority — promote decentralization.
  • Hackers should be judged by their hacking, not bogus criteria such as degrees, age, race, or position.
  • You can create art and beauty on a computer.
  • Computers can change your life for the better.

Interpretation

In this tradition, "hacker" denotes an expert or enthusiast who delights in playful cleverness and elegant solutions, rather than a criminal intruder — a sense the community distinguishes from "cracker". The ethic holds that information-sharing is a powerful positive good, and that it is an ethical duty of hackers to share their expertise by writing open-source code and facilitating access to information and computing resources wherever possible. The Finnish philosopher Pekka Himanen explored the ethic as a wider work philosophy in The Hacker Ethic and the Spirit of the Information Age (2001), contrasting it with the Protestant work ethic.

Influence

The hacker ethic underlies the free software movement founded by Richard Stallman, the open-source software movement, and collaborative projects such as Wikipedia itself. Its principles of decentralisation and meritocracy have also influenced internet culture, maker culture, and debates about net neutrality and digital rights, though critics note tensions between the ideal of total information freedom and privacy, security, and intellectual property.
