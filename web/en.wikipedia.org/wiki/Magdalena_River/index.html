Magdalena River

The Magdalena River (Spanish: Río Magdalena) is the principal river of Colombia. Flowing northward about 1,528 kilometres through the western half of the country, between the Cordillera Oriental and Cordillera Central of the Andes, it empties into the Caribbean Sea at Barranquilla. Its drainage basin covers roughly 257,000 square kilometres, about a quarter of Colombia's territory.

History

Named by Spanish explorers for Saint Mary Magdalene after its discovery in 1501, the river was the main route into the interior of the New Kingdom of Granada throughout the colonial period. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, steamboats made it the principal artery of Colombian commerce, linking the Caribbean coast with the river port of Honda and, by rail and road, with Bogotá.

Ecology

The river and its floodplain wetlands (ciénagas) support rich fisheries and diverse wildlife, but face severe pressures from deforestation, sedimentation, pollution, and the introduced hippopotamus population descended from animals kept at Pablo Escobar's Hacienda Nápoles. The Magdalena is celebrated in Colombian culture, notably in Gabriel García Márquez's novels Love in the Time of Cholera and The General in His Labyrinth.
