In medicine, a pulse represents the tactile arterial palpation of the cardiac cycle — the rhythmic expansion and recoil of an artery as each heartbeat forces blood through it. Because the pulse rate usually equals the heart rate, measuring the pulse is one of the most basic steps of the physical examination and forms one of the vital signs, alongside temperature, respiratory rate and blood pressure.

Physiology

Each contraction of the left ventricle ejects a stroke volume of blood into the aorta, generating a pressure wave that travels through the arterial tree much faster than the blood itself flows. Where an artery lies close to the surface and can be compressed against bone — such as the radial artery at the wrist or the carotid artery in the neck — the wave can be felt as a pulse. The strength of the pulse reflects pulse pressure and stroke volume; its rhythm reveals the regularity of the cardiac cycle.

Measurement

The pulse is most commonly taken at the wrist (radial pulse) or neck (carotid pulse) by counting beats for 15, 30 or 60 seconds. Other palpable sites include the brachial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries, whose examination is important in assessing peripheral circulation. Normal resting heart rate in adults is roughly 60–100 beats per minute; trained athletes may be lower, and children have faster rates. Devices such as pulse oximeters and ECG monitors record the pulse automatically, and photoplethysmography in smartphones and smartwatches uses light to detect the pulsatile change in blood volume.

Clinical significance

An abnormal pulse may indicate disease: a rapid pulse (tachycardia) can accompany fever, blood loss, hyperthyroidism or anxiety, while a slow pulse (bradycardia) may be physiological in athletes or pathological in heart block. An irregular pulse suggests an arrhythmia such as atrial fibrillation. Characteristic pulse forms — the collapsing "water-hammer" pulse of aortic regurgitation, the slow-rising pulse of aortic stenosis, or a weak/absent pulse in shock — provide diagnostic clues. In first aid, absent carotid pulse in an unresponsive person is a sign to begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

History

Pulse diagnosis has ancient roots: Egyptian, Chinese and Indian physicians all described pulse lore, and in traditional Chinese medicine detailed palpation of the radial pulse at several positions remains a central diagnostic art. Galen wrote extensively on the pulse, and the quantitative era began with early pulse watches and, later, the sphygmograph of the 19th century.
