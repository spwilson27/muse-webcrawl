Group fitness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Group fitness is a concept in evolutionary biology referring to the fitness — the expected reproductive success — of a group of organisms as a collective unit, rather than (or in addition to) the fitness of its individual members. It is central to theories of group selection, which propose that natural selection can act simultaneously at multiple levels: genes, individuals, and groups.

Background

The idea that traits might evolve "for the good of the group" was common in early Darwinian writing, and Charles Darwin himself invoked group-level benefits when discussing human morality in The Descent of Man. In 1962, V. C. Wynne-Edwards argued in Animal Dispersion that animals restrain their reproduction for the benefit of the population. This view was sharply criticised in the 1960s by George C. Williams and others, who showed that most supposed group adaptations could be explained by individual-level selection, and group selection fell out of favour.

Multilevel selection

Interest revived with the development of multilevel selection theory, formalised with the Price equation, which partitions evolutionary change into within-group and between-group components. A trait harmful to its bearer — such as altruism — can spread if groups containing more altruists outproduce other groups strongly enough to offset the altruists' disadvantage within their own group. David Sloan Wilson and Elliott Sober defended this framework in Unto Others (1998).

Relationship to inclusive fitness

Whether group fitness frameworks and kin selection (inclusive fitness) theory are equivalent descriptions or genuinely rival approaches remains a subject of debate. In many models the two are mathematically interchangeable, but practitioners argue they suggest different causal stories and different empirical measurements. Group fitness is measured empirically as the per-capita or total reproductive output of a group, and is used in studies of eusocial insects, microbial communities, and cultural group selection in humans.
