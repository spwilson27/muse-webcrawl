Polypeptide

A polypeptide is a long, continuous chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Proteins consist of one or more polypeptides folded into a biologically functional shape; the term "polypeptide" refers to the chain itself regardless of folding. By convention, short chains are called peptides or oligopeptides, while longer chains — roughly more than 50 amino acids — are generally termed polypeptides or proteins.

A polypeptide chain folds into a three-dimensional protein structure.

Peptide bonds

Polypeptides are formed by condensation reactions in which the carboxyl group of one amino acid joins the amino group of the next, releasing a water molecule. The resulting peptide bond has partial double-bond character and is planar, constraining the chain's geometry. In cells, polypeptides are assembled by ribosomes translating messenger RNA, growing from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.

Relation to proteins

Many functional proteins are single polypeptides (such as myoglobin), while others are complexes of several polypeptide subunits (such as haemoglobin, with four). After synthesis, polypeptides may be modified — by phosphorylation, glycosylation, cleavage, or disulfide-bond formation — before or as they fold into their mature form. Insulin, for example, is synthesised as a single proinsulin chain and then cut into two chains held together by disulfide bonds.

Synthesis in the laboratory

Short polypeptides can be made chemically by solid-phase peptide synthesis, developed by Robert Bruce Merrifield, enabling production of peptide hormones and research reagents.
