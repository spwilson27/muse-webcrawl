Revised Julian calendar

The Revised Julian calendar, also known as the Milanković calendar or the New calendar, is a calendar proposed in 1923 by the Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković. It is more accurate than the Gregorian calendar in aligning the calendar year with the tropical year, and it was adopted by several Eastern Orthodox churches for the calculation of fixed feasts, while the date of Easter in most of those churches continues to be computed by the Julian calendar.

Leap year rule

Like the Julian and Gregorian calendars, the Revised Julian calendar has years of 365 days divided into 12 months, with a leap day added to February in leap years. Century years are leap years only if they leave a remainder of 200 or 600 when divided by 900. The years 2000 and 2400 are therefore leap years, as in the Gregorian calendar, but 2900 (for example) is a leap year in the Revised Julian calendar and not in the Gregorian, while 2800 is a leap year in the Gregorian calendar and not in the Revised Julian.

Because of this rule the average year is 365.242222 days — closer to the mean tropical year than the Gregorian average of 365.2425 days — and the two calendars will not differ until the year 2800.

Adoption

The calendar was proposed at a synod of Orthodox churches in Constantinople in May 1923, originally as part of a reform that would also have changed the calculation of Easter; the Easter provision was abandoned. Churches that adopted the Revised Julian calendar for fixed dates include the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople, the Patriarchates of Alexandria, Antioch and Romania, and the Churches of Greece, Cyprus, Poland, Albania, Bulgaria and others. The Russian Orthodox Church, the Serbian Orthodox Church, the Georgian Orthodox Church, the Patriarchate of Jerusalem and the Church of Finland's Eastern counterpart positions vary — several churches retained the Julian calendar for all purposes, and some, such as the Orthodox Church of Finland, use the Gregorian calendar entirely.

Parishes and churches using the Revised Julian calendar celebrate Christmas on 25 December (Gregorian-style), while Julian-calendar churches celebrate it on 7 January of the Gregorian calendar in the 20th and 21st centuries.
