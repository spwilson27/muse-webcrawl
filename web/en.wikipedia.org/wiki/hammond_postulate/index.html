Hammond's postulate

Hammond's postulate (or alternatively the Hammond–Leffler postulate), first proposed by George Hammond in 1955, states that if two states, for example a transition state and an intermediate, occur consecutively during a reaction process and have nearly the same energy content, their interconversion will involve only a small reorganization of molecular structure.

Interpretation

In an exothermic step, the transition state lies early along the reaction coordinate and resembles the reactants; in an endothermic step, the transition state lies late and resembles the products. The postulate therefore lets chemists infer the structure of an unobservable transition state from the stable species it most resembles.

Applications

The postulate is widely used to rationalise selectivity: for example, the lower selectivity of highly reactive species such as free radicals in hydrogen abstraction follows because their early transition states resemble the starting materials rather than the product radicals.

Relation to other principles

Hammond's postulate is related to the Bell–Evans–Polanyi principle and to Leffler's treatment of transition-state structure, and is sometimes combined with the Curtin–Hammett principle in analyses of reaction outcomes.
