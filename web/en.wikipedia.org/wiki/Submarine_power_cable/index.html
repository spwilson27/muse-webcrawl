Submarine power cable

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A submarine power cable is a transmission cable for carrying electric power below the surface of the water. These cables carry electrical power beneath salt water (armoured cables) or fresh water (unarmoured). Submarine power cables are used to connect islands to mainland electricity grids, to link the networks of different countries and regions, and increasingly to connect offshore wind farms and oil and gas platforms to shore.

Construction

A modern submarine power cable is a complex engineered product. A typical three-core alternating-current cable contains copper or aluminium conductors surrounded by insulation — historically impregnated paper, now commonly cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) — together with screening, a water-blocking layer, a lead or aluminium sheath, and outer armouring of steel wires that protects against anchors, fishing gear, and abrasion. Direct-current cables for high-voltage direct current (HVDC) links are usually single-core and laid in pairs or bundles.

Installation

Cables are laid by specialised cable-laying ships, which unroll the cable from a rotating carousel or tank as the vessel follows a surveyed route. Where protection is needed, the cable is buried below the seabed using a subsea plough or remotely operated jetting vehicle; in shallow water it may be trenched several metres deep. Landfalls, where the cable comes ashore, are often installed by horizontal directional drilling to avoid disturbing beaches and coastal habitats.

Notable links

Long submarine power links include the NorNed cable between Norway and the Netherlands, the Basslink interconnector between Tasmania and mainland Australia, the North Sea Link between Norway and the United Kingdom — at about 720 kilometres among the longest subsea power cables in the world — and numerous shorter crossings such as those linking the Baltic states and the Danish islands.

Damage and repair

Most faults in submarine cables are caused by external aggression, particularly ship anchors and fishing trawlers, though insulation failure and abrasion also occur. Repair requires a cable ship to grapple the damaged section to the surface, cut out the fault, and splice in a new length — an operation that can take weeks and is highly weather-dependent.
