Royal prerogative

The royal prerogative is a body of customary authority, privilege and immunity recognised in common law as belonging to the sovereign alone, and in modern constitutional practice exercised mostly by ministers of the Crown. It comprises the residue of powers once held personally by the monarch that have not been removed by statute — in A. V. Dicey's phrase, "the residue of discretionary or arbitrary authority... legally left in the hands of the Crown".

Scope

In the United Kingdom surviving prerogative powers include: declaring war and deploying the armed forces; making treaties; issuing passports; granting pardons (the prerogative of mercy); appointing ministers and awarding honours; summoning, proroguing and dissolving Parliament (modified by legislation over time); and granting royal assent to bills. The monarch personally retains a small number of "reserve" functions, such as appointing a prime minister. In foreign affairs and defence, most prerogative powers are wielded by the prime minister and cabinet without statutory authorisation.

Limits and case law

Statute overrides the prerogative where the two conflict (Attorney General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel, 1920), and new prerogative powers cannot be created (Case of Proclamations, 1611). Since the GCHQ case (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service, 1985) the courts have accepted that exercises of the prerogative are in principle reviewable. In R (Miller) v The Prime Minister (2019) the Supreme Court held the 2019 prorogation of Parliament unlawful, confirming judicial limits on the power; the 2016 Miller case held the prerogative could not be used to withdraw from the EU treaties without Parliament.

Other Commonwealth realms

Analogous prerogatives exist in other Commonwealth realms and are exercised by governors-general on ministerial advice, occasionally controversially — as in the 1975 Australian constitutional crisis, when the Governor-General dismissed the Whitlam government.
