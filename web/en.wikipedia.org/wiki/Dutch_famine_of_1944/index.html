The Dutch famine of 1944–1945, known in Dutch as the Hongerwinter ("Hunger Winter"), was a famine that struck the German-occupied Netherlands, especially the densely populated western provinces, during the exceptionally harsh winter of 1944–45, near the end of the Second World War.

Causes

After the failure of Operation Market Garden in September 1944, the Dutch government-in-exile called a railway strike to aid the Allied advance. The German administration retaliated with an embargo on food transport to the western Netherlands. Although the embargo was partially lifted, a combination of the transport ban, an early and severe winter that froze the canals, destroyed infrastructure and dwindling German-held stocks caused rations in the cities to collapse, at times to well under 1,000 calories per day.

Effects

Between about 18,000 and 22,000 people died of starvation and related causes. City dwellers made "hunger trips" to the countryside to barter valuables for food, and people ate tulip bulbs and sugar beets. Relief came with Allied food drops (Operations Manna and Chowhound) in late April 1945 and liberation in May.

Legacy

The famine is the basis of the Dutch Hunger Winter Study, a long-running epidemiological investigation showing that children of women pregnant during the famine had higher rates of certain health conditions in later life — evidence widely cited in the study of fetal programming and epigenetics.
