Binocular disparity refers to the difference in image location of an object seen by the left and right eyes, resulting from the eyes' horizontal separation (parallax). The brain uses binocular disparity to extract depth information from the two-dimensional retinal images in stereopsis.

Mechanism

Because the eyes are separated by about 6–7 cm, each eye views the world from a slightly different position, so a near object shifts more between the two images than a far object. Points on the horopter — an imaginary surface passing through the fixation point — fall on corresponding retinal points and have zero disparity. Objects nearer than fixation produce crossed disparity; objects farther away produce uncrossed disparity.

Disparity-selective neurons in the primary visual cortex, first demonstrated in the 1960s by work following Bela Julesz's random-dot stereograms, encode these differences. Julesz showed that disparity alone, without any monocular cues, is sufficient to perceive depth. Human stereoacuity can be as fine as a few seconds of arc.

Applications

Binocular disparity is exploited in stereoscopes, 3D films, virtual and augmented reality headsets, and stereo camera systems for robot navigation and photogrammetry. Clinical tests of disparity thresholds (stereo tests) screen for amblyopia and strabismus in children.
