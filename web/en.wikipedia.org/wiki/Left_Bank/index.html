Left Bank (Paris)

The Left Bank (French: Rive Gauche, pronounced [ʁiv ɡoʃ]) is the southern bank of the River Seine in Paris. Here the river flows roughly westward, cutting the city in two: when facing downstream, the southern bank is on the left. The Left Bank is associated above all with the Latin Quarter and the neighbourhoods of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the Faubourg Saint-Germain and Montparnasse.

Character

The Left Bank has long been the centre of Parisian intellectual and artistic life. The Sorbonne and other colleges of the University of Paris drew scholars to the Latin Quarter from the Middle Ages. In the twentieth century the cafés of Saint-Germain-des-Prés — Les Deux Magots, Café de Flore and Brasserie Lipp — were meeting places for existentialist writers and philosophers including Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, earning the district the nickname "Rive Gauche" as shorthand for a bohemian, intellectual lifestyle. Philosophers, publishers, antique dealers and the famous open-air booksellers (bouquinistes) along the quays remain characteristic of the area.

Arrondissements

The Left Bank comprises the 5th, 6th and 7th arrondissements together with parts of the 13th, 14th and 15th. Landmarks south of the river include the Panthéon, the Luxembourg Gardens, Les Invalides, the Musée d'Orsay, the Eiffel Tower and the French National Assembly at the Palais Bourbon.

Counterpart

The Right Bank (Rive Droite), to the north, is traditionally associated with commerce, finance and fashion, including the Champs-Élysées, the Louvre and the grands magasins. The contrast between the two banks remains a familiar trope in descriptions of Parisian life.
