Work (physics)

In physics, work is the energy transferred to or from an object via the application of force along a displacement. In its simplest form, it is often represented as the product of force and displacement. Work is a scalar quantity, so it has only magnitude and no direction. Energy is transferred into a system when a force, applied to the system, does work; the SI unit of work is the joule (J), the same unit as energy.

Definition

For a constant force F acting along a straight displacement d, the work done is W = Fd cos θ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. For a variable force along a path, work is the line integral of the force along the trajectory. A force does no work if the displacement is zero or perpendicular to the force—as with the centripetal force in uniform circular motion.

Work–energy theorem

The work done by the net force on a particle equals the change in the particle's kinetic energy: W = ΔKE = ½mv₂² − ½mv₁². This principle, derived from Newton's second law, extends to systems of particles and, with the inclusion of potential energy for conservative forces, underlies the principle of conservation of mechanical energy. In thermodynamics, work is one of the two principal modes of energy transfer between a system and its surroundings, the other being heat.

History

The modern concept emerged in the 19th century, notably in the work of Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who defined work as "weight lifted through a height", and Jean-Victor Poncelet. The unit, the joule, is named after James Prescott Joule, whose experiments established the mechanical equivalent of heat.
