Intersex in history

Intersex people — historically called hermaphrodites — have been documented in mythology, law, medicine, and art from antiquity onward. Societies have variously treated intersex births as omens, religious signs, legal puzzles, or medical conditions, and attitudes toward intersex people have shifted dramatically over time.

Antiquity and the Middle Ages

Greek mythology gave the figure of Hermaphroditus, child of Hermes and Aphrodite, whose body combined male and female characteristics. Roman law, as recorded in the Digest of Justinian, assigned intersex persons to the sex that predominated. Medieval canon and civil law generally followed the Roman approach, allowing some intersex people to choose a legal sex at adulthood, with the choice then treated as permanent.

Early modern and medical eras

In early modern Europe, cases of sex ambiguity were examined by physicians, midwives, and courts; some individuals lived in one gender and later in another. From the nineteenth century, medicine increasingly classified intersex conditions, and in the twentieth century surgical and hormonal "normalisation" of infants became standard practice in many countries, following theories of gender development that emphasised nurture.

Activism and human rights

From the 1990s, intersex activists — beginning with the founding of the Intersex Society of North America in 1993 — challenged non-consensual infant surgeries and secrecy. In the twenty-first century, bodies such as the United Nations and the Council of Europe have recognised intersex rights, and Malta's 2015 legislation was the first to prohibit non-consensual modifications of sex characteristics.
