The Ferranti Mark 1, also known as the Manchester Electronic Computer in its sales literature, was produced by the British electrical engineering firm Ferranti Ltd. It was the world's first commercially available general-purpose stored-program electronic computer, with the first machine delivered to the University of Manchester in February 1951 — narrowly beating the UNIVAC I (delivered to the U.S. Census Bureau that June) into service. The design was the commercial development of the university's Manchester Mark 1, itself descended from the 1948 "Baby" (Small-Scale Experimental Machine).

Technical description

The Mark 1 retained the Manchester Mark 1's basic architecture: 40-bit words, a main store of Williams–Kilburn cathode-ray tubes holding 1,024 words, and a magnetic drum as backing storage. It contained about 4,050 thermionic valves (vacuum tubes). Ferranti's engineers, led on the engineering side by Dietrich Prinz, refined the prototype into a manufacturable product, adding improved input/output equipment — five-track paper tape readers and punches and a teleprinter — and hardware hardware for multiply operations that the prototype had implemented largely in software. Alan Turing wrote the programming manual for the machine, in his characteristically terse style.

Software and notable uses

The Mark 1 was programmed by some of the earliest professional programmers, including Mary Berners-Lee and Audrey Bates. Christopher Strachey wrote a draughts (checkers) program for it and, in 1952, a program that generated love letters — an early work of computer-generated literature. The machine also produced music: a recording made by the BBC in 1951 of the Mark 1 playing "God Save the King," "Baa Baa Black Sheep," and "In the Mood" is the earliest known recording of computer-generated music, and Strachey later became a major figure in programming-language theory. Dietrich Prinz wrote one of the first chess programs for the machine, solving mate-in-two problems.

Sales and successors

Nine machines of the Mark 1 family were delivered between 1951 and 1957, including a simplified variant, the Mark 1*, sold to universities and government laboratories in Britain, Canada (the FERUT at the University of Toronto), the Netherlands, Italy, and Australia, as well as to the British Atomic Weapons Research Establishment and an insurance company. Ferranti's Manchester computer line continued with the Mercury (1957) and Atlas (1962) before the business was absorbed into ICT.
