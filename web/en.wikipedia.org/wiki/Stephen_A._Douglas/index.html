Stephen Arnold Douglas (April 23, 1813 – June 3, 1861) was an American politician and lawyer from Illinois. A senator, he was one of two nominees of the badly split Democratic Party for president in the 1860 presidential election, which was won by Republican Abraham Lincoln. Douglas had previously defeated Lincoln in the 1858 United States Senate election in Illinois, known for the famous Lincoln–Douglas debates of 1858.

Nicknamed the "Little Giant" because of his short stature but powerful oratory and political influence, Douglas was a leading advocate of popular sovereignty — the doctrine that settlers in a territory should decide for themselves whether to allow slavery.

Early life and career

Douglas was born in Brandon, Vermont, and moved to Illinois as a young man, where he read law and rose rapidly in Democratic politics. He served in the Illinois legislature, as an Illinois Supreme Court justice, and in the U.S. House of Representatives before entering the Senate in 1847.

Senate career

In the Senate, Douglas chaired the Committee on Territories and played a key role in the Compromise of 1850. He authored the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which repealed the Missouri Compromise's restriction on slavery and organised the territories of Kansas and Nebraska on the basis of popular sovereignty. The act provoked violent conflict in "Bleeding Kansas" and helped spur the creation of the Republican Party.

Lincoln–Douglas debates and the 1860 election

During his 1858 re-election campaign, Douglas debated Abraham Lincoln seven times across Illinois. In the Freeport Debate, Douglas articulated the "Freeport Doctrine", holding that a territory could effectively exclude slavery by refusing to enact protective legislation — an answer that preserved his Senate seat but deepened the Democratic Party's sectional split. In 1860 the Democratic Party fractured: Northern Democrats nominated Douglas, while Southern Democrats nominated John C. Breckinridge. Douglas finished second in the popular vote but won only Missouri and part of New Jersey's electoral votes.

Death and legacy

After Lincoln's victory, Douglas toured the South urging loyalty to the Union. Following the outbreak of the Civil War he pledged support to Lincoln's administration, but he died of typhoid fever in Chicago on June 3, 1861. Historians continue to debate whether his popular-sovereignty doctrine was a pragmatic democratic solution or a moral evasion on slavery.
