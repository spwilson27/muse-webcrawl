Behavioral ecology

Behavioural ecology explains behaviour as an adaptation to ecological pressures.

Behavioral ecology, also spelled behavioural ecology, is the study of the evolutionary basis for animal behaviour due to ecological pressures. It emerged from ethology, ecology and evolutionary biology in the 1970s, drawing on the earlier work of Niko Tinbergen and on the sociobiology debates that followed E. O. Wilson's Sociobiology (1975).

Approach

Behavioural ecologists treat behaviour as a trait shaped by natural selection and ask how a behaviour contributes to an animal's survival and reproductive success in its environment. Key tools include optimality models — such as optimal foraging theory, which predicts how animals should allocate time and energy when feeding — game theory applied to contests, cooperation and signalling, and the comparative method across species.

Major topics

Central subjects include foraging and anti-predator behaviour, territoriality, mating systems and sexual selection, parental care, kin selection and altruism (developed from W. D. Hamilton's theory of inclusive fitness), social behaviour and communication, and coevolutionary arms races between hosts and parasites, predators and prey. The field overlaps with evolutionary psychology when applied to humans.
