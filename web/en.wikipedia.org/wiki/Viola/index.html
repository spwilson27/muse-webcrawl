Viola

The viola (/viˈoʊlə/; Italian: [ˈvjɔːla]) is a string instrument that is bowed, plucked, or played with varying techniques. It is slightly larger than the violin, has a lower and deeper sound, and since the 18th century has been the middle voice of the violin family, between the violin and the cello.

Description

The viola is tuned a perfect fifth below the violin, with strings C–G–D–A, the lowest open C of any instrument in the family commonly played under the chin. It is read primarily in the alto clef (and treble clef for high passages), a feature unique among string instruments. Viola body length varies, typically 38–43 cm (15–18 in), a compromise between the acoustically ideal size and what a player can hold.

Role in music

In orchestral and chamber music the viola supplies inner harmonies, rhythmic figures, and counter-melodies; its relatively few concertos reflect a historically smaller solo role, though composers such as Mozart (Sinfonia Concertante), Berlioz (Harold in Italy), Walton, Bartók, and Hindemith — himself a virtuoso violist — wrote major solo works for it. Notable modern violists include Lionel Tertis, William Primrose, Kim Kashkashian, Tabea Zimmermann, and Lawrence Power.

History

The viola emerged with the rest of the violin family in northern Italy in the early 16th century. Early instruments existed in tenor and alto sizes; the smaller alto survived as the standard. Viola jokes, a long-standing orchestral folk tradition, poke fun at the instrument's quieter profile and historically less virtuosic repertoire.
