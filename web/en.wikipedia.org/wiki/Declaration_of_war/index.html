Declaration of war

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A declaration of war is a formal act by which one state announces the initiation or existence of a state of war against another. The Hague Convention III (1907) required hostilities to be preceded by a reasoned declaration or an ultimatum with conditional declaration. Formal declarations were characteristic of warfare into the early 20th century; the last US declarations of war were in 1941–42 (against Japan, Germany, Italy and their allies).

Decline and substitutes

Since 1945, states rarely declare war — the UN Charter prohibits the use of force except in self-defence or with Security Council authorisation, removing war's legal respectability, and governments prefer "armed conflict", "interventions", authorisations for the use of military force (AUMFs), and domestic war-powers compromises (the US War Powers Resolution of 1973, born of undeclared wars in Korea and Vietnam, remains disputed terrain). The legal consequences once triggered by declarations (enemy-alien status, trading-with-the-enemy law, neutrality duties, treaty suspension) now attach to the fact of armed conflict itself under the Geneva Conventions' Common Article 2. Debates over undeclared uses of force — drone campaigns, proxy warfare, cyber operations — are modern echoes of the declaration's obsolescence.
