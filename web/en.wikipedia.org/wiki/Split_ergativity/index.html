Split ergativity

In linguistic typology, split ergativity is a phenomenon in which a language shows ergative–absolutive case marking or agreement in some grammatical contexts but nominative–accusative alignment in others. The split is typically conditioned by factors such as tense and aspect, the semantic class of the noun phrase, or the distinction between main and subordinate clauses.

Aspect-based splits

The most widely discussed type is the tense–aspect split found in Indo-Iranian languages such as Hindi-Urdu and Pashto. In Hindi-Urdu, ergative marking of the subject of a transitive verb (with the postposition ne) occurs only in perfective aspect clauses; in imperfective clauses the language behaves accusatively, with an unmarked nominative subject and verb agreement with the subject. This pattern is generally explained historically: the perfective construction descends from a passive construction with a past participle, in which the agent was expressed in an oblique case.

Noun-phrase-based splits

In many Australian Aboriginal languages, such as Dyirbal, the alignment split follows the nominal hierarchy: pronouns behave accusatively, while full nouns behave ergatively. Silverstein's hierarchy (first and second person pronouns > third person pronouns > proper names > human common nouns > other animates > inanimates) predicts where the boundary between the two alignment systems falls in such languages.

Significance

Split ergativity demonstrates that "ergativity" is not an all-or-nothing property of whole languages but can be distributed across constructions, informing theories of case, agreement and syntactic alignment.
