Foreign policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A foreign policy is the set of goals, strategies and measures by which a state (or union, such as the EU) seeks to guide its actions and interactions with other states, international organisations and non-state actors. It operationalises the state's perceived national interests — security, prosperity, values and status — through diplomacy, alliances, trade and aid, sanctions, intelligence and the threat or use of military force.

Determinants and analysis

Foreign policy analysis (FPA) examines levels from leaders' beliefs and psychology, through bureaucratic politics and domestic coalitions (parties, lobbies, diasporas, public opinion), to systemic pressures (anarchy, the balance of power, interdependence). International-relations theories give competing explanations: realism (power and survival), liberalism (institutions, trade and democracy), constructivism (identities and norms) and Marxist and postcolonial accounts (dependency, empire). Grand strategies crystallise orientations: isolationism and restraint, liberal internationalism, primacy, offshore balancing — vocabularies from US debates generalisable with care.

Institutions

Foreign ministries, national-security councils, intelligence services and legislatures (through war powers, budgets and treaties) contend over policy; heads of government have centralised it in many systems. Public diplomacy and digital communications have expanded the cast of actors, while courts and parliaments exert growing oversight of deployments, arms exports and surveillance.
