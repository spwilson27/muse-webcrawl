SECAM

SECAM, also written SÉCAM (French: Système électronique couleur avec mémoire, "electronic colour system with memory"), is an analogue colour television system first used in France beginning in 1967. It was one of three major analogue colour television standards, the others being PAL and NTSC.

SECAM was developed by a team led by Henri de France, working at Compagnie Française de Télévision. It encodes colour information using frequency modulation on two alternate colour subcarriers, transmitting the two colour-difference signals on alternate lines — the "memory" in its name referring to the delay line in receivers used to average colour information across successive lines.

History

France adopted SECAM in 1967 for its second television channel, motivated partly by a desire to protect the French electronics industry. The Soviet Union adopted the system in 1967 as well, and SECAM spread to the USSR's satellite states in Eastern Europe, parts of Africa, and the Middle East. Many former SECAM countries in Eastern Europe switched to PAL after the fall of communism, while Russia and France retained SECAM until the transition to digital broadcasting (DVB-T) completed in the 2010s.

Technical details

SECAM uses a 625-line, 50 Hz interlaced raster like PAL. Unlike PAL and NTSC, which use quadrature amplitude modulation for a composite chrominance signal, SECAM frequency-modulates two colour subcarriers (4.40625 MHz and 4.25000 MHz) carrying the R−Y and B−Y colour-difference signals on alternating lines. Several variants (SECAM B/G, D/K, L, M and others) existed depending on the broadcast standard of the country.
