Desiccant

A desiccant is a hygroscopic substance used to induce or sustain a state of dryness (desiccation) in its vicinity. Desiccants absorb water from the air or from materials with which they are packed, and are widely used in packaging, storage, laboratories and industrial processes to protect goods and materials from moisture damage.

Types

Common desiccants include silica gel, the beads found in small paper packets inside shoe boxes, electronics packaging and medicine bottles; activated charcoal; calcium sulfate (sold as Drierite); calcium chloride; montmorillonite clay; and molecular sieves (synthetic zeolites), which can achieve very low residual humidity. In the laboratory, strong chemical drying agents such as phosphorus pentoxide and metallic sodium are used to dry solvents and gases. Pre-packaged desiccants for shipping containers, sometimes called container desiccants, protect cargo during long sea voyages.

Mechanism and properties

Desiccants work either by physical adsorption of water onto a large internal surface area (as with silica gel and molecular sieves) or by chemical reaction with water to form hydrates (as with calcium chloride and calcium oxide). Their performance is measured by the amount of water absorbed per unit mass and by the equilibrium humidity they maintain. Many desiccants can be regenerated by heating, which drives off the absorbed water; indicating silica gel changes colour — traditionally with cobalt chloride, from blue when dry to pink when saturated — to show when it is exhausted.

Safety

Most packet desiccants are labelled "do not eat". Silica gel itself is essentially non-toxic, but packets pose a choking hazard, and some desiccants, such as calcium oxide (quicklime), are corrosive or react exothermically with water.
