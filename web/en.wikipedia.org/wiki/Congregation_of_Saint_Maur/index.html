Congregation of Saint Maur

Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the mother house of the Maurist congregation.

The Congregation of Saint Maur, often known as the Maurists, was a congregation of French Benedictine monks founded in 1621 and named after Saint Maurus, a disciple of Saint Benedict. Approved by Pope Gregory XV, the congregation united reformed Benedictine monasteries across France under a common discipline, with its centre at the abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés in Paris.

The Maurists became celebrated throughout Europe for their scholarship. Their monks produced monumental critical editions of the Church Fathers and medieval texts, works on diplomatics, palaeography and chronology, and vast collections of historical documents. The most famous Maurist was Dom Jean Mabillon, whose De re diplomatica (1681) founded the modern science of diplomatics. Other notable Maurists include Bernard de Montfaucon, founder of Greek palaeography, and Luc d'Achery.

At its height the congregation numbered some 180 monasteries. It was suppressed during the French Revolution in 1790, and its scholarly collections were dispersed, many passing to French state libraries.
