Upper Egypt (Arabic: صعيد مصر, Ṣaʿīd Miṣr, shortened to es-Ṣaʿīd, "the upland"; Coptic: ⲙⲁⲣⲏⲥ) is the southern portion of Egypt, comprising the narrow Nile valley from the vicinity of Memphis and modern Cairo southward to Aswan at the First Cataract. It is called "upper" because it lies upstream — the Nile flows northward to the Mediterranean — so Upper Egypt is on the map below Lower Egypt (the Nile Delta). Its inhabitants are known as Sa'idis.

Ancient history

In predynastic times Upper Egypt was the land of the Naqada culture, and its principal early centers included Nekhen (Hierakonpolis) and Naqada, contrasted with the Buto-Maadi culture of the Delta. Around 3100 BCE the Upper Egyptian king Narmer (or Menes) unified the "Two Lands," founding pharaonic civilization; the pharaoh's Double Crown combined the White Crown of Upper Egypt with the Red Crown of Lower Egypt. The region's great city was Thebes (modern Luxor), capital during much of the Middle and New Kingdoms, seat of the god Amun and of the temples of Karnak and Luxor and the royal tombs of the Valley of the Kings. Upper Egypt was traditionally divided into 22 nomes (districts) from the apex of the Delta to Aswan.

Modern period

Today Upper Egypt covers governorates including Aswan, Luxor, Qena, Sohag, Asyut, Minya and Beni Suef. Its economy rests on agriculture (especially sugar cane), tourism drawn to pharaonic monuments, and quarrying. The region is generally less wealthy than the Delta and Cairo, with a distinct Sa'idi dialect and culture, a substantial Coptic Christian population, and historical tension with the north. Construction of the Aswan High Dam (completed 1970) ended the annual Nile flood that had defined Upper Egyptian agriculture for millennia.
