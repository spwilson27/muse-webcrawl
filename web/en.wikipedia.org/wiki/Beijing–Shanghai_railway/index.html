The Beijing–Shanghai railway, or Jinghu railway, is a major conventional railway line in China connecting the capital, Beijing, with Shanghai, the country's largest city. Running 1,462 kilometres (908 mi) through Tianjin, Hebei, Shandong, Anhui, and Jiangsu, it traverses some of China's most densely populated and economically important regions and has historically been the busiest conventional rail corridor in the country.

History

The line was assembled from several railways built in the late Qing dynasty and early Republic. The northern section grew out of the imperial railways around Beijing and Tianjin; the Tianjin–Pukou railway, financed with German and British loans, opened in 1912; and the Shanghai–Nanjing railway opened in 1908. For decades the route was broken by the Yangtze River at Nanjing, where trains were carried across by train ferry from 1933. Through operation became possible only in 1968, when the Nanjing Yangtze River Bridge — a landmark of Mao-era engineering — opened. The line was progressively double-tracked and was fully electrified in 2006.

Role and the high-speed successor

Before 2011, the Jinghu corridor carried an outsized share of China's passenger and freight traffic on a small fraction of national track length, and trains were chronically overcrowded. The parallel Beijing–Shanghai high-speed railway, opened on 30 June 2011, took over most long-distance passenger demand, freeing the conventional line primarily for freight and regional and overnight passenger services. Stations on the conventional line include Beijing, Tianjin West, Jinan, Xuzhou, Bengbu, Nanjing, Zhenjiang, Changzhou, Suzhou, and Shanghai.
