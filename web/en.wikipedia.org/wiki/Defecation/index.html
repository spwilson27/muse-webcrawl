Defecation

Defecation, also known as a bowel movement, is the final act of digestion by which organisms eliminate a solid, semisolid, or sometimes liquid waste material known as feces from the digestive tract via the anus. The waste material consists of indigestible food residues, bacteria, metabolic waste products such as bile pigments, and shed cells from the lining of the gut.

Physiology

Waves of muscular contraction known as peristalsis move fecal matter through the colon, where water is absorbed and the feces are stored in the rectum. Distension of the rectal wall triggers the defecation reflex, producing the urge to defecate and relaxing the internal anal sphincter. Voluntary control of the external anal sphincter allows defecation to be postponed until a suitable time; when defecation proceeds, the abdominal and pelvic floor muscles contract and the sphincters relax to expel the feces.

Frequency and variations

Frequency of defecation varies widely between individuals and is influenced by diet, fluid intake, physical activity and gut health. A range from three times a day to three times a week is generally considered normal. The consistency of stool is often assessed clinically using the Bristol stool scale. Abnormalities include constipation, in which defecation is infrequent or difficult, and diarrhoea, in which it is excessively frequent and loose.

Cultural and sanitary aspects

Human societies have developed a wide range of practices surrounding defecation, from open defecation to the flush toilet connected to sewerage systems. Safe disposal of human feces is a central goal of sanitation policy, since fecal contamination of water supplies is a major route for the transmission of diseases such as cholera and typhoid fever. The World Health Organization and UNICEF estimate that billions of people still lack access to safely managed sanitation services.
