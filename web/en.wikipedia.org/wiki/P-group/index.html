P-group

In mathematics, specifically group theory, given a prime number p, a p-group is a group in which the order of every element is a power of p. That is, for each element g of the group, there exists a nonnegative integer n such that g raised to the power pn equals the identity element. If the group is finite, this is equivalent to the group order itself being a power of p, by Lagrange's theorem. Finite p-groups are a central object of study in group theory: by the Sylow theorems, every finite group contains p-subgroups (its Sylow p-subgroups) that are p-groups.

Properties

Finite p-groups have several characteristic structural features. Their centre is always nontrivial for a nontrivial finite p-group, a fact proved using the class equation; as a consequence, every finite p-group is nilpotent. Groups of order p2 are necessarily abelian — either cyclic or the direct product of two cyclic groups of order p. The number of groups of order pn grows rapidly with n: there are 51 groups of order 64 (26) and 2,328 groups of order 128 (27), and the enumeration of 2-groups of order 256 gives 56,092 groups.

Examples

Abelian examples include the cyclic group Cpn and the Prüfer group, an infinite p-group in which every element has order a power of p. Important nonabelian examples are the dihedral groups of order 2n, the quaternion group of order 8, and matrix groups such as the group of upper triangular 3×3 matrices over the field with p elements, of order p3. Extraspecial p-groups and pro-p groups (inverse limits of finite p-groups) are further major topics.

Applications

Finite p-groups play a key role in the classification of finite simple groups and in local analysis, where a group is studied through the normalisers of its nontrivial p-subgroups. They also appear in algebraic topology and number theory, for instance in Galois theory, where pro-p Galois groups encode the p-part of field extensions.
