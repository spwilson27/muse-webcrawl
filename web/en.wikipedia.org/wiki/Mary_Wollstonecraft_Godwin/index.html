Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley (née Godwin; 30 August 1797 – 1 February 1851) was an English novelist best known for Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus (1818), one of the most influential works of Gothic fiction and an early example of science fiction. Born Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin, she was the daughter of the political philosopher William Godwin and the feminist writer Mary Wollstonecraft, who died eleven days after her birth.

Life

Raised by her father in a household frequented by radical intellectuals, Mary began a relationship with the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley in 1814; they eloped to the continent despite his existing marriage, and married in 1816 after his first wife's death. In the rainy summer of 1816 at the Villa Diodati on Lake Geneva, a ghost-story contest with Lord Byron prompted her to conceive Frankenstein, published anonymously when she was 20. Three of her four children died young, and Percy drowned in Italy in 1822, after which she returned to England and devoted herself to writing and editing his works.

Works

Beyond Frankenstein, her writings include the apocalyptic novel The Last Man (1826), the historical novels Valperga (1823) and Perkin Warbeck (1830), Lodore (1835) and Falkner (1837), travel writing, and biographical essays. Modern scholarship has restored her reputation as a major Romantic author whose work engages with politics, gender and the ethics of scientific ambition — themes her husband explored in verse in Prometheus Unbound.
