South Caucasus

The South Caucasus, also known as Transcaucasia, is a geographical region on the border of Eastern Europe and Western Asia, straddling the southern Greater Caucasus Mountains. It roughly corresponds to the territory of the modern states of Armenia, Azerbaijan, and Georgia, which emerged from the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. The region lies between the Black Sea to the west and the Caspian Sea to the east.

Geography

The South Caucasus encompasses the Lesser Caucasus range, the Armenian Highlands, and the lowlands along the Kura and Araxes rivers. Its population is roughly 17 million, ethnically and linguistically highly diverse, including Armenians, Azerbaijanis, Georgians, and numerous minority peoples.

History

The region was contested for centuries by the Roman, Persian, Arab, Ottoman, and Russian empires. In the 19th century it was incorporated into the Russian Empire. After brief independence in 1918–1921, the three states were absorbed into the USSR as the Transcaucasian SFSR and then as separate union republics. Since 1991, the region has been marked by conflicts over Nagorno-Karabakh (between Armenia and Azerbaijan), Abkhazia and South Ossetia (in Georgia), and by its role as a corridor for Caspian oil and gas pipelines to Europe.

Economy

Energy resources (especially Azerbaijani oil and gas), agriculture, mining, and transit trade are economically central; the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline and the Southern Gas Corridor pass through the region.
