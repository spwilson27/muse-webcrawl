Cabaret

Cabaret is a form of theatrical entertainment featuring music, song, dance, recitation, or drama. The audience, often dining or drinking, does not typically dance but usually sits at tables. Performances are usually introduced by a master of ceremonies (MC). The entertainment, as performed by an ensemble of actors and according to its European origins, is often (but not always) oriented towards adult audiences and of a clearly underground nature. In the United States, striptease, burlesque, drag shows, or a solo vocalist with a pianist are terms for forms of cabaret.

History

Cabaret began in Paris in 1881 with the opening of Le Chat Noir in Montmartre by Rodolphe Salis, where poets, singers and satirists performed for drinking audiences. The Moulin Rouge, founded in 1889, became famous for the can-can and for stars such as La Goulue, immortalised in Toulouse-Lautrec's posters. In Germany, the Berlin Kabarett of the Weimar era (notably at venues associated with writers such as Kurt Tucholsky and performers such as Claire Waldoff) developed a sharper political satire, later suppressed under the Nazi regime.

Forms

Dutch, French and German cabaret traditions emphasise spoken satire and chanson; American cabaret developed toward intimate nightclub singing and variety bills. The genre was popularised for modern audiences by the 1966 Kander and Ebb musical Cabaret and its 1972 film adaptation starring Liza Minnelli, set in the Kit Kat Klub of 1931 Berlin.
