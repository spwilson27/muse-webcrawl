Dargah

A dargah (Persian: درگاه dargāh or درگاہ dargâh, Turkish: dergâh, Hindi: dargāh, Bengali: দরগাহ dorgah) is the tomb or shrine built over the grave of a revered religious figure, often a Sufi saint or dervish. Dargahs are frequently associated with Sufi gathering halls and hostels, known as khanqahs or zawiyas, and are commonly found across the Muslim world, particularly in Iran, Turkey, South Asia and North Africa.

Devotees visit dargahs to seek blessings (barakah), offer prayers and fulfil vows, often on the saint's death anniversary, celebrated as urs. In South Asia, many dargahs draw pilgrims of all faiths.

Etymology and meaning

The Persian word dargāh literally means "place of the door" and denotes a threshold or royal court; in a spiritual sense it signifies the court of the saint, where the seeker presents himself before the friend of God. Over time it came to mean specifically the saint's mausoleum and its surrounding complex.

Practices

Pilgrimage to a dargah (ziyarat) typically involves prayer at the tomb, recitation of the Fatiha, circumambulation in some regions, tying threads, offering flowers, chadars (embroidered cloths) and sweets, and listening to devotional music such as qawwali in South Asia. Custodians (khadims or mujawirs) traditionally guide pilgrims and maintain the shrine. Large dargahs often operate soup kitchens (langar) and charitable services.

Famous dargahs

Among the most visited are the shrine of Muinuddin Chishti at Ajmer (Ajmer Sharif) in Rajasthan; the tomb of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi; Data Darbar of Ali Hujwiri in Lahore, one of the oldest Muslim shrines in South Asia; the Haji Ali Dargah in Mumbai, set in the sea; and the Mevlana Mausoleum of Rumi in Konya, Turkey. In Iran, comparable shrines are often called imamzadeh when dedicated to descendants of Shi'a Imams.

Criticism

Some Islamic reform movements, including Salafism and Wahhabism, regard tomb veneration and intercession-seeking at dargahs as shirk (idolatry) and have opposed or destroyed such shrines, while mainstream Sufi tradition defends ziyarat as a means of blessing and remembrance.
