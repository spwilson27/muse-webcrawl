Necrotizing fasciitis (NF), also known in the media as the "flesh-eating disease", is a rapidly progressive infection of the deeper layers of skin and subcutaneous tissues that spreads along the fascial planes and causes extensive tissue death (necrosis). It is a surgical emergency; without rapid treatment it is frequently fatal.

Causes

Necrotizing fasciitis is classed by microbiology. Type I infections are polymicrobial, mixing aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, and typically affect older or immunocompromised patients. Type II infections are usually caused by group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes), sometimes with Staphylococcus aureus, and can strike healthy people after minor trauma. Infections following exposure of wounds to warm seawater may involve Vibrio vulnificus. Bacteria typically enter through a break in the skin, which may be as small as a cut, insect bite, or injection site.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms resemble cellulitis but progress within hours: pain "out of proportion" to the visible findings, rapidly spreading redness and swelling, fever, and systemic toxicity. Later signs include purple blistering (bullae), skin discoloration, crepitus from gas in the tissues, and numbness as cutaneous nerves are destroyed. Shock and organ failure may follow.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is primarily clinical and is confirmed by surgical exploration, which reveals grey necrotic fascia and "dishwater" fluid. Laboratory scoring systems such as LRINEC can support suspicion but must never delay treatment. Management requires immediate broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and urgent, often repeated, surgical debridement of all dead tissue; amputation is sometimes necessary. Hyperbaric oxygen is occasionally used as an adjunct. Even with optimal care, mortality remains around 20–30 percent, rising sharply when surgery is delayed.
