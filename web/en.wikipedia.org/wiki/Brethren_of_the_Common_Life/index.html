Brethren of the Common Life

The Brethren of the Common Life (Latin: Fratres Vitae Communis) were a Roman Catholic religious community founded in the 14th century in the Netherlands by Gerard Groote, a wealthy burgher of Deventer converted to a life of piety. Together with the related Sisters of the Common Life and the Windesheim Congregation of Augustinian canons, they embodied the Devotio Moderna ("Modern Devotion"), a movement of lay and clerical piety emphasising inner devotion, humility, and practical Christianity over scholastic theology.

Life and organisation

The Brethren lived in community, held goods in common, and supported themselves by copying manuscripts — and later by printing — and by teaching, yet they took no perpetual monastic vows and could in principle leave. Their houses, beginning with Florens Radewyns' house at Deventer, combined communal prayer, manual work, and the reading and discussion of devotional texts in the vernacular. Groote's preaching inspired the movement; after his death in 1384, his followers founded the monastery of Windesheim (1387), which became the centre of a congregation of Augustinian canons.

Education and influence

The Brethren ran famous schools at Deventer, Zwolle, and elsewhere, educating thousands of boys, including, for a time, Desiderius Erasmus and possibly Nicolaus Copernicus's contemporaries in the northern Renaissance. Their most famous literary fruit is The Imitation of Christ, attributed to Thomas à Kempis, a member of the Windesheim circle — one of the most widely read devotional books in Christian history. The movement's stress on personal devotion, Bible reading, and educated lay piety is often seen as preparing the ground for both the Reformation and Catholic reform. The communities declined after the Reformation and the rise of state schooling, with most houses disappearing by the 17th century.
