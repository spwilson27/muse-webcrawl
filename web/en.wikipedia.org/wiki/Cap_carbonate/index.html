Cap carbonate

Cap carbonates are layers of distinctively textured carbonate rocks (limestone or dolostone) that occur at the top of glacial deposits of late Neoproterozoic age, capping diamictites associated with the Marinoan (c. 635 Ma) and Sturtian (c. 717–660 Ma) glaciations. Typically a few metres to tens of metres thick, these layers are found on many continents and are a key line of evidence for the Snowball Earth hypothesis, which proposes that the oceans were nearly or completely frozen during these events.

Characteristics

Cap carbonates sit directly and sharply on top of glacial diamictite with little or no intervening sediment, indicating extremely rapid deposition at the end of glaciation. They commonly display unusual sedimentary structures: sheet cracks, tepee-like structures, crystal fans (former aragonite sea-floor precipitates), barite fans, and stromatolites. Their carbon isotope values are characteristically negative, with δ13C declining to about −5‰, a signature used to correlate successions globally.

Formation models

In Snowball Earth models, prolonged global glaciation suppressed the hydrological cycle and weathering while volcanic CO2 built up in the atmosphere. When the ice finally melted under extreme greenhouse conditions, intense weathering of freshly exposed rock delivered huge quantities of alkalinity to the oceans, driving rapid precipitation of carbonate — first as deep-water "cap dolostones" and then limestones. Alternative or complementary mechanisms include upwelling of alkaline deep ocean water stored during glaciation and methane release from destabilized gas hydrates.

Significance

Because Marinoan cap carbonates are so widespread and distinctive — well-studied examples occur in Namibia, South Australia, South China, and Death Valley (California) — they serve as global marker horizons for the end of the Cryogenian period, dated by ash beds within them to about 635 million years ago, at the boundary with the Ediacaran period.
