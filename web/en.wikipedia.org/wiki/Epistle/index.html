Epistle

An epistle (from the Greek epistolē, "letter") is a formal letter intended for public reading or a wide audience, as distinct from a private letter. The term is used both for a literary genre — the epistolary poem or treatise — and for the letters of the New Testament.

Ancient epistles

The letter form was highly developed in the Hellenistic world. Surviving collections include the letters of Cicero, Seneca's Epistulae Morales, and Pliny the Younger. Horace's Epistles and Ovid's Heroides adapted the letter into verse, and the verse epistle remained a major literary form through Alexander Pope and the eighteenth century.

New Testament epistles

Twenty-one books of the New Testament are epistles. The Pauline epistles — Romans, Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, Thessalonians, Timothy, Titus and Philemon — are letters attributed to Paul the Apostle addressed to churches or individuals. The "general" or Catholic epistles include Hebrews, James, Peter, John and Jude. In liturgy, "the Epistle" is the reading from one of these letters, traditionally sung or read before the Gospel at Mass and other services.

Epistles in other traditions

Epistles figure in many religious literatures: the encyclical letters of popes in Catholicism, the letters of Baháʼu'lláh in the Baháʼí Faith, and the ra'sail genres of Arabic and Persian literature. Some works called "epistles" are didactic treatises in letter form rather than genuine correspondence.
