Dopamine transporter

The dopamine transporter (DAT, also known as the sodium-dependent dopamine transporter) is a membrane-spanning protein that pumps the neurotransmitter dopamine out of the synaptic cleft back into the cytosol of the presynaptic neuron. In this way DAT is the primary mechanism through which dopamine is cleared from synapses, regulating the duration and intensity of dopaminergic signalling. It is encoded by the SLC6A3 gene in humans.

Structure and function

DAT belongs to the solute carrier family of neurotransmitter sodium symporters, along with the serotonin and norepinephrine transporters. It has twelve transmembrane domains and uses the sodium ion gradient to drive dopamine uptake. Once recaptured, dopamine is repackaged into vesicles by the vesicular monoamine transporter VMAT2 or broken down by enzymes such as monoamine oxidase.

Clinical significance

DAT is the target of many pharmacological agents. Cocaine and amphetamine-type stimulants act at the transporter: cocaine blocks it, whereas amphetamine is a substrate that reverses its direction, causing dopamine efflux. Medications that inhibit DAT, such as methylphenidate, are used in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Reduced DAT function has been studied in relation to Parkinson's disease, schizophrenia, depression, and rare mutations cause dopamine transporter deficiency syndrome, a childhood movement disorder. Brain imaging of DAT density is used clinically to help distinguish Parkinson's disease from essential tremor.
