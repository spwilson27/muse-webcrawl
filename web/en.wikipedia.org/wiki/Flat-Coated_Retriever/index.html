The Flat-Coated Retriever is a breed of gun dog from England, developed as a retriever of game both on land and in water. It is known for its glossy flat coat — black or liver in colour — its distinctive long, moulded head, and an exuberant, youthful temperament that often persists into old age, earning the breed the nickname "the Peter Pan of retrievers".

History

The breed was developed in the mid-19th century, drawing on St. John's water dogs imported from Newfoundland crossed with setter and collie types. It was the most popular retriever in Britain in the late Victorian era, widely used on shooting estates, before being overtaken in popularity by the Labrador Retriever and the Golden Retriever in the 20th century. Numbers fell sharply after the Second World War but were rebuilt by dedicated breeders.

Characteristics

Flat-Coats are medium-to-large, strongly built dogs with feathering on the legs, chest and tail. They are intelligent, eager and birdy workers, prized for game-finding ability and a soft mouth. The breed is slower to mature than other retrievers and retains a playful disposition.

Health

The Flat-Coated Retriever has a relatively high incidence of certain cancers, including histiocytic sarcoma and haemangiosarcoma, which significantly shorten its average lifespan compared with similar breeds; breed health surveys consistently identify cancer as the leading cause of death. Hip dysplasia and patellar luxation also occur.
