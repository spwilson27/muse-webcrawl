Shell money

Shell money is a form of currency made from seashells that was used as a medium of exchange in many parts of the world, including the Pacific Islands, parts of Asia and Africa, and pre-colonial North America. Durable, portable, difficult to counterfeit and inherently valued as ornament, shells served monetary functions for centuries and sometimes still do in ceremonial exchange.

Major forms

Cowrie shells (Monetaria moneta), harvested mainly in the Maldives, circulated across Africa and Asia for over a thousand years and were a major currency in West Africa and Bengal. In North America, Native peoples of the East Coast made wampum — polished purple and white beads from quahog and whelk shells — which Dutch and English colonists adopted as legal tender in the 17th century. In Melanesia, graded shell valuables include the Tolai tambu (tabu) of New Britain, still used in bride price and ritual payments in Papua New Guinea, and the shell "money necklaces" and arm rings of the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu. In Yap, the famous stone money complemented shell valuables.

Decline and survival

Colonial imports flooded markets — Europeans shipped cowries by the ton to West Africa, causing inflation — and colonial states eventually suppressed shell currencies in favour of coinage. Yet shell money persists in customary exchange, compensation and ceremonial contexts across the Pacific, where its value is social as much as economic.
