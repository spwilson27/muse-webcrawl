Spindle checkpoint

The spindle checkpoint, also known as the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC), is a cell-cycle surveillance mechanism that prevents a cell from progressing from metaphase to anaphase during mitosis until every chromosome is properly attached to the mitotic spindle. It ensures that sister chromatids are separated equally between the two daughter cells, guarding against aneuploidy.

Mechanism

Unattached kinetochores — the protein complexes assembled on each chromosome's centromere — catalyse the production of a diffusible inhibitory signal, the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC). The MCC, containing the proteins Mad2, BubR1 (Mad3 in yeast), Bub3, and Cdc20, binds and inhibits the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C). While the APC/C is inhibited, its substrates securin and cyclin B are not degraded: separase remains inactive, cohesin between sister chromatids stays intact, and the cell remains in metaphase.

Once the last kinetochore achieves stable bipolar (amphitelic) attachment to spindle microtubules, checkpoint signalling ceases, the MCC disassembles, and active APC/CCdc20 triggers securin and cyclin B destruction, initiating anaphase and mitotic exit.

Key components

Core checkpoint proteins include the kinases Mps1 and Bub1, the scaffold KNL1, and the Mad1–Mad2 complex at unattached kinetochores. Attachment and tension are monitored with help from the chromosomal passenger complex containing Aurora B kinase, which destabilises incorrect microtubule attachments so the checkpoint remains active until errors are corrected.

Medical relevance

Weakened checkpoint function can cause chromosome missegregation and aneuploidy, features common in cancer cells; conversely, many anti-mitotic chemotherapy drugs, such as taxanes and vinca alkaloids, act by keeping the checkpoint persistently activated, driving mitotic arrest and cell death.
