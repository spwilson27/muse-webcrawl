Führerprinzip

The Führerprinzip (German for "leader principle") was the basis of executive authority in Nazi Germany. It held that the word of the Führer, Adolf Hitler, stood above all written law, and that government policy, decisions and offices should work toward the realisation of his will.

Meaning

The principle demanded absolute obedience: each subordinate was responsible only to the leader immediately above them and owed unconditional obedience upward, while exercising authority downward. It rejected collective decision-making, majority voting and legal constraint as weaknesses of liberal democracy.

Application

After 1933 the principle was extended beyond the Nazi Party into the state, the armed forces (whose members swore a personal oath to Hitler from 1934), industry, education and other organisations, each headed by a "leader" answerable upward. It underpinned the regime's structure and was invoked to legitimise actions including the Night of the Long Knives.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the Führerprinzip figured in debates over superior orders and individual responsibility; the tribunal rejected obedience to criminal orders as a complete defence.
