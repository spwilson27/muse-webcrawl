The Restoration Movement (also known as the Stone–Campbell Movement, and historically as the Campbellites) is a Christian movement that began on the United States frontier during the Second Great Awakening (1790s–1840s). It sought to restore the church to the beliefs and practices of the New Testament, abandoning denominational creeds and structures in favour of congregational autonomy under the Bible alone, summarised in slogans such as "No creed but Christ, no book but the Bible."

History

Two movements merged in 1832: the "Christians" led by Barton W. Stone, which emerged from the Cane Ridge Revival (1801) in Kentucky, and the "Disciples of Christ" associated with Thomas Campbell and his son Alexander Campbell, whose Declaration and Address (1809) called for Christian unity based on Scripture. Walter Scott's evangelism on the Western Reserve added a simple plan of salvation — the "five-finger exercise" of faith, repentance, baptism, remission of sins, and the gift of the Holy Spirit.

Divisions

Disagreements over missionary societies, instrumental music in worship, and theological liberalism produced two major separations. In 1906 the Churches of Christ were recognised as a distinct body (non-instrumental, congregational), and by 1968 the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) had formally organised as a denomination, while independent Christian churches and churches of Christ maintained the congregational ideal.

Principles

  • Unity of all Christians in one body.
  • The Bible as the only rule of faith and practice.
  • Believer's baptism by immersion for the remission of sins.
  • Weekly observance of the Lord's Supper.
  • Local congregational autonomy without a denominational hierarchy.
