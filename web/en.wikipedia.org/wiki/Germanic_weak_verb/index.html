Germanic weak verb

TypeVerb conjugation class
Marking featureDental suffix in the past tense
Contrasts withGermanic strong verb

In Germanic linguistics, a weak verb is a verb whose past tense and past participle are formed with a dental suffix — in English, the -ed ending (as in love, loved) — rather than by changing the stem vowel (ablaut), as strong verbs do (sing, sang, sung). Weak verbs form the great majority of verbs in modern Germanic languages, and nearly all newly coined verbs join the weak class.

Origins

The dental preterite is an innovation of Proto-Germanic, not inherited from Proto-Indo-European. Its origin is debated: one influential theory derives the suffix from a periphrastic construction with the verb *dōną "to do", so that a form like "love-did" contracted into "loved". Weak verbs already existed in the earliest attested Germanic languages, including Gothic.

Classes

Proto-Germanic had several weak classes distinguished by their present-tense stem formation, conventionally numbered I–IV: class I verbs in *-janą (often causatives, with umlaut), class II in *-ōną, class III stative verbs in *-janą/-aną, and the small class IV in *-naną (inchoatives). In the modern languages these classes have largely merged; in English the distinction survives chiefly in irregularities such as think/thought and teach/taught, where an old class I weak verb shows vowel change caused by historical umlaut.

In modern languages

English regular verbs, German verbs ending in -te in the past tense, and their counterparts in Dutch and the Scandinavian languages all continue the weak conjugation. Because the pattern is fully productive, it is the default for borrowings and neologisms in all Germanic languages.
