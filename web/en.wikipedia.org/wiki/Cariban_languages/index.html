Cariban languages

The Cariban languages are a family of languages indigenous to northeastern South America. They are spoken by the Carib peoples and related groups in Venezuela, the Guianas, Brazil, Colombia, and formerly the Lesser Antilles.

Distribution and classification

The family comprises several dozen known languages, many now extinct or critically endangered. Among the better-documented members are Carib (Kalinago/Kariña) proper, Macushi, Pemon, Waiwai, Ye'kuana (Makiritare), Panare, and Yukpa. Linguists generally regard Cariban as a well-established family, though proposals linking it to the Tupian and Arawakan families in a wider "Je–Tupi–Carib" stock remain unproven.

Features

Cariban languages are notable for their complex systems of person marking, in which the choice of prefix depends on a hierarchy of speech-act participants, and for their extensive use of nominalisation and adverbial derivation. Many are ergative in at least part of their grammar.

Status

Most Cariban languages have small speaker populations and are endangered, though some, such as Pemon and Macushi, retain thousands of speakers and are used in bilingual education in Venezuela and Brazil.
