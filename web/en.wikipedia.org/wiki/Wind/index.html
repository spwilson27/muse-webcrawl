Wind is the natural movement of air or other gases relative to a planet's surface. Winds occur on a range of scales, from thunderstorm flows lasting tens of minutes, to local breezes generated by heating of land surfaces lasting a few hours, to global winds resulting from the difference in absorption of solar energy between the climate zones on Earth. The two main causes of large-scale atmospheric circulation are the differential heating between the equator and the poles, and the rotation of the planet (Coriolis effect).

Causes

Wind is caused by differences in atmospheric pressure: air flows from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure, and the greater the difference, the faster the flow. On a rotating planet the flow is deflected by the Coriolis effect—to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere—so that large-scale winds blow roughly parallel to isobars (the geostrophic wind). Near the surface, friction slows the wind and allows it to cross isobars toward low pressure.

Types of wind

Persistent global wind systems include the trade winds of the tropics, the westerlies of the mid-latitudes, and the polar easterlies. Seasonal winds include monsoons, driven by the seasonal reversal of pressure over land and sea. Local winds have traditional names in many regions: the mistral of southern France, the sirocco of the Mediterranean, the foehn of the Alps, the chinook of the Rocky Mountains, and land and sea breezes along coasts. Severe winds are features of tropical cyclones, tornadoes, and thunderstorms; a tornado is a violently rotating column of air, while a downburst is a powerful downdraught spreading outward at the surface.

Measurement

Wind direction is reported by the direction from which it originates—for example, a northerly wind blows from the north to the south. Wind speed is measured with an anemometer and classified on scales such as the Beaufort scale, which relates speed to observed conditions at sea and on land, ranging from 0 (calm) to 12 (hurricane force). The fastest wind gust ever recorded was 408 km/h (253 mph) on Barrow Island, Australia, during Tropical Cyclone Olivia in 1996.

Effects and uses

Wind shapes landscapes through erosion and deposition, disperses seeds, spores and pollutants, drives ocean surface currents and waves, and moderates climate. Humans have used wind for sailing, milling grain, pumping water, and—most importantly today—generating electricity with wind turbines. Wind can also be destructive: structural damage, wildfire spread, and aviation hazards are all associated with strong winds and wind shear.
