Stille reaction

The Stille reaction is a chemical reaction widely used in synthetic organic chemistry, involving the coupling of an organotin compound (an organostannane) with a variety of organic electrophiles via palladium-catalysed coupling. It is named after John Kenneth Stille, who developed it with David Milstein in the late 1970s.

In the general scheme, an organic group R1 bonded to tin (as R1SnR3) is coupled with an organic electrophile R2–X, where X is typically a halide or pseudohalide such as a triflate. The catalytic cycle proceeds through oxidative addition of the electrophile to palladium(0), transmetalation from tin to palladium, and reductive elimination to form the new carbon–carbon bond.

The reaction is valued for its mild conditions, tolerance of many functional groups, and the stability and ease of handling of organostannanes, which are air- and moisture-stable. Its principal drawback is the toxicity of the tin reagents and the difficulty of removing tin by-products. The Stille reaction has been extensively used in the total synthesis of complex natural products.
