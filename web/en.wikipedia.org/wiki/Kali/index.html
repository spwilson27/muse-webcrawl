Kali (Sanskrit: काली, Kālī), also called Kalika, is a major Hindu goddess associated with time (kala), death, destruction and ultimate power. She is a fierce form of the supreme goddess Mahadevi, and in many traditions is regarded as identical with, or an aspect of, the warrior goddess Durga and of Parvati, the consort of Shiva. Kali is the foremost of the ten Mahavidyas, the wisdom goddesses of Tantra.

Iconography

Kali is typically depicted with blue or black skin, a gaunt face with a protruding red tongue, and four or more arms. She wears a garland of severed heads (mundamala) and a skirt of severed arms, and stands with one foot upon the chest of her consort Shiva, who lies beneath her. In her hands she carries a sword, a trident, a severed head and a bowl that catches the blood. Her wild, unbound hair and her cremation-ground setting emphasise her role as the power that dissolves all forms back into time.

Mythology

In the Devi Mahatmyam, Kali springs from the furrowed brow of Durga during battle against the demons Shumbha and Nishumbha, and destroys the demon armies, drinking the blood of the self-replicating demon Raktabija before any drop can fall to earth and spawn a copy. In a famous later episode, the uncontrollable Kali, trampling the battlefield after victory, is calmed only when Shiva lies down in her path; seeing her consort beneath her foot, she sticks out her tongue in shame and her rage subsides — the moment captured in her standard iconography.

Worship

Kali is especially venerated in Bengal, Assam, Nepal and parts of southern India. Her principal festival, Kali Puja, is celebrated on the new-moon night of the Hindu month of Kartik, coinciding with Diwali. The Dakshineswar Kali Temple near Kolkata is closely associated with the 19th-century mystic Ramakrishna, for whom Kali was the Divine Mother; his disciple Swami Vivekananda also wrote devotional poetry to her. In Tantric practice, Kali is approached as the supreme reality beyond form, and her worship forms the core of the Shakta tradition of eastern India.

In modern culture

Kali's fierce image has made her a recurring figure in art, literature and popular culture worldwide, sometimes misunderstood as a symbol of evil; in her own tradition she is both destroyer of demons and a compassionate mother who liberates devotees from the fear of death. Her name and imagery also gave rise to the (largely legendary) Thuggee cult in colonial-era accounts.
