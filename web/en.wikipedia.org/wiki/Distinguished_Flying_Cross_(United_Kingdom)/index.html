The Distinguished Flying Cross (DFC) is the third-level military decoration awarded to officers, and since 1993 to other ranks, of the United Kingdom's Royal Air Force and other services, and formerly to officers of other Commonwealth countries, for "an act or acts of valour, courage or devotion to duty whilst flying in active operations against the enemy".

History

The award was established on 3 June 1918, shortly after the formation of the Royal Air Force, by Royal Warrant published in the London Gazette. It was initially awarded to commissioned and warrant officers of the RAF. During the Second World War, it was also awarded to officers of the Royal Artillery serving on attachment to the RAF as pilots-cum-artillery observers. Since the 1993 review of the honours system, as part of the drive to remove distinctions of rank in gallantry awards, all ranks of all arms of the Armed Forces have been eligible, and the Distinguished Flying Medal, previously awarded to other ranks, has been discontinued.

Description

The cross is a silver cross flory, 2⅛ inches (54 mm) wide. The obverse bears aeroplane propellers superimposed on the arms, a central roundel depicting Hermes on a hawks' outstretched wings, and the Royal Cypher within a laurel wreath. The ribbon is 1¼ inches (32 mm) wide, originally of violet and white alternate horizontal stripes, changed in 1919 to alternating diagonal stripes. Bars are awarded for subsequent acts meeting the criteria, and recipients are entitled to the post-nominal letters DFC.

Recipients

Well over 20,000 DFCs have been awarded, the majority during the Second World War to aircrew of Bomber Command, Fighter Command, and Coastal Command. Notable recipients include Guy Gibson, leader of the Dambusters raid; Douglas Bader, the legless fighter ace; and Leonard Cheshire, who also received the Victoria Cross. Posthumous awards were not permitted until policy changes late in the twentieth century.
