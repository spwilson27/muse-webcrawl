Spitalfields

LocationLondon Borough of Tower Hamlets, East End of London, England
Known forSpitalfields Market, Huguenot silk weaving, successive waves of immigration, Christ Church Spitalfields

Spitalfields is a district in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets in the East End of London, near Liverpool Street station and Brick Lane. The area takes its name from St Mary's Hospital and Priory ("the spital"), founded in 1197; the fields adjoining it became known as Spitalfields. For centuries the district has been a landing place for successive waves of immigrants, each reshaping its trades, languages and places of worship.

In the late 17th century, Huguenot refugees fleeing persecution in France settled in Spitalfields and established a celebrated silk-weaving industry; their elegant early-Georgian houses, with large weavers' windows in the attic storeys, survive around streets such as Fournier Street. As the silk trade declined, the area became synonymous with poverty in the Victorian era—the novelist Jack London described its slums in The People of the Abyss (1903), and the district was part of the hunting ground of Jack the Ripper in 1888. Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe dominated the local garment trade from the 1880s, and since the 1970s the area has been home to a large Bangladeshi community centred on Brick Lane.

Landmarks

Old Spitalfields Market, established by charter in the 17th century and rebuilt in the Victorian era, is now a major retail and food market. Christ Church, Spitalfields, designed by Nicholas Hawksmoor and completed in 1729, is one of the masterpieces of English Baroque architecture. Dennis Severs' House on Folgate Street is a well-known "still-life drama" museum of Huguenot family life. The area's proximity to the City of London has driven rapid gentrification since the 1990s, bringing artists, galleries and technology firms while sparking continuing debate about the displacement of older communities.
