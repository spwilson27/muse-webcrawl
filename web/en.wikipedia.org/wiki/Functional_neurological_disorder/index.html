Functional neurological disorder

Functional neurological disorder
Other namesFunctional neurological symptom disorder; conversion disorder (DSM-5)

Functional neurological disorder (FND), also called functional neurological symptom disorder, is a condition in which patients experience neurological symptoms — such as weakness, movement problems, sensory symptoms and attacks resembling epileptic seizures — that arise from a problem with the functioning of the nervous system rather than from structural disease of it. In the DSM-5 it is classified under somatic symptom and related disorders and remains associated with the older term conversion disorder.

Signs and symptoms

Common presentations include functional weakness (for example of a limb), functional movement disorders (tremor, dystonia, gait disturbance), functional (dissociative / non-epileptic) seizures, and functional sensory loss. Symptoms are real and often disabling, and are not feigned. They may occur together with recognised neurological diseases such as multiple sclerosis or epilepsy.

Diagnosis

FND is a "rule-in" diagnosis made on positive clinical features rather than merely by exclusion of other disease. Examples include Hoover's sign (involuntary extension of a "weak" leg when the opposite hip is flexed against resistance), the hip abductor sign, midline splitting of sensory loss, and tremor that entrains to or is suppressed by voluntary movement of another limb. Investigations such as MRI are typically normal, though normal investigations alone are not the basis of the diagnosis.

Mechanism

The mechanisms of FND are incompletely understood. Current models emphasise abnormal attention to the body, altered sense of agency, and abnormal predictive processing in brain networks, often triggered by physical events such as injury, illness or panic, and sometimes — but not always — by psychological stress. Historical theories attributed symptoms to conversion of psychological conflict into physical form; modern practice treats FND as a disorder at the interface of neurology and psychiatry in which such a stressor need not be present.

Treatment

A clear, non-dismissive explanation of the diagnosis is itself a key first step in treatment. Evidence supports specialist physiotherapy for functional motor symptoms (aimed at retraining movement with diverted attention), psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Prognosis is better with early diagnosis and acceptance of the explanation.

Epidemiology and history

FND is common: it accounts for a substantial minority of neurology outpatient referrals — studies suggest around 5–16% of new neurology consultations — and functional seizures make up roughly a fifth to a third of admissions to epilepsy monitoring units. Symptoms resembling FND have been described since antiquity and were central to nineteenth-century neurology under the label "hysteria", a term now abandoned.
