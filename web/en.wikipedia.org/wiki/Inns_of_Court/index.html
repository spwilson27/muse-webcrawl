Inns of Court

The Inns of Court in London are the professional associations for barristers in England and Wales. There are four inns: Lincoln's Inn, Inner Temple, Middle Temple, and Gray's Inn. All barristers must belong to one of them, and the inns alone retain the power to call a student to the Bar. Their historic precincts — courtyards, halls, gardens and libraries between the City of London and Westminster — date in part to the medieval period.

[Image: the gardens and hall of Middle Temple in London]

History

The inns emerged in the fourteenth century as hostels (hospicia) where law students and practitioners lodged near the royal courts at Westminster. Together with the smaller Inns of Chancery, they formed a collegiate system of legal education sometimes described as England's "third university". Students learned by attending court, participating in moots and readings, and dining in hall. The inns survived the Civil War and the decline of the old learning exercises, evolving into professional bodies providing education, scholarships, dining, and chambers.

Modern role

Each inn is governed by its senior members, the Masters of the Bench ("benchers"). Today the inns provide qualifying sessions and advocacy training for Bar students, scholarships, libraries, and collegiate facilities; formal regulation of the profession rests with the Bar Standards Board and the Bar Council. Barristers traditionally take chambers within or near their inn. The Temple area — shared by Inner and Middle Temple — is also home to the Temple Church, built by the Knights Templar in the twelfth century.
