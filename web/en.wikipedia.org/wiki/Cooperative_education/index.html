Cooperative education

Cooperative education (or co-op education) is a structured method of combining classroom-based education with practical work experience, providing academic credit for structured job experience. Cooperative education is taking on a new importance in helping young people to make the school-to-work transition. It can also be contrasted with internships, which are typically shorter, sometimes unpaid, and not always integrated into the academic programme.

History

The co-op model is commonly credited to Herman Schneider, dean of the College of Engineering at the University of Cincinnati, who founded the first cooperative education programme in 1906. The idea spread to other institutions, including Northeastern University and Antioch College, and later internationally. In Canada, the University of Waterloo developed one of the world's largest co-op programmes, and the model is widely used in Germany's dual-education system in related forms.

Models

Common models include the alternating model, in which students alternate terms of full-time study with terms of full-time employment, and the parallel model, in which students work part-time while studying. Placements are generally paid and related to the student's field, and universities typically vet employers and monitor student progress on the job.

Benefits and criticism

Supporters argue that co-op students graduate with professional experience, networks, and lower debt, and often receive job offers from their co-op employers. Critics note that alternating programmes extend the time to graduation and that quality of placements varies.
