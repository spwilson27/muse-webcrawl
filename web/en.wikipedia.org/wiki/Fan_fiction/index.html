Fan fiction or fanfiction (also abbreviated to fan fic, fanfic, fic, or ff) is fictional writing written in an amateur capacity by fans, unauthorised by, but based on an existing work of fiction. The author uses copyrighted characters, settings, or other intellectual properties from the original creator(s) as a basis for their writing. Fan fiction ranges from a couple of sentences to an entire novel, and fans can retain the creator's characters and settings, add their own, or both.

History

Before the modern fandom era, sequels and retellings by other hands were common throughout literary history. Organised fan fiction began in science fiction fandom, with fanzines publishing amateur stories from the 1930s. Media fandom took off with Star Trek: the fanzine Spockanalia (1967) is widely credited as the first media fanzine to publish fan fiction. Through the 1970s and 1980s, stories circulated in print zines, often through mailing lists; the internet transformed distribution, with Usenet groups, mailing lists, FanFiction.Net (founded 1998), LiveJournal communities and, from 2009, the Archive of Our Own (AO3), run by the nonprofit Organization for Transformative Works.

Genres and terms

  • Canon / fanon / headcanon — the original material, widely accepted fan inventions, and an individual fan's personal take.
  • AU (alternate universe) — settings that diverge from canon.
  • Crossover — combining two or more fictional worlds.
  • Shipping — stories centred on romantic pairings of characters; "slash" denotes same-sex pairings.
  • Mary Sue — an idealised self-insert character (term coined in a 1973 Star Trek parody).
  • Beta reader — a volunteer editor within the community.

Legality

Fan fiction occupies a contested legal position. Rights holders vary from tolerance to active opposition; some authors (such as Anne Rice) historically forbade it, while others encourage it. Defences generally rest on fair use (US) as non-commercial transformative work. Amazon's Kindle Worlds (2013–2018) briefly offered a licensed commercial route. The Organization for Transformative Works advocates for the legality of fanworks.

Notable examples

Some fan fiction has crossed into commercial publishing after "filing off the serial numbers": E. L. James's Fifty Shades of Grey began as Twilight fan fiction, and Anna Todd's After series began as One Direction fan fiction on Wattpad. In 2019 AO3 won the Hugo Award for Best Related Work.
