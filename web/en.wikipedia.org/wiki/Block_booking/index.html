Block booking

Block booking
IndustryFilm distribution
Practice ended (US)1948, United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc.

Block booking was a system of selling multiple films to a theatre as a unit, or "block". The customer — the theatre — was forced to take a bundle of films, including inferior pictures, in order to obtain a few desirable ones. The practice was widespread in the American film industry during the studio era of the 1930s and 1940s.

How it worked

Under the studio system, the major studios sold their annual output in advance, often before films were even completed ("blind bidding"). Independent exhibitors had to accept entire packages of A-pictures, B-movies, shorts and newsreels, which guaranteed distribution for the studios' weaker products and squeezed independent producers out of screens.

Legal challenge

The United States Department of Justice pursued the studios under antitrust law. In United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. (1948), the Supreme Court ruled against the majors, and the resulting Paramount Decrees banned block booking and blind bidding and forced the studios to divest their theatre chains.

Legacy

The decrees reshaped Hollywood economics, weakening the vertically integrated studio system. Analogous bundling practices have been debated in television syndication and in modern streaming and licensing markets.
