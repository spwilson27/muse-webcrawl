Cross-contamination

Cross-contamination is the process by which bacteria or other microorganisms are unintentionally transferred from one substance or object to another, with harmful effect. The term is used mainly in food safety, but also applies in medicine, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics and laboratory work.

In food handling

In food preparation, cross-contamination occurs when pathogens such as Salmonella, Campylobacter or E. coli pass from raw foods — especially raw meat, poultry and seafood — to ready-to-eat foods via hands, knives, cutting boards, cloths or work surfaces. It is a major cause of foodborne illness. Prevention measures include separating raw and cooked foods in storage and preparation, using different or colour-coded chopping boards, washing hands and surfaces thoroughly, and keeping raw meat on the lowest shelf of the refrigerator so it cannot drip onto other foods.

Cross-contact is the related transfer of food allergens from an allergen-containing food to another food, of concern for people with food allergies.

In other settings

In laboratories and pharmaceutical manufacturing, cross-contamination between samples or product batches can invalidate experiments or endanger patients, and is controlled through cleanrooms, validated cleaning and segregated equipment. In forensic science, transfer of trace evidence or DNA between exhibits must be avoided to preserve the integrity of evidence.
