Parasitoid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In evolutionary ecology, a parasitoid is an organism that lives in close association with its host at the host's expense, eventually resulting in the death of the host. Parasitoidism is one of six major evolutionary strategies within parasitism, distinguished by the fact that the parasitoid's development culminates in the host's death, foreshadowing the strategy's evolutionary link to predation.

Among parasitoids, strategies range from living inside the host (endoparasitism), allowing it to continue growing before emerging as an adult, to paralysing the host and living externally on it (ectoparasitism). Hosts include other insects, arachnids, and, in a few cases, other invertebrates. The great majority of parasitoids are wasps (Hymenoptera); other groups include flies (Diptera, especially Tachinidae), some beetles and moths, and parasitoid fungi and worms.

Terminology and strategies

The term "parasitoid" was coined in 1913 by the Swedo-Finnish writer and entomologist Odo Reuter and adopted in English by William Morton Wheeler. Parasitoids may be koinobionts, which allow the host to continue feeding and developing, or idiobionts, which paralyse or kill the host at oviposition. Some parasitoids alter host behaviour to protect their developing offspring — the basis of popular comparisons to "zombie" hosts.

Biological control

Parasitoids are of great importance in biological pest control. Species of wasps such as Encarsia formosa (against greenhouse whitefly) and Trichogramma species (against moth pests) are mass-reared and released commercially. Because many parasitoids are highly host-specific, they are favoured where chemical pesticides are undesirable, though host specificity must be evaluated carefully before introductions.

Interactions with viruses

Many parasitoid wasps carry polydnaviruses integrated into their genomes. When the wasp lays eggs in a host, the virus is injected and suppresses the host's immune system, allowing the parasitoid larva to develop. This mutualism is one of the most striking examples of virus–host co-evolution.

In culture

Parasitoids have influenced science fiction and horror; the life cycle of the fictional xenomorph in the Alien films was inspired by parasitoid wasps. Charles Darwin famously remarked, in a letter to Asa Gray, that he could not persuade himself that a beneficent God would have designed ichneumon wasps with the express intention of feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars.
