The Mongolian Cyrillic alphabet (Mongolian: Монгол Кирилл үсэг) is the writing system used for the standard variety of the Mongolian language in the modern state of Mongolia. It consists of the 33 letters of the Russian Cyrillic alphabet together with two additional letters, Өө and Үү, representing the front rounded vowels /ö/ and /ü/ that Russian lacks.

History

Mongolia historically wrote its language in the vertical classical Mongolian script. During the 1930s, under Soviet influence, a Latin-based alphabet was briefly promoted, and in 1941 the government adopted Cyrillic; after a short reversal, Cyrillic was reinstated and became universal in education and publishing from 1946 onward. It is based principally on the Khalkha dialect, and its spelling reflects Khalkha pronunciation more closely than the classical script does.

Following the democratic revolution of 1990, the classical script was revived as a symbol of national identity, and successive governments have adopted plans to restore it to official use alongside Cyrillic. A law adopted in 2015 and subsequent policy set a goal of using both scripts in official documents from 2025, though Cyrillic remains dominant in daily life.

Letters and sounds

The alphabet has 35 letters: А Б В Г Д Е Ё Ж З И Й К Л М Н О Ө П Р С Т У Ү Ф Х Ц Ч Ш Щ Ъ Ы Ь Э Ю Я. Some letters, such as ф, щ and ъ, occur almost only in loanwords, chiefly from Russian. Short and long vowels are distinguished by doubling, and vowel harmony shapes the choice of vowel letters in suffixes.

Use outside Mongolia

Cyrillic-based writing for Mongolic languages is also used in Russia for the Buryat and Kalmyk languages, with small differences in their alphabets. In China's Inner Mongolia, by contrast, the classical vertical script remains the standard.
