Askia Muhammad I

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Tomb of Askia at Gao, a mud-brick pyramid tomb in the Sudano-Sahelian style]

Askia Muhammad I (c. 1443 – 1538), born Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr Ture, was the founder of the Askia dynasty and the greatest ruler of the Songhai Empire of West Africa. A general under the Sunni dynasty, he seized the throne in 1493 from Sunni Ali's son (a traditionalist he accused of neglecting Islam), then ruled thirty-five years of expansion and organisation: conquering Mali's remnants and the Hausa states, professionalising the army and bureaucracy with ministries and provincial governors, standardising weights and market inspection, and making the pilgrimage to Mecca (1496–97) that won him recognition as caliph of the Sudan from the Abbasid shadow-caliph in Cairo. His deposition by his son Askia Musa (1528) began the dynasty's unravelling toward the Moroccan invasion of 1591.

The empire organised

Askia Muhammad inherited Sunni Ali's conquests and gave them a state: a standing core of cavalry and the Niger river fleet, slave and free officials checked by royal inspectors, encouraged agriculture and trade, and lavish patronage of Timbuktu's scholars (the Sankore circle flourished; he imported the jurist al-Maghili's treatise on government). His hajj — with 1,500 followers and gold spent so freely in Cairo and Mecca as to depress its price there, a deliberate echo of Mansa Musa — proclaimed Songhai the Sudan's leading Muslim power.

Fall

Blind and ageing, Askia was deposed in 1528 by his son Musa, exiled to a Niger island, recalled briefly amid the coups of the 1530s, and died in 1538 — his tomb at Gao, the great mud-brick pyramidal Askia Tomb (a UNESCO site), anchoring the dynasty's memory. The chronicles (the Tarikh al-Sudan and Tarikh al-Fattash, written a century later) preserve him as the model just Muslim king against which his successors all failed.
