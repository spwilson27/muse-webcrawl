A Fischer projection of a tetrahedral carbon centre

A Fischer projection is a two-dimensional representation of a three-dimensional organic molecule by projection, used chiefly for depicting stereochemistry at tetrahedral carbon atoms, especially in sugars and amino acids. It was devised by the German chemist Emil Fischer in 1891.

Conventions

In a Fischer projection, the carbon chain is drawn vertically with the most oxidised group at the top. Horizontal bonds are understood to project out of the plane toward the viewer, while vertical bonds recede behind the plane. The central carbon atom is often omitted, the crossing of the horizontal and vertical lines implying its position.

Manipulations

Certain operations preserve the configuration: a rotation of 180° in the plane of the paper, or cyclic permutation of three groups. A rotation of 90°, or swapping any two groups, inverts the depicted configuration and gives the enantiomer. Fischer projections are related to D/L notation: for sugars, a hydroxyl group on the right of the highest-numbered stereocentre in the standard projection denotes the D configuration.

Related representations

Fischer projections complement Haworth projections (used for cyclic sugars), wedge-dash drawings, and Newman projections. Fischer received the 1902 Nobel Prize in Chemistry in part for his work on sugar stereochemistry.
