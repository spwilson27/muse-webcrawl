The Book of Isaiah (Hebrew: סֵפֶר יְשַׁעְיָהוּ, Sefer Yeshayahu) is the first of the Latter Prophets in the Hebrew Bible and the first of the Major Prophets in the Christian Old Testament. It is identified by a superscription as the words of the 8th-century BCE prophet Isaiah ben Amoz, but there is evidence that much of it was composed during and after the Babylonian exile. The book can be divided into three main sections: chapters 1–39 (Proto-Isaiah), containing the words of the historical Isaiah and warning of judgment; chapters 40–55 (Deutero-Isaiah), written by an anonymous author during the exile, offering consolation and the promise of restoration; and chapters 56–66 (Trito-Isaiah), composed after the return to Jerusalem.

The book's central themes include the holiness of God, the coming judgment on Judah and the nations, the promise of a remnant that will survive, and the hope of a future messianic age of peace. Its passages, such as the "suffering servant" songs and the vision of swords beaten into plowshares, have been profoundly influential in both Judaism and Christianity.

Composition and authorship

Tradition ascribes the whole book to Isaiah, son of Amoz, who prophesied in Jerusalem during the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah (roughly 740–700 BCE). Modern scholars, noting abrupt shifts in historical setting, style, and theology, generally attribute only parts of chapters 1–39 to the historical Isaiah. Chapters 40–55 presuppose the Babylonian exile of the 6th century BCE and address an audience for whom Cyrus the Great of Persia is a contemporary figure. Chapters 56–66 reflect conditions in Judah after the return from exile, including disputes over the rebuilt Temple.

The Great Isaiah Scroll, found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran and dated to about the 2nd century BCE, contains nearly the complete text of the book and is among the oldest surviving manuscripts of any biblical book.

Structure

Proto-Isaiah (chapters 1–39)

The first major section contains oracles against Judah and Jerusalem (chapters 1–12), oracles against the foreign nations (13–23), an "apocalypse" section (24–27), further oracles (28–35), and a historical narrative about Hezekiah and the Assyrian invasion under Sennacherib (36–39). Famous passages include Isaiah's vision of God in the Temple (chapter 6), the prophecy of Immanuel (7:14), and the oracle of the Prince of Peace (9:6).

Deutero-Isaiah (chapters 40–55)

Beginning with the words "Comfort, comfort my people," this section proclaims that the exile is ending and that God will lead Israel home through the desert. It contains the four Servant Songs (42:1–9; 49:1–12; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12), describing a figure whose suffering brings healing to others, and sustained arguments for monotheism against Babylonian idolatry.

Trito-Isaiah (chapters 56–66)

The final section addresses the restored community in Jerusalem, emphasizing justice, Sabbath observance, and inclusion of foreigners, and closes with visions of a new heaven and a new earth (65:17; 66:22).

Themes

Recurring themes include God's holiness and sovereignty over history; judgment arising from injustice and idolatry; the survival of a faithful remnant; the Davidic promise and messianic expectation; Zion theology; and universalism — the vision that all nations will one day stream to Jerusalem to learn God's ways (2:2–4). The book also develops the idea of God as Creator of the universe and Redeemer of Israel.

Influence

Isaiah is quoted or alluded to more frequently in the New Testament than any other prophetic book; Christian tradition reads the Servant Songs and the Immanuel prophecy in relation to Jesus. In Jewish liturgy, many haftarot (prophetic readings) are drawn from Isaiah. Handel's Messiah sets numerous Isaianic texts, including "For unto us a child is born" and "Comfort ye my people."
