Proof coinage

Proof coinage refers to early samples of a coin issue, historically made for checking the dies and for archival purposes, and nowadays to specially manufactured collector coins struck to a mirror-like finish. Modern proofs are struck multiple times with polished dies on polished planchets, producing frosted designs against reflective fields.

History

Mints originally produced proofs as presentation and record pieces; the British Royal Mint and the United States Mint both made small numbers for officials and collectors from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the twentieth century, mints began selling proof sets — complete sets of a year's coinage in proof finish — directly to collectors, and proof versions of bullion coins became a major product.

Finishes

Related special finishes include reverse proofs (mirrored devices, frosted fields), enhanced uncirculated finishes, and cameo proofs, in which the contrast between frosted and mirrored surfaces is especially pronounced. Proofs are graded on the same 70-point scale as other coins, with grades such as PF-70 denoting perfection.
