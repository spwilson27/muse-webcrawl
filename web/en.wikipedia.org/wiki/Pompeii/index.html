Pompeii was an ancient Roman city near modern Naples in Campania, Italy, largely destroyed and buried under 4–6 metres of volcanic ash and pumice in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in AD 79.

History

Founded by Oscans and influenced successively by Greeks, Etruscans, Samnites and Romans, Pompeii became a prosperous Roman colony of perhaps 11,000–20,000 people, with a forum, amphitheatre (the oldest surviving stone amphitheatre), baths, theatres, markets, bakeries and richly painted villas. An earthquake in AD 62 badly damaged it shortly before the eruption.

Eruption and rediscovery

The eruption, described by Pliny the Younger, killed at least some thousands; the exact death toll is unknown. Burial preserved the city remarkably. Sporadic rediscovery began in the 16th century, systematic excavation from 1748 under the Bourbons, and Giuseppe Fiorelli's 19th-century methods — including plaster casts of victims' voids in the ash — made Pompeii world famous. Only about two-thirds of the city has been excavated.

Significance

Pompeii offers an unmatched snapshot of everyday Roman life — graffiti, election notices, food remains, frescoes — and is one of the world's most visited archaeological sites, though conservation against decay and mass tourism is a continuing challenge.
