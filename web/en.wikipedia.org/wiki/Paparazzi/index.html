Paparazzi (US: /ˌpɑːpəˈrɑːtsi/, UK: /ˌpæpəˈrætsi/) are independent photographers who take pictures of high-profile people — typically celebrities, politicians and their families — usually while subjects go about their usual lives. Paparazzi tend to be independent contractors, unaffiliated with mainstream media organisations, and photos taken are usually sold to news and gossip media. The word is plural; a single photographer is a paparazzo.

Etymology

The term comes from Paparazzo, the surname of a news photographer played by Walter Santesso in Federico Fellini's film La Dolce Vita (1960). Fellini took the name from an Italian dialect word for a buzzing, bothersome insect — likening the photographers swarming around celebrities to mosquitoes — and possibly from the surname in George Gissing's travel book By the Ionian Sea. The word passed into English and many other languages within a few years of the film's release.

Practice

Paparazzi work in competitive markets such as Los Angeles, New York and London, following celebrities to airports, restaurants and homes using tips, flight records and social-media posts. Exclusive or surprising images — a new relationship, a celebrity without makeup, a public confrontation — can command very high fees, while routine pictures may sell for little. Agencies distribute images to outlets worldwide, and in the smartphone era ordinary bystanders and social media compete with professionals.

Controversy and law

Paparazzi methods are frequently criticised as intrusive and dangerous; the death of Diana, Princess of Wales in a 1997 Paris car crash during a pursuit by photographers brought the issue to global prominence, though the official French investigation attributed the crash primarily to the intoxicated driver. Celebrities have fought photographers, destroyed equipment, and sued publications. Several jurisdictions have enacted anti-paparazzi laws: California has repeatedly strengthened statutes against reckless pursuit and invasion of privacy, including measures protecting celebrities' children, and France enforces strict privacy rights that limit publication of unauthorised images.
