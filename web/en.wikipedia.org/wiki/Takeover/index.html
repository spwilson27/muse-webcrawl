Takeover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In corporate finance, a takeover is the acquisition of one company (the target) by another (the acquirer or bidder), achieved by purchasing enough shares to gain control. Takeovers may be friendly, negotiated with the target's board, or hostile, pursued against the board's wishes by appealing directly to shareholders through a tender offer or proxy fight. The term usually refers to acquisitions of public companies; related transactions include mergers, buyouts and reverse takeovers.

Motives

Acquirers pursue takeovers for synergies (cost savings and revenue gains), market power, undervalued assets, diversification, tax advantages, or—in critics' accounts—management empire-building. Targets' shareholders typically receive a control premium above the pre-bid share price; a large empirical literature finds that target shareholders gain substantially in takeovers, while acquirer shareholders on average gain little.

Mechanisms and regulation

A bidder may buy shares in the open market (subject to disclosure thresholds), make a tender offer to all shareholders, or propose a merger for shareholder approval. In the United States, the Williams Act (1968) regulates tender offers and disclosure; in the United Kingdom, the City Code on Takeovers and Mergers, administered by the Takeover Panel, requires equal treatment of shareholders and mandatory bids once a stake passes 30%. Competition authorities may block or condition deals on antitrust grounds.

Defences

Target boards deploy defences including the poison pill (shareholder rights plan), staggered boards, golden parachutes, white knight counter-bidders, crown-jewel sales and litigation. Delaware case law (e.g. Unocal, Revlon) governs directors' duties in resisting bids and in selling the company. Defensive tactics remain controversial: they can extract higher prices for shareholders or entrench underperforming management.

Notable takeovers

Landmark deals include the leveraged buyout of RJR Nabisco (1989), Vodafone's hostile acquisition of Mannesmann (2000)—the largest takeover of its era—AOL's merger with Time Warner (2000), and Kraft's contested bid for Cadbury (2010), which prompted reforms to UK takeover rules.
