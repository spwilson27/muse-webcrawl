The Twenty-first Amendment (Amendment XXI) to the United States Constitution repealed the Eighteenth Amendment, which had mandated nationwide prohibition of alcohol. It was proposed by the 72nd Congress on 20 February 1933 and ratified by the requisite number of states on 5 December 1933. It is unique in being the only amendment to repeal a prior amendment, and the only one ratified by state ratifying conventions rather than state legislatures.

Text

Section 1. The eighteenth article of amendment to the Constitution of the United States is hereby repealed.
Section 2. The transportation or importation into any State, Territory, or possession of the United States for delivery or use therein of intoxicating liquors, in violation of the laws thereof, is hereby prohibited.
Section 3. This article shall be inoperative unless it shall have been ratified as an amendment to the Constitution by conventions in the several States, as provided in the Constitution, within seven years from the date of the submission hereof to the States by the Congress.

Background and ratification

By the early 1930s, Prohibition was widely blamed for organised crime, lost tax revenue during the Great Depression, and corruption. The Democratic platform of 1932 promised repeal. Congress deliberately chose ratification by state conventions — the first and only time this method was used — because convention delegates elected on a single issue were thought more likely to reflect popular wet sentiment than rural-dominated legislatures. Utah became the 36th and decisive state to ratify on 5 December 1933.

Effects

Section 2 gives states broad power to regulate alcohol within their borders, producing today's varied state and local liquor laws and the three-tier distribution system; the Supreme Court has nevertheless held that this power does not override other constitutional provisions, such as the dormant Commerce Clause (Granholm v. Heald, 2005) and the First Amendment.
