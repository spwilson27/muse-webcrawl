The Woodward–Hoffmann rules (or the pericyclic selection rules) are a set of rules devised by Robert Burns Woodward and Roald Hoffmann to rationalise or predict certain aspects of the stereochemistry and activation energies of pericyclic reactions, an important class of concerted organic reactions. The rules are best understood in terms of the concept of "conservation of orbital symmetry": a pericyclic reaction is symmetry-allowed when the number of (4q+2)s and (4r)a π-components is odd.

The Woodward–Hoffmann rules were first formulated in 1965 to explain the striking stereospecificity observed in electrocyclic reactions under thermal and photochemical control. Hoffmann was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1981 for this work (shared with Kenichi Fukui); Woodward, who had died in 1979, was not eligible, though he had already won the prize in 1965 for organic synthesis.

Statement of the rules

For a ground-state (thermal) pericyclic process, the reaction is symmetry-allowed if the total number of (4q+2)-electron suprafacial components plus (4r)-electron antarafacial components is odd. Under photochemical conditions, with one component promoted to an excited state, the rule is reversed: the reaction is allowed when that total is even.

In simpler terms for electrocyclic reactions: a system of 4n π-electrons reacts thermally in a conrotatory fashion and photochemically in a disrotatory fashion, whereas a system of (4n+2) π-electrons reacts thermally in a disrotatory fashion and photochemically in a conrotatory fashion.

Applications

Electrocyclic reactions

The rules explain why the thermal ring opening of cyclobutene gives only the (E,E)- or (E,Z)-butadiene products dictated by conrotation, while photolysis of 1,3,5-hexatriene derivatives follows the complementary stereochemistry. They were famously applied in Woodward's total synthesis of vitamin B12.

Cycloadditions

Thermal [4+2] cycloadditions such as the Diels–Alder reaction are allowed (suprafacial on both components), whereas thermal [2+2] cycloadditions are forbidden suprafacially and proceed instead photochemically or stepwise.

Sigmatropic rearrangements

The rules predict the suprafacial course of thermal [1,5]-hydrogen shifts and the behaviour of Cope and Claisen rearrangements, which proceed through chair-like transition states consistent with orbital symmetry conservation.

Derivation

The rules can be derived using correlation diagrams, which track the symmetry of molecular orbitals from reactants to products, or through frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis focusing on the HOMO and LUMO of the reacting components. Equivalent formulations were given by Fukui's frontier orbital theory and by the Dewar–Zimmerman analysis in terms of aromatic transition states.
