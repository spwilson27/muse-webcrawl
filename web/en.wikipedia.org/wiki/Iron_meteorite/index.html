Iron meteorite

Iron meteorites, also called siderites or ferrous meteorites, are a type of meteorite composed primarily of an iron–nickel alloy known as meteoric iron, usually consisting of two mineral phases: kamacite and taenite. Iron meteorites are derived from the planetary cores of differentiated planetesimals that were shattered by catastrophic impacts early in the history of the Solar System.

Although they make up only about 5–6% of observed meteorite falls, iron meteorites are disproportionately represented among large finds because they are dense, resistant to weathering, and easily recognised as unusual. Historically, meteoric iron was the earliest source of usable iron for humans, predating smelting.

Composition and structure

Most iron meteorites consist of iron–nickel alloys with nickel contents between about 5% and 25%, together with minor amounts of cobalt and accessory minerals such as schreibersite, troilite and graphite. When a polished surface is etched with dilute acid, many iron meteorites reveal an interlocking pattern of kamacite and taenite lamellae called the Widmanstätten pattern, which forms only through extremely slow cooling — on the order of degrees per million years — inside a parent body.

Classification

Iron meteorites are divided into structural classes based on their crystal structure: hexahedrites (almost pure kamacite), octahedrites (the most common, showing Widmanstätten patterns), and ataxites (nickel-rich, with no visible macrostructure). A chemical classification based on trace elements such as gallium, germanium and iridium groups irons into thirteen or more parent-body groups (IAB, IIAB, IIIAB, IVA and others), plus a large number of "ungrouped" specimens.

Notable specimens

The largest known iron meteorite is the Hoba meteorite in Namibia, weighing roughly 60 tonnes, which remains where it fell. Other famous irons include the Canyon Diablo fragments associated with Meteor Crater in Arizona, the Greenland Cape York meteorites (used for centuries by Inuit metalworkers), the Sikhote-Alin fall of 1947 in Siberia, and the Willamette meteorite of Oregon. The Campo del Cielo field in Argentina contains many large masses.

Cultural history

Before the Iron Age, meteoric iron was worked into beads, blades and ceremonial objects in Egypt, Mesopotamia and the Arctic. An iron dagger found in the tomb of Tutankhamun has been shown by its nickel content to have been made from meteoritic iron. The Inuit of Greenland cold-hammered fragments of the Cape York meteorite into tools for centuries.
