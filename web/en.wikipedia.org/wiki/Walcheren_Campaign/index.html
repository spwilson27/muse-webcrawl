Walcheren Campaign

Date: 30 July – 23 December 1809

Location: Walcheren and the Scheldt estuary, Netherlands

Belligerents: United Kingdom vs. French Empire and Kingdom of Holland

Result: British withdrawal; strategic failure

The Walcheren Campaign (30 July – 23 December 1809) was an unsuccessful British expedition during the Napoleonic Wars, the largest British expeditionary force of the era. Its objectives were to destroy the French fleet and dockyards at Antwerp and Flushing (Vlissingen), to divert French strength from Austria during the War of the Fifth Coalition, and to open the Scheldt estuary.

The expedition

Commanded on land by John Pitt, 2nd Earl of Chatham, and at sea by Sir Richard Strachan, the armada comprised some 39,000 troops and hundreds of warships and transports. The British landed on the island of Walcheren on 30 July 1809 and, after a heavy bombardment that devastated the town, captured Flushing on 15 August. By then, however, the opportunity to take Antwerp had been lost: the French under Marshal Bernadotte reinforced the city's defences, and the British advance up the Scheldt stalled. The strategic rationale also collapsed because Austria had been defeated at Wagram and had signed an armistice in July, before the landing.

"Walcheren fever"

Encamped in the marshy polders of Walcheren, the army was ravaged by an epidemic — dubbed "Walcheren fever" — a combination of malaria, typhoid, typhus and dysentery. By September thousands were sick; in total more than 4,000 British soldiers died, the overwhelming majority from disease, with only about 100 killed in action, and over 11,000 were still reported ill when the force was evacuated. The decision to evacuate was taken in September, though a garrison remained at Flushing until December.

Aftermath

The fiasco provoked a parliamentary inquiry in 1810 and a political scandal, including a duel-era feud between ministers and the exposure of the Mary Anne Clarke affair, which had already forced the Duke of York's resignation as commander-in-chief. Lord Chatham's reputation never recovered. The campaign is often cited as a classic case of disease defeating an army more decisively than the enemy.
