Electronic waste

Electronic waste (or e-waste) describes discarded electrical or electronic devices. Used electronics which are destined for refurbishment, reuse, resale, salvage recycling through material recovery, or disposal are also considered e-waste. The Global E-waste Monitor estimated that the world generated about 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, of which only around a fifth was documented as formally collected and recycled, and generation is rising by roughly 2.6 million tonnes per year.

Composition and hazards

E-waste contains valuable materials—including gold, silver, copper, aluminium, and rare-earth elements—alongside hazardous substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium, and brominated flame retardants. Informal processing in developing countries, including open burning of cables and acid leaching of circuit boards in places such as Agbogbloshie in Ghana and Guiyu in China, has caused severe local pollution and health harms to workers and nearby residents.

Management and regulation

International movement of hazardous e-waste is governed by the Basel Convention. The European Union's WEEE Directive makes producers responsible for financing collection and recycling, and the RoHS Directive restricts hazardous substances in new equipment. Many jurisdictions have adopted extended producer responsibility schemes, right-to-repair laws, and design-for-disassembly standards to lengthen product lifetimes and improve recovery rates.

Recycling

Formal recycling involves dismantling, shredding, and separating metals, glass, and plastics, with precious metals recovered by smelting or hydrometallurgical processes. "Urban mining" of e-waste can recover metals at grades far richer than natural ores, but much of the value is lost when devices are landfilled or processed informally.
