Electrode

An electrode is an electrical conductor used to make contact with a nonmetallic part of a circuit, such as an electrolyte, a semiconductor, a vacuum or a gas. The word was coined by William Whewell at the request of Michael Faraday, from the Greek elektron and hodos ("way"). Electrodes transfer charge between the electronic conduction of an external circuit and the ionic conduction of the medium.

Anodes and cathodes

In an electrochemical cell the electrode at which oxidation occurs is the anode, and the electrode at which reduction occurs is the cathode. In a galvanic cell (battery discharging) the anode is the negative terminal; in an electrolytic cell driven by an external supply the polarities are reversed. Electrodes are the sites of the half-reactions that define the cell's voltage.

Types and uses

Electrode materials range from inert metals such as platinum and graphite, used where the electrode merely conducts, to active materials such as the lead and lead dioxide of a lead–acid battery or the lithium compounds of a lithium-ion battery, which take part in the reaction. Reference electrodes, including the standard hydrogen electrode and the silver/silver chloride electrode, provide stable potentials for measurement. Electrodes also appear in welding, in medical devices that record signals such as the ECG and EEG or deliver stimulation, in electrolysis and electroplating plant, and in vacuum tubes and semiconductor devices.
