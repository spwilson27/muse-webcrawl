The string quartets of Ludwig van Beethoven comprise sixteen works (plus the Große Fuge, originally the finale of Op. 130) written between 1798 and 1826. They are conventionally divided into early, middle and late groups, and are regarded as among the supreme achievements of chamber music; the five late quartets in particular have been described as some of the greatest music ever written.

Early quartets, Op. 18

The six quartets of Op. 18 (1798–1800), commissioned by Prince Lobkowitz, are modelled on the quartets of Haydn and Mozart but already show Beethoven's individual voice—for example in the slow movement of No. 1, said to depict the tomb scene from Romeo and Juliet, and the melancholy "La Malinconia" finale of No. 6.

Middle quartets

The three "Razumovsky" quartets, Op. 59 (1806), commissioned by the Russian ambassador Count Andreas Razumovsky and incorporating Russian themes, are far larger in scale and difficulty. They are joined by the "Harp" Quartet, Op. 74 (1809), named for its pizzicato effects, and the compact, intense "Serioso" Quartet, Op. 95 (1810).

Late quartets

After a gap of more than a decade, Beethoven returned to the quartet in 1824 with commissions from Prince Nikolai Galitzin, producing Opp. 127, 132 and 130 (with the Große Fuge), followed by Opp. 131 and 135. These works—in multi-movement designs of unprecedented freedom, with the C-sharp minor Quartet Op. 131 in seven continuous movements and the "Heiliger Dankgesang" (holy song of thanksgiving) at the heart of Op. 132—were long considered baffling but are now central to the repertory. The last movement of Op. 135 carries the famous motto "Muss es sein? Es muss sein!" ("Must it be? It must be!").
