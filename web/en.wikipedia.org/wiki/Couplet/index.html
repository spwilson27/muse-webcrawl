Couplet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A couplet is a pair of successive lines of poetry, typically of the same length and metre, that form a unit, often bound together by rhyme. The couplet is one of the simplest stanzaic forms in poetry and is found in many literary traditions worldwide, including the Chinese duilian, the Persian masnavi (composed entirely of couplets), and the Turkish and Arabic bayt.

A pair of rhymed lines forms a couplet, the smallest stanzaic unit in English verse.

Forms

Closed and open couplets

A closed couplet is grammatically and logically complete within its two lines; an open couplet runs its syntax on into the following lines (enjambment across the couplet boundary). The closed couplet was the ideal of Augustan poets such as John Dryden and Alexander Pope, who used the heroic couplet — rhymed pairs of iambic pentameter — for epic, satire and didactic verse:

True ease in writing comes from art, not chance,
As those move easiest who have learned to dance. — Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism

Heroic couplet

The heroic couplet was introduced into English by Geoffrey Chaucer and perfected in the Restoration and 18th century. Its balance and antithesis made it the vehicle of much of the age's wit; Samuel Johnson remarked of Pope that if he "be not a poet, no one is".

In other traditions

In Chinese literature, couplets (duilian) are paired lines matched in length, structure and tonal pattern, written for celebrations such as the Lunar New Year (chunlian) and displayed on doorways. The Persian masnavi form — used for Rumi's Masnavi and many narrative poems — consists of rhyming couplets in which each pair has its own rhyme (aa, bb, cc...). In South Asian traditions the Hindi/Urdu doha and the Tamil kural are couplet forms; the Tirukkural is composed of 1,330 couplets.

Uses

Couplets are used for epigrams, proverbs, epitaphs, riddles and refrains, and frequently close scenes in Shakespeare's plays, where a rhymed couplet signals an exit or a conclusion. In modern writing they remain common in light verse, song lyrics and children's poetry.
