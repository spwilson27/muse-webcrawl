John Austin (3 March 1790 – 1 December 1859) was an English legal theorist who posthumously influenced British and American law with his analytical approach to jurisprudence and his theory of legal positivism. Building on the work of Jeremy Bentham, Austin developed the command theory of law, according to which law consists of the commands of a sovereign, habitually obeyed by the bulk of a society, backed by the threat of sanctions.

Austin served as the first Professor of Jurisprudence at the newly founded University of London (now University College London) from 1828. His lectures there formed the basis of The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832), which remained relatively neglected during his lifetime but became highly influential after his widow, Sarah Austin, published his Lectures on Jurisprudence in 1863.

Life

Austin was born at Mill Stream in Suffolk, the eldest son of a miller. At sixteen he entered the army and served in Sicily and Elba before selling his commission in 1812 to read for the bar. Called to the bar in 1818, he practised on the Chancery side for seven years with little success, an experience that turned him towards the systematic study of law rather than practice. In 1819 he married Sarah Taylor, who became a distinguished translator and later edited and preserved his work.

In 1826 Austin was appointed to the new chair of jurisprudence at University College London. To prepare, he travelled to Germany and studied Roman law and the work of German jurists at Bonn. His London lectures, begun in 1828, attracted a small but notable audience that at various times included John Stuart Mill's circle of Benthamites, but attendance dwindled and Austin resigned the chair in 1834. He later served on the Criminal Law Commission and, in 1836, as a commissioner inquiring into the government of Malta. He died at Weybridge in Surrey in 1859.

The command theory

In The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, Austin sought to delimit the proper subject matter of jurisprudence. His central claim is that every law "properly so called" is a command: an expression of a wish by a rational being, backed by the power and purpose to inflict an evil (a sanction) in the event of non-compliance, which imposes a duty on those addressed. Laws set by a sovereign — a determinate human superior who receives habitual obedience from the bulk of a society and who habitually obeys no one else — constitute positive law, the subject matter of jurisprudence.

Austin distinguished positive law from several neighbouring phenomena: positive morality (rules such as customary morality and international law that are set by opinion rather than by a sovereign), laws set by God (divine law), and laws by metaphor or analogy (such as the "laws" of nature). He also drew a celebrated distinction between the existence of a law and its merit: "The existence of law is one thing; its merit or demerit is another." This separation of law as it is from law as it ought to be became a defining tenet of legal positivism.

Influence and criticism

Austin's analytical method — clarifying concepts such as right, duty, liberty, injury, punishment and redress — shaped generations of English-speaking jurisprudence. His theory was later criticised by H. L. A. Hart in The Concept of Law (1961), which argued that the command theory could not account for power-conferring rules, the continuity of legal systems, or the internal perspective of officials towards rules. Nevertheless, Austin is conventionally regarded, alongside Bentham, as a founder of the analytical school of jurisprudence and of legal positivism in Britain.
