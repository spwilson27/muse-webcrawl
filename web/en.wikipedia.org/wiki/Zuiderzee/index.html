Zuiderzee

The Zuiderzee (Dutch: [ˈzœydərˌzeː], "southern sea") was a shallow bay of the North Sea in the northwest of the Netherlands, extending about 100 km inland and at most 50 km wide, with an overall depth of about 4 to 5 metres. Formed by storm surges in the early medieval period, it gave maritime access to cities such as Amsterdam and the ports of Holland and Friesland, but its storms repeatedly flooded the surrounding lowlands. In the 20th century it was closed off by the Afsluitdijk (completed in 1932) as part of the Zuiderzee Works, transforming the bay into the freshwater IJsselmeer and enabling the creation of the Flevoland polders.

History

In Roman times the area held a lake the Romans called Lacus Flevo, connected to the sea by a narrowing channel. Over the early Middle Ages the coastline eroded and storm floods — culminating in events such as the St. Lucia's flood of 1287 — breached the dunes and greatly enlarged the inland sea, which took the name Zuiderzee. The bay made Amsterdam and other towns into major trading ports during the Dutch Golden Age, while islands such as Urk, Schokland, Marken, and Wieringen dotted its waters. At the same time, floods such as those of 1421 and 1916 killed many and damaged farmland, keeping the problem of the Zuiderzee at the centre of Dutch water policy.

Closure and reclamation

Engineer Cornelis Lely developed plans in the 1890s to dam and partly reclaim the bay. Legislation passed in 1918, spurred by the flood of 1916 and wartime food concerns. The Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, sealed the bay from the Wadden Sea; the remaining water, fed by the IJssel river, gradually became fresh and was renamed the IJsselmeer. Large polders — the Noordoostpolder and the two Flevoland polders — were drained in the following decades, creating the province of Flevoland. A southern remnant, the Markermeer, was later separated by the Houtribdijk.
