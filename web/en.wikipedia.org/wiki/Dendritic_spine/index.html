Dendritic spine

A dendritic spine (or spine) is a small membranous protrusion from a neuron's dendrite that typically receives input from a single axon at the synapse. Dendritic spines serve as a storage site for synaptic strength and help transmit electrical signals to the neuron's cell body. First described by Santiago Ramón y Cajal in 1888, spines were proposed by him to be the anatomical substrate of learning before the existence of synapses was established.

Structure

Spines are typically 0.5–2 micrometres long, consisting of a bulbous head connected to the dendrite by a thin neck. They are classified by shape as thin, stubby, mushroom, and branched. The head contains the postsynaptic density, an electron-dense region rich in glutamate receptors (AMPA and NMDA types), signalling molecules, and scaffolding proteins such as PSD-95. The spine neck can restrict the diffusion of calcium and other signalling molecules, so that each spine functions as a partially isolated biochemical compartment — a property central to synapse-specific plasticity.

Plasticity and function

Spines are remarkably dynamic. Long-term potentiation (LTP) is associated with enlargement of spine heads and incorporation of more AMPA receptors, while long-term depression (LTD) is associated with spine shrinkage or elimination. New spines can form within minutes to hours of learning experiences, and a subset are stabilized as long-term memories form. A single pyramidal neuron may carry thousands of spines, and much of the excitatory input to the mammalian cortex arrives on them. Abnormal spine density and morphology are reported in neurodevelopmental conditions, including fragile X syndrome (excess of long, thin spines), schizophrenia (reduced cortical spine density), and Alzheimer's disease (spine loss correlating with cognitive decline).
