Antivenom

Antivenom (also called antivenin) is a biological product used in the treatment of venomous bites or stings. It is created by milking venom from the desired snake, spider, or insect and immunising a host animal, whose blood serum is then purified.

Antivenom, also known as antivenin, venom antiserum and antivenom immunoglobulin, is a specific treatment for envenomation. It is composed of antibodies and used to treat certain venomous bites and stings. Antivenoms are recommended only if there is significant toxicity or a high risk of toxicity. The specific antivenom needed depends on the species involved. It may be given by injection.

Side effects may be severe. They include serum sickness, shortness of breath, and allergic reactions including anaphylaxis. Antivenom is traditionally made by collecting venom from the relevant animal and injecting small amounts of it into a domestic animal. The antibodies that form are then collected from the domestic animal's blood and purified.

History

The development of antivenom is associated with Albert Calmette, a French physician working at the Pasteur Institute in the 1890s. Calmette produced a serum against the Indian cobra after witnessing the toll of snakebite in Indochina, and his work laid the foundation for modern serum therapy against animal venoms.

Production

Antivenoms are usually produced by immunising a large domestic animal, most commonly a horse or sheep, with non-lethal doses of venom over a period of weeks or months. The animal develops antibodies, and the antibody-rich fraction of its blood (immunoglobulin) is harvested, purified, and in modern products often enzymatically treated to produce F(ab')2 or Fab fragments, which reduce adverse reactions.

Medical use

Antivenom is the only specific treatment for venomous snakebite recognised by the World Health Organization and appears on its List of Essential Medicines. Effective treatment depends on identifying the biting species or using polyvalent preparations that neutralise the venoms of several species found in a region. Supportive care, such as management of coagulopathy, neurotoxic paralysis, and tissue necrosis, remains essential.
