The Haabʼ (Mayan pronunciation: [haːɓ], Yucatec Maya: Haab, modern orthography Jaab) was the Maya solar calendar, part of the Maya calendar system. It comprised eighteen months of twenty days each plus an additional period of five days (named Wayebʼ) at the end of the year, for a total of 365 days — an approximation of the solar year without any leap-year correction.

Structure

The eighteen Haabʼ months, known from the Yucatec tradition, are Pop, Woʼ, Sip, Sotzʼ, Sek, Xul, Yaxkʼin, Mol, Chʼen, Yax, Sak, Keh, Mak, Kʼankʼin, Muwan, Pax, Kʼayab, and Kumkʼu. Days within each month were numbered from 0 (the "seating" of the month) to 19. The five closing days, the Wayebʼ, were considered an unlucky and dangerous period; during Wayebʼ, portals between the mortal realm and the underworld dissolved, and people stayed indoors and avoided ordinary activity.

Calendar Round

The Haabʼ ran concurrently with the 260-day Tzolkʼin ritual calendar. Because 365 and 260 have a least common multiple of 18,980 days, a combined date repeated only after 52 Haabʼ years (73 Tzolkʼin cycles) — the Calendar Round. A typical Maya date, such as 8 Kumkʼu 0 Pop, specified a day by both calendars.

Accuracy and use

Lacking intercalation, the Haabʼ drifted against the seasons by about one day every four years. Nevertheless it governed the agricultural cycle and civic festivals of Maya cities through the Classic period (c. 250–900 CE) and in modified form into the colonial era. The Long Count calendar was used alongside it for recording absolute dates.
