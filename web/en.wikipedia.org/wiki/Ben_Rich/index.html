Benjamin Robert Rich (June 18, 1925 – January 5, 1995) was an American aeronautical engineer and the second head of Lockheed's famed Skunk Works advanced development division, succeeding its founder Kelly Johnson in 1975 and leading it until his retirement in 1991. Under Rich the Skunk Works developed the F-117 Nighthawk, the world's first operational stealth aircraft, and he is widely regarded as the "father of stealth".

Early life and career

Rich was born in Manila, where his father was a timber contractor, and the family escaped the Philippines shortly before the Japanese occupation in 1941. He earned a degree in mechanical engineering from UC Berkeley and a master's in aeronautical engineering from UCLA, joining Lockheed in 1950 as a thermodynamicist. At the Skunk Works he worked on the F-104 Starfighter, the U-2 spy plane, the A-12 and SR-71 Blackbird, contributing expertise in propulsion inlets, thermodynamics and the exotic materials demanded by Mach 3 flight.

Head of the Skunk Works

As Johnson's deputy and successor, Rich championed the radical faceted design that became the F-117, based on a Soviet radar-scattering paper by Pyotr Ufimtsev and the "Hopeless Diamond" proof-of-concept shape. The programme was developed in deep secrecy at Area 51, first flying in 1981. Rich also oversaw the Have Blue demonstrators, Sea Shadow stealth ship experiments, and advanced concepts including the SR-72 precursors and the early work toward what became the F-22. His memoir, Skunk Works: A Personal Memoir of My Years at Lockheed (1994), written with Leo Janos, became a bestseller and a classic of aerospace literature.

Legacy

Rich received the Collier Trophy and was inducted into the National Aviation Hall of Fame in 2005. His management philosophy — small empowered teams, trust, minimal bureaucracy, and close contact with the customer — extended Johnson's famous "14 rules" and remains a model for advanced development organisations.
