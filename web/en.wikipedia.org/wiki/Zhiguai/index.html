Zhiguai

Zhiguai (Chinese: 志怪; pinyin: zhìguài), literally "records of the strange" or "tales of anomalies," is a genre of Chinese literature that flourished during the Six Dynasties period (220–589 CE). Zhiguai collections consist of short accounts — often only a few lines — of gods, ghosts, fox spirits, strange creatures, omens, miracles and other extraordinary phenomena, presented as records of fact.

Characteristics

Unlike the later chuanqi tales, which were consciously literary fiction, zhiguai were typically framed as documentary accounts, compiled from hearsay, older texts and local lore. Their authors were often historians and officials; the historian Gan Bao, compiler of the famous Soushen Ji ("In Search of the Supernatural," c. 350 CE), stated that he collected his accounts to demonstrate that the spirit world was real. Other major collections include the Bowuzhi attributed to Zhang Hua and Ge Hong's Shenxian Zhuan, biographies of Daoist immortals.

Themes

Recurring subjects include encounters with the dead and the underworld bureaucracy, animal spirits (especially foxes) taking human form, magical Daoist adepts, karmic retribution, and anomalies of nature interpreted as portents. Many stories reflect the religious ferment of the period, when Buddhism was entering China and competing with Daoism and folk religion.

Legacy

Zhiguai laid the foundation for the Chinese fantastic tale tradition, directly influencing Tang dynasty chuanqi, Ming dynasty story collections, and Pu Songling's Qing dynasty masterpiece Liaozhai Zhiyi ("Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio"). Modern scholars treat zhiguai as key sources for early medieval Chinese religion and society.
