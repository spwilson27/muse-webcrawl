Bug bounty program

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Crowdsourced security program
First program: Netscape, 1995

A bug bounty program is a deal offered by many websites, organizations and software developers by which individuals can receive recognition and compensation for reporting bugs, especially those pertaining to security exploits and vulnerabilities. The first such program is generally credited to Netscape Communications, which in 1995 offered cash rewards for bugs found in the Netscape Navigator browser.

Operation

Programs define a scope—the systems, applications and vulnerability classes eligible for rewards—and rules requiring "good faith" research, responsible disclosure and no disruption of services. Reports are triaged, validated, and paid according to severity; critical flaws such as remote code execution or authentication bypasses command the largest bounties, sometimes exceeding one million US dollars at major technology companies. Platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd intermediate between organizations and the global community of independent security researchers.

Adoption

Bug bounties have spread from technology companies to banks, airlines, automobile manufacturers and governments, including the United States Department of Defense's "Hack the Pentagon" initiative of 2016. They complement—but do not replace—internal security teams, penetration testing and code review.

Criticism

Critics note inconsistent triage, disputes over duplicates and scope, and the risk that low average payouts undervalue researchers' labour. Coordinated disclosure standards and safe-harbor legal provisions have emerged to address some concerns.
