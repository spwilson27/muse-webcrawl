Johannine literature is the collection of New Testament works traditionally attributed to John the Apostle or to the Johannine community associated with his name: the Gospel of John, the three Epistles of John, and — more debatably — the Book of Revelation. These writings share vocabulary, style and theological themes that set them apart from the Synoptic Gospels and the Pauline letters.

Characteristics

Johannine writing is notable for its dualistic contrasts — light and darkness, truth and falsehood, above and below — and for themes of love, abiding, witness and eternal life. The Gospel presents a "high Christology" in which Jesus is the pre-existent Logos (Word) made flesh, and uses long discourses and symbolic signs in place of the parables and short sayings typical of the Synoptics. The First Epistle shares much of this language and warns against secessionists who denied that Jesus had come in the flesh.

Authorship and community

Early church tradition assigned the works to John, son of Zebedee. Most modern scholars instead posit a "Johannine community" — a network of churches, often located around Ephesus, within which the Gospel and epistles were composed and edited over time. The authorship of Revelation, written in a rougher Greek and a very different genre (apocalyptic prophecy) by a "John" on Patmos, is usually treated as a separate question; already in the third century, Dionysius of Alexandria argued it was by a different John.
