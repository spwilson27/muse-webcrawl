Scalding

Scalds are commonly caused by hot liquids and steam.

Scalding is a form of thermal burn caused by contact with hot liquids, such as boiling water or oil, or with steam. Scalds are among the most common types of burn injury, particularly in young children, who may pull containers of hot liquid onto themselves, and in the elderly. They are classified, like other burns, by depth: superficial, partial-thickness and full-thickness.

Causes and severity

The severity of a scald depends on the temperature of the liquid and the duration of contact. Water at 60 °C (140 °F) can cause a deep burn in a child within a second or two, whereas at 49 °C (120 °F) several minutes of contact are needed for a serious injury; this relationship underlies recommendations that domestic hot water heaters be set to around 49 °C. Steam scalds can be especially severe because steam carries latent heat that is released as it condenses on the skin.

Treatment and prevention

First aid consists of cooling the burn under cool running water for at least 20 minutes, removing constricting items, and covering the area with a clean, non-adherent dressing; ice, creams and home remedies should be avoided, and large or deep scalds require medical attention. Prevention measures include thermostatic mixing valves, safe hot-water temperatures, turning saucepan handles away from the edge of stoves, and keeping hot drinks away from children.

The term also refers to scalding as a food preparation technique, such as immersing poultry or pigs in hot water to ease removal of feathers or hair, and heating milk to just below boiling point.
