Michael James Harner (27 April 1929 – 3 February 2018) was an American anthropologist and author, best known as the founder of the Foundation for Shamanic Studies and the originator of core shamanism, a synthesis of shamanic practices drawn from cultures worldwide and adapted for Western practitioners.

Fieldwork

Harner studied anthropology at the University of California, Berkeley (PhD 1963). He conducted fieldwork among the Shuar (Jívaro) of Ecuador — published as The Jívaro: People of the Sacred Waterfalls (1972) — and among the Conibo of Peru, where he experienced ayahuasca under indigenous guidance. He also worked with shamans among the Coast Salish and Pomo in North America and the Sami in Scandinavia.

Core shamanism

In 1980 he published The Way of the Shaman, which codified "core shamanism": techniques — especially the shamanic journey induced by repetitive drumming — stripped of their particular cultural contexts and presented as universal. In 1985 he founded the Foundation for Shamanic Studies, which teaches workshops and maintains a "Living Treasures" programme documenting endangered indigenous shamanic knowledge.

Criticism

Harner's approach has been criticised by some indigenous activists and scholars as cultural appropriation and as part of the "plastic shaman" phenomenon; supporters counter that he credited his teachers and channelled resources to indigenous preservation. His work was highly influential on the neoshamanism movement.
