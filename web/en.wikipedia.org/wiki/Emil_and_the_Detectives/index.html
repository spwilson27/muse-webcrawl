Emil and the Detectives (German: Emil und die Detektive) is a 1929 novel for children by the German writer Erich Kästner. It follows a young boy who is robbed on a train journey to Berlin and enlists a gang of local children to track down the thief. The book was an immediate success, has been translated into dozens of languages, and is considered a classic of children's literature.

Plot

Emil Tischbein, a boy from a small town, is sent by train to Berlin carrying 140 marks for his grandmother. On the train he falls asleep, and a fellow passenger, the bowler-hatted Herr Grundeis, steals the money, which Emil has pinned inside his jacket. Waking at the wrong station, Emil follows the thief through Berlin, where he is befriended by Gustav and a large team of child "detectives". After a long chase through the city, the children corner Grundeis in a bank as he tries to change the stolen notes; the pin holes in the banknotes prove the theft, and Grundeis turns out to be a wanted bank robber. Emil receives a reward of 1,000 marks.

Background and legacy

Set in the vivid, modern Berlin of the Weimar Republic, the novel broke with fairy-tale conventions of children's fiction: its hero is a resourceful working-class boy navigating a real city of trams, cafés and banks. Illustrations were by Walter Trier. The book was among the few children's works of its time to survive Nazi censorship largely intact, though Kästner's other writings were banned. It has been adapted repeatedly for film — notably in 1931 (with a screenplay involving Billy Wilder), 1954, and by Disney in 1964 — and for the stage.
