Horse whisperer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Natural horsemanship in a round pen

A horse whisperer is a practitioner of natural horsemanship, a method of training horses based on understanding the animals' body language and behaviour rather than on force or "breaking" the horse's spirit. The term was popularised by the 1995 novel The Horse Whisperer by Nicholas Evans and its 1998 film adaptation directed by and starring Robert Redford.

Methods

Horse whisperers work with the horse as a prey and herd animal, using pressure-and-release, round-pen work, and techniques such as "join-up" — in which the trainer sends the horse away and then invites it to approach and follow voluntarily — to establish trust and leadership. Handling is quiet and incremental, rewarding small responses so that the horse learns to accept saddling, riding, loading into trailers, and other tasks without panic.

History

Noted early whisperers include the nineteenth-century Irish-American Daniel "Doc" Sullivan and the American horseman John Solomon Rarey, who became famous in Britain for taming vicious horses. In the twentieth century, trainers such as Tom and Bill Dorrance, Ray Hunt, and Buck Brannaman developed the modern natural horsemanship movement in the American West, and Monty Roberts brought "join-up" to international attention with his demonstrations for Queen Elizabeth II and his book The Man Who Listens to Horses (1996).
