Criminal negligence

In criminal law, criminal negligence is a state of mind — a form of mens rea — in which a person fails to perceive a substantial and unjustifiable risk that their conduct will cause a prohibited result, where the failure amounts to a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe. It is more culpable than the ordinary negligence of civil (tort) law, but less culpable than recklessness, which requires conscious awareness of the risk.

Distinction from civil negligence and recklessness

Civil negligence is an objective failure to take reasonable care, compensable in damages. Criminal negligence, by contrast, must generally be "gross" or "culpable": the Model Penal Code (§2.02(2)(d)) defines acting negligently, for criminal purposes, as a failure to perceive a substantial and unjustifiable risk involving "a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the actor's situation." The boundary with recklessness turns on awareness: the reckless actor consciously disregards the risk; the criminally negligent actor should have been aware of it but was not.

Applications

Criminal negligence underlies offences such as negligent homicide, involuntary or gross-negligence manslaughter, and criminally negligent endangerment. Examples include deaths caused by extreme carelessness — for example, leaving a loaded firearm within reach of a child, or grossly incompetent medical treatment. In England and Wales, gross negligence manslaughter, restated in R v Adomako [1995], requires a duty of care, a breach causing death, and negligence so gross as to be considered criminal. In Canada, criminal negligence causing death is an indictable offence with a maximum penalty of life imprisonment.

Criticism

Some theorists argue that punishing inadvertence is problematic because the defendant, by definition, did not choose to run the risk; others reply that the grossness of the departure from reasonable care, and the defendant's capacity to have noticed, justify censure.
