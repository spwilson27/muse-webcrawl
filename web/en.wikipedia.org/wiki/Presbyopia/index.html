Presbyopia is the physiological insufficiency of accommodation associated with the ageing of the eye, resulting in a progressively worsening ability to focus clearly on close objects. The word derives from Greek presbys (πρέσβυς) "old" and ops (ὤψ) "eye". It is a natural, almost universal part of ageing: symptoms are usually first noticed in the early to mid-40s, and the condition continues to develop until around age 65. Worldwide, well over a billion people are presbyopic.

Signs and symptoms

The first symptoms are often difficulty reading fine print, especially in dim light, needing to hold books, menus or phones at arm's length ("long-arm syndrome"), eyestrain, and headaches after close work. Unlike far-sightedness (hyperopia), which is caused by the shape of the eyeball, presbyopia is caused by the lens losing flexibility. People with myopia may notice they can read comfortably by removing their distance glasses.

Mechanism

To focus on near objects, the ciliary muscle contracts, zonular fibres relax, and the elastic lens becomes more curved, increasing its optical power — the process of accommodation. With age the lens grows continuously, hardens (sclerosis) and loses elasticity, while the capsule and ciliary apparatus also change; the amplitude of accommodation falls from roughly 15 dioptres in childhood to about 1 dioptre by age 60. The near point recedes beyond a comfortable reading distance, typically once amplitude drops below about 4–5 dioptres.

Treatment

Correction is usually optical: reading glasses (including over-the-counter readers), bifocal or progressive addition lenses for those who also need distance correction, and multifocal or monovision contact lenses. Surgical options include corneal inlays, conductive keratoplasty, presbyLASIK, and intraocular lens replacement with multifocal or accommodating lenses. Pilocarpine eye drops (marketed as Vuity, approved in the United States in 2021) improve near vision temporarily by constricting the pupil to increase depth of focus.
