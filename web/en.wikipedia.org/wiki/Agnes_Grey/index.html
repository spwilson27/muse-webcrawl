Agnes Grey is the debut novel of English author Anne Brontë, published in December 1847 under her pseudonym "Acton Bell". It appeared in a three-volume set together with her sister Emily's Wuthering Heights. The novel follows a young governess, drawing on Anne Brontë's own experiences working in that role, and is notable for its restrained realism in depicting the hardships of a governess's life.

Plot

Agnes Grey, the younger daughter of a clergyman who has lost his savings, determines to earn money as a governess. Her first position, with the Bloomfield family, ends in failure: the children are spoiled and unruly, and Agnes, forbidden to punish them, is blamed for their behaviour and dismissed. In her second post, with the Murray family at Horton Lodge, she endures the vanity and selfishness of her employers but grows into maturity; her elder pupil, Rosalie, marries for money and status and comes to regret it.

Agnes falls in love with the curate Edward Weston, whose quiet kindness contrasts with the worldliness around her. After her father's death, Agnes and her mother open a small school at the seaside, where Weston reappears; the novel ends with their marriage.

Themes and reception

The novel criticizes the treatment of governesses, the moral effects of wealth without responsibility, and the upbringing of children by indulgent parents. Though long overshadowed by the works of her sisters Charlotte and Emily, Agnes Grey has been revalued by modern critics; George Moore praised its prose and called it among the most perfect narratives in English literature.
