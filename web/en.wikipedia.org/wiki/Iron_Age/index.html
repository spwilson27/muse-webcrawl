Iron Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Iron Age swords and tools of the Hallstatt culture]

The Iron Age is the last of the three-age system (Stone, Bronze, Iron) for prehistoric societies, characterised by the widespread use of iron and steel for tools and weapons. In the Near East it began around 1200 BC, after the Bronze Age collapse, as ironworking — harder to smelt than bronze but using far more abundant ore — spread despite early iron's inferiority to good bronze. In Europe the Iron Age runs to the Roman conquests (and beyond, in Scandinavia to about AD 800); in India and Africa the periodisation differs, with ironworking arising early in sub-Saharan Africa, possibly independently.

Spread of iron

Iron smelting appeared in Anatolia and the Caucasus by the later 2nd millennium BC; the Hittites were once cast as its secretive inventors, though the technology was likely more widespread. After 1200 BC iron spread through the Levant, Cyprus and Greece (where the 'Greek Dark Ages' are its first phase), then with Phoenician and Greek colonisation around the Mediterranean, and with Celtic peoples — Hallstatt and La Tène cultures — across temperate Europe. In China, cast iron was produced from about the 5th century BC, centuries before Europe managed cast iron at all.

Societies

Cheap iron transformed agriculture (ploughshares, axes for forest clearance) and warfare (iron swords and spearheads for mass infantry). The Iron Age world of the Near East was that of the Neo-Assyrian and Persian empires, Phoenician and Greek city-states, Etruscans and early Rome, Celts and Scythians; northern Europe's Iron Age produced hillforts, oppida towns, bog bodies and the societies Rome would conquer.
