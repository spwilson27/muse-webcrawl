Perlite

Perlite is an amorphous volcanic glass with a relatively high water content, formed by the hydration of obsidian. Its distinguishing commercial property is that when heated rapidly to temperatures around 850–900 °C (1,560–1,650 °F), the trapped water vaporises and the material expands dramatically — by seven to sixteen times its original volume — producing a lightweight, white, porous material resembling popcorn.

Properties

Natural perlite is a glassy, grey-black to greenish rock with concentric, onion-like fractures (perlitic structure). Expanded perlite is very light (bulk densities commonly 30–150 kg/m³), chemically inert, sterile, has a near-neutral pH, and is an excellent thermal insulator. Its softening point is around 870–1,100 °C and it melts at roughly 1,260–1,340 °C.

Uses

  • Horticulture — expanded perlite is mixed into potting soils and used in hydroponics to improve drainage and aeration while retaining some moisture.
  • Construction — used as loose-fill insulation, in lightweight plasters and concretes, ceiling tiles, and as an aggregate.
  • Filtration — perlite filter aids are widely used to filter liquids in food, beverage, and pharmaceutical processing, similar in role to diatomaceous earth.
  • Industrial — cryogenic insulation, foundry work, and as a carrier for chemicals.

Production

Perlite ore is mined in open pits in countries including Greece (notably the island of Milos), Turkey, the United States, and China. The ore is crushed, dried, and graded before expansion in furnaces close to the point of use, since expanded perlite is bulky to transport. Perlite is not a trade name but a generic term for the material.
