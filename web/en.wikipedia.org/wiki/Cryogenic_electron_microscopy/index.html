Cryogenic electron microscopy

Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) is a cryomicroscopy technique applied to specimens studied under an electron microscope at cryogenic temperatures. In the best-known form, single-particle cryo-EM, purified biological macromolecules suspended in a thin layer of vitreous (glassy, non-crystalline) ice are imaged from many random orientations; computational classification and averaging of hundreds of thousands to millions of individual particle images then reconstructs a three-dimensional density map of the molecule, which can reach near-atomic resolution.

Development

The method was built over decades: vitrification of water without crystallization was developed by Jacques Dubochet and colleagues in the 1980s; Richard Henderson obtained the first high-resolution structure (of bacteriorhodopsin, using electron crystallography) in 1990; and Joachim Frank developed the image-processing foundations of single-particle reconstruction. The three shared the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. A "resolution revolution" beginning around 2012–2013, driven by direct electron detectors and improved motion-correction software, brought routine resolutions of 3 ångströms or better, and by 2020 structures at true atomic resolution (~1.2 Å) were reported.

Methods and applications

Related modalities include cryo-electron tomography, which images thicker specimens such as cells and viruses in three dimensions from tilt series; microcrystal electron diffraction (MicroED); and time-resolved cryo-EM. Cryo-EM has transformed structural biology by solving structures of large, flexible, or membrane-embedded complexes that resist crystallization — including ribosomes, spliceosomes, ion channels such as TRPV1, and viral spike proteins central to vaccine and drug design, notably during the COVID-19 pandemic.
