River Tame

There are two principal rivers named the River Tame in England, both tributaries of the River Trent system or the Mersey system.

River Tame, West Midlands

River Tame (West Midlands)
LocationWest Midlands and Staffordshire, England
MouthRiver Trent near Alrewas
Length59 mi (95 km)

The River Tame is a river in the West Midlands of England, flowing 59 miles (95 km) from the Black Country through the northern districts of Birmingham to join the River Trent near Alrewas in Staffordshire. It is the Trent's largest tributary by discharge after the Dove and Derwent and gives its name to Tamworth, the town at its confluence with the River Anker ("Tame-worth", the enclosure by the Tame).

Course

The Tame is formed from streams rising near Oldbury and Willenhall in the Black Country. It flows north-east beneath the M5 and M6 motorways, through Sandwell Valley and Perry Barr, then along Birmingham's northern edge through Witton and beneath Spaghetti Junction (the Gravelly Hill motorway interchange), where it runs alongside the Tame Valley Canal. It continues past Water Orton, through the green Kingsbury Water Park area, and past Tamworth—where the Anker joins—before meeting the Trent at Alrewas. At Birmingham the river was heavily culverted and industrialised in the 19th and 20th centuries and was notoriously polluted; water quality has improved markedly with modern sewage treatment, and otters have returned to parts of the catchment.

River Tame, Greater Manchester

River Tame (Greater Manchester)
LocationGreater Manchester and West Yorkshire border, England
MouthRiver Mersey at Stockport
LengthApprox. 18 mi (29 km)

The northern River Tame flows from Denshaw on the Saddleworth moors, on the West Yorkshire boundary, through Uppermill, Mossley, Stalybridge, Ashton-under-Lyne, Dukinfield, Hyde, and Denton, to Stockport, where it joins the River Goyt to form the River Mersey. This Tame defines much of the historic boundary between Lancashire and Cheshire, and the borough of Tameside is named after it. Its valley carried the Huddersfield Narrow Canal and the woollen and cotton mills of the Tame valley towns; the river powered many mills and was once severely polluted by the textile and chemical industries, but fish have returned to its lower reaches in recent decades.

Name

The name Tame is believed to be Celtic in origin, possibly meaning "dark" or simply "river", and is shared with the River Thame in Oxfordshire and the Tamar in the south-west.
