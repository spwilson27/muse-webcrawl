Work hardening

Work hardening, also known as strain hardening or cold working, is the strengthening of a metal or polymer by plastic deformation. The deformation — produced by processes such as rolling, drawing, bending, forging, and shot peening — increases the density of dislocations in the material's crystal structure. Moving dislocations increasingly interfere with one another, so progressively greater stress is required for further deformation: the material becomes harder and stronger, but less ductile.

Mechanism

In a ductile metal, plastic deformation proceeds by the motion of dislocations through the crystal lattice. Cold work multiplies dislocations — their density can rise from about 106 per cm2 in an annealed metal to 1011–1012 per cm2 after heavy deformation — and they tangle and pile up at grain boundaries and other obstacles. The result is a rise in yield strength and hardness accompanied by a fall in ductility and remaining formability. On a stress–strain curve this appears as the upward slope of the plastic region, often modelled by a power law σ = Kεn, where n is the strain-hardening exponent: materials with higher n (such as austenitic stainless steels) harden faster and distribute strain more evenly, resisting necking.

Effects and use

Work hardening is exploited deliberately: cold-rolled sheet, drawn wire, and cold-headed fasteners gain much of their strength from it, and some alloys (notably Hadfield manganese steel and many copper alloys) cannot be hardened by heat treatment at all, so cold work is their only strengthening route. Conversely, work hardening limits further forming, so multi-stage processes interleave deformation with annealing, which resets ductility through recovery and recrystallisation. Heating a work-hardened metal therefore softens it — full annealing restores the original low dislocation density.

Undesirable hardening

Strain hardening can be a nuisance: repeatedly bending a wire back and forth hardens it until it fractures; machining of work-hardening alloys such as stainless steels hardens the surface ahead of the tool, increasing tool wear; and shot peening, while beneficial for fatigue life because it leaves the surface in compression, relies on the same local plasticity. Forming simulations and die design must account for hardening to predict springback and press loads.
