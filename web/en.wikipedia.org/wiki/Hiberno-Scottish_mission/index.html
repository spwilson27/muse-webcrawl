Hiberno-Scottish mission

The Hiberno-Scottish mission was a missionary movement led by Irish and Scottish (Gaelic) monks between the late 6th and the early 13th centuries that spread Christianity, learning and distinctive monastic practices from Ireland and western Scotland across Anglo-Saxon England and the Frankish continent of Europe.

Origins

The mission grew out of the monastery of Iona, founded by the Irish exile Columba (Colum Cille) in 563, and out of the Irish practice of peregrinatio — voluntary exile for Christ. From Iona, Aidan founded Lindisfarne in 635 and evangelised Northumbria. In parallel, the Irish monk Columbanus left Bangor around 590 and founded monasteries in Francia and Italy, including Luxeuil and Bobbio, imposing the strict Irish monastic rule on his houses.

Activities

Hiberno-Scottish monks founded monasteries across what are now France, Germany, Switzerland, Austria and Italy — among them St Gall, Würzburg, Regensburg and Vienna's Schottenstift. They were noted for scholarship, manuscript illumination, penitential literature, private confession and their own Easter dating and tonsure, which brought them into conflict with Roman practice until the Synod of Whitby (664) and later reforms aligned the Irish church with Rome.

Legacy

The mission preserved and transmitted Latin learning during the early medieval period and shaped monasticism in northern Europe; the Schottenklöster (Scots monasteries) of the Holy Roman Empire remained in Irish hands into the later Middle Ages.
