Progestogens, also sometimes written progestogens or gestogens, are a class of steroid hormones that bind to and activate the progesterone receptor (PR). The principal endogenous progestogen is progesterone, which is produced by the ovaries (corpus luteum), the placenta during pregnancy, and the adrenal glands in smaller amounts.

Progestogens are one of the three major classes of sex hormones, alongside estrogens and androgens. They are involved in the menstrual cycle, pregnancy (supporting gestation — hence the name, from Latin pro- "for" and gestation), and embryogenesis. Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation, maintains pregnancy, promotes mammary gland development, and has effects on the brain, immune system, and body temperature.

Synthetic progestogens

Synthetic compounds with progesterone-like activity are called progestins. They are widely used in hormonal contraception (alone and combined with estrogens), hormone replacement therapy, and the treatment of conditions such as endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, and some cancers. Successive "generations" of progestins were developed with differing androgenic, estrogenic, and glucocorticoid activity, including norethisterone, levonorgestrel, desogestrel, and drospirenone.

Biochemistry

Like all steroid hormones, progestogens are synthesised from cholesterol via pregnenolone. Progesterone is itself a precursor to other steroids, including corticosteroids and androgens. See also Progesterone, Progestin, and Sex hormone.
