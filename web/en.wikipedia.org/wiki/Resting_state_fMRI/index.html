Resting state fMRI

Resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI or rfMRI) is a method of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) that is used in brain mapping to evaluate regional interactions that occur in a resting or task-negative state, when an explicit task is not being performed. A number of resting-state conditions are identified in the brain, the so-called resting-state networks (RSNs), one of which is the default mode network. These resting brain networks are observed through changes in blood flow in the brain, which creates what is referred to as a blood-oxygen-level dependent (BOLD) signal that can be measured using fMRI.

History

In 1995, Bharat Biswal and colleagues at the Medical College of Wisconsin discovered that spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations (below ~0.1 Hz) in the BOLD signal of the resting brain were temporally correlated between the left and right motor cortices, even when the subject performed no task. Further work by Michael Greicius and colleagues in 2003 showed coherent fluctuations in the set of regions later called the default mode network, a system described by Marcus Raichle and co-workers. The approach rapidly grew into the field of functional connectivity research.

Resting-state networks

Commonly identified networks include the default mode network, the sensorimotor, visual, auditory, dorsal attention, frontoparietal (central executive) and salience networks. These networks closely resemble systems activated during tasks and are consistent across individuals, though connectivity patterns vary with age, state of consciousness and disease.

Analysis methods

Data are typically preprocessed to remove artefacts from head motion, cardiac and respiratory cycles, and scanner drift. Connectivity is then examined with seed-based correlation, independent component analysis (ICA), graph-theoretical measures and amplitude measures such as ALFF and regional homogeneity. Head motion is a notorious confound: small movements can systematically inflate short-distance correlations, and rigorous motion correction remains an active area of methods research.

Applications

Resting-state fMRI is attractive clinically because it requires no task performance, allowing use in children, patients and pre-surgical mapping of eloquent cortex. Altered connectivity has been reported in Alzheimer's disease, schizophrenia, autism, depression and epilepsy, though individual diagnostic use remains limited. Large open datasets such as the Human Connectome Project and the 1000 Functional Connectomes Project have made resting-state data widely available for research.
