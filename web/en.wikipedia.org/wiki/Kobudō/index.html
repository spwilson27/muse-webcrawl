Kobudō (古武道, "old martial ways") is a Japanese term for traditional martial arts in their classical form. It has two main usages: it can refer to the pre-modern martial traditions (koryū) of mainland Japan, and, most commonly in the West, to the weapon systems of Okinawa — Okinawan kobudō — traditionally practised alongside karate.

Okinawan kobudō

Okinawan kobudō is the art of the traditional weapons of the Ryukyu Islands. Its principal weapons are the bō (six-foot staff), sai (trident-shaped metal baton), nunchaku (two sticks joined by cord or chain), tonfa (handled baton), kama (sickle), tekko (knuckle weapon), tinbe-rochin (shield and short spear), eku (oar) and sansetsukon (three-section staff). Many are popularly said to derive from farm implements — a story linked to weapons restrictions under Ryukyu kings and later Satsuma rule, though the historical record is more nuanced and several weapons have clear Chinese and Southeast Asian connections.

Training is centred on kata (forms) and partner drills. Major traditional systems include Matayoshi kobudō, founded by Matayoshi Shinkō and his son Shinpō, and Ryūkyū kobudō as systematised by Taira Shinken, who catalogued and preserved dozens of weapons kata in the 20th century.

Koryū usage

In Japan, kobudō may also denote the classical martial schools (koryū budō) founded before the Meiji Restoration — traditions of swordsmanship, jujutsu, archery and other battlefield arts — as distinct from modern gendai budō such as judo, kendo and aikido.
