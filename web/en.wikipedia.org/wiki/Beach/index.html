Beach

Material: Sand, gravel, pebbles, or cobbles
Formed by: Wave and current action

A beach is a landform alongside a body of water which consists of loose particles. The particles composing a beach are typically made from rock, such as sand, gravel, shingle, or pebbles, or from biological material such as mollusc shells or coralline algae. Sediments settle in different densities and structures, depending on the local wave action and weather, creating different textures, colours, and gradients.

Formation

Beaches are the result of wave action by which currents move sand or other loose sediments of which the beach is made as these particles are held in suspension. Alternatively, sand may be moved by saltation. Material is eroded from cliffs and reefs, carried along the shore by longshore drift, and deposited where the water's energy falls. Constructive waves push sediment up the beach face, building a berm, while destructive storm waves pull it offshore into bars.

Profiles and zones

A typical beach profile includes the backshore (above high water, reached only by storm waves), the foreshore or intertidal zone exposed at low tide, and the nearshore extending to the breaker zone. Seasonal changes steepen beaches in winter and flatten them in summer on many coasts.

Ecology and human use

Beaches support specialised wildlife — crabs, shorebirds, turtles that nest in sand above the tide line — and are fragile habitats easily damaged by development and pollution. They are also among the most popular recreation landscapes in the world, central to seaside tourism, and are defended against erosion by measures such as beach nourishment and groynes.
