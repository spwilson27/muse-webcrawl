Obshchina

The obshchina (Russian: община, "commune"), also called the mir (мир), was the peasant village community characteristic of Imperial Russia. It was a self-governing association of peasant households that held the village's arable land in collective ownership and periodically redistributed it among its members according to family size and need.

Organization

The commune governed itself through the skhod, an assembly of heads of households, which elected an elder (starosta), allocated tax burdens, admitted or expelled members, and decided on the redistribution (peredel) of allotments. Arable land was typically divided into strips scattered across the open fields so that every household received a share of good and poor soil, and the whole village followed a common crop rotation and grazing calendar. After the Emancipation of 1861, land redeemed from the nobility was generally vested in the commune rather than in individual peasants, and the commune was made collectively responsible — through krugovaya poruka, mutual guarantee — for redemption payments and taxes.

Debates and decline

The obshchina was a central topic of nineteenth-century Russian thought. Slavophiles and Populists (narodniki) idealised it as an indigenous, egalitarian institution that might let Russia bypass capitalism and move directly to socialism; Karl Marx, in correspondence with Vera Zasulich in 1881, allowed that it might serve as a starting point for communist development. Critics countered that periodic redistribution discouraged improvement of the land and that collective responsibility bound peasants to the village. The Stolypin reforms, begun in 1906, sought deliberately to dissolve the commune by enabling peasants to withdraw their allotments as private property. Communal landholding revived briefly after the 1917 revolution, before collectivization in the 1930s replaced the obshchina with the kolkhoz and sovkhoz.
