Bulletin board system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Screenshot-style image of ANSI art and a text menu on a 1980s BBS welcome screen]

A bulletin board system (BBS) is a computer server running software that allows users to connect with a terminal program, log in, and perform functions such as uploading and downloading software and data, reading news and bulletins, and exchanging messages with other users through public message boards and sometimes direct chatting.

The first BBS, CBBS, was created in Chicago in February 1978 by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess during a blizzard, letting hobbyists dial in by modem to a single phone line — one user at a time. Through the 1980s BBSes multiplied into the tens of thousands: community noticeboards, shareware libraries (the origin of the shareware industry), multiplayer door games, elaborate ANSI art, and networks such as FidoNet (1984) that relayed mail and discussion between boards worldwide, forming a grassroots global network parallel to the academic Internet.

Commercial online services (CompuServe, AOL) and then the World Wide Web absorbed the BBS's functions and users in the mid-1990s; most boards closed, though a nostalgic scene keeps hundreds running, now reached by telnet and web instead of dial-up. The BBS era invented online community conventions — handles, sysops, flame wars, files areas — that the Internet inherited wholesale.
