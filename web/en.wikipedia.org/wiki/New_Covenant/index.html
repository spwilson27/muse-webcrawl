The New Covenant (Hebrew: ברית חדשה berit hadashah; Greek: διαθήκη καινή diatheke kaine) is a biblical interpretation originally derived from a phrase in the Book of Jeremiah (31:31–34) which, in the Christian tradition, is understood as a prophecy of the covenant mediated by Jesus. It is a central concept in Christian theology, structuring the relationship between God and humanity after Christ, and gives its name to the "New Testament" (testament being another rendering of "covenant").

Jeremiah's prophecy

Writing during the crisis of the Babylonian exile, Jeremiah prophesied: "The days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the people of Israel and with the people of Judah... I will put my law in their minds and write it on their hearts... For I will forgive their wickedness and will remember their sins no more." The passage emphasises interiorisation of the law and direct forgiveness.

In the New Testament

At the Last Supper, Jesus is reported to say of the cup: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you" (Luke 22:20; cf. 1 Corinthians 11:25). The Epistle to the Hebrews develops the theme at length, presenting Jesus as high priest and mediator of a "better covenant" (Hebrews 8), quoting Jeremiah 31 in full and declaring the first covenant "obsolete". Paul contrasts letter and spirit, law and promise, in similar covenantal terms (2 Corinthians 3).

Christian views

Most Christians hold that the New Covenant superseded or fulfilled the Mosaic Covenant, replacing the sacrificial system and ceremonial law with salvation by grace through faith in Christ. Covenant theology (in the Reformed tradition) sees one overarching "covenant of grace" administered differently across biblical eras, whereas dispensationalism distinguishes Israel and the Church more sharply. Views differ on the continuing role of Torah, the Sabbath, and the place of ethnic Israel; "New Covenant theology" and Messianic Judaism represent intermediate positions.

Jewish views

In Judaism, Jeremiah's new covenant is generally understood as a renewal of the existing covenant with Israel in the messianic age — the Torah internalised by the Jewish people — rather than its replacement, and not as mediated by Jesus. The phrase also appears in sectarian texts such as the Damascus Document of the Dead Sea Scrolls, whose community styled itself the "New Covenant in the land of Damascus".
