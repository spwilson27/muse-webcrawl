William James Dixon (July 1, 1915 – January 29, 1992) was an American blues musician, vocalist, songwriter, arranger and producer. He was proficient on upright bass and guitar, and sang with a distinctive voice. Widely regarded as one of the most prolific songwriters of the blues, Dixon stands alongside Muddy Waters as one of the most influential figures in shaping the post–World War II sound of Chicago blues.

Working at Chess Records as house songwriter, session bassist and producer, Dixon wrote or co-wrote more than 500 songs, including standards recorded by Waters ("Hoochie Coochie Man"), Howlin' Wolf ("Spoonful", "Little Red Rooster"), Little Walter ("My Babe") and Koko Taylor ("Wang Dang Doodle"). British blues-rock bands of the 1960s — Led Zeppelin, the Rolling Stones, Cream and The Doors — drew heavily on his catalogue; Led Zeppelin's "Whole Lotta Love" led to a copyright settlement crediting Dixon.

Early life

Dixon was born in Vicksburg, Mississippi, one of fourteen children. He sang in gospel quartets, hoboed north to Chicago in 1936, took up boxing (briefly, as a Golden Gloves heavyweight prospect), and served a prison term on a Mississippi prison farm after refusing the wartime draft as a conscientious objector. In Chicago he formed the Big Three Trio, which recorded for Columbia.

Chess Records years

From 1950 Dixon was a fixture at Chess Records (and its predecessor Aristocrat), playing bass on sessions for Waters, Wolf, Chuck Berry and Bo Diddley, and increasingly supplying the label's signature material. His songwriting partnership with Waters and rivalry-fuelled writing for Howlin' Wolf defined electric Chicago blues. In the 1960s he organised the American Folk Blues Festival tours that introduced the music to Europe.

Songs and influence

Signature compositions include "Back Door Man", "I Can't Quit You Baby", "I'm Ready", "Seventh Son", "Little Red Rooster", "Spoonful", "You Can't Judge a Book by the Cover" and "I Just Want to Make Love to You". Dixon described his role plainly: "The blues are the roots and the other musics are the fruits."

Royalties and activism

Long underpaid under contracts signed early in his career, Dixon sued successfully for back royalties and in 1985 founded the Blues Heaven Foundation to recover royalties for blues artists and preserve the Chess studios at 2120 South Michigan Avenue, Chicago.

Death and honours

Dixon died of heart failure in Burbank, California, in 1992. He was inducted into the Blues Hall of Fame in 1980 and posthumously into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1994.
