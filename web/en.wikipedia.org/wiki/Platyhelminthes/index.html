Platyhelminthes (flatworms)
KingdomAnimalia
PhylumPlatyhelminthes
Major groupsTurbellaria, Trematoda, Monogenea, Cestoda
SpeciesOver 29,000 described

The Platyhelminthes, from the Greek platy ("flat") and helminth ("worm"), are a phylum of relatively simple, bilaterally symmetrical, unsegmented invertebrates known as flatworms. Unlike other bilaterians, they have no body cavity (they are acoelomate) and lack specialised circulatory and respiratory organs, relying on diffusion across their flattened bodies. About two-thirds of known species are parasitic, including the medically important flukes and tapeworms; the remainder are mostly free-living predators of fresh and salt water and damp terrestrial habitats.

Characteristics

Flatworms are triploblastic, with three true tissue layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm), and an incomplete gut with a single opening serving as both mouth and anus; tapeworms have lost the gut entirely and absorb nutrients through their surface. The body is dorsoventrally flattened, and the space between organs is filled with a spongy connective tissue called parenchyma. Gas exchange occurs across the body surface, and excretion is handled by flame cells (protonephridia). Cephalisation is evident in free-living forms, with a bilobed brain, longitudinal nerve cords, eyespots, and chemosensory organs concentrated at the head.

Classification and life styles

The traditional class Turbellaria comprises mostly free-living flatworms, including the freshwater planarians famous for their regenerative powers — a planarian cut into pieces can regenerate each piece into a complete animal, a capacity underpinned by pluripotent stem cells called neoblasts. The parasitic groups form the Neodermata: the Trematoda (flukes, such as the blood fluke Schistosoma and the liver fluke Fasciola), the Monogenea (mostly ectoparasites of fish), and the Cestoda (tapeworms, such as Taenia), which have elaborate multi-host life cycles.

Medical importance

Parasitic flatworms infect hundreds of millions of people. Schistosomiasis alone affects over 200 million people in tropical regions, and tapeworm infections cause diseases ranging from mild intestinal complaints to neurocysticercosis, a leading cause of acquired epilepsy in the developing world. Control relies on sanitation, snail control, and mass drug administration with anthelmintics such as praziquantel.
