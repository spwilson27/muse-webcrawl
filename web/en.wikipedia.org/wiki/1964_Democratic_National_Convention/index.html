The 1964 Democratic National Convention was held from 24 to 27 August 1964 at Convention Hall in Atlantic City, New Jersey. It nominated President Lyndon B. Johnson, who had succeeded the assassinated John F. Kennedy nine months earlier, for a full term, with Senator Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota as his running mate.

The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party

The convention is best remembered for the credentials challenge by the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP), an integrated delegation organised by civil rights activists to challenge the legitimacy of the state's all-white regular Democratic delegation, which had been chosen in defiance of the national party's rules. Activist Fannie Lou Hamer's televised testimony about being beaten for attempting to register to vote electrified the nation. Under pressure from Johnson, the convention offered the MFDP two at-large seats — a compromise the MFDP rejected as tokenism.

Proceedings

Johnson, wary of a walkout by Southern delegates and of any hint of division, tightly controlled the proceedings. The convention featured a memorial film tribute to President Kennedy, and Johnson's acceptance came on the closing night. The Johnson–Humphrey ticket went on to win the November election in a landslide over Barry Goldwater.

Legacy

The MFDP fight exposed deep racial divisions within the Democratic Party and led to reforms requiring state delegations to be chosen without racial discrimination; by 1968 integrated delegations from the South were seated. Historians regard the convention as a pivotal moment in the realignment of the American party system.
