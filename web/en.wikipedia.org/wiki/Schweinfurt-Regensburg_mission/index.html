The Schweinfurt–Regensburg mission was a strategic bombing mission flown by the United States Army Air Forces on 17 August 1943, during the Second World War. It was the first major "double strike" deep-penetration mission of the Eighth Air Force, in which American B-17 Flying Fortress heavy bombers attacked two targets in southern Germany — the ball-bearing factories at Schweinfurt and the Messerschmitt aircraft plant at Regensburg — on the same day.

Background

By the summer of 1943 the Eighth Air Force, based in England, was committed to daylight precision bombing of German industry. Ball bearings were identified as a critical bottleneck in German war production, since they were essential components in aircraft, vehicles, and machinery. Schweinfurt, in Bavaria, produced a large share of Germany's ball bearings. At Regensburg, the Messerschmitt plant assembling Bf 109 fighters was one of the largest aircraft production facilities in Europe.

The plan called for two task forces to fly in sequence. The first, 146 B-17s led by Colonel Curtis LeMay, would bomb Regensburg and then continue south across the Alps to land in North Africa — one of the first "shuttle" missions of the war. The second force, 230 B-17s under Brigadier General Robert B. Williams, would bomb Schweinfurt and return to England. The split was designed to divide the German fighter defences.

The mission

On the morning of 17 August 1943, fog over the English bases delayed the Schweinfurt force for several hours, while the Regensburg force took off into clearing weather. The delay destroyed the intended timing: instead of facing a divided defence, each force met the full weight of the Luftwaffe fighter arm separately. Once beyond the range of escorting fighters, the bombers were attacked repeatedly by German single- and twin-engine fighters, which used head-on passes and, in some cases, air-to-air rockets and bombs.

The Regensburg force bombed its target with reasonable accuracy and continued to North Africa, losing 24 bombers. The Schweinfurt force fought its way to the target, bombed the ball-bearing plants, and fought its way back, losing 36 bombers over Germany and more to fuel exhaustion and accidents. In total, 60 B-17s were lost out of 376 dispatched, and many more were damaged; roughly 600 airmen were killed, wounded, or captured.

Aftermath

Although the raids caused real damage — particularly at Regensburg, where production was set back — ball-bearing output at Schweinfurt recovered quickly through dispersal, stocks, and imports from neutral countries. The losses were unsustainable: the Eighth Air Force suspended unescorted deep-penetration raids into Germany until long-range escort fighters, principally the P-51 Mustang, became available in strength. A second major raid on Schweinfurt on 14 October 1943 — remembered as "Black Thursday" — confirmed the lesson, with another 60 bombers lost.
