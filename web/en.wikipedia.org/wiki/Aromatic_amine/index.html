Aromatic amine

An aromatic amine is an organic compound consisting of an aromatic ring attached to an amine group. The parent member of the class is aniline (C6H5NH2). Aromatic amines are widely used as intermediates in the manufacture of dyes, pharmaceuticals, rubber chemicals and polymers such as polyurethanes.


Aniline, the simplest aromatic amine

Structure and properties

In aromatic amines the nitrogen lone pair is conjugated with the π system of the ring, making them substantially less basic than aliphatic amines: the conjugate acid of aniline has a pKa of about 4.6, compared with about 10.7 for cyclohexylamine. The conjugation also flattens the geometry at nitrogen and strongly activates the ring toward electrophilic substitution at the ortho and para positions.

Preparation

Aromatic amines are most commonly prepared by reduction of nitro compounds, for example with iron and acid (the Béchamp reduction) or by catalytic hydrogenation. They may also be made by nucleophilic aromatic substitution of aryl halides bearing electron-withdrawing groups, or by the Buchwald–Hartwig amination of aryl halides with amines.

Uses

Aniline is converted on a large scale to methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI), a precursor of polyurethane foams. Other aromatic amines are diazotised to produce azo dyes, sulfonamide drugs, and rubber antioxidants such as p-phenylenediamine derivatives.

Health effects

Several aromatic amines, including benzidine, 2-naphthylamine and 4-aminobiphenyl, are established human bladder carcinogens, and their use has been heavily restricted since the mid-20th century. Aniline itself can cause methaemoglobinaemia on significant exposure.
