Battle of Chippawa

The Battle of Chippawa (also spelled Chippewa) was fought on 5 July 1814 during the War of 1812, on the Niagara Peninsula in present-day Ontario, Canada. A United States army under Major General Jacob Brown and Brigadier General Winfield Scott defeated a British force under Major General Phineas Riall in a stand-up fight between regulars — the first time in the war that American regular troops defeated their British counterparts on equal terms in the open field.

Background

American forces crossed the Niagara River on 3 July 1814 and captured Fort Erie. Brown's Left Division then advanced north along the river. Scott's brigade had spent the spring at Buffalo being drilled intensively in French Revolutionary tactics — a training program that transformed the regulars' performance. Because blue cloth was unavailable, Scott's men were uniformed in grey jackets.

Battle

On 5 July Riall, underestimating the Americans and reportedly mistaking Scott's grey-clad regulars for militia ("Those are regulars, by God!" he is said to have exclaimed), advanced south from Chippawa Creek. After preliminary skirmishing in the woods by Native warriors and militia on both flanks, the British regulars deployed on the open plain south of Street's Creek. Scott's brigade advanced under artillery fire, dressed its lines, and exchanged close-range volleys with the British infantry, which it outlasted; a final American advance against the British flank forced Riall to withdraw across the Chippawa in good order, destroying the bridge behind him.

Aftermath

Chippawa restored American confidence and demonstrated the value of Scott's training; the grey uniforms worn there are traditionally said to be the origin of the grey dress uniforms of the West Point cadets. Brown's advance continued, leading three weeks later to the far bloodier Battle of Lundy's Lane.
