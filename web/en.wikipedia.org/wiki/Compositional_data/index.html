Compositional data

In statistics, compositional data are quantitative descriptions of the parts of some whole, conveying relative information. Mathematically, a composition can be represented as a point in a simplex. Measurements commonly described as compositions include proportions, percentages, and concentrations such as parts per million. Because the components of a composition must sum to a constant (such as 1 or 100%), the data carry only relative information, and standard statistical methods designed for unconstrained real-valued data can give misleading results when applied directly.

History

The problem of spurious correlation between ratios was noted by Karl Pearson in 1897. The modern theory of compositional data analysis was developed principally by John Aitchison in the 1980s, who proposed working with log-ratios of components, which map the simplex to ordinary real space where standard multivariate methods can be applied.

Log-ratio transformations

Common transformations include the additive log-ratio (alr), the centred log-ratio (clr), and the isometric log-ratio (ilr) transformations. These allow compositional data to be analysed with conventional techniques while respecting the geometry of the simplex, sometimes called Aitchison geometry.

Applications

Compositional data arise in geology (geochemistry), economics, microbiome studies, and many other fields where measurements are naturally expressed as parts of a whole. Zeros in components pose special difficulties because logarithms of zero are undefined, and various imputation strategies have been proposed.
