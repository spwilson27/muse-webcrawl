Potsdam Conference

The Potsdam Conference (17 July – 2 August 1945) was the last of the World War II summit meetings of the "Big Three" Allied powers, held at the Cecilienhof Palace in Potsdam, in occupied Germany just outside Berlin. The participants were the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom and the United States, represented respectively by Joseph Stalin, Winston Churchill (replaced on 28 July by Clement Attlee after the British general election) and Harry S. Truman, who had succeeded Franklin D. Roosevelt on the president's death in April. The conference's business was the practical settlement of Europe after Germany's surrender of 8 May 1945: the administration of occupied Germany, Poland's western border, reparations, the treatment of the defeated Axis states, and the expulsion of German populations from Eastern Europe. Its proceedings — marked by suspicion very different from the atmosphere of Tehran and Yalta — are usually counted among the opening scenes of the Cold War.

Background

Since Yalta the wartime alliance had frayed badly: the Soviets had installed a Communist government in Poland without the promised free elections, the Western powers protested at Soviet conduct in Romania and Bulgaria, and friction over reparations and the administration of Germany grew by the week. Truman arrived determined to be firmer than Roosevelt had been; during the conference he received news of the successful Trinity atomic test (16 July), which visibly stiffened his manner — he confided to Stalin on 24 July that the United States had "a new weapon of unusual destructive force", to which Stalin, well informed by his spies, replied blandly that he hoped it would be used against the Japanese.

The Potsdam Agreement

The conference's protocol, issued on 2 August, set out the principles for Germany's occupation: complete disarmament and demilitarisation; the destruction of the Nazi Party and its laws; denazification of public life; the trial of war criminals (then being prepared at Nuremberg); democratisation, including free elections and a free press; decentralisation of the political structure and of the economy, with cartels broken up; and Germany to be treated as a single economic unit under the four-power Allied Control Council. In practice the rule of each commander in his own zone, and the decision that reparations should be taken primarily from each occupying power's own zone (with supplementary deliveries to the USSR from the western zones), pushed Germany toward division.

On Poland, the Western powers accepted — as a provisional arrangement pending a final peace settlement — the administration by Poland of the German territories east of the Oder–Neisse line, which entailed the flight and expulsion of millions of Germans westward; the protocol stipulated that population transfers "should be effected in an orderly and humane manner", a clause that bore little relation to what was already happening. The conference recognised the reorganised Polish Provisional Government of National Unity, and the Western powers withdrew recognition from the London-based government-in-exile. Italy's colonies, the admission of states to the United Nations, and the revision of the Montreux Convention on the Turkish Straits (a Soviet demand the West resisted) were referred to a new Council of Foreign Ministers, charged also with drafting peace treaties with Germany's former satellites.

The end of the Big Three

Potsdam was overshadowed by the war still raging in the Pacific: on 26 July the three powers (together with China, invited to concur) issued the Potsdam Declaration demanding Japan's unconditional surrender, threatening "prompt and utter destruction" if it refused. Within days of the conference's close, atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and the Soviet Union declared war on Japan (8 August), honouring the timetable agreed at Yalta. No further summit of the three wartime leaders ever met; the Council of Foreign Ministers quarrelled through 1946–47, the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan followed, and the provisional arrangements of Potsdam hardened into the forty-year division of Germany and Europe.
