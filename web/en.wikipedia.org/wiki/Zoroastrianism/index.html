Zoroastrianism is one of the world's oldest monotheistic religions, founded by the prophet Zoroaster (Zarathustra) in ancient Iran. It worships Ahura Mazda, the Wise Lord, as the uncreated creator, and frames existence as a cosmic struggle between asha (truth, order) and druj (the lie), in which every person freely chooses a side — summed up in the maxim 'good thoughts, good words, good deeds'. It was the state religion of successive Persian empires for over a millennium.

History and influence

Zoroaster's hymns, the Gathas, are the oldest layer of the Avesta. Under the Achaemenid, Parthian and especially Sasanian empires the faith spread across the Iranian world; the Arab conquest of the 7th century AD reduced it, and refugees (the Parsis) carried it to Gujarat in India, where the community became prominent in trade, industry and science. Zoroastrian ideas — heaven and hell, final judgement, resurrection, a saviour, angels and a devil figure — plausibly influenced Second Temple Judaism and through it Christianity and Islam. Today perhaps 100,000–200,000 adherents remain, mainly Parsi and Iranian, facing demographic decline debated within the community.

Practice

Worship centres on fire temples, where a consecrated flame symbolises truth and purity; priests tend it in white garments with covered mouths. Traditional funerary practice exposed the dead in 'towers of silence' to vultures, avoiding pollution of earth, fire and water — a rite now restricted by law and by the collapse of vulture populations in India. Navroz, the spring new year, is the best-known festival.
