Hydrophobic effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hydrophobic effect
TypeNon-covalent interaction / solvent effect
Driving forceEntropy of water (at room temperature)
Key rolesProtein folding, membrane formation, micelle formation

The hydrophobic effect is the observed tendency of non-polar substances to aggregate in an aqueous solution and to exclude water molecules. It is not a true attractive force between the non-polar molecules themselves, but a consequence of the behaviour of water: a non-polar surface forces surrounding water molecules into more ordered, cage-like arrangements, reducing their entropy. Aggregation minimises the total non-polar surface exposed to water, releasing those ordered molecules and increasing the entropy of the system.

Thermodynamics

Transfer of a hydrocarbon from a non-polar solvent into water is unfavourable in free energy, characterised at room temperature by a large negative entropy change and a positive heat-capacity change. The relative contributions of enthalpy and entropy shift with temperature, a phenomenon connected with cold denaturation of proteins. Walter Kauzmann's 1959 analysis of "hydrophobic bonding" established the effect's central role in biochemistry, building on earlier ideas of Frank and Evans on the "iceberg" structure of water around solutes.

Biological significance

The hydrophobic effect is the principal driver of protein folding: hydrophobic amino-acid side chains (such as leucine, isoleucine and phenylalanine) are buried in the protein core away from water. It likewise drives the spontaneous formation of lipid bilayers and membranes, micelles formed by detergents, and contributes to ligand binding in enzyme active sites when ordered water is displaced.
