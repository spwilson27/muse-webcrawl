Bride of Frankenstein is a 1935 American science fiction horror film, the first sequel to Universal Pictures' Frankenstein (1931). Directed by James Whale, it stars Boris Karloff as the Monster, Colin Clive as Henry Frankenstein, Valerie Hobson as Elizabeth, Ernest Thesiger as Doctor Pretorius, and Elsa Lanchester in the dual roles of Mary Shelley and the Monster's mate. The film is widely regarded as one of the greatest horror films ever made and frequently cited as superior to its predecessor.

Plot

In a prologue, Mary Shelley explains to Percy Shelley and Lord Byron that the story of Frankenstein did not end with the burning windmill. Henry Frankenstein, believed dead, is carried home and revives. The Monster, too, survived, and wanders the countryside, learning fragments of speech from a blind hermit before being hunted again by villagers. The sinister Doctor Pretorius, Henry's former mentor, proposes that they collaborate to create a mate for the Monster, blackmailing Henry by having the Monster abduct Elizabeth. In a laboratory during a storm, the female creature is brought to life — but she recoils in horror from the Monster, who, despairing, destroys the laboratory, telling Henry and Elizabeth to go and live, while he and the Bride "belong dead."

Production

Universal had planned a sequel, provisionally titled The Return of Frankenstein, soon after the first film's success, but James Whale was initially reluctant to return. Production ran from January to March 1935 and substantially overran its budget and schedule. Franz Waxman's score, written for the film, was among the first great horror scores and was reused by Universal in many later productions. The character of the Monster learns to speak, drawing on Mary Shelley's novel, though Karloff objected to giving the Monster dialogue. The Bride's striking appearance — a tall, conical hairstyle with white lightning-blot streaks, designed with makeup artist Jack Pierce — became one of the most iconic images in horror cinema, despite the character appearing on screen for only a few minutes.

Reception and legacy

The film was a commercial and critical success on release and has grown further in reputation; it holds a near-unanimous approval rating on review aggregators and is listed on the American Film Institute's 100 Years... 100 Thrills list. In 1998 it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." Film historians have read the film in terms of its themes of loneliness, creation and rejection, and critics have frequently interpreted the work through James Whale's experience as a gay man in 1930s Hollywood.
