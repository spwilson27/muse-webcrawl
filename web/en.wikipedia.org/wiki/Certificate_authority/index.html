Certificate authority

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a high-security CA signing ceremony with hardware security modules behind glass]

In cryptography, a certificate authority or certification authority (CA) is an entity that stores, signs, and issues digital certificates. A CA acts as a trusted third party — trusted both by the subject (owner) of the certificate and by the party relying upon the certificate — and its root certificates are distributed in the trust stores of browsers and operating systems, which is what makes HTTPS work at Internet scale.

CAs validate that an applicant controls the domain named in the certificate (and, for higher-assurance certificates, the applicant's legal identity) before issuing. The industry is governed by the CA/Browser Forum's Baseline Requirements and policed by the browser root programmes, which can distrust a misbehaving CA — an existential sanction, applied in the past to DigiNotar (2011, after a breach produced fraudulent certificates used in interception) and to Symantec's CA business (2017–2018, after systematic mis-issuance).

The market was transformed by Let's Encrypt (2015), a non-profit CA issuing free, automated, domain-validated certificates, which made HTTPS the web's default; the largest commercial CAs (DigiCert, Sectigo, GoDaddy) remain significant, and the total population of trusted roots numbers in the dozens of organisations.
