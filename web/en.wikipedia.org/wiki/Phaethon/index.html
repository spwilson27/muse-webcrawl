Phaethon (Ancient Greek: Φαέθων, Phaéthōn, "the shining one") was the son of the Oceanid Clymene and the sun god Helios in Greek mythology. His name means "shining" or "radiant". Phaethon is best known for the myth in which, seeking proof of his divine paternity, he begged to drive his father's sun chariot for a day, lost control of the immortal horses, and nearly set the earth ablaze before Zeus struck him down with a thunderbolt.

Mythology

According to the most famous version, preserved in Ovid's Metamorphoses, Phaethon grew up in Ethiopia (or India) with his mother Clymene. Teased by his friend Epaphus, who doubted that Phaethon was really the son of the Sun, Phaethon travelled to the palace of Helios to ask for proof of his birth. Helios joyfully acknowledged him as his son and, swearing by the river Styx — an oath no god could break — promised to grant any wish. Phaethon asked to drive the chariot of the Sun for one day.

Helios tried in vain to dissuade him, explaining that not even Zeus could control the fiery horses. Bound by his oath, he surrendered the chariot. The inexperienced Phaethon could not hold the reins: the horses bolted, veering too close to the earth, burning whole regions (in the aetiological myth, this scorched Africa into desert and darkened the skin of its peoples), and too far away, freezing others. To save the world from destruction, Zeus killed Phaethon with a thunderbolt, and his body fell into the river Eridanus (often identified with the Po in Italy).

Phaethon's sisters, the Heliades, mourned him so inconsolably that the gods turned them into poplar trees, their tears becoming amber. His friend Cycnus, king of Liguria, was transformed into a swan.

Other versions

Earlier and alternative traditions differ. In some accounts Phaethon was an epithet of Helios himself; a fragment of Hesiod and later sources place his body in the care of the Heliades from the start. The lost tragedy Phaethon by Euripides made him the son of Helios and Clymene, who had married the Ethiopian king Merops; Aeschylus also wrote a play, the Heliades, of which fragments survive. Plato, in the Timaeus, presents the story as a veiled reference to periodic celestial deviations and conflagrations.

In astronomy and culture

The asteroid 3200 Phaethon, discovered in 1983 and the parent body of the Geminid meteor shower, is named after the mythological figure because its orbit brings it exceptionally close to the Sun. The word "phaeton" later came to denote a type of light, open horse-drawn carriage, and later an automobile body style.
