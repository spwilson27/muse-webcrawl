Cornering the market

Cornering the market consists of obtaining sufficient control of a particular stock, commodity, or other asset in an attempt to manipulate its price. A trader or group acquires enough of the deliverable supply — and often a large long position in futures contracts — that short sellers cannot obtain the asset to settle their positions except by buying from the cornerer at inflated prices, producing a short squeeze.

Corners typically target the deliverable supply behind futures contracts.

Mechanics and risks

The cornerer profits only if they can sell into the squeeze before the position collapses; because the cornerer ends up holding much of the supply, prices usually crash once the squeeze ends, and regulators may intervene by changing margin rules, ordering liquidation-only trading, or extending delivery. Corners are therefore risky and frequently ruinous even when technically executed.

Famous attempts

Classic episodes include the Gould–Fisk gold corner that led to Black Friday (1869); the Northern Pacific corner of 1901, which contributed to a stock-market panic; Clarence Saunders's failed Piggly Wiggly corner (1923), after which exchanges changed their rules; the Hunt brothers' attempt to corner silver, which peaked on "Silver Thursday", 27 March 1980, and prompted the COMEX "Silver Rule 7" liquidation-only order; Sumitomo trader Yasuo Hamanaka's copper positions in the 1990s; and Porsche's 2008 accumulation of Volkswagen shares and options, which briefly made VW the world's most valuable company during a short squeeze. In securities law, corners generally constitute market manipulation and are prohibited, for example under the US Securities Exchange Act and the Commodity Exchange Act.
