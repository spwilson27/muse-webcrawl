Practice theory

Practice theory (or theory of practices) is a family of theories in sociology and anthropology that analyse social life in terms of practices — routinised, embodied activities such as cooking, working, exercising and consuming — rather than in terms of individual choices or abstract social structures alone.

Key thinkers

The approach draws on Ludwig Wittgenstein and Martin Heidegger, and was developed by Pierre Bourdieu, whose concepts of habitus, field and capital explain how durable dispositions generate practices; by Anthony Giddens, whose structuration theory treats structure as both medium and outcome of practice; and by Theodore Schatzki, Andreas Reckwitz and Shove and colleagues, who define practices as bundles of meanings, competences and materials.

Core ideas

Practices are the unit of analysis: individuals are "carriers" of practices, which persist and spread through society. Much of social action is tacit and bodily rather than reflective. Because practices bundle materials (objects, infrastructures), competences (skills, know-how) and meanings (images, aspirations), changing behaviour — for example toward sustainability — requires reconfiguring whole practices rather than merely informing individuals.

Applications

Practice theory has been applied to consumption, energy use, mobility, health, organisations, technology studies and media research, and underlies the "practice turn" in contemporary social theory.
