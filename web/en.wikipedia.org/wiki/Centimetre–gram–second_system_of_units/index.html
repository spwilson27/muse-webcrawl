Centimetre–gram–second system of units

The CGS system builds on the centimetre, gram, and second.

The centimetre–gram–second system of units (abbreviated CGS or cgs) is a metric system based on the centimetre as the unit of length, the gram as the unit of mass, and the second as the unit of time. Proposed by the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1873 and developed from the work of Carl Friedrich Gauss, it was the dominant system in physics for decades before being largely superseded by the metre–kilogram–second (MKS) system and ultimately the International System of Units (SI).

Mechanical units

Derived CGS mechanical units include the dyne (force), the erg (energy), the poise (dynamic viscosity), the stokes (kinematic viscosity), and the barye (pressure). Many remain familiar in specialised fields: astronomers often express luminosity and flux in erg-based units.

Electromagnetic variants

Unlike SI, CGS does not have a single electromagnetic convention. The electrostatic (esu), electromagnetic (emu), Gaussian, and Heaviside–Lorentz variants define electric and magnetic units differently, leading to different forms of Maxwell's equations — with or without factors of 4π, for example. Gaussian units remain common in theoretical physics, astrophysics, and relativity.
