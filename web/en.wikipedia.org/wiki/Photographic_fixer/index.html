Photographic fixer

A photographic fixer is a chemical solution used in the final stages of photographic processing to stabilise the developed image. After development, film or paper still contains unexposed silver halide, which would darken on exposure to light. The fixer dissolves this residual silver halide, rendering the image permanent and light-insensitive. Fixing is colloquially called "hypo", from the older name hyposulphite of soda for sodium thiosulfate.

Chemistry

The active ingredient is usually sodium thiosulfate or the faster-acting ammonium thiosulfate ("rapid fixer"). Thiosulfate forms soluble complexes with silver ions, allowing the unreacted silver halide to be washed out of the emulsion. Fixers often also contain sodium sulfite as a preservative, an acid or buffer, and sometimes a hardener such as potassium alum that toughens the gelatin emulsion. The fixing capacity of a bath is exhausted as dissolved silver accumulates; exhausted fixer is a significant source of recoverable silver, and its disposal is regulated in many places.

Practice

Typical fixing times are a few minutes for film and around a minute for resin-coated paper, followed by thorough washing to remove residual thiosulfate, which would otherwise slowly attack the silver image. A hypo clearing agent (sodium sulfite solution) shortens washing. Inadequately fixed or washed prints fade and stain over time, a major concern of archival processing standards.
