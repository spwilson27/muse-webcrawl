Fixed-gear bicycle

A fixed-gear bicycle (commonly known in some places as a fixie) is a bicycle that has a drivetrain with no freewheel mechanism. The freewheel was developed early in the history of bicycle design but the fixed-gear bicycle remained the standard design for track racing. More recently the "fixie" has become a popular alternative among urban cyclists, offering the advantages of simplicity compared with a multi-geared bicycle.

With a fixed gear, the rear sprocket is threaded directly onto the hub; consequently, the pedals are directly coupled to the drive wheel. When the rear wheel turns, the pedals turn in the same direction. This allows a cyclist to stop without using a brake, by resisting the rotation of the cranks (known as a skid stop when the rear wheel is locked), and also to ride slowly backwards.

Uses

Fixed-gear bicycles are mandatory in velodrome track racing, where brakes are forbidden. They are also used by some bicycle messengers, artistic cyclists, and urban riders. Many fixed-gear bicycles have a "flip-flop hub" allowing the rider to switch between fixed and single-speed freewheel modes.

Legality and safety

In many jurisdictions, bicycles used on public roads must be fitted with brakes; riding a brakeless fixed-gear on the street is illegal in some places and controversial elsewhere, though skilled riders can slow the bicycle by resisting the pedals or performing skid stops.
