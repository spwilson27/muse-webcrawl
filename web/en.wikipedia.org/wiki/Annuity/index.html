Annuity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An annuity is a series of payments made at equal intervals. Examples of annuities are regular deposits to a savings account, monthly home mortgage payments, monthly insurance payments and pension payments. Annuities can be classified by the frequency of payment dates, which may be weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly, or at any other regular time interval.

Types

An annuity that begins payments at once is an annuity-immediate (or ordinary annuity); one whose payments begin at the end of each period is distinguished from an annuity-due, whose payments are made at the beginning of each period. In insurance usage, an annuity contract is a product sold by financial institutions that accepts and grows funds and then, upon annuitization, pays out a stream of payments at a later point in time. Annuity contracts may be fixed, variable, or indexed, and may pay for a fixed term or for the life of the annuitant (a life annuity).

Valuation

The present value of an annuity is the sum of the discounted values of its future payments. For an annuity-immediate of n payments of amount P at periodic interest rate i, the present value is P × (1 − (1+i)−n) / i. Actuaries use mortality tables to value life annuities, which continue only while the annuitant survives.

History

Annuity-like arrangements date to antiquity: Roman citizens could purchase annua contracts providing income for life, and tontines and state annuities were used to finance European governments from the seventeenth century onward.
