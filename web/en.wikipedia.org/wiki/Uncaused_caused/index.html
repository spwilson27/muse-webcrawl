Uncaused caused

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The phrase "uncaused caused" arises in discussions of the cosmological argument for the existence of God, where it is sometimes raised as an apparent paradox: if everything that exists must have a cause, then the supposed first cause must itself be caused, contradicting its status as first; if the first cause is uncaused, then not everything requires a cause. The classical response, developed in Aristotelian and Thomistic metaphysics, distinguishes between things that are caused (contingent beings whose existence is derived from another) and a being that is uncaused — not "caused by nothing," but existing through its own nature, with essence and existence identical.

Context in the arguments

In Aquinas's Five Ways, the arguments from motion and from efficient causation trace a series of movers or causes that cannot regress infinitely in an essentially ordered series, concluding to a "first mover" or "first cause" that is itself unmoved and uncaused — ipsum esse subsistens, subsistent being itself. Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason similarly seeks an ultimate reason outside the series of contingent facts. Critics argue the move is special pleading or that causal series may not need a first member; defenders reply that the first cause is not an exception to the principle but its metaphysical ground, since only contingent things need causes.

Related ideas

The phrase has also surfaced in modern popular debates, where "who caused the uncaused cause?" is treated as a rejoinder. Philosophers generally note that the question trades on ambiguity between "uncaused" (having no cause because existence belongs to its nature) and "self-caused" (causing itself, which classical theists also reject as incoherent). The related notion of causa sui appears in Descartes and Spinoza, whose God or substance exists necessarily and is, in that qualified sense, its own cause.
