Salt effect

In chemistry, a salt effect is the influence of added dissolved salts — including salts not sharing any ion with the reactants — on the solubility of a substance or on the rate of a reaction in solution. Several distinct phenomena carry the name.

Solubility effects

The diverse-ion effect (or salt effect on solubility) is the tendency of a sparingly soluble salt to become more soluble in a solution containing an unrelated electrolyte. It is explained by the effect of ionic strength on activity coefficients, as described by Debye–Hückel theory: added ions shield the ions of the precipitate from one another, lowering the activity product relative to the concentration product and shifting equilibrium toward dissolution. It contrasts with the common-ion effect, which decreases solubility.

Related phenomena include salting out, in which high salt concentrations reduce the solubility of non-electrolytes and proteins (exploited in ammonium-sulfate precipitation of proteins), and salting in, the modest increase in protein solubility at low salt concentrations.

Kinetic salt effect

The primary kinetic salt effect describes how ionic strength changes the rate of a reaction between ions: according to the Brønsted–Bjerrum equation, reactions between ions of like charge speed up as ionic strength rises, while reactions between oppositely charged ions slow down. A secondary kinetic salt effect arises when added salt shifts a pre-equilibrium (for example, the dissociation of a weak acid) that feeds the rate-determining step.
