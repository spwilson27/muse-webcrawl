Grain boundary

Grain boundaries in a polycrystalline material

In materials science, a grain boundary is the interface between two grains, or crystallites, in a polycrystalline material. Grain boundaries are two-dimensional defects in the crystal structure, and tend to decrease the electrical and thermal conductivity of the material. Most grain boundaries are preferred sites for the onset of corrosion and for the precipitation of new phases from the solid. They are also important to many of the mechanisms of creep.

Description

Grain boundaries can be described by the orientation difference between the two grains on either side. Boundaries with a small misorientation (less than roughly 15 degrees) are called low-angle grain boundaries and can be modelled as arrays of dislocations. High-angle boundaries have larger misorientations and a more disordered structure. The energy of a grain boundary varies with misorientation; certain special orientations, described by coincident site lattice theory, have notably low energies.

Importance to material properties

The size of grains, and hence the total grain boundary area, strongly influences the strength of a material. The Hall–Petch relationship describes how yield strength increases as grain size decreases, because boundaries obstruct dislocation motion. Grain boundaries also act as pinning points impeding further dislocation propagation. At high temperatures, boundaries can slide and migrate, contributing to creep. Impurity atoms often segregate to grain boundaries, which can either strengthen the material or cause intergranular corrosion and embrittlement.

Grain boundary migration

During annealing, grain boundaries migrate so that larger grains grow at the expense of smaller ones, a process known as grain growth, driven by the reduction in total boundary energy.
