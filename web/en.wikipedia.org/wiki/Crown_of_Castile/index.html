The Crown of Castile (Spanish: Corona de Castilla) was a medieval and early modern polity in the Iberian Peninsula. It originated in 1230, when Ferdinand III of Castile inherited the Kingdom of León and permanently united the two crowns, together with Galicia, into a single composite monarchy. Through the Reconquista the crown absorbed the Muslim kingdoms of Córdoba, Murcia, Jaén, Seville and finally Granada (1492), becoming the largest and most populous Christian state in Iberia. After the dynastic union with the Crown of Aragon under the Catholic Monarchs, Castile financed and administered Spain's overseas empire — the Americas and the Philippines were possessions of the Crown of Castile — and its institutions, language and law became the core of the Spanish monarchy until the Nueva Planta decrees of the early eighteenth century abolished the separate crowns.

Formation and expansion

The County of Castile emerged within the Kingdom of León in the ninth and tenth centuries and became a kingdom in 1065 under Ferdinand I. Castile and León separated and reunited several times before Ferdinand III's definitive union of 1230. His campaigns and those of Alfonso X took Córdoba (1236) and Seville (1248), reducing Muslim rule in the south to the Nasrid Emirate of Granada. Within the crown, each kingdom retained its own laws and cortes (parliaments), while the royal council and, from 1508, the Council of the Indies governed common affairs; the Siete Partidas of Alfonso X provided a lasting legal code.

The Catholic Monarchs and empire

The marriage of Isabella I of Castile to Ferdinand II of Aragon (1469) and Isabella's victory in the Castilian War of Succession joined the two crowns dynastically, though each kept its institutions. Granada fell in 1492, the year Isabella sponsored Columbus's voyage; by the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) Castile's sphere of expansion lay to the west. The conquests of Mexico and Peru under Charles I (the Emperor Charles V) and the accession of Philip II made Castile the fiscal and demographic engine of a global monarchy, funding it through American silver and Castilian taxation — burdens that provoked the Revolt of the Comuneros (1520–1521) and repeated royal bankruptcies.

Decline and abolition

By the seventeenth century Castile suffered demographic decline, plague and war exhaustion, while the attempt by the Count-Duke of Olivares to share burdens across the monarchy (the Union of Arms) triggered revolts in Catalonia and Portugal. After the War of the Spanish Succession, Philip V's Nueva Planta decrees (1707–1716) dissolved the institutions of the Crown of Aragon and completed the legal unification of Spain, ending the Crown of Castile as a distinct juridical entity while its territories formed the bulk of the new unitary kingdom.
