Gas lighting


Gas street lamps of the kind that lit 19th-century cities.

Gas lighting is the production of artificial light from combustion of a gaseous fuel, such as coal gas, natural gas, propane, or hydrogen. Before electric lighting became widespread in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, gas lighting was the dominant means of illuminating streets, factories, theatres and homes in industrialised cities. The technology originated with William Murdoch, who lit his house and the Boulton & Watt foundry in Cornwall and Birmingham with coal gas in the 1790s and early 1800s.

Development

London's Pall Mall became the first street lit by gas in 1807, and the Gas Light and Coke Company (founded 1812) built the first public gasworks. Lamplighters lit and extinguished street lamps by hand until automatic ignition and the incandescent gas mantle — invented by Carl Auer von Welsbach in the 1880s — greatly increased efficiency and brightness, allowing gas to compete with early electric light for decades.

Decline and legacy

Electric lighting displaced gas in most applications during the 20th century, but gas lamps survive in historic districts (London retains around 1,500) and in camping lanterns. The phrase "gaslight" also gave rise to the modern term "gaslighting" for psychological manipulation, after the 1938 play Gas Light.
