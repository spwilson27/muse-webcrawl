Goblin


A goblin as pictured in nineteenth-century folklore illustration.

A goblin is a small, grotesque, monstrous humanoid creature that appears in the folklore of multiple European cultures. First attested in stories from the Middle Ages, goblins are traditionally ascribed various — and sometimes conflicting — abilities, temperaments and appearances depending on the story and country of origin; they are commonly mischievous, greedy and possessed of magical abilities similar to those of a fairy or demon.

Origins and etymology

The word derives from the Middle English gobelin, borrowed from the Old French gobelin, itself possibly related to the Medieval Latin gobelinus — a spirit said to haunt the country around Évreux in twelfth-century accounts by Orderic Vitalis — and ultimately perhaps to the Greek kobalos, a rogue or sprite, via the Germanic Kobold. In Germanic folklore, related creatures include the kobold of mines and households, the hobgoblin (a friendlier domestic spirit in English tradition), and mine-spirits such as the Cornish knockers. The Earl of Oxford's goblin lore and place names such as Goblin Combe in Somerset record the creature's deep roots in British tradition.

In fiction

Goblins became a staple of modern fantasy through J. R. R. Tolkien, who used "goblin" interchangeably with "orc" for the footsoldiers of evil in The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings. Role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons codified them as small, tribal, green-skinned monsters, an image popularised further by Warhammer, the comic Goblin of tabletop gaming, and video games. J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter series recast goblins as the sharp-witted, gold-loving bankers of Gringotts, while fairy tales such as Christina Rossetti's poem "Goblin Market" (1862) and George MacDonald's The Princess and the Goblin (1872) gave them earlier literary form. In the Spider-Man comics, the Green Goblin and Hobgoblin are among the hero's most famous adversaries.
