Occam's razor

Occam's razor
Named afterWilliam of Ockham (c. 1287–1347)
Latin maximEntia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem — "Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity"
FieldPhilosophy of science, logic, problem solving

Occam's razor (also Ockham's razor or the law of parsimony) is the problem-solving principle that, when presented with competing hypotheses about a phenomenon, one should prefer the explanation that makes the fewest assumptions — the simpler theory. The principle is named after the English Franciscan friar and scholastic philosopher William of Ockham, who wielded it extensively, though the idea predates him in the works of Aristotle, Ptolemy and Thomas Aquinas.

Formulations

The familiar Latin formulation "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity" does not appear verbatim in Ockham's writings but summarizes his repeated injunctions that "it is futile to do with more things that which can be done with fewer" and that "plurality must never be posited without necessity." The metaphor of a "razor" refers to shaving away unnecessary concepts and assumptions.

Applications

The razor is a heuristic, not a proof: it guides scientists toward economical models — such as heliocentrism over elaborated geocentric epicycles, or evolution by natural selection over special creation — while allowing that a more complex theory may ultimately prove correct. It has been invoked in physics, biology, medicine (where it is balanced against Hickam's dictum), statistics and machine learning, where overly complex models risk overfitting data.

Criticisms

Critics note that "simplicity" is ill-defined — syntactic, ontological and computational simplicity can point in different directions — and that nature is under no obligation to be simple. The razor is best understood as a guide to inquiry and explanation rather than a rule of logic.
