Note-taking

Note-taking (sometimes written as notetaking or note taking) is the practice of recording information from sources or lectures for later review and use. Notes serve as an external memory aid and as a learning activity in themselves: selecting, paraphrasing, and organising material promotes understanding and retention, an effect known as generative learning.

Handwritten notes using a structured format

Methods

Several structured systems are widely taught. The Cornell method divides the page into a narrow cue column, a main note-taking area, and a summary strip at the bottom. The outline method arranges points hierarchically by indentation. Mind mapping places a central idea in the middle of the page with radiating branches. The Zettelkasten method, associated with sociologist Niklas Luhmann, builds a network of small, individually numbered notes linked to one another. In legal and official settings, minutes serve a related function of recording decisions and actions.

Research

Studies of students find that reviewing notes is crucial: notes are typically incomplete, capturing only part of a lecture's key points. A widely cited 2014 study by Mueller and Oppenheimer suggested that taking notes longhand, which forces summarisation, produced better conceptual learning than typing verbatim on laptops; later replications have found the advantage smaller and less consistent than first reported. Digital note-taking tools add search, linking, and multimedia but also raise the risk of verbatim transcription and distraction. See also Cornell Notes, mind map, and Zettelkasten.
