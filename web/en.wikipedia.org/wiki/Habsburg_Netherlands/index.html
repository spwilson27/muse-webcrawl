The Habsburg Netherlands were the parts of the Low Countries held by the House of Habsburg from 1482 to 1797, succeeding the Burgundian Netherlands. They comprised most of modern Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg, plus adjoining parts of France and Germany. The territories were joined administratively as the Seventeen Provinces under Emperor Charles V, who was born in Ghent and raised in Mechelen, and formed the Burgundian Circle within the Holy Roman Empire.

History

The Habsburg inheritance came through the marriage of Mary of Burgundy to Maximilian of Austria in 1477; after her death in 1482 their son Philip the Handsome inherited the Netherlands. Charles V consolidated the provinces, acquiring Utrecht, Overijssel, Groningen, Drenthe and Guelders in the 1520s–1540s, and issued the Pragmatic Sanction of 1549 declaring the Seventeen Provinces an indivisible inheritance.

Under Charles's son Philip II of Spain, heavy taxation, religious persecution of Protestants and centralising policies provoked the Dutch Revolt (1568–1648). The northern provinces declared independence in the Act of Abjuration (1581) and were recognised as the Dutch Republic in 1648, while the loyal southern provinces — the Spanish Netherlands — remained Habsburg. After the War of the Spanish Succession, the south passed to the Austrian branch of the dynasty as the Austrian Netherlands (1714–1797). French revolutionary armies conquered the territory in 1794–95, ending three centuries of Habsburg rule.

Government and society

The provinces were governed by a governor-general, usually a close Habsburg relative — including Mary of Hungary and Margaret of Austria, who ruled from Mechelen — working through the Council of State, Privy Council and Council of Finance in Brussels, while each province retained its States and privileges. The region was among Europe's most urbanised and prosperous: Antwerp was the commercial metropolis of northern Europe in the mid-16th century, and the Southern Netherlands were a centre of printing, cartography and art (the age of Bruegel, Rubens and Van Dyck).
