History of the Internet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A timeline graphic from the ARPANET's four nodes in 1969 to the global network of today]

The history of the Internet begins with research into packet switching in the 1960s and the development of the ARPANET, the first wide-area packet-switched network, which connected its first four nodes — UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah — in late 1969.

Through the 1970s, networks proliferated (CYCLADES in France, the UK's NPL network, ALOHAnet in Hawaii) but could not talk to each other. Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn solved the interconnection problem with the TCP/IP architecture, published in 1974; on 1 January 1983 the ARPANET switched to TCP/IP, the date often taken as the Internet's operational birthday. The NSFNET (1985) linked American universities, and networks in Europe, Asia and beyond joined, while Usenet and email built the first mass user communities.

In 1989–1991 Tim Berners-Lee at CERN invented the World Wide Web, and the graphical Mosaic browser (1993) made it vivid to non-specialists. The US government lifted commercial restrictions and retired NSFNET in 1995, the year of Netscape's IPO, igniting the dot-com boom — and its 2000 crash. Broadband, Wi-Fi and then the smartphone (2007) made the Internet continuous and personal; social media and cloud computing reorganised it around platforms in the 2000s and 2010s; and the network that began as a resilient military research project became the general-purpose infrastructure of human communication, commerce and culture — used by more than five billion people.
