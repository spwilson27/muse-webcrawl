Conditional cash transfer

Conditional cash transfer (CCT) programmes are social welfare programmes that make welfare payments conditional on the receivers' actions — for example, sending children to school, attending health clinics, or getting vaccinations. They aim to reduce current poverty through cash assistance while increasing human capital to break the intergenerational cycle of poverty.

History and spread

The modern CCT model was pioneered in Latin America in the 1990s, notably Brazil's Bolsa Escola (later merged into Bolsa Família) and Mexico's Progresa (later Oportunidades/Prospera), launched in 1997. The model spread across Latin America and to countries in Africa, Asia, and beyond, with support from the World Bank and the Inter-American Development Bank. By the late 2000s, dozens of countries operated CCT programmes covering hundreds of millions of people.

Design

Payments are usually made to mothers or female heads of household, on evidence that resources controlled by women are more likely to be spent on children. Conditions are monitored through schools and clinics; non-compliance can lead to suspension of benefits. Programmes differ in payment size, targeting methods, and the strictness of enforcement.

Evidence and debate

Rigorous evaluations, including randomized trials of Progresa, found improvements in school enrolment, health visits, nutrition, and child health. Critics question whether the conditions themselves add value beyond the cash, note administrative costs and exclusion errors, and point out that benefits depend on the availability and quality of schools and clinics. Unconditional cash transfers have also shown strong results in some contexts, fuelling debate about conditionality.
