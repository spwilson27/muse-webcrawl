Consideration (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the common law of contracts, consideration is something of value exchanged by the parties — a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee — which makes a promise legally enforceable. The classic definition is that of Currie v Misa (1875): a right, interest, profit or benefit accruing to one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss or responsibility undertaken by the other. A promise without consideration is generally a bare (nudum pactum) promise, unenforceable except when made by deed.

Consideration need not be adequate — courts do not weigh the fairness of the exchange, and a peppercorn or nominal sum suffices — but it must be sufficient in law: past consideration is generally no consideration; existing duties (legal or contractual) traditionally do not count, though cases on practical benefit (Williams v Roffey Bros, 1991) complicate the rule on renegotiated contracts; and part-payment of a debt is not consideration for forgiving the balance (Foakes v Beer, 1884), subject to promissory estoppel.

Comparative note

Civil-law systems do not require consideration: France historically used the related but broader notion of cause (removed as a named requirement in the 2016 reform of the Code civil), and Germany's BGB enforces gratuitous promises in limited, formalised cases. The doctrine is thus a distinctive common-law filter against enforcing informal gifts and social promises.
