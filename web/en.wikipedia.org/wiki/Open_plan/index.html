Open plan

Open plan is a term in architecture and interior design for a floor plan in which large, open spaces are used instead of smaller enclosed rooms. It is applied both to houses — where living, dining, and kitchen areas are combined — and, most prominently, to open plan offices, in which rows of desks occupy shared space with few partitions or private offices.

Offices

The open office developed from early twentieth-century designs such as Frank Lloyd Wright's Larkin Building, and from post-war German Bürolandschaft ("office landscape") layouts. From the 1960s onward, systems furniture and cubicles attempted to balance openness with some privacy. Later designs removed most partitions entirely, aiming to encourage collaboration, make efficient use of expensive floor space, and signal egalitarian culture, particularly in technology companies.

Debate

Research has repeatedly questioned the assumed benefits. Studies have found that open plan offices are associated with more distractions, noise, reduced face-to-face interaction in some settings (with communication shifting to email and messaging), greater dissatisfaction with privacy, and higher rates of sick leave. Proponents respond that well-designed open spaces with quiet rooms, acoustic treatment, and activity-based zones can mitigate these problems.

Residential use

In housing, open plan living became popular in the later twentieth century, combining kitchen, dining, and living rooms into a single space, often organized around a kitchen island. It suits informal entertaining but can suffer from noise and cooking odors spreading through the home.
