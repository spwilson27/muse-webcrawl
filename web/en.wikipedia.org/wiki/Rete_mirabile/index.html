Rete mirabile

A rete mirabile (Latin for "wonderful net"; plural retia mirabilia) is a complex network of arteries and veins lying very close to each other, found in some vertebrates. The network functions as a countercurrent exchange system, transferring heat, ions or gases between the vessels flowing in opposite directions.

Functions

In many fish, a rete mirabile attached to the swim bladder uses countercurrent exchange of gases and the Root effect to generate very high partial pressures of oxygen, allowing the bladder to be filled even at great depths. In tuna and lamnid sharks, retia in the muscles and viscera conserve metabolic heat, supporting regional endothermy. In the legs of wading birds and the flippers of marine mammals, retia transfer heat from outgoing arterial blood to returning venous blood, reducing heat loss. Artiodactyls and some other mammals possess a carotid rete that cools arterial blood flowing to the brain using venous blood cooled in the nasal passages (selective brain cooling).

History

The term was coined by Galen, who described the carotid rete of ungulates and mistakenly believed a similar structure existed in humans and served as the seat of the soul's vital spirit — an error corrected by later anatomists such as Vesalius. In human anatomy the name survives in the rete mirabile of the hepatic portal system variants described by Haller.
