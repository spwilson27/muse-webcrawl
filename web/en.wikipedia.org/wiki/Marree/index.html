Marree is a small town in the north of South Australia, located where the Birdsville Track and the Oodnadatta Track branch from the road to Lyndhurst. It lies roughly 650 kilometres (400 miles) north of Adelaide, at the southern edge of the Lake Eyre basin. The town is a service centre for the surrounding cattle stations and for travellers on the outback tracks, including traffic connected with the Marree Man, a giant geoglyph discovered from the air nearby in 1998.

History

The town was first surveyed in 1883 near the Hergott Springs and known as Hergott Springs; the name was changed to Marree during the First World War, when names of German origin were replaced. Marree was an important stop on the original narrow-gauge Central Australia Railway — the line on which the famous Ghan train ran — reaching the town in 1884. For decades it was a railhead and transport hub: goods were transferred between trains and camel trains, and later road trains, for the journey into the far north.

Marree was one of the principal centres of Australia's Afghan cameleer community, the Muslim cameleers who underpinned outback transport in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The town had one of Australia's first mosques, built of mud brick by cameleers. When the standard-gauge railway was built on a new alignment further west in 1980, the old line through Marree closed and the town declined, though its railway heritage remains visible.

Marree Man

In 1998 a charter pilot discovered a huge figure of a man holding a throwing stick or boomerang etched into the desert plateau west of Marree. The geoglyph, about 4.2 kilometres long with a perimeter of some 28 kilometres, became known as the Marree Man. Its creators were never conclusively identified; the figure faded over time and was redrawn in 2016 using GPS-guided graders.
