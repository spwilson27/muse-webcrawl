D-Day (military term)

The term D-Day is used by the armed forces to refer to the day on which a combat attack or operation is to be initiated. "D-Day" is perhaps most famous for June 6, 1944 — the day of the Normandy landings in the Second World War — but the designation was used for many other invasions and operations before and since.

Terminology

The "D" in D-Day does not stand for any specific word; it is simply a placeholder for the actual date, which may not yet be known or may be secret. The same convention applies to "H-Hour", the time at which an operation begins. Days before or after D-Day are indicated with minus and plus signs: D-3 denotes three days before D-Day, and D+2 denotes two days after. The convention is still used by NATO and other armed forces when planning operations whose exact dates are to be determined later.

History

The earliest use of the term by the United States Army that the United States Center of Military History has been able to find was during the First World War: Field Order No. 9 of the First Army of the American Expeditionary Forces, dated 7 September 1918, ordered that "The First Army will attack at H hour on D day". In the Second World War the most famous D-Day was 6 June 1944, the beginning of the Normandy landings (Operation Overlord), the largest seaborne invasion in history, which began the liberation of German-occupied Western Europe. Other notable D-Days include the landings on Sicily, at Salerno and Anzio in Italy, and numerous island assaults in the Pacific theatre, such as those at Leyte, Iwo Jima and Okinawa.

Other meanings

Because of its association with the Normandy landings, "D-Day" has passed into general usage to mean any important day of reckoning or decision. In French it is rendered as Jour J.
