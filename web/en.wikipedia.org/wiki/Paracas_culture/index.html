Paracas culture

Periodc. 800 BCE – 100 BCE
RegionSouth coast of Peru (Ica Region)
Major sitesParacas Peninsula, Cerro Colorado
Preceded byChavín influence
Followed byNazca culture

The Paracas culture was an Andean society that flourished on the Paracas Peninsula and the south coast of present-day Peru between roughly 800 BCE and 100 BCE. It is renowned above all for its textiles — among the finest ever produced in the pre-Columbian Americas — and for its elaborate mummy bundles, cranial deformation, and evidence of trepanation.

Discovery

The culture was identified in the 1920s by Peruvian archaeologist Julio C. Tello, who excavated cemeteries at Cerro Colorado on the peninsula. Tello distinguished two phases: Paracas Cavernas, with shaft tombs, and Paracas Necropolis, whose dead were wrapped in layer upon layer of embroidered cloth to form large mummy bundles containing some of the most spectacular woven garments known from the ancient world.

Textiles

Paracas weavers used plain-weave cotton and camelid (alpaca and vicuña) wool, embroidered in the stem-stitch with vividly dyed yarns. Mantles depict flying shamans, the "Oculate Being," trophy heads, animals, and hybrid creatures in repeated block patterns. The dry desert climate preserved the fabrics in extraordinary condition.

Other practices

Paracas skulls frequently show artificial cranial deformation, producing elongated head shapes, and many bear healed trepanation holes — surgical openings cut into the skull, apparently with a notable survival rate. The culture was succeeded in the region by the Nazca culture, famous for the Nazca Lines, which developed directly from Paracas traditions.
