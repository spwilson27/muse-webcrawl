The Haughton–Mars Project (HMP) is an international interdisciplinary field research project centred on the scientific study of the Haughton impact crater and surrounding terrain on Devon Island, in Canada's High Arctic, viewed as an analogue for the Moon and Mars. Led by planetary scientist Pascal Lee, the project is managed jointly by the Mars Institute and the SETI Institute in collaboration with NASA, and is the largest and longest-running planetary analogue field research project of its kind.

Setting

Devon Island is the largest uninhabited island on Earth. Its polar desert landscape — cold, dry, rocky and largely barren — and the 23-million-year-old Haughton crater, about 20 km in diameter, reproduce many features of lunar and Martian terrain: impact-shocked rocks, hydrothermal deposits, permafrost landforms, valleys and gullies carved by meltwater, and ancient lake sediments.

Research

Each summer since 1997, the HMP Research Station has hosted geologists, astrobiologists, engineers and mission planners. Work includes studying the crater's geology and microbiology, testing exploration technologies such as prototype spacesuits, rovers, drones and drilling systems, and running simulated planetary surface operations to develop strategies for human exploration. The nearby Flashline Mars Arctic Research Station, operated by the Mars Society, is a separate facility also located in the crater area.
