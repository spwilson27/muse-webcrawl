The Altar of Victory was an altar in the Curia Julia, the Senate house of ancient Rome, erected by Augustus in 29 BC to commemorate his victory at the Battle of Actium. Before it stood a golden statue of Victoria, the winged goddess of victory, captured from Tarentum; senators traditionally burned incense and poured libations at the altar before sessions and swore their oaths of loyalty there. In the fourth century AD the altar became the most famous symbol of the struggle between traditional Roman paganism and ascendant Christianity.

Removals and controversy

The Christian emperor Constantius II had the altar removed during his visit to Rome in 357; it was restored, probably under Julian, and removed again by Gratian in 382, who also confiscated the revenues of the pagan priesthoods and removed the statue. The pagan senator Quintus Aurelius Symmachus petitioned for its restoration, most famously in his Relatio III of 384, arguing for religious tolerance with the celebrated reflection that "it is not by one road alone that men can arrive at so great a secret." Ambrose, bishop of Milan, answered with forceful letters, and the petition failed. Further requests in 389 and 392–394 also failed; after 394 the altar disappears from the record, and nothing of it survives today.

Significance

The exchange between Symmachus and Ambrose is a classic document of the late Roman religious transition and is still cited in arguments about the separation of church and state and about religious pluralism.
