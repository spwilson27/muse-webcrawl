Feral pigeons (Columba livia domestica) are pigeons that are descended from domestic pigeons that have become feral. The domestic pigeon, in turn, was bred from the rock dove, which naturally inhabits sea cliffs and mountains. Feral pigeons find the ledges of buildings to be a substitute for sea cliffs, have become adapted to urban life, and are abundant in towns and cities all over the world.

Description

Feral pigeons are essentially the same size and shape as the original wild rock dove, but show much greater variation in colour and pattern. The blue-barred pattern of the rock dove is common, but city populations include checker, red, pied, white, and near-black birds, a legacy of centuries of selective breeding in dovecotes, for racing, and for fancy breeds. The average city pigeon weighs about 300 grams and lives two to three years in the wild, though individuals may live far longer.

Behaviour and ecology

Pigeons mate for life and can breed throughout the year, raising two squabs at a time and producing several broods annually — a key reason for their urban success. They feed on seeds and human food waste, and they are notable navigators: the homing instinct that made them messenger birds persists in feral stock. Their main urban predators include peregrine falcons, which are often encouraged to nest in cities partly for pigeon control.

Feral pigeons are sometimes labelled a pest because their droppings foul buildings and can carry diseases such as histoplasmosis and psittacosis, though public-health authorities generally rate the risk to healthy people as low. Cities have tried deterrents, spikes, contraceptive feed, and managed dovecotes; feeding bans are common but contested. Pigeons also remain culturally significant, from the famous flocks of Trafalgar Square in London and St Mark's Square in Venice to their celebrated wartime service as message carriers.
