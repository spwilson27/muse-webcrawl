Binary asteroid

90 Antiope, a binary system of two roughly equal components.

A binary asteroid is a system of two asteroids orbiting their common barycenter. Binaries are common among minor planets: roughly one in six near-Earth asteroids larger than a few hundred metres has a companion, and binary systems are also known in the main belt, among Trojans, and in the Kuiper belt.

Discovery and observation

The first binary asteroid to be imaged directly was 243 Ida and its moon Dactyl, photographed by the Galileo spacecraft in 1993. Most binaries have been detected by radar imaging (at Goldstone and the former Arecibo Observatory), by photometric light-curve observations of mutual eclipses, and by adaptive-optics imaging from large telescopes. Measuring a binary's orbit allows astronomers to determine the system's mass and hence the bulk density of the bodies — one of the few direct ways to probe asteroid interiors.

Formation

Near-Earth binaries are thought to form mainly through the YORP effect: sunlight slowly spins up a rubble-pile asteroid until it sheds mass that coalesces into a satellite. Other mechanisms include tidal disruption during close planetary encounters, capture, and collisions. The binary 90 Antiope consists of two nearly equal bodies that evidently formed from a single disrupted parent.

Notable examples

Well-studied binaries include 90 Antiope, 87 Sylvia (a triple system), 216 Kleopatra, 66391 Moshup, and the near-Earth asteroid Didymos and its moon Dimorphos, the target of NASA's DART planetary-defence test in 2022, which measurably altered Dimorphos's orbital period.
