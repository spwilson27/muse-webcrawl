Sacramental union

Sacramental union (Latin: unio sacramentalis; Luther's German: Sakramentliche Einigkeit; German: sakramentliche Vereinigung) is the Lutheran theological doctrine of the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist, according to which the body and blood of Christ are truly present and distributed "in, with, and under" the forms of the consecrated bread and wine.

Doctrine

The doctrine holds that in the celebration of the Lord's Supper, the body and blood of Christ are not merely signified or spiritually received, but are sacramentally united to the bread and wine, so that those who eat and drink the elements also receive the true body and blood of Christ. The union is termed "sacramental" because it is not a natural, local, or permanent conjunction of the elements with Christ's body, but one that exists in the action of the sacrament by virtue of Christ's institution and word of promise.

Lutherans reject the Roman Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation, which teaches that the substance of the bread and wine is converted into the substance of Christ's body and blood while the accidents remain. They likewise reject the memorialist view associated with Huldrych Zwingli, in which the Supper is only a sign or commemoration. Instead, the Formula of Concord confesses that "in the Holy Supper the body and blood of Christ are truly and substantially present and are truly offered, taken, and received with the bread and wine."

History

The term was developed during the eucharistic controversies of the Reformation. At the Marburg Colloquy of 1529, Martin Luther and Zwingli agreed on fourteen articles but divided over the mode of Christ's presence in the Supper. Luther insisted on the words of institution, "This is my body," writing them on the table before him. The Wittenberg Concord of 1536 brought Lutherans and South German reformers closer by confessing that "with the bread the body of Christ is truly present and offered." The doctrine received its definitive confessional form in Article VII of the Formula of Concord (1577), which both affirmed the sacramental union and rejected errors on either side.

"In, with, and under"

The phrase "in, with, and under the bread and wine" (German: in, mit und unter) became the popular shorthand for the doctrine. It seeks to confess the reality of the presence while denying that the union is a local inclusion or a mixture of substances. Because the phrase does not appear in the Lutheran Confessions themselves, later Lutheran theologians have treated it as a helpful summary rather than a confessional formula in its own right.
