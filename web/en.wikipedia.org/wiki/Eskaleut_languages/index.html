Eskaleut (Eskimo–Aleut)

Region: Alaska, northern Canada, Greenland, Russian Far East (Chukotka)
Branches: Aleut (Unangam Tunuu); Eskimo (Yupik and Inuit languages)
Speakers: roughly 100,000+ in total
Typology: Polysynthetic, ergative–absolutive

The Eskaleut languages — formerly called Eskimo–Aleut — are a language family native to the Arctic coasts of Alaska, northern Canada (Nunavut, the Inuvialuit Settlement Region, Nunavik, and Nunatsiavut), Greenland, and the Russian Far East (Chukotka). The newer name "Eskaleut" avoids the word "Eskimo", which is considered offensive by many in Canada and Greenland, while combining the names of the family's two branches.

Branches

The family divides into two branches:

  • Aleut (Unangam Tunuu), spoken in the Aleutian and Pribilof Islands, with a handful of speakers also in Russia's Commander Islands.
  • Eskimo, which divides further into:
    • Yupik languages — Central Alaskan Yup'ik, Alutiiq (Pacific Gulf Yupik), Central Siberian Yupik, Naukan, and the extinct Sirenik language of Chukotka.
    • Inuit languages — a dialect continuum stretching from Iñupiaq in northern Alaska through Inuvialuktun and Inuktitut in Canada to Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) in Greenland, which has tens of thousands of speakers and official status.

Features

Eskaleut languages are famous for polysynthesis: a single word, built from a root plus many derivational and inflectional suffixes, can express what requires a whole sentence in English — the classic example is Greenlandic words conventionally glossed like "they say he hasn't yet said again that he wants to go". They are ergative–absolutive in alignment, have relatively small consonant inventories in many varieties, and possess rich systems of demonstratives encoding fine spatial distinctions. Postbases — derivational suffixes numbering in the hundreds — are a hallmark of the family.

History and status

The family's homeland is thought to lie near the Bering Strait, with the Aleut branch splitting off several thousand years ago and the Inuit expansion eastward across the Canadian Arctic to Greenland occurring within roughly the last thousand years, associated with the Thule culture. Most Eskaleut languages are endangered, though Greenlandic is vigorous and official in Greenland, and Inuktitut has official status in Nunavut; revitalisation efforts are under way for Yupik and Iñupiaq in Alaska. Writing systems include Latin orthographies in Canada and Greenland, Cyrillic for Siberian Yupik in Russia, and the Canadian Aboriginal syllabics for some Inuktitut varieties.
