An earthquake warning system, or earthquake early warning (EEW) system, is a network of seismometers and communications systems designed to detect an earthquake as it begins and to alert people and automated systems seconds to tens of seconds before the strongest shaking arrives. Such systems do not predict earthquakes; they exploit the fact that the fast-moving, less damaging primary (P) waves travel through the Earth more quickly than the slower, destructive secondary (S) waves and surface waves, and that electronic signals travel faster still.

How it works

Dense networks of seismometers near known fault zones detect the initial P-waves and rapidly estimate the earthquake's location, magnitude and expected shaking intensity. If shaking above a threshold is expected, alerts are distributed to the public and to operators of trains, elevators, factories, hospitals and power systems, which can take automatic protective action. Warning time depends on distance from the epicentre: places very close to the source may receive little or no warning (the "blind zone"), while cities 100 km away may gain 20–30 seconds or more.

Operational systems

Japan operates the world's most advanced public system, run by the Japan Meteorological Agency since 2007, which broadcasts alerts by television, radio and mobile phone and automatically slows bullet trains. During the great Tōhoku earthquake of 2011 the system gave Sendai roughly 15 seconds of warning and Tokyo about a minute. Mexico's Seismic Alert System (SASMEX), built after the devastating 1985 Mexico City earthquake, provides warnings for the capital from subduction-zone earthquakes off the Pacific coast. In the United States, the ShakeAlert system, developed by the US Geological Survey with university partners, delivers alerts along the West Coast of California, Oregon and Washington, including through mobile phone apps and the Wireless Emergency Alerts system. Other systems operate in Taiwan, Turkey, Romania, China, India, South Korea and elsewhere.

Limitations

Early estimates of magnitude can saturate for the very largest earthquakes, warning times are inherently short, and false or missed alerts occur. Nevertheless, even a few seconds can allow people to drop, cover and hold on, and allow automated systems to stop trains, open fire-station doors, pause surgery and shut gas valves.
