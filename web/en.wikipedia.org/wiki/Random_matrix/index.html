Random matrix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A random matrix is a matrix-valued random variable: a matrix whose entries are random numbers drawn from some probability distribution. Random matrix theory studies the statistical properties of such matrices — especially the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of large matrices — and has found applications in physics, statistics, finance, wireless communications and machine learning.

History and key results

The subject began in statistics with John Wishart's work in 1928 and in nuclear physics with Eugene Wigner's proposal in the 1950s that the energy levels of heavy atomic nuclei behave like the eigenvalues of random matrices. For large matrices from the classical Gaussian ensembles, the distribution of eigenvalues converges to the semicircle law, while the spacing between neighbouring eigenvalues follows the Wigner surmise, exhibiting "level repulsion" — eigenvalues are far less likely to be close together than independent random points would be.

Applications

Random matrix statistics describe quantum chaotic systems, the zeros of the Riemann zeta function (linked through the Montgomery–Odlyzko law), the singular values of large data sets in principal component analysis, and channel capacity in multiple-antenna wireless systems. Central further results include the Marchenko–Pastur law for sample covariance matrices and the Tracy–Widom distribution for the largest eigenvalue, which appears across probability, combinatorics and growth processes.
