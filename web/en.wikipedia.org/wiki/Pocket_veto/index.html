Pocket veto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pocket veto is a legislative maneuver in the United States by which the President can indirectly veto a bill: under Article I, Section 7, if Congress adjourns within the ten days (Sundays excepted) allowed for presidential action, a bill the President neither signs nor returns simply fails to become law — it is “pocketed”. Unlike a regular veto, a pocket veto cannot be overridden, because there is no bill returned to override and Congress is no longer sitting to do so.

The device depends on the meaning of adjournment: modern practice (intersession adjournments and the use of agents to receive messages) has narrowed the true pocket veto largely to final adjournments of a Congress, and presidents often issue “protective return” vetoes claiming both effects. Franklin D. Roosevelt used the pocket veto most extensively (263 times); in total, US presidents have used it more than a thousand times, with regular vetoes outnumbering them overall. Analogous powers exist in some other systems (the Indian President's indefinite withholding of assent, used famously by Zail Singh in 1986–87 over the Postal Bill).
