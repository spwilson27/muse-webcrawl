Yellow fever

CauseYellow fever virus (Flaviviridae)
TransmissionMosquito bite (Aedes and other genera)
SymptomsFever, chills, headache, jaundice, haemorrhage
Prevention17D vaccine; mosquito control

Yellow fever is an acute viral haemorrhagic disease transmitted by infected mosquitoes. The "yellow" in the name refers to the jaundice that affects some patients. The disease is caused by the yellow fever virus, an RNA virus of the genus Flavivirus, and is endemic in tropical areas of Africa and Central and South America.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of three to six days, most infected people develop a sudden fever, chills, severe headache, back pain, muscle aches, nausea, and fatigue; most improve within a week. About 15% of cases enter a second, toxic phase marked by jaundice, abdominal pain, vomiting of blood, bleeding from the mouth, nose, and eyes, and liver and kidney failure. Of those who enter the toxic phase, 20–50% die.

Transmission

The virus circulates in sylvatic (jungle) cycles between mosquitoes and non-human primates and in urban cycles between mosquitoes — principally Aedes aegypti — and humans. There is no person-to-person transmission.

History

The virus originated in Africa and was carried to the Americas during the slave trade. Devastating epidemics struck port cities including Philadelphia (1793), Barcelona (1821), and Memphis (1878). Walter Reed's experiments in Cuba in 1900–1901 confirmed mosquito transmission, enabling control measures that allowed completion of the Panama Canal. In 1937, Max Theiler developed the 17D vaccine, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1951.

Prevention and treatment

A single dose of the live-attenuated 17D vaccine provides lifelong protection for most recipients, and proof of vaccination is required for entry into many countries. There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive. The World Health Organization's EYE strategy (Eliminate Yellow fever Epidemics) aims to end epidemics by 2026 through mass vaccination, rapid outbreak response, and surveillance.
