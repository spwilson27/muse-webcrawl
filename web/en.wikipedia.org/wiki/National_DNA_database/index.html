National DNA database

A national DNA database is a government-maintained repository of DNA profiles used by law-enforcement agencies to identify suspects by matching biological material recovered from crime scenes against stored profiles, and to link offences to one another. Profiles are typically generated from short tandem repeat (STR) markers that do not code for physical traits.

Major databases

The United Kingdom's National DNA Database (NDNAD), established in 1995, was the first of its kind and remains among the largest per capita. In the United States, the FBI's Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) links federal, state, and local databases and holds tens of millions of offender and arrestee profiles. Other substantial programmes operate in France (FNAEG), Germany, China, Brazil, and the member states of the European Union, which exchange profiles under the Prüm framework.

Operation

Samples are collected — usually by buccal swab — from convicted offenders, and in some jurisdictions from arrestees, suspects, or volunteers. Crime-scene profiles that match a stored individual generate an investigative lead, not proof of guilt. Familial searching, which looks for partial matches indicating a relative, is permitted in some jurisdictions and restricted in others.

Criticism and legal challenges

Civil-liberties concerns centre on the retention of profiles from people never convicted, racial disproportionality, function creep toward trait prediction, and breaches or misuse. In S. and Marper v United Kingdom (2008), the European Court of Human Rights held that blanket indefinite retention of profiles of unconvicted persons violated the right to privacy, prompting the UK's Protection of Freedoms Act 2012 to require deletion in most such cases. The rise of consumer genetic-genealogy databases — used to identify the Golden State Killer in 2018 — has raised further ethical questions distinct from official databases.
