Neutron temperature

The neutron temperature, also called the neutron energy, indicates a free neutron's kinetic energy, usually given in electronvolts. The term temperature is used because hot, thermal, and cold neutrons are moderated in a medium with a certain temperature. The neutron energy distribution is then adapted to the Maxwell distribution known for thermal motion. Qualitatively, the higher the temperature, the higher the kinetic energy of the free neutrons.

Classification

Neutrons are conventionally grouped by energy:

CategoryEnergy range
Cold neutronsbelow about 0.025 eV (down to ultracold, < 3×10−7 eV)
Thermal neutronsabout 0.025 eV, in equilibrium with room-temperature surroundings
Epithermal neutronsroughly 0.025 eV to 1 eV
Resonance / intermediate neutronsabout 1 eV to 10 keV
Fast neutronsabove about 10 keV, typically ~1 MeV from fission

A thermal neutron at 0.0253 eV has a speed of about 2,200 m/s and a de Broglie wavelength near 1.8 ångströms, comparable to interatomic spacings — which is why thermal and cold neutrons are used for neutron scattering and diffraction studies of materials and biological structures.

Moderation

Neutrons born in fission have energies around 2 MeV and must be slowed down (moderated) in materials such as light water, heavy water, or graphite to thermal energies, where the probability of inducing further fission in uranium-235 is far higher. The temperature of the moderator therefore affects reactor behaviour: in most thermal reactors a rise in moderator temperature reduces its density and reactivity, a negative feedback that contributes to safety. Conversely, fast reactors operate with unmoderated fast neutrons. See also neutropenia for an unrelated medical term sharing the "neutro-" prefix.
