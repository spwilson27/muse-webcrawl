Pilot error

The flight deck environment, in which human performance is a critical factor in aviation safety.

Pilot error generally refers to an action or decision of an aircraft pilot that is identified as a cause or contributing factor in an aviation accident or incident. It is the most commonly cited category in accident statistics: studies of airline accidents typically attribute a majority — often around half to three quarters — of events wholly or partly to the flight crew, though modern safety science regards "error" as a symptom of wider system weaknesses rather than a final explanation.

Categories

Investigators commonly distinguish decision errors (poor choices, such as continuing into bad weather), skill-based errors (failures of basic flying technique or attention), and perceptual errors (misreading instruments or the environment, including spatial disorientation). Violations — deliberate departures from procedures — are usually classified separately from unintentional error.

Contributing factors

Fatigue, high workload, poor crew resource management (CRM), confusing automation behaviour ("automation surprise"), inadequate training, and organisational pressure all increase the likelihood of error. Classic cases include the Tenerife airport disaster (1977), in which a captain began takeoff without clearance in fog, and controlled flight into terrain accidents in which crews descended below safe altitudes.

Mitigation

Responses include CRM training, standardised checklists and callouts, sterile-cockpit rules, ground-proximity warning systems and other alerting technology, fatigue regulation, and "just culture" reporting systems that encourage crews to report mistakes so that systemic fixes can be made.
