Aldehyde

Aldehyde
Functional group–CHO (formyl group)
General formulaR–CHO
Simplest memberFormaldehyde (methanal)

An aldehyde is an organic compound containing a functional group with the structure R–CHO, consisting of a carbonyl centre (a carbon–oxygen double bond) bonded to hydrogen and to an R group, which may be an alkyl or aryl side chain. Aldehydes are a major class of reactive organic compounds widely used in synthesis and found throughout nature and industry.

Structure and bonding

The carbonyl carbon of an aldehyde is sp²-hybridised and trigonal planar. The polar carbon–oxygen double bond makes aldehydes reactive toward nucleophiles and reducing or oxidising agents. Unlike ketones, aldehydes bear a hydrogen atom on the carbonyl carbon, which makes them easier to oxidise to carboxylic acids.

Nomenclature

In IUPAC nomenclature, aldehydes take the suffix -al: methanal (formaldehyde), ethanal (acetaldehyde), propanal, and benzaldehyde for the aromatic compound C₆H₅CHO. The carbon of the aldehyde group is assigned position 1 in the parent chain.

Preparation

Aldehydes are commonly prepared by the controlled oxidation of primary alcohols, by ozonolysis of alkenes, by reduction of carboxylic acid derivatives (for example with DIBAL-H), and industrially by hydroformylation of alkenes.

Reactions

Characteristic reactions include nucleophilic addition (forming hemiacetals and acetals with alcohols, imines with amines), oxidation to carboxylic acids (detected by Tollens' and Fehling's tests), reduction to primary alcohols, and enolate chemistry such as the aldol reaction.

Occurrence and uses

Formaldehyde is produced on a huge industrial scale for resins and disinfectants. Natural aldehydes include cinnamaldehyde (the flavour of cinnamon), vanillin, retinal (essential for vision) and the sugar glucose, which exists partly in an open-chain aldehyde form.
