Endorphins

Endorphins (contracted from "endogenous morphine") are endogenous opioid neuropeptides and peptide hormones produced by the central nervous system and the pituitary gland. They bind to opioid receptors in the brain and body, producing analgesia and feelings of well-being. The principal endorphins are α-endorphin, β-endorphin, and γ-endorphin, cleaved from the precursor protein pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC); β-endorphin, a 31-amino-acid peptide, is the most studied.

Discovery

The existence of endogenous opioids was inferred in the early 1970s from the discovery of opioid receptors in the brain. In 1975, John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz isolated the first such peptides, the enkephalins, from pig brain, and β-endorphin was isolated from pituitary extracts shortly afterwards by several groups. The name "endorphin" was coined as a generic term for these "morphine-like substances formed within the body."

Functions

Endorphins are released in response to pain and stress, where they inhibit the transmission of pain signals and buffer the stress response. They act on the same μ-opioid receptors targeted by morphine, though with much shorter duration in the body. Release also occurs during exercise, laughter, and social bonding. The popular notion of a "runner's high" was long attributed to endorphins; modern research suggests endocannabinoids such as anandamide, which cross the blood–brain barrier more readily, may play the larger role, though endorphins contribute to post-exercise analgesia.

Regulation and storage

Endorphins are produced from POMC in the pituitary gland and hypothalamus and stored in vesicles until released. In the pituitary, POMC cleavage also yields ACTH and melanocyte-stimulating hormones, so endorphin release accompanies the hormonal stress response. Their activity is terminated by peptidase enzymes, and the opioid antagonist naloxone blocks their effects, which is how researchers demonstrate endorphin involvement experimentally.
