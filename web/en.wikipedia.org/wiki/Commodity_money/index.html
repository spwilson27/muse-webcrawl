Commodity money

Gold and silver coins are classic examples of commodity money.

Commodity money is money whose value comes from a commodity of which it is made. Commodity money consists of objects having value or use in themselves (intrinsic value) as well as value in buying goods. This is in contrast to representative money, which has no intrinsic value but represents something of value such as gold or silver, and fiat money, which has value only because a government decrees it.

History

Many commodities have served as money at different times and places, including precious metals, cowrie shells, salt, cattle, tobacco, cocoa beans, large stone disks (such as the Rai stones of Yap), and cigarettes in prisoner-of-war camps and post-war economies. Commodity money systems evolved as barter economies grew too cumbersome for direct exchange.

Metals as money

Gold and silver became the most widespread forms of commodity money because they are durable, divisible, portable, fungible, and relatively scarce. Coins made of precious metals were minted with weights and purity guaranteed by an issuing authority, reducing the cost of verifying value in transactions. The gold standard later formalised the link between paper currency and a fixed quantity of gold.

Characteristics

For a commodity to function well as money it generally needs to be:

  • widely accepted and recognised;
  • durable and not perishable;
  • divisible into smaller units;
  • uniform or fungible;
  • portable relative to its value; and
  • limited in supply.

Problems

Commodity money ties the money supply to the supply of the underlying commodity, which can cause deflation when output of the commodity lags economic growth, or inflation when large new deposits are discovered, as happened with American silver in the sixteenth century. Debasement — reducing the precious metal content of coins — was a recurring source of inflation in pre-modern states.
