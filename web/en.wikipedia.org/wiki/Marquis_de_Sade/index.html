Donatien Alphonse François, Marquis de Sade (2 June 1740 – 2 December 1814) was a French writer, libertine, political activist, and nobleman best known for his libertine novels and imprisonment for sex crimes, blasphemy, and pornography. His works include novels, short stories, plays, dialogues, and political tracts. Some of them were published under his own name during his lifetime, but most appeared anonymously or posthumously.

Sade is best known for his erotic works, which combined philosophical discourse with pornography, depicting sexual fantasies with an emphasis on violence, suffering, criminality, and blasphemy against Christianity. His works are a depiction of extreme absolute freedom, unrestrained by morality, religion, or law. The words sadism and sadist are derived from his name.

Life and imprisonments

Born into one of Provence's oldest noble families, Sade served as a cavalry officer in the Seven Years' War. A series of scandals involving prostitutes and servants led to repeated arrests; in 1772 he was sentenced to death in absentia after the "Marseille affair", fled to Italy, and was later held under a lettre de cachet at Vincennes and the Bastille, where in 1785 he wrote The 120 Days of Sodom on a scroll of paper. Transferred days before the Bastille fell in 1789, he lost the manuscript and believed it destroyed. Freed during the Revolution, he became a citizen-delegate but was arrested again under the Terror, then finally under Napoleon for the novels Justine and Juliette; he died in the asylum of Charenton in 1814, having spent roughly 32 years confined.

Legacy

Long suppressed, Sade's work was championed by the surrealists and thinkers such as Simone de Beauvoir, and the publication of his complete works in the mid-20th century fuelled debates on freedom of expression, censorship, and the philosophy of transgression. Major works include Justine, or The Misfortunes of Virtue (1791), Philosophy in the Bedroom (1795), Juliette (1797–1801), and The 120 Days of Sodom, first published in 1904.
