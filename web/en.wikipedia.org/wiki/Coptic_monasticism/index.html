Coptic monasticism is the monastic tradition of the Coptic Orthodox Church of Egypt, and the historical origin of Christian monasticism generally. Beginning in the late 3rd century, Egyptian Christians withdrew to the desert to pursue lives of prayer, asceticism and contemplation, creating the patterns — anchoritic (solitary) and cenobitic (communal) — that spread throughout the Christian world.

Origins

Anthony the Great (c. 251–356) is honoured as the father of monasticism; his Life by Athanasius of Alexandria spread the monastic ideal across the Roman world. Around the same period, Pachomius founded the first cenobitic monasteries in Upper Egypt, gathering monks under a rule, common worship and organised labour. The deserts of Nitria, Kellia and Scetis (modern Wadi Natrun) became home to thousands of monks, whose sayings were collected in the Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers).

Development

Figures such as Macarius of Egypt, Evagrius Ponticus and Shenoute shaped Coptic monastic spirituality. Monasteries became centres of manuscript production, preserving both Coptic and Greek texts. Monasticism survived the Arab conquest of Egypt, though monasteries suffered periodic persecution; the patriarchate itself was for centuries associated with the Monastery of Saint Macarius. Bishops of the Coptic Church are traditionally chosen from among monks.

Modern revival

The 20th century saw a marked revival: ancient desert monasteries such as those of St Anthony and St Paul at the Red Sea, St Macarius, St Pishoy and the Syrian Monastery in Wadi Natrun were repopulated, and new communities founded. Under Pope Shenouda III (pope 1971–2012), himself a monk, hundreds of men and women entered monastic life, and Coptic monasteries were established in Europe, North America and Australia.
