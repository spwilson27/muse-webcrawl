Neoclassical economics

[Image: a chalkboard filled with marginal-utility curves and optimisation equations]

Neoclassical economics is the dominant approach in modern mainstream Economics, arising from the marginal revolution of the 1870s — independently in Jevons's England, Menger's Austria and Walras's Switzerland — which replaced the classical labour theory of value with marginal Utility: prices reflect the value of one more unit, not total usefulness (resolving the water–diamond paradox of Adam Smith).

Framework

Neoclassical theory models rational agents optimising subject to constraints, markets reaching equilibrium through Supply and demand, and — in Walrasian general equilibrium, formalised by Arrow and Debreu in the 1950s — all markets clearing simultaneously, with the fundamental theorems of welfare economics stating when the outcome is efficient. Alfred Marshall synthesised marginalism with classical concerns in the approach still taught in Microeconomics: constrained optimisation, elasticity, and cost curves. Critics — Keynesians, Austrians, Marxians, institutionalists and behavioural economists — attack its assumptions about rationality, information, equilibrium and distribution; the neoclassical synthesis nonetheless frames most contemporary research and the standard textbook model.
