The Bergius process is the direct liquefaction of coal by high-temperature, high-pressure hydrogenation, producing liquid hydrocarbons usable as synthetic fuel. It was developed by the German chemist Friedrich Bergius, who received the patent in 1913 and shared the 1931 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Carl Bosch for the invention and development of chemical high-pressure methods.

Chemistry

Pulverised coal is mixed into a slurry with heavy oil and a catalyst (historically iron-based or tin compounds), then reacted with hydrogen at about 400–500 °C and pressures of several hundred atmospheres. The large polynuclear aromatic structures of coal are thermally cracked into free radicals, which are capped ("stabilised") by hydrogen, preventing re-polymerisation and yielding lighter, hydrogen-richer liquid products. The net effect: (CH)n (coal) + H2 → liquid hydrocarbons of lower carbon-to-hydrogen ratio. Output is separated into motor petrol, diesel, aviation gasoline feedstock and heavy oils, the heaviest fraction being recycled as slurry oil.

Industrial history

IG Farben industrialised the process at Leuna from 1927 (the "Leuna-Benzin" synthetic petrol). Under Nazi Germany's autarky programme, hydrogenation plants — using the Bergius process alongside the indirect Fischer–Tropsch process — became a principal source of fuel, especially aviation gasoline, and were priority targets of the Allied oil campaign of 1944–45. After the war the process was uneconomic against cheap petroleum; interest revived during the oil crises and in coal-rich economies. A modern descendant, the Shenhua direct coal liquefaction plant in Inner Mongolia, China (operational from 2008), is the world's largest direct liquefaction facility.
