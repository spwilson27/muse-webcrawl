Maunder Minimum

The Maunder Minimum, also known as the "prolonged sunspot minimum", is a period around 1645 to 1715 during which sunspots became exceedingly rare. During a 28-year period (1672–1699) within the minimum, observations revealed fewer than 50 sunspots — compared to the typical 40,000–50,000 spots seen in modern times over a similar span.

Together with the later Spörer Minimum (c. 1460–1550) and Dalton Minimum (c. 1790–1830), it is one of the grand minima of solar activity of the last millennium.

History of the concept

The unusually low activity was first noted in the 19th century by Gustav Spörer and publicised by Edward Walter Maunder, after whom it is named. Solar astronomer Jack Eddy brought the phenomenon to wide attention in a landmark 1976 paper in Science, combining historical sunspot records with carbon-14 evidence from tree rings showing a real reduction in solar activity, not merely gaps in observation.

Observations

Astronomers such as Johannes Hevelius and Giovanni Domenico Cassini recorded few spots during the period; Cassini noted a "new" spot in 1671 after years of rarity. The few sunspots that did appear were concentrated in the Sun's southern hemisphere, and auroras were also reported less frequently.

Climate connection

The Maunder Minimum coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age, when the Thames froze in London frost fairs and Norse Greenland settlements were abandoned. A reduction in total solar irradiance of a few tenths of a percent is thought to have contributed to regional cooling, especially in Europe, though volcanic eruptions and internal variability were also major factors; the link remains an active topic of study.
