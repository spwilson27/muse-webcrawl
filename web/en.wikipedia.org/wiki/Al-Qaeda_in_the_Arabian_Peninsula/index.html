Al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula (AQAP) is a militant Islamist organisation, formed in January 2009 by the merger of al-Qaeda's Yemeni and Saudi branches. Based principally in Yemen, it has been regarded by the United States for much of its existence as the most dangerous of al-Qaeda's regional affiliates because of its repeated attempts to attack international aviation and targets in the West.

History

AQAP emerged after Saudi counterterrorism campaigns drove Saudi militants into Yemen, where they joined Yemeni veterans of the Afghan jihad. The group announced itself with an attempted assassination of a Saudi counterterrorism chief in 2009. On 25 December 2009, a Nigerian operative, Umar Farouk Abdulmutallab, attempted to detonate explosives concealed in his underwear aboard Northwest Airlines Flight 253 over Detroit; the plot was directed by AQAP, whose bombmaker Ibrahim al-Asiri pioneered concealable non-metallic devices. In 2010 AQAP placed printer-cartridge bombs on cargo aircraft, discovered before detonation, and launched the English-language magazine Inspire, associated with the American-born cleric Anwar al-Awlaki, who was killed in a US drone strike in Yemen in September 2011.

Insurgency in Yemen

Amid Yemen's 2011 uprising, AQAP's local front, Ansar al-Sharia, seized towns in Abyan and Shabwa governorates before being driven out. During the Yemeni civil war from 2015, AQAP took the port city of Mukalla, governing it for about a year until Emirati-backed forces retook it in 2016. The group has since waged a lower-level insurgency in the south and east, competing with the Islamic State's Yemeni affiliate and suffering attrition from US strikes that killed emirs Nasir al-Wuhayshi (2015) and Qasim al-Raymi (2020). AQAP claimed responsibility for the January 2015 Charlie Hebdo attack in Paris, saying it had directed the Kouachi brothers.
