Seismic risk

Seismic risk refers to the risk of damage from earthquakes to a building, system, or other entity. It is commonly defined as the combination of seismic hazard, the exposure of people and assets, and their vulnerability: in other words, the expected human, economic, and environmental losses from earthquake phenomena such as ground shaking, ground failure, tsunamis, and fire following an earthquake.

Components

  • Seismic hazard — the probability that ground motion of a given intensity will occur at a site within a specified time period. Hazard is determined by the regional seismicity, fault locations, and local soil conditions.
  • Exposure — the inventory of people, buildings, infrastructure, and economic value located in the hazard zone.
  • Vulnerability — the propensity of the exposed elements to suffer damage at a given level of shaking, expressed through fragility curves for particular building types.

Assessment

Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) estimates the likelihood of exceeding various ground-motion levels, integrating uncertainties in earthquake location, size, and ground-motion prediction. Risk assessments multiply hazard by exposure and vulnerability to produce loss estimates, such as probable maximum loss used by insurers and catastrophe models, or casualty estimates used in emergency planning.

Mitigation

Seismic risk can be reduced by land-use planning that avoids the most hazardous sites, by building codes requiring earthquake-resistant design, by retrofitting vulnerable structures — particularly unreinforced masonry and non-ductile concrete buildings — and by early warning systems that provide seconds of notice before strong shaking arrives.
