Paris Peace Conference (1919–1920)

Date18 January 1919 – 21 January 1920
LocationParis and Versailles, France
ParticipantsDelegations of 32 Allied and associated states

The Paris Peace Conference was a set of formal diplomatic meetings in 1919 and 1920, after the end of the First World War, in which the victorious Allies set the peace terms for the defeated Central Powers. Dominated by the leaders of Britain, France, Italy and the United States — the "Big Four": David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau, Vittorio Emanuele Orlando and Woodrow Wilson — the conference produced five treaties and created the League of Nations.

Proceedings

The conference opened on 18 January 1919. Decisions were made first by the Council of Ten and later by the Council of Four. Germany and its allies were excluded from the negotiations and presented with the terms largely as a fait accompli. Wilson promoted his Fourteen Points and the League of Nations; Clemenceau sought security against Germany and reparations; Lloyd George tried to balance French demands against the revival of European trade; Orlando pressed Italian territorial claims and walked out temporarily over Fiume.

Results

The Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) with Germany imposed disarmament, territorial losses, the "war guilt" clause (Article 231) and reparations. Separate treaties followed: Saint-Germain with Austria, Neuilly with Bulgaria, Trianon with Hungary and Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire (later replaced by Lausanne). The conference redistributed German colonies and Ottoman territories as League of Nations mandates, recognised new states in central and eastern Europe, and redrew borders whose grievances contributed to the instability of the interwar years.

Legacy

The settlement has been widely debated: John Maynard Keynes, an adviser to the British delegation, resigned and denounced it in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919). The United States Senate refused to ratify the treaties, and the United States never joined the League of Nations.
