Free monoid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In abstract algebra, the free monoid on a set is the monoid formed by all finite sequences (strings) of elements of that set, with concatenation as the monoid operation and the empty sequence as the identity element. It is the free object in the category of monoids: intuitively, it is the monoid whose elements obey no relations other than those forced by the monoid axioms of associativity and identity. Free monoids are fundamental in computer science, where the free monoid on an alphabet — usually written Σ* and called the set of strings or the Kleene star of the alphabet — is the basic object of formal language theory.

Definition

For a set A, the free monoid A* consists of all finite sequences of elements of A, including the empty sequence ε. The operation is concatenation: the product of sequences (a1…am) and (b1…bn) is (a1…amb1…bn), which is associative with identity ε. It satisfies the universal property: every function from A into a monoid M extends uniquely to a monoid homomorphism from A* to M.

Examples

The natural numbers under addition form the free monoid on one generator, with each number corresponding to a string of that many copies of the generator. The free monoid on two or more generators is noncommutative. In computing, strings over the binary alphabet {0, 1} form the free monoid underlying data representation, and regular expressions describe subsets of a free monoid.

Properties

A monoid is free if and only if it is isomorphic to A* for some set A; the elements of A, called generators or letters, are precisely the elements of the free monoid that cannot be split into a product of two nonempty elements. Free monoids are cancellative and equidivisible, and their theory connects to codes: a subset of a free monoid is a (uniquely decodable) code if it generates a free submonoid with itself as basis.
