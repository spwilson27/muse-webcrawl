Kakure kirishitan

Kakure kirishitan (Japanese: 隠れキリシタン, "hidden Christians") is a modern term for the Japanese Catholics who went underground after Christianity was outlawed by the Tokugawa shogunate in the early 17th century, preserving a transformed form of the faith in secret for roughly two and a half centuries.

History

Christianity was introduced to Japan by Francis Xavier in 1549 and grew to perhaps 300,000 adherents. Toyotomi Hideyoshi and then the Tokugawa shogunate moved to suppress it: missionaries were expelled in 1614, believers were forced to renounce the faith by trampling on sacred images (fumi-e), and suspected Christians were executed. After the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38, surviving Christians in the Nagasaki region, the Gotō Islands, Amakusa and Sotome practised in complete secrecy.

Practice

Hidden Christian communities transmitted prayers (orasho, from Portuguese oração) orally, in forms that drifted far from their Latin and Portuguese originals. Devotional objects were disguised: the Virgin Mary was venerated in statues resembling the Buddhist bodhisattva Kannon ("Maria-Kannon"), and crosses were concealed inside Buddhist-style shrines and lanterns. Lay ritual leaders — mizukata (who baptised), chōkata (who kept the calendar of feasts) and others — replaced priests.

Rediscovery

In 1865, shortly after Japan reopened to the West, villagers from Urakami approached French priest Bernard Petitjean at the newly built Ōura Church in Nagasaki and revealed that they were Christians — an event celebrated as the "Discovery of the Christians". When religious toleration came in 1873, many hidden Christians rejoined the Catholic Church, while others (called hanare kirishitan, "separated Christians") continued their own evolved tradition. In 2018 UNESCO inscribed the "Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region" as a World Heritage Site.
