Prototype theory

Prototype theory is a theory of categorization in cognitive science, particularly in psychology and cognitive linguistics, in which there is a graded degree of belonging to a conceptual category, and some members are more central than others. It emerged in the 1970s, principally through the research of psychologist Eleanor Rosch, as an alternative to the classical view that categories are defined by necessary and sufficient conditions.

Overview

Under prototype theory, a category such as bird is organised around a prototype — the clearest, most representative example, such as a robin or a sparrow. Other members resemble the prototype to varying degrees: a penguin or an ostrich is still a bird, but a less typical one. Category membership is therefore a matter of degree, with fuzzy boundaries, rather than an all-or-nothing classification.

Rosch's experiments showed that people judge typical members faster and more consistently, learn them earlier, and use them as cognitive reference points. Typicality effects also appear in the internal structure of colour categories, which Rosch studied through work on focal colours across languages.

Family resemblance

The theory draws on Ludwig Wittgenstein's notion of family resemblance: members of a category may share overlapping sets of features without any single feature being common to all. A related account, exemplar theory, holds that categories are represented by stored examples rather than by a single abstract prototype.

Applications

Prototype theory has influenced linguistics (notably the work of George Lakoff in Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things, 1987), anthropology, philosophy of science, and research on artificial intelligence and machine learning, where prototype-based classification remains a simple and interpretable method.
