In cricket, the follow-on is a tactical option available in two-innings matches when the team that batted second scores substantially fewer runs than the team that batted first. The captain of the leading team may require ("enforce") the trailing team to bat again immediately — to follow on — rather than taking their own second innings in the normal order.

The law

Under Law 14 of the Laws of Cricket, the minimum first-innings lead needed to enforce the follow-on depends on the scheduled length of the match:

  • Five-day match (Test cricket): 200 runs
  • Three- or four-day match: 150 runs
  • Two-day match: 100 runs
  • One-day two-innings match: 75 runs

Where the start of a match is delayed, the required lead is reduced according to the number of days remaining. The decision rests entirely with the captain of the side in the lead, who must notify the opposing captain and the umpires.

Strategy

Enforcing the follow-on is usually done to press home an advantage: the leading side hopes to bowl the opposition out cheaply for a second time and win by an innings, and it also manages the match clock when time is short. Against this, captains weigh the fatigue of their bowlers and the risk that the wicket will deteriorate further for their own possible final innings. In modern Test cricket, with heavy bowling workloads and back-to-back matches, captains increasingly decline to enforce it.

Famous instances

Teams forced to follow on only rarely win, but the exceptions are celebrated. India defeated Australia at Eden Gardens, Kolkata in 2001 after following on, powered by V. V. S. Laxman's 281 and Rahul Dravid's 180 — only the third such victory in Test history, following England's wins at Sydney in 1894 and at Headingley in 1981 (the latter famous for Ian Botham's 149 not out against Australia). New Zealand added a fourth instance by beating England at Wellington in 2023 by one run after following on.
