Neologism

A neologism (from Greek νέο- néo-, "new", and λόγος lógos, "speech, utterance") is a newly coined term, word, or phrase that has achieved some usage but has not yet been fully accepted into mainstream language. Neologisms arise from technological and cultural change, from science, fiction, politics, and popular culture, and from language play. Most fade quickly; a minority become permanent additions to the vocabulary.

Formation

New words enter language through several routes: coinage from scratch (quark, coined by Murray Gell-Mann, borrowing Joyce's spelling); compounding (smartphone); blending or portmanteau (brunch, smog); derivation with affixes (prequel); borrowing from other languages; acronyms that become words (laser, scuba); and semantic extension of existing words (mouse, viral, cloud). A newly invented word used only once is called a nonce word; a term restricted to one author's work is a protologism until it spreads.

Examples

  • Science and technology: robot (from Karel Čapek's 1920 play R.U.R.), cyberspace (William Gibson, 1982), meme (Richard Dawkins, 1976), blog, selfie, emoji.
  • Literature: Shakespeare is credited with popularising many words; Lewis Carroll coined chortle; Orwell's Newspeak and doublethink entered political vocabulary.
  • Politics and society: McCarthyism, Brexit (a blend of "British" and "exit"), fake news in its modern sense.

Acceptance

Lexicographers track corpus evidence before admitting words to dictionaries; publishers such as the Oxford English Dictionary and Merriam-Webster publicise notable additions each year. Attitudes vary: language purists and academies such as the Académie française often resist borrowings, while descriptivists treat neologisms as the normal mechanism of language change. In psychiatry, "neologism" also denotes the idiosyncratic invented words sometimes produced by people with schizophrenia or aphasia.
