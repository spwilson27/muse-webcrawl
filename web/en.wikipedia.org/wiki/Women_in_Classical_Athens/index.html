Women in Classical Athens

Women in Classical Athens were subject to a legal and social system that sharply restricted their public role. The lives of citizen women in Athens during the fifth and fourth centuries BCE are known only imperfectly, since almost all surviving sources were written by men, and much of the literary evidence presents an ideal rather than a description of everyday reality.

Legal status

Athenian women had no political rights: they could not vote, hold office, or speak in the assembly or the law courts. Throughout her life a woman was under the guardianship (kyrieia) of a male relative — her father, husband, or nearest male kin — who represented her in law. Citizen women could not own substantial property, though they controlled their dowries in a limited sense and could engage in small transactions, conventionally up to the value of a medimnos of barley.

Marriage

Marriage was arranged by a woman's guardian, usually when she was about fourteen years old, to a man often twice her age. The bride brought a dowry, which her husband managed but was obliged to return if the marriage ended in divorce. Marriage's primary purpose in the eyes of the polis was the production of legitimate citizen children; after Pericles' citizenship law of 451/0 BCE, citizenship required an Athenian mother as well as an Athenian father, which raised the importance of citizen women's status even as their public role remained minimal.

Domestic life and seclusion

The ideal respectable citizen wife managed the household (oikos), supervised slaves, stored provisions, and oversaw textile production, above all weaving. Literary sources praise women who were rarely seen in public, and the orator Lysias and the writer Xenophon both present female seclusion as a mark of respectability. In practice, poorer women could not afford seclusion: they worked as market sellers, wet nurses, wool-workers and in other trades, and all women left the house for funerals, festivals and visits to neighbours and fountain houses.

Religion

Religion was the one sphere in which Athenian women held prominent public roles. Women served as priestesses of major cults, including the priestess of Athena Polias, the city's chief cult. Girls of good family served as arrephoroi and kanephoroi (basket-bearers) in processions, and women celebrated festivals closed to men, most famously the Thesmophoria in honour of Demeter, as well as the Adonia. Funerals and the tending of tombs were also largely women's work.

Non-citizen women

Alongside citizen wives stood metic women, slaves, and hetairai — courtesans who, unlike citizen women, might be educated and move in male social circles. Aspasia, the Milesian partner of Pericles, is the most famous example. Slave women had no legal protection of their persons and performed much of the city's domestic and commercial labour.
