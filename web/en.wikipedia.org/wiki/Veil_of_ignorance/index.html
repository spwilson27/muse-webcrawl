Veil of ignorance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The veil of ignorance is a method of determining the morality of political issues proposed in 1971 by American philosopher John Rawls in his influential work A Theory of Justice. It is a key component of Rawls's original position: decision-makers choosing the principles of a just society are placed behind a veil that deprives them of knowledge of their own characteristics and social circumstances — including their wealth, class, intelligence, race, sex, religion, and talents — while leaving them in possession of general facts about human society. Since the choosers do not know who they will be in the society they are designing, they have reason to design it fairly for everyone.

Rationale

Rawls designed the veil to nullify the effects of what he called "natural and social contingencies" — advantages that are arbitrary from a moral point of view. No one deserves to be born rich rather than poor, or with greater rather than lesser native talents; a fair procedure for selecting principles of justice should therefore prevent anyone from exploiting such accidents of fortune. The veil is meant to model impartiality: it ensures that the principles chosen are ones no one could reasonably reject as unfair, because anyone might occupy the worst position those principles permit.

How it works

Parties behind the veil know general truths — the laws of economics and psychology, the circumstances of justice (moderate scarcity, pluralism of values), and the fact that people have differing plans of life. They do not know which particular person they represent. Choosing under this uncertainty, Rawls argued, they would choose his two principles of justice: equal basic liberties for all, and social and economic inequalities arranged to benefit the least advantaged and attached to positions open to all (fair equality of opportunity).

An intuition behind the device is sometimes expressed in homelier terms: a cake is fairly divided if the person who cuts it does not know which slice they will receive, and so cuts equal slices.

Precursors and parallels

Impartial-spectator and ignorance devices have earlier analogues. The economist John Harsanyi had, in the 1950s, used impartial choice under ignorance to defend utilitarianism, assuming a chooser who might turn out to be anyone would maximise expected average utility. Enlightenment thinkers from Rousseau to Kant had appealed to generalised perspectives, and Adam Smith's "impartial spectator" served a related role. Rawls's distinctive use was to ground a deontological, rights-protecting liberalism rather than utilitarian aggregation.

Criticism

Critics dispute both the device's assumptions and its conclusions. Harsanyi objected that a rational chooser behind the veil, assigning equal probability to being anyone, would choose average utilitarianism, not Rawls's difference principle; Rawls replied that the parties have no basis for probabilities and face a momentous, one-off choice in which guaranteeing a tolerable minimum matters most. Libertarians such as Robert Nozick held that historical entitlements cannot legitimately be overridden by end-state principles chosen in ignorance of them. Communitarians questioned whether a self stripped of all attachments could deliberate meaningfully at all, and feminists pointed out that assuming choosers are heads of families could veil injustices within the household itself. Nonetheless, the veil of ignorance remains one of the most widely taught and applied ideas in contemporary political philosophy and public reasoning.
