Nabataean Kingdom
Capital: Petra
Period: 4th century BC – AD 106
Language: Nabataean Aramaic, Arabic
Status: Client kingdom, annexed by Rome as Arabia Petraea

The Nabataean Kingdom was a political state of the Arab Nabataeans centred on Petra, in modern Jordan, flourishing from the 4th century BC until its annexation by the Roman Empire in AD 106. At its height it controlled trade routes across Arabia, the Negev, the Sinai, and parts of Syria, including Damascus for a time.

Wealth and trade

The Nabataeans grew rich as middlemen in the incense trade, carrying frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia to Mediterranean ports, as well as bitumen from the Dead Sea and goods from the silk routes. Their mastery of water harvesting — dams, cisterns, and channels hidden in the desert — enabled cities and caravan stations to thrive in arid land.

Culture

The kingdom is famed for the rock-cut architecture of Petra, including Al-Khazneh (the Treasury) and Ad Deir (the Monastery), blending Hellenistic, Roman, and local styles. The Nabataeans wrote in a form of Aramaic whose cursive script is an ancestor of the Arabic alphabet, while their spoken language was an early form of Arabic. Principal deities included Dushara and al-Uzza.

Decline and annexation

As sea routes displaced caravan trade, Nabataean power waned. After the death of the last king, Rabbel II, the emperor Trajan annexed the kingdom in AD 106, organizing it as the province of Arabia Petraea, with Bostra as a second capital.
