American frontier (Wild West)

Period: c. 1607 – 1912

Location: Western United States

The American frontier, also known as the Old West or the Wild West, encompasses the geography, history, folklore, and culture associated with the forward wave of American expansion in mainland North America that began with European colonial settlements in the early 17th century and ended with the admission of the last few western states in 1912.

A frontier is a zone of contact at the edge of a line of settlement, where European immigrants and their descendants encountered Indigenous peoples, harsh landscapes, and weak governmental authority. The era was defined by westward migration, the displacement and violent subjugation of Native American nations, the growth of ranching and mining, and the building of railroads.

Key themes

Popular imagery of the Wild West includes cowboys driving cattle along trails such as the Chisholm Trail, gunfighters and outlaws like Billy the Kid and Jesse James, lawmen such as Wyatt Earp, gold rushes in California and the Black Hills, and conflicts including the Battle of the Little Bighorn. Much of this imagery was shaped during the period itself by dime novels and Buffalo Bill's Wild West shows.

Historiography

Historian Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 "Frontier Thesis" argued that the frontier experience shaped American democracy and character. Later historians have criticised the thesis for minimising the role of Indigenous peoples, women, and environmental costs. The U.S. Census Bureau declared the frontier line dissolved in 1890, a milestone Turner took as the end of an era.
