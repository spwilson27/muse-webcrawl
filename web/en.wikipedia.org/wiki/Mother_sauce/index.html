Mother sauces

The mother sauces (French: sauces mères) are a group of basic sauces in French cuisine from which a large family of derivative sauces ("daughter sauces") can be made by adding additional ingredients. The classification was systematized in the 19th century by chef Antonin Carême, who identified four grandes sauces, and was revised by Auguste Escoffier in the early 20th century into the five mother sauces that remain standard in culinary education: béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise, and tomate.

The five mother sauces

  • Béchamel — a white sauce of milk thickened with a white roux.
  • Velouté — a light stock (veal, chicken, or fish) thickened with a blond roux.
  • Espagnole — a brown sauce of browned veal stock thickened with a brown roux, with tomato and mirepoix.
  • Hollandaise — an emulsion of egg yolk, melted butter, and lemon juice or vinegar, served warm.
  • Tomate — a sauce of tomatoes, traditionally cooked with stock and pork and thickened (classically with roux, in modern practice by reduction).

History

Carême's original four grandes sauces were béchamel, velouté, espagnole, and allemande (a velouté finished with egg yolk and cream); he also regarded tomato and hollandaise as petites sauces. Escoffier's Le Guide Culinaire (1903) replaced allemande with tomate and promoted hollandaise, giving the canonical five. Some modern teachers, including later editors of Escoffier and the makers of standard culinary textbooks, have debated adding mayonnaise or vinaigrette as further "mothers" since they too are emulsion bases for many derivatives.

Derivative sauces

From béchamel come Mornay (with cheese), Soubise (onion), and mustard sauce; from velouté come suprême and allemande; from espagnole comes demi-glace and bordelaise; from hollandaise come béarnaise and choron; and from tomate come Portuguese and Provençale sauces. Mastering the five mothers historically formed the foundation of a saucier's training.
