Theory of multiple intelligences

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The theory of multiple intelligences proposes that human intelligence is not a single general ability but a set of relatively distinct modalities. It was introduced by developmental psychologist Howard Gardner of Harvard University in his 1983 book Frames of Mind: The Theory of Multiple Intelligences, and has been highly influential in education, though it remains contested within mainstream psychology.

The intelligences

Gardner originally identified seven intelligences, later adding an eighth (and considering a ninth):

  • Linguistic — sensitivity to spoken and written language.
  • Logical-mathematical — capacity for analysis, reasoning and calculation.
  • Musical — skill in performance, composition and appreciation of music.
  • Bodily-kinesthetic — control of bodily motion, as in athletes and dancers.
  • Spatial — ability to visualise and manipulate spatial patterns.
  • Interpersonal — understanding the intentions and feelings of others.
  • Intrapersonal — understanding of oneself.
  • Naturalistic — recognition of patterns in the natural world.

Gardner selected these using criteria including isolation by brain damage, the existence of savants and prodigies, distinctive developmental trajectories, and evolutionary plausibility. A proposed existential intelligence concerning ultimate questions has remained tentative.

Reception and criticism

Educators have used the theory to justify pluralised teaching and assessment that reach students through different strengths. Psychologists, however, criticise the theory for lacking empirical support: performance across the supposed intelligences correlates substantially, consistent with a general intelligence factor (g), and critics argue Gardner's "intelligences" are better described as talents or abilities. Gardner responds that the theory is based on a synthesis of evidence rather than psychometric testing alone. The theory is also frequently conflated in schools with the similarly disputed notion of "learning styles".
