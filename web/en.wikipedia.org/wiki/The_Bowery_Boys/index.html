The Bowery Boys

The Bowery Boys were a troupe of actors who made a series of 48 comedy films for Monogram Pictures and its successor Allied Artists between 1946 and 1958. The series, among the longest-running in American cinema, starred Leo Gorcey as Terrence "Slip" Mahoney, the fast-talking leader of a gang of New York street kids, and Huntz Hall as his dim-witted sidekick Horace DeBussy "Sach" Jones. The films were low-budget comedies built around the gang's hangout at Louie's Sweet Shop on the Bowery, Gorcey's torrents of malapropisms ("a very permiscuous situation"), and plots involving crooks, ghosts, spies, boxers and mad scientists.

Origins

The Bowery Boys descended directly from the Dead End Kids, the young actors who appeared in Sidney Kingsley's 1935 play Dead End and its 1937 film adaptation opposite Humphrey Bogart, then continued as the East Side Kids at Monogram (1940–1945). In 1946 the series was retooled with a more comic accent, Leo Gorcey receiving top billing and his father Bernard Gorcey joining as sweet-shop proprietor Louie Dumbrowski. Early entries such as Live Wires and Mr. Hex (both 1946) set the template of roughly four films a year.

Later films and decline

The series weathered cast changes — Bobby Jordan left in 1947, and Gabe Dell departed in 1950 — and remained profitable through such entries as The Bowery Boys Meet the Monsters (1954). Leo Gorcey quit in 1956 after the death of his father and disputes over money; Stanley Clements briefly replaced him as gang leader "Duke" Coveleskie, but the series ended with In the Money in 1958. Huntz Hall carried on as Sach. The films have since enjoyed a long afterlife on American television and home video as staples of Saturday-morning reruns.
