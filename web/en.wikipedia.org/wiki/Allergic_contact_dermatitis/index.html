Allergic contact dermatitis

SpecialtyDermatology, immunology
SymptomsItchy, red, blistering rash at contact site
Onset24–72 hours after exposure (in sensitised persons)
CausesType IV hypersensitivity to allergens such as nickel, fragrances, poison ivy urushiol
Diagnostic methodPatch testing
TreatmentAllergen avoidance, topical corticosteroids

Allergic contact dermatitis (ACD) is a form of contact dermatitis caused by a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction: after prior sensitisation, re-exposure of the skin to an allergen triggers a T-cell–mediated inflammation, producing an itchy, red, sometimes blistering rash confined largely to the area of contact. It differs from irritant contact dermatitis, which requires no prior sensitisation.

Causes

Common allergens include nickel (in jewellery and fastenings), fragrances and preservatives in cosmetics, rubber accelerators, hair dyes (paraphenylenediamine), topical antibiotics, chromates in cement and leather, and urushiol, the oil in poison ivy, poison oak and poison sumac. More than 3,000 chemicals are known contact allergens.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the history and distribution of the rash, confirmed by patch testing, in which standardised allergens are applied to the back under occlusion for 48 hours. Management centres on identifying and avoiding the culprit allergen; flares are treated with topical corticosteroids and emollients, with systemic steroids reserved for severe or widespread cases such as bad poison ivy reactions.
