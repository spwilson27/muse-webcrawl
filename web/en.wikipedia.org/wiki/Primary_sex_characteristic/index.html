Primary sex characteristics are the anatomical structures directly involved in reproduction — the gonads and the internal and external reproductive organs. They are present from birth (though they mature at puberty) and contrast with secondary sex characteristics, such as breasts, facial hair and voice changes, which emerge at puberty but are not themselves part of the reproductive system.

Development

In humans, embryos initially develop an undifferentiated (bipotential) gonad. In the presence of the SRY gene on the Y chromosome, the gonads become testes and secrete testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone, driving development of male internal ducts and external genitalia; in its absence, ovaries develop and the female pattern proceeds. This process, sex determination and differentiation, is largely complete before birth, so primary characteristics are the basis on which sex is typically assigned at birth.

In humans

Male primary sex characteristics include the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, prostate and penis; female ones include the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, vagina and vulva. At puberty, gonadal hormones cause these organs to mature and become functional — spermatogenesis in males and ovulation and menstruation in females.

Variations

Intersex conditions involve variations in sex characteristics — chromosomal, gonadal or anatomical — that do not fit typical binary notions of male or female bodies, and may affect primary characteristics, secondary characteristics or both.
