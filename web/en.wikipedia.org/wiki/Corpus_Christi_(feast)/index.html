Corpus Christi (feast)

The Feast of Corpus Christi (Latin for "Body of Christ"), also known as the Solemnity of the Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ, is a Christian liturgical solemnity celebrating the Real Presence of the Body and Blood of Christ in the Eucharist. The feast is observed by the Catholic Church, as well as by some Anglican and Lutheran churches.

History

The feast arose from the visions of St Juliana of Liège (1192–1258), an Augustinian nun who reported visions of the Church under a full moon with one dark spot, symbolizing the absence of a feast devoted to the Blessed Sacrament. In 1246 Robert de Torote, Bishop of Liège, instituted the feast for his diocese. After the reported Eucharistic miracle of Bolsena in 1263, Pope Urban IV — who had been archdeacon in Liège — issued the bull Transiturus in 1264, extending the feast to the entire Latin Church. St Thomas Aquinas composed liturgical texts for the feast, including the hymns Pange lingua, Lauda Sion, and Sacris solemniis.

Celebration

The feast is traditionally marked by public processions in which the consecrated host, displayed in a monstrance, is carried through the streets, often over carpets of flowers. In many countries, including Austria, Poland, Spain, Brazil, and parts of Germany, Corpus Christi is a public holiday. Since the liturgical reforms after the Second Vatican Council, in many countries the solemnity is transferred to the following Sunday so more of the faithful can attend.
