Short squeeze

Short squeeze
FieldFinance, securities markets
CauseShort sellers forced to buy back shares as price rises
PrerequisitesHigh short interest, limited float
Notable examplesVolkswagen (2008), GameStop (2021)

A short squeeze is a rapid increase in the price of a stock or other security that occurs when there is a lack of supply and an excess of demand, driven primarily by short sellers buying back the shares to cover their positions. Because short sellers borrow shares and sell them expecting to repurchase them more cheaply, a rising price forces them to buy at ever higher prices to limit losses, which in turn pushes the price higher still — a self-reinforcing feedback loop.

Mechanism

Short interest that exceeds a large fraction of a company's free float makes a squeeze possible, since the number of shares short sellers must eventually repurchase approaches or even exceeds the number available to buy. Triggers can include unexpectedly good news, a takeover bid, or coordinated buying. Margin calls and automatic stop-outs by brokers accelerate the process. Unlike a long investor, whose maximum loss is the purchase price, a short seller's potential loss is theoretically unlimited, which makes squeezes especially violent.

Notable examples

In October 2008, Porsche's disclosure that it controlled most of Volkswagen's shares left short sellers scrambling for a tiny remaining float; Volkswagen briefly became the most valuable company in the world by market capitalisation. In January 2021, retail traders coordinating on forums such as Reddit's WallStreetBets bought GameStop shares and options en masse, inflicting billions of dollars of losses on short-selling hedge funds including Melvin Capital, and prompting several brokers to restrict purchases — an episode that triggered congressional hearings and lasting debate about market structure. Earlier famous squeezes include the Northern Pacific corner of 1901 and Clarence Saunders' Piggly Wiggly corner in 1923.

Risks and regulation

Squeezes are typically short-lived and end in sharp collapses once covering is complete, leaving late buyers with steep losses. Regulators monitor short interest disclosures and, in crises, have occasionally imposed temporary bans on short selling, though the effectiveness and wisdom of such bans are debated.
