Spanish dollar

Denomination8 reales
CompositionSilver, ~0.90 fine
Minted1497–1864 (various mints)
NicknamesPiece of eight, peso, pillar dollar

The Spanish dollar, also known as the piece of eight (Spanish: real de a ocho), was a silver coin worth eight reales minted by the Spanish Empire. First issued under the monetary reforms of Ferdinand and Isabella in 1497 and produced in enormous quantities at mints in Mexico City, Potosí, Lima, and elsewhere in Spanish America, it became the dominant currency of international trade from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century.

Global circulation

Spanish dollars circulated legally and by custom across the Americas, Europe, and Asia. They were carried to China by the Manila galleons and accepted there, often bearing merchants' "chop marks", well into the nineteenth century. In British North America and the early United States the coin was ubiquitous: it remained legal tender in the United States until the Coinage Act of 1857, and the United States dollar was deliberately modelled on it in weight and fineness. Prices on the New York Stock Exchange were quoted in eighths of a dollar — from the coin's eight reales — until 1997, and expressions such as "two bits" (two reales, a quarter dollar) survive from its use.

Design

Early coins were irregular "cobs" struck by hand; milled coinage from the 1730s produced the famous "pillar dollars", showing the Pillars of Hercules and two globes. Later portrait dollars bore the bust of the reigning monarch. The coin could be cut into bits to make small change, hence "pieces of eight". The Spanish dollar is the ancestor of the modern currencies of the United States, Canada, Mexico, and much of Latin America and Asia, including in the origins of the dollar sign.
