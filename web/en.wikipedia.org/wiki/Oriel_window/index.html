Oriel window

An oriel window is a form of bay window that projects from the main face of a building but does not reach the ground. It is supported from below by corbels, brackets or a moulded base, and is most often found on an upper storey. Oriel windows increase light and floor space in the room behind them and were used to display status and craftsmanship on the street facade.

History

Oriel windows are characteristic of English late medieval and Tudor architecture, appearing in colleges at Oxford and Cambridge, great houses and guildhalls; notable examples include the oriels of Hampton Court Palace. The term may derive from the medieval Latin oriolum, a porch or gallery. The feature was revived enthusiastically in the Gothic Revival and Arts and Crafts movements of the nineteenth century, and is common on Victorian and Edwardian houses, where it is often confused with the bay window proper.

Distinction from bay windows

A bay window rises from the ground (or from a ground-floor base) and forms an internal recess; an oriel is cantilevered from the wall above ground level. In Islamic architecture the related mashrabiya is an enclosed wooden lattice oriel.
