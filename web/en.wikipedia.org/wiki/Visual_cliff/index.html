Visual cliff

The visual cliff is an apparatus created by psychologists Eleanor J. Gibson and Richard D. Walk at Cornell University to investigate depth perception in human and animal infants. It consists of a sturdy glass surface with a patterned material directly beneath the glass on one side (the "shallow side") and the same pattern placed far below on the other side (the "deep side"), creating the visual impression of a cliff while the glass provides a solid surface.

The experiments

In Gibson and Walk's original 1960 study, 36 infants aged 6 to 14 months were placed on a centre board and encouraged by their mothers to crawl either toward the shallow side or across the apparent drop. Of the infants who moved, 27 crawled onto the shallow side, while only three ventured onto the deep side; many crawled away from the mother toward the shallow side or cried. The researchers concluded that most infants could perceive depth by the time they could crawl, though the experiments could not show whether the avoidance was innate or learned through crawling experience.

Animal studies

The apparatus was also used with rats, chicks, lambs, goats, cats and monkeys. Precocial animals such as chicks and lambs avoided the deep side from the first day of life, supporting the view that depth perception is present at birth in species that must move about immediately. Dark-reared kittens initially lacked avoidance, but developed it after exposure to light.

Later research

Later researchers, notably Joseph Campos and colleagues, placed pre-crawling infants directly over the deep side and measured heart rate: young infants showed heart-rate deceleration (interest), while experienced crawlers showed acceleration (fear/wariness). This suggested that what develops with crawling experience is not depth perception itself but a wariness of heights. The visual cliff remains one of the best-known demonstrations in developmental psychology.
