Gironde estuary

The Gironde estuary (French: Estuaire de la Gironde) is a navigable estuary in southwest France, formed by the confluence of the Dordogne and Garonne rivers just downstream of Bordeaux. It is the largest estuary in Western Europe, and it flows into the Atlantic Ocean (Bay of Biscay) between Royan and Le Verdon-sur-Mer.

Geography

The estuary is roughly 75 kilometres (47 miles) long and up to 11 kilometres (7 miles) wide, covering an area of about 635 square kilometres. The Dordogne and Garonne meet at the Bec d'Ambès, and from there the Gironde runs northwest to the sea. The estuary contains a number of islands, including Île de Patiras, Île Sans-Pain, Île Bouchaud, and Île Nouvelle, many of which are important refuges for migratory birds. Its waters carry an enormous load of sediment, and powerful tides produce a tidal bore known locally as the mascaret.

Navigation and economy

The Gironde has been a major shipping route since antiquity, giving the port of Bordeaux access to the Atlantic. It is deep enough to accommodate large ocean-going vessels, although the shifting channels require constant dredging and pilotage. The banks are lined with famous wine-growing districts, including the Médoc on the left bank and Blaye and Bourg on the right bank. The Cordouan lighthouse, built at the mouth of the estuary in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, is the oldest active lighthouse in France and is often called the "Versailles of the sea."

History

During the Second World War, the estuary and its ports were occupied by German forces, and a major submarine base was constructed at Bordeaux. In December 1942, British Royal Marines of Operation Frankton paddled canoes up the Gironde to attack shipping in Bordeaux harbour with limpet mines, an exploit later dramatized in the film The Cockleshell Heroes.
