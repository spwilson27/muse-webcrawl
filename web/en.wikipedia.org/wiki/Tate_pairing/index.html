Tate pairing

In mathematics, the Tate pairing is any of several closely related bilinear pairings involving elliptic curves or abelian varieties, usually over a finite field or local field. Introduced in work of John Tate and developed in the form used for cryptography by Gerhard Frey and others, it maps pairs of points on an elliptic curve to elements of a finite field, and underlies pairing-based cryptosystems.

Definition

For an elliptic curve E over a finite field Fq and an integer n dividing q − 1, the Tate pairing takes a point P of order n and a second point Q, and returns an element of Fq* obtained by evaluating at Q a rational function whose divisor is n(P) − n(O). Well-defined modulo n-th powers, the pairing is bilinear and non-degenerate under suitable hypotheses.

Computation

Pairings are computed efficiently with Miller's algorithm, which evaluates the required function using a double-and-add loop over the bits of n. A final exponentiation produces the reduced Tate pairing used in practice. Variants such as the ate and optimal ate pairings shorten the Miller loop and are standard in implementations.

Applications

Together with the Weil pairing, the Tate pairing enables identity-based encryption (as in the Boneh–Franklin scheme), short signatures (BLS), and other pairing-based protocols. The MOV and Frey–Rück attacks also use these pairings to reduce elliptic-curve discrete logarithms to finite-field discrete logarithms on weak curves.
