Gesta Danorum

Title page of the first printed edition of Saxo's work (Paris, 1514).

Gesta Danorum ("Deeds of the Danes") is a patriotic work of Danish history, written by the 12th–13th-century cleric and historian Saxo Grammaticus. Composed in ornate Latin around 1185–1208 (and revised thereafter) under the patronage of Archbishop Absalon and his successor Anders Sunesen, it is the first full history of Denmark and a foundational source for Danish national identity and for early Scandinavian legend.

Contents

The work comprises sixteen books. The first nine treat legendary and mythical kings, blending euhemerised Norse mythology—Odin and Thor appear as ancient human potentates—with heroic legend: here are found the stories of Amleth (the source for Shakespeare's Hamlet), of the shield-maiden Lagertha, of Ragnar Lodbrok, and of the Battle of Brávellir. Books ten to sixteen move into more historical territory, covering the Christian kings from Harald Bluetooth to the reign of Valdemar I and Knud VI, Saxo's own time. Interspersed verses render in Latin metres what Saxo presents as ancient Danish poems.

Sources and transmission

Saxo drew on Icelandic oral tradition and sagas, runic inscriptions, older chronicles, and the accounts of contemporaries at Absalon's court. The complete text survives not in Saxo's autograph but through copies; the first printed edition was published in Paris in 1514 by Christiern Pedersen, who famously searched for a manuscript across Denmark. Only fragments of original medieval manuscripts survive, the most notable being the Angers fragment.

Significance

The Gesta Danorum is prized both as a stylistic monument of medieval Latin and as a repository—often the unique source—for Scandinavian myths and legends otherwise lost, complementing the Icelandic Eddas and sagas. Its Amleth narrative, transmitted through later retellings, underlies the tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark.
