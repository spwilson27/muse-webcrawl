Annie Hall

Annie Hall is a 1977 American romantic comedy-drama film directed by Woody Allen from a screenplay he co-wrote with Marshall Brickman, and produced by Charles H. Joffe. Allen stars as Alvy Singer, a neurotic New York comedian who reflects on his failed relationship with the free-spirited Annie Hall, played by Diane Keaton. Widely regarded as one of the greatest films ever made, it won the Academy Award for Best Picture.

Plot

Stand-up comedian Alvy Singer narrates, often directly to the camera, the story of his romance with aspiring singer Annie Hall, whom he meets at a tennis match. Told in non-linear flashbacks, the film traces their courtship, cohabitation, arguments, a trip to Los Angeles that Alvy despises, their break-up and brief reconciliation, and Alvy's final reflection that relationships are irrational and absurd — yet, like the man in his closing joke who keeps a brother who thinks he is a chicken because "we need the eggs", we continue with them anyway.

Production

The film was shot in New York and Los Angeles in 1976 with cinematographer Gordon Willis. It was originally conceived as a more diffuse, free-associative comedy (with the working title Anhedonia) including a murder-mystery subplot; editor Ralph Rosenblum helped reshape it in the cutting room into the focused story of the relationship. Diane Keaton, who had previously been romantically involved with Allen, modelled aspects of Annie on herself — "Hall" was her birth surname, and Keaton's own wardrobe, chosen with costume designer Ruth Morley, launched a fashion trend for menswear-inspired clothing.

Reception and legacy

At the 50th Academy Awards, Annie Hall won four Oscars: Best Picture, Best Director, Best Actress (Keaton), and Best Original Screenplay; Allen did not attend the ceremony. It famously beat Star Wars for Best Picture. The American Film Institute ranked it among the greatest American films and the second-greatest romantic comedy; in the 2012 Sight and Sound critics' poll of screenplays and the Writers Guild of America list, its script was named the funniest ever written. The film's fourth-wall breaks, subtitles revealing characters' real thoughts, split-screen therapy scene, and animated sequence remain frequently imitated devices in romantic comedy.
