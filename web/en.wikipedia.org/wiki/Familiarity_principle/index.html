Familiarity principle

The familiarity principle, also known as the mere-exposure effect, is a psychological phenomenon by which people tend to develop a preference for things merely because they are familiar with them. In social psychology, this effect is sometimes called the familiarity principle: repeated exposure to a stimulus — a face, a melody, a word, a brand — increases liking for it, even when the exposure is brief or not consciously noticed.

Research

The effect was described by Gustav Fechner in the 19th century and was systematically investigated by social psychologist Robert Zajonc, whose 1968 monograph summarised evidence that mere repeated exposure is sufficient to enhance attitudes. Zajonc also proposed that the effect has an evolutionary basis: familiarity signals safety, whereas novel stimuli initially provoke caution. Meta-analyses have confirmed the effect across many stimulus types, though it follows an inverted-U shape — after many repetitions, boredom and wear-out can reduce liking.

Mechanisms

Two main explanations are offered. Perceptual fluency theory holds that repeated exposure makes a stimulus easier to process, and this fluent processing is experienced as pleasant and is misattributed to the stimulus itself. The two-factor model proposes that initial exposures reduce uncertainty and negative feelings (habituation), while further exposures eventually produce tedium, accounting for the inverted-U curve.

Applications

The familiarity principle is exploited in advertising, where repeated brand exposure builds preference, and it helps explain phenomena such as the popularity of familiar songs, the "illusory truth effect" (repeated statements being judged more true), and propinquity in relationship formation — people are more likely to befriend those they encounter often.
