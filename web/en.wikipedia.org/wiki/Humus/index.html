In classical soil science, humus is the dark organic matter in soil that is formed by the decomposition of plant and animal matter. It is a kind of soil organic matter that has reached a point of stability, where it will break down no further under the conditions present and might remain essentially unchanged for centuries or millennia. In modern usage the term is applied more loosely to mature compost and to the stable fraction of soil organic matter generally.

Formation

Humus forms through the microbial degradation and chemical transformation of plant litter, root exudates and animal remains — a process called humification. Lignin and other resistant compounds are partially oxidised and polymerised into large, complex molecules. The main constituents are conventionally divided by solubility into humic acids, fulvic acids and humin.

Properties and significance

Humus greatly improves soil structure, moisture retention and aeration, and acts as a reservoir of plant nutrients, especially nitrogen, phosphorus and sulphur, which it releases slowly as it mineralises. Its high cation-exchange capacity helps retain nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium in plant-available form. Humus-rich soils also sequester large amounts of carbon: soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and all vegetation combined, making humus dynamics important to the global carbon cycle and to agriculture.

Humus forms

Forest floor humus is classically classified into mor (raw, acidic humus of coniferous forests), moder (intermediate), and mull (well-mixed, biologically active humus typical of deciduous woodland and grassland, where earthworms incorporate organic matter into the mineral soil).
