Immunity (medicine)

In medicine, immunity is the state of having sufficient biological defences to avoid infection, disease, or other unwanted biological invasion. It involves both specific and non-specific components: the innate immune system, present from birth, and the adaptive immune system, which develops specific responses to pathogens and retains immunological memory of them.

Types

Immunity may be active, arising when exposure to a pathogen or a vaccine triggers the production of antibodies and memory cells, or passive, arising from the transfer of ready-made antibodies, such as from mother to infant across the placenta or through breast milk, or by injection of immunoglobulin. Active immunity is usually long-lasting; passive immunity is immediate but temporary.

History

The concept dates to antiquity — Thucydides observed that survivors of the plague of Athens could nurse the sick without falling ill again — and was developed through variolation, Edward Jenner's smallpox vaccination of 1796, and the germ theory work of Louis Pasteur and others.

Disorders

Failures of immunity include immunodeficiency (congenital or acquired, as in AIDS), autoimmune diseases in which the system attacks the body's own tissues, and hypersensitivities such as allergies.
