Elephant seals are large, oceangoing earless seals in the genus Mirounga, comprising the northern elephant seal (Mirounga angustirostris) of the north-eastern Pacific and the southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina) of the Southern Ocean. They take their name from the large, inflatable proboscis of the adult male, used to produce loud roaring sounds during the mating season.

Description

Elephant seals are the largest pinnipeds and among the largest living carnivorans: southern bulls can exceed 5 metres in length and 3,000 kilograms in weight, several times the size of females. They are exceptional divers, routinely reaching depths beyond 1,000 metres and sometimes over 2,000 metres in search of squid and fish, spending most of their lives at sea and coming ashore mainly to breed and moult.

Conservation

Both species were hunted nearly to extinction for their blubber oil in the nineteenth century; the northern species may have fallen to fewer than a hundred individuals. Under legal protection both populations have recovered dramatically — the northern elephant seal now numbers well over 100,000 — though the bottleneck left the northern species with very low genetic diversity.
