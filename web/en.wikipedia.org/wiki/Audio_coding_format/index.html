Audio coding format

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio coding format (or audio compression format) is a content representation format for storage or transmission of digital audio — including music, speech and other sounds. The encoding is performed by an audio codec (encoder/decoder). Formats are broadly divided into uncompressed, lossless and lossy categories.

Types

Uncompressed

Uncompressed formats store raw pulse-code modulation samples. WAV (built on the RIFF container) and AIFF are the standard examples on Windows and Apple platforms respectively; CD audio uses PCM at 44.1 kHz, 16-bit stereo.

Lossless compression

Lossless formats such as FLAC, Apple Lossless (ALAC), Monkey's Audio and WavPack reduce file size by roughly 30–50% while allowing bit-perfect reconstruction of the original audio.

Lossy compression

Lossy formats exploit psychoacoustics — discarding information masked or inaudible to human hearing — to achieve compression ratios near 10:1. Major examples include MP3, AAC, Vorbis and Opus, which dominate streaming and broadcasting.

Comparison

Choice of format trades off file size, sound quality, computational complexity, latency, licensing and hardware support. Speech-specific codecs such as AMR and Opus's SILK mode are optimised for voice at very low bit rates, while professional production favours uncompressed or lossless formats to avoid generation loss.
