Coherence (linguistics)

Coherence (linguistics)
Field: Linguistics, discourse analysis
Related concepts: Cohesion, discourse, pragmatics

Coherence in linguistics is the property of a text or discourse that makes it hang together as a meaningful whole for a reader or listener. Where cohesion concerns the surface grammatical and lexical links between sentences, coherence refers to the underlying semantic and pragmatic connections that allow an audience to build a consistent mental model of what is being communicated.

Definition

A text is coherent when its parts are related to one another in ways that are relevant and interpretable. Coherence is generally held to depend partly on the text itself and partly on the knowledge, expectations, and inferences of the audience. Because of this, the same sequence of sentences may be judged coherent by one reader and incoherent by another.

Coherence and cohesion

Halliday and Hasan distinguished cohesion, the set of explicit linking devices such as reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, and lexical repetition, from coherence, the overall sense of unity. Cohesive devices often support coherence, but a text can be highly cohesive yet incoherent, or coherent with few overt cohesive ties.

Coherence relations

Discourse analysts have proposed inventories of coherence relations, such as cause–effect, elaboration, contrast, background, and evidence, which describe how successive segments of a text connect. Rhetorical Structure Theory and Segmented Discourse Representation Theory formalise these relations to explain how texts are organised above the sentence level.

Applications

The study of coherence is relevant to language teaching, writing assessment, natural language processing, and the evaluation of automatically generated text, where maintaining coherence over long passages remains a significant challenge.
