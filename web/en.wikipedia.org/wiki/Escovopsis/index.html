Escovopsis is a genus of parasitic fungi in the family Hypocreaceae. It is best known as a specialised parasite (a mycoparasite) of the fungal gardens cultivated by fungus-growing ants (tribe Attini), including the leafcutter ants Atta and Acromyrmex. Escovopsis attacks and consumes the ants' fungal cultivar (usually Leucoagaricus gongylophorus), and an outbreak can devastate or destroy an entire ant colony.

Ecology and coevolution

The association is an ancient one: Escovopsis appears to have coevolved with the ants and their cultivar for tens of millions of years, diversifying into distinct lineages that infect particular ant hosts. The ants defend their gardens behaviourally by weeding out infected material, and chemically through antibiotic-producing actinobacteria (especially Pseudonocardia) that live on the ants' cuticle and selectively suppress Escovopsis while sparing the cultivar. This four-part symbiosis — ant, fungus, parasite and bacterium — is a classic model system for the study of coevolution and symbiosis.

Species

The genus was described in 1990 with Escovopsis weberi, named for the myrmecologist Neal Weber and isolated from gardens of the higher attine ants. Additional species such as E. aspergilloides and E. lentecrescens, among others described since, differ in growth rate, spore form and host range.
