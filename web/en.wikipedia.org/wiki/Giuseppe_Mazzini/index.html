Giuseppe Mazzini (22 June 1805 – 10 March 1872) was an Italian politician, journalist, philosopher and activist for the unification of Italy (Risorgimento). His thought and propaganda helped define modern Italian nationalism and influenced republican and nationalist movements across Europe and beyond. A committed republican, he spent most of his life in exile, organising conspiracies and uprisings from Marseille and, above all, London.

Early life and Young Italy

Born in Genoa, then annexed to Napoleonic France, Mazzini studied law at the University of Genoa and joined the secret Carbonari society, whose vague aims left him dissatisfied. Arrested in 1830 and exiled, he moved to Marseille, where in 1831 he founded Young Italy (La Giovine Italia), a movement aiming to unite the Italian states into a single democratic republic through popular insurrection. Its journal of the same name smuggled his essays into Italy. Young Italy's planned invasions and revolts repeatedly failed, and Mazzini was condemned to death in absentia by Piedmont.

Exile and Young Europe

Driven from France and Switzerland, Mazzini settled in London in 1837, which remained his base for most of his life. There he founded the newspaper L'Apostolato popolare, supported Italian workers and exiles, helped open a school for poor Italian children, and in 1834 had already launched Young Europe, an alliance of national movements (Young Italy, Young Poland, Young Germany and others) foreshadowing later internationalisms. A scandal in 1844 — when the British government was found to have opened his mail and passed information to Naples — caused a public outcry and strengthened his reputation.

The Roman Republic and after

During the revolutions of 1848 Mazzini returned to Italy. When the Roman Republic was proclaimed in February 1849 after Pope Pius IX fled, he became its effective leader as a member of the ruling triumvirate, with Carlo Armellini and Aurelio Saffi. His government introduced universal male suffrage, abolished the death penalty and taxes on basic necessities, and guaranteed religious freedom. The republic fell to French troops in July 1849; Giuseppe Garibaldi, who had commanded its defence, fled and Mazzini returned to exile.

Final years and thought

Mazzini continued plotting republican uprisings through the 1850s and 1860s, though unification was achieved instead under the Piedmontese monarchy, which he refused to accept. His major work, The Duties of Man (1860), addressed to Italian working men, set out his creed: nations as the God-given units of humanity, democracy grounded in moral duty rather than mere rights, and social reform without class struggle — positions that put him at odds with both Marxists and liberal monarchists, even as his ideas inspired later democratic, republican and anti-colonial leaders, including, in India, several early nationalists.

He died in Pisa in 1872 under an assumed name while planning another uprising; tens of thousands followed his funeral procession in Genoa. He is honoured in Italy as the prophet of the nation, though the unified state that emerged was the monarchy he opposed rather than his republic.
