The Ikhwan (Arabic: الإخوان, "the Brethren"), also spelled Akhwan, were a Wahhabi religious militia of settled Bedouin tribesmen that formed the shock troops of Ibn Saud's conquests in Arabia between about 1912 and 1930. Recruited from nomadic tribes settled in military-religious agricultural colonies called hujar, the Ikhwan helped Ibn Saud unify the Arabian Peninsula, but their zealotry eventually brought them into rebellion against him.

Origins

Beginning in 1912, Bedouin were encouraged to abandon nomadic life and settle in hujar, where they received religious instruction in the Wahhabi creed and military training. The first settlement was Artaweeiyah. By the 1920s the Ikhwan could field tens of thousands of fighters.

Conquests

Ikhwan forces were central to the Saudi conquests of Ha'il (1921) and of the Hejaz (1924–1925), including the capture of Taif, Mecca, and Jeddah, which ended Hashemite rule. Their raids and strict enforcement of Wahhabi practice, including violence against those they deemed idolaters, also caused friction with the British in Iraq, Transjordan, and Kuwait.

Revolt and defeat

Frustrated by Ibn Saud's compromises with Britain and his curbs on raiding, Ikhwan leaders such as Faisal al-Dawish and Sultan bin Bajad rebelled in 1927. Ibn Saud defeated them at the Battle of Sabilla in March 1929, using machine guns and motor vehicles; the last rebels surrendered in 1930. Surviving elements were absorbed into what became the Saudi Arabian National Guard.
