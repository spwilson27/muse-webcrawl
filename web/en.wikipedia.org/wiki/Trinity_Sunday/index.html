Trinity Sunday is a feast day in the liturgical calendar of many Western Christian churches celebrating the doctrine of the Holy Trinity — the belief that God exists as three consubstantial persons: the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ), and the Holy Spirit. It falls on the first Sunday after Pentecost, eight weeks after Easter Sunday, and marks the beginning of the long season of Ordinary Time (in some traditions, the "Trinity season") that runs until Advent.

History

Devotion to the Trinity grew during the early Middle Ages, and local observances of a feast in its honour are recorded from the 10th century, notably in the monasteries of Cluny. Tradition credits Bishop Stephen of Liège (early 10th century) with composing an office for the feast. Its spread was gradual: it was observed in England under the influence of Thomas Becket, who was consecrated archbishop on the Sunday after Pentecost in 1162 and promoted the feast. In 1334 Pope John XXII extended Trinity Sunday to the whole Western Church. In the Eastern Orthodox tradition, Pentecost itself is the feast of the Trinity, and the following Sunday is All Saints' Day, so no separate Trinity Sunday is kept.

Observance

The liturgy of the day centres on creedal affirmation of the Trinity: the Athanasian Creed is traditionally recited in some churches, and readings and hymns — such as "Holy, Holy, Holy! Lord God Almighty" (written by Reginald Heber for Trinity Sunday) — expound the doctrine. The liturgical colour is white. In the Church of England, the Sundays after Trinity are numbered through the summer and autumn ("the Nth Sunday after Trinity"), and the feast traditionally closes the cycle of celebrations of Christ's life that began at Advent. In parts of Europe, Trinity Sunday is associated with processions, well-dressing, and local fairs, and in some countries the following Thursday's feast of Corpus Christi is closely linked in popular observance.

Dates

Because it depends on the date of Easter, Trinity Sunday can fall as early as 17 May or as late as 20 June. It is exactly one week after Pentecost and eight weeks after Easter Sunday.
