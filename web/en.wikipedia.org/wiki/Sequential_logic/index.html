Sequential logic

In digital circuit theory, sequential logic is a type of logic circuit whose output depends not only on its present inputs but also on the history of past inputs — that is, on its internal state. This contrasts with combinational logic, whose outputs are pure functions of the current inputs. Sequential circuits implement memory and are the basis of counters, registers, finite-state machines, and computer memory.

Synchronous and asynchronous

Most sequential circuits are synchronous: their state changes only in step with a global clock signal, typically on a clock edge, using storage elements called flip-flops. Asynchronous sequential circuits instead change state whenever inputs change, relying on latches and feedback delays; they are harder to design and analyse but are used in specialised areas such as clock-domain interfaces and low-power design.

Building blocks

The elementary storage elements are the latch, which is level-sensitive, and the flip-flop, which is edge-triggered. Common flip-flop types include SR, D, JK, and T. Feedback of outputs into combinational logic that computes the next state and the outputs gives rise to the standard finite-state-machine models: in a Moore machine the outputs depend only on the state, while in a Mealy machine they also depend on the inputs.
