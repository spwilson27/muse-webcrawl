Jahangir

Nur-ud-din Muhammad Salim (31 August 1569 – 28 October 1627), known by his imperial name Jahangir (Persian for "conqueror of the world"), was the fourth Mughal Emperor, who ruled from 1605 until his death in 1627. He was the eldest surviving son of Akbar the Great and his wife Mariam-uz-Zamani.

Early life

Salim was born in Fatehpur Sikri after Akbar prayed at the shrine of Sheikh Salim Chishti. He received a princely education in Persian, Arabic, Turkish, Hindi, arithmetic, history and warfare. Impatient for power, he rebelled against Akbar in 1599, had the minister Abu'l-Fazl murdered, but was reconciled before Akbar's death.

Reign

Jahangir continued Akbar's policies of tolerance and centralisation, issuing twelve ordinances, forbidding forced conversions and maintaining the mansabdari system. His son Khusrau rebelled in 1606 and was blinded; the Sikh Guru Arjan was executed the same year. Campaigns continued in Mewar, the Deccan against Malik Ambar, and Kangra.

He was a great patron of painting; Mughal miniature reached a peak of naturalism under artists such as Ustad Mansur. He kept detailed memoirs, the Tuzuk-e-Jahangiri, recording flora, fauna and court life. His love of justice was symbolised by the Chain of Justice hung outside the Agra Fort.

Nur Jahan and later years

In 1611 he married Mehr-un-Nissa, titled Nur Jahan, who became the power behind the throne, issuing farmans and appearing on coinage. Jahangir's addiction to opium and alcohol weakened his health. Sir Thomas Roe, ambassador of James I of England, resided at his court 1615–1619.

Death and legacy

Jahangir died on the way from Kashmir near Rajouri and was buried at Shahdara Bagh, Lahore. He was succeeded after a struggle by Shah Jahan. See also Mughal Empire, Taj Mahal.
