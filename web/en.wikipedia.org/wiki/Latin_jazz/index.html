Latin jazz is a genre of jazz with Latin American rhythms, generally understood to include music from the Caribbean, especially Cuba, and from Brazil. Its defining feature is the use of Afro-Cuban rhythmic patterns — above all the clave — as the structural foundation of the music, together with instruments such as congas, bongos, timbales, and cajón alongside the standard jazz ensemble.

History

Latin influence reached jazz early — Jelly Roll Morton spoke of the "Spanish tinge" in New Orleans music — but the genre proper began in the 1940s. Mario Bauzá, musical director of Machito and his Afro-Cubans, built a big band around authentic Cuban rhythm sections. In 1947 Dizzy Gillespie hired the Cuban conguero Chano Pozo, producing landmarks such as "Manteca." Stan Kenton's and Stan Getz's recordings widened the audience, and in the 1960s the Brazilian bossa nova — by Antônio Carlos Jobim, João Gilberto, and others — gave Latin jazz a second, quieter mainstream ("The Girl from Ipanema", 1964).

Styles

Afro-Cuban jazz organises arrangements around the 3–2 or 2–3 son clave and montuno piano figures, with horn sections riffing over a full percussion battery. Brazilian jazz uses samba and bossa nova rhythms, typically gentler in dynamics and harmony. Later artists such as Tito Puente, Eddie Palmieri, Poncho Sanchez, Paquito D'Rivera, and Arturo Sandoval carried the genre forward, and its dancefloor cousin, salsa, developed in parallel in New York.
