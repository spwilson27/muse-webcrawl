Income-driven repayment

Income-driven repayment (IDR) is a category of repayment plans for federal student loans in the United States under which a borrower's monthly payment is capped at a percentage of their discretionary income — generally defined as income above a multiple (typically 150%) of the federal poverty guideline — and any remaining balance is forgiven after a set repayment period, usually 20 or 25 years. Payments can fall to zero for borrowers with very low incomes while they remain in good standing.

Plans

The United States Department of Education has offered several IDR plans, including:

  • Income-Contingent Repayment (ICR) — the first IDR plan, created in 1994 for Direct Loans.
  • Income-Based Repayment (IBR) — created by the College Cost Reduction and Access Act of 2007 and available from 2009, capping payments at 10 or 15 percent of discretionary income depending on when the borrower first borrowed.
  • Pay As You Earn (PAYE) — introduced in 2012, capping payments at 10 percent with forgiveness after 20 years for eligible newer borrowers.
  • Revised Pay As You Earn (REPAYE) — introduced in 2015, extending the 10 percent cap to all Direct Loan borrowers regardless of when they borrowed.
  • Saving on a Valuable Education (SAVE) — launched in 2023 as a replacement for REPAYE, with lower payments for undergraduate loans and a higher income exemption; the plan was blocked by federal court injunctions in 2024 and its future became the subject of litigation and legislative change.

How it works

Borrowers must apply and annually recertify their income and family size, usually by authorising access to tax data. Payments are recalculated each year. Interest may accrue if payments do not cover it, causing balances to grow for some borrowers. Forgiveness at the end of the repayment term may be treated as taxable income depending on current law; borrowers working full-time for qualifying public or non-profit employers may instead receive tax-free forgiveness after 10 years under the Public Service Loan Forgiveness program while enrolled in an IDR plan.

Policy debate

Supporters argue IDR provides essential insurance against low earnings and default. Critics argue the plans can subsidise expensive graduate and professional programs, increase total borrowing, and be complex to administer. A one-time account adjustment by the Department of Education credited many long-term borrowers with additional qualifying payments toward forgiveness.
