Bézout's theorem

Bézout's theorem is a fundamental result in algebraic geometry concerning the number of common points, or intersection numbers, of two algebraic curves. It states that, given two projective plane curves of degrees m and n over an algebraically closed field, with no common component, the number of intersection points counted with multiplicity is exactly mn.

Statement and intuition

In the simplest case, a line (degree 1) meets a conic (degree 2) in at most two points, and two conics meet in at most four. To make the count exact, three corrections are required: intersections must be counted with their multiplicities (so a tangent line counts twice), points at infinity in the projective plane must be included, and coordinates must be allowed to take complex values.

The theorem generalises to higher dimensions: hypersurfaces of degrees d1, …, dn in projective n-space intersect, counted with multiplicity, in d1···dn points.

History

Special cases were known to Descartes and Newton. Étienne Bézout published a proof for curves in his Théorie générale des équations algébriques in 1779, though his argument assumed what needed to be shown. Rigorous proofs came in the 19th and 20th centuries with the development of commutative algebra and the theory of resultants and intersection multiplicity.
