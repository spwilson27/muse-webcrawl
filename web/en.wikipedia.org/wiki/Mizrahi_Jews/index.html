Mizrahi Jews

Mizrahi Jews (Hebrew: יהודי המִזְרָח, Yehudei ha-Mizraḥ, "Eastern Jews"), sometimes referred to as Mizrahim, are the descendants of local Jewish communities of the Middle East and North Africa. The term encompasses the Jewish communities native to the region from biblical times into the modern era, including Babylonian, Persian, Yemenite, Kurdish, Bukharian, Georgian, Mountain Jewish, and North African communities, among many others.

Terminology

Mizrah is the Hebrew word for "east." The term Mizrahi came into widespread use in Israel in the decades after the state's founding to describe Jews who immigrated from, or whose families originated in, the Muslim-majority countries of the Middle East and North Africa. It is sometimes used interchangeably with Sephardi, though strictly the Sephardim are descendants of Jews expelled from Spain and Portugal in 1492, while many Mizrahi communities never passed through Iberia. The umbrella term Edot ha-Mizrah ("Communities of the East") is also used.

History

Jewish communities in Babylonia (modern Iraq), Persia, Yemen and North Africa are among the oldest in the diaspora, some dating to the Babylonian exile of the 6th century BCE. The Babylonian Talmud was compiled in Mesopotamia, and communities across the region maintained distinctive liturgical rites, languages and customs for over two millennia.

Following the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948, the great majority of Mizrahi Jews left or were expelled from Arab and Muslim countries in an exodus that brought roughly 850,000 Jews to Israel, France and elsewhere between 1948 and the 1970s. Operations such as Magic Carpet (Yemen, 1949–50) and Ezra and Nehemiah (Iraq, 1950–51) airlifted whole communities. In Israel, Mizrahi immigrants were frequently housed in transit camps (ma'abarot) and development towns, and disparities between Mizrahi and Ashkenazi Israelis became a central theme in Israeli politics and culture.

Culture

Mizrahi religious practice includes distinctive liturgical melodies, piyyutim (liturgical poems), and customs surrounding holidays and life-cycle events. Mizrahi foodways — including dishes such as kubbeh, jachnun, sabich and malawach — have become integral to Israeli cuisine. Musica Mizrahit, blending Middle Eastern melodies with Mediterranean and Western instrumentation, moved from the margins to the centre of Israeli popular music from the 1970s onward, with artists such as Zohar Argov and later Eyal Golan achieving mainstream success.
