Assassination

Assassination is the murder of a prominent or important person, such as a head of state, head of government, politician, world leader, member of a royal family or CEO. The murder may be prompted by political or military motives, or by financial gain, to avenge a grievance, from a desire for fame, or from a mandate from a group, government, or individual — or from mental illness.


The assassination of Julius Caesar by senators, 44 BC.

Etymology

The word "assassin" is commonly believed to derive from the Arabic ḥaššāšīn, the name given in Western sources to the Nizari Ismaili order founded by Hassan-i Sabbah, whose agents carried out targeted killings of political and religious figures in Persia and Syria during the eleventh to thirteenth centuries. The term entered European languages through Crusader accounts and gradually generalised to any political murderer.

History

Assassination is one of the oldest tools of power politics. Famous ancient cases include the killing of the Athenian tyrant Hipparchus (514 BC), of Philip II of Macedon (336 BC), and of Julius Caesar by a conspiracy of senators on the Ides of March 44 BC. In the modern era, assassins killed four United States presidents — Lincoln (1865), Garfield (1881), McKinley (1901) and Kennedy (1963) — and the shooting of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914 triggered the First World War. Other history-changing assassinations include those of Mohandas Gandhi (1948), Martin Luther King Jr. (1968), Anwar Sadat (1981), Indira Gandhi (1984), Yitzhak Rabin (1995), and Shinzo Abe (2022).

Motives and methods

Motivations range from ideology and religion to nationalism, personal grievance, and the strategic aims of states. Methods have included blades, poison, firearms, explosives, and, in contemporary state practice, drone strikes and other "targeted killings", which many governments distinguish legally and morally from assassination proper. The prospect of assassination has shaped security practice, from bodyguards and armoured vehicles to intelligence vetting of crowds.

Law and ethics

Most legal systems simply treat assassination as murder. Internationally, the deliberate killing of foreign leaders in peacetime conflicts with the UN Charter and customary law; the United States has maintained an explicit executive ban on assassination since Executive Order 11905 (1976), reaffirmed in Executive Order 12333. Philosophers and political theorists have debated tyrannicide — the killing of a tyrant — since antiquity.
