John Gneisenau Neihardt (January 8, 1881 – November 3, 1973) was an American writer and poet, amateur historian and ethnographer. He is best known for Black Elk Speaks (1932), his rendering of the life and visions of the Oglala Lakota holy man Black Elk, which became a classic of Native American literature and a key text of the 1960s–70s revival of interest in Indigenous spirituality. Nebraska named him its Poet Laureate in 1921 — by later legislative act, "Poet Laureate in Perpetuity."

Life and work

Raised in Nebraska, Neihardt graduated from Nebraska Normal College at Wayne and worked as a teacher and banker before turning to writing. His early narrative poem The Song of Hugh Glass (1915) began his epic A Cycle of the West, five long poems — on Hugh Glass, Jedediah Smith, the Indian Wars, Three Rivers, and the Messiah (Ghost Dance) movement — completed in 1941 and published together in 1949. He wrote studies of the Omaha and other Plains peoples and travelled the Great Plains gathering material throughout his life.

Black Elk Speaks

In 1930 Neihardt travelled to the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation to research the Ghost Dance, met Nicholas Black Elk, and was chosen by the aging holy man to receive his life story and "great vision." Working through interpreters and his son Ben Black Elk, with notes taken by his daughters, Neihardt shaped the interviews into Black Elk Speaks (1932). The book sold modestly at first but became a best-seller after its 1961 reissue; it has been translated widely and studied both as spiritual testimony and as a literary collaboration, with the full transcripts later published as The Sixth Grandfather (1984).

Later life

Neihardt taught literature at the University of Missouri in Columbia for many years, lecturing memorably into old age, and remained a celebrated oral performer of his own verse. He died in Columbia in 1973, aged 92.
