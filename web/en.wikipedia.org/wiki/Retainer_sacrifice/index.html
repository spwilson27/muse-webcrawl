Retainer sacrifice

Retainer sacrifice is the practice of killing the servants, attendants, concubines or other members of a ruler's household at the ruler's death, so that they might continue to serve their master in the afterlife. Whether all such victims were killed, died by suicide, or in some cases were buried having died of other causes is debated by archaeologists. The practice is documented in several early complex societies.

Examples

The best-known archaeological examples are the subsidiary burials around the royal tombs of the First Dynasty of Egypt at Abydos, and the "royal tombs" of the Early Dynastic city of Ur in Mesopotamia, excavated by Leonard Woolley in the 1920s–30s, where the tomb attributed to Queen Puabi and the so-called Great Death Pit contained dozens of attendants, soldiers and musicians. Retainer sacrifice is also attested for Shang-dynasty China, where royal burials at Anyang included large numbers of human sacrificial victims; for early Japan, where it was formally prohibited and replaced symbolically by clay haniwa figures; for Scythian royal burials described by Herodotus; and in several pre-Columbian and African societies, including the kingdoms of Dahomey and the burials of the Mississippian culture.

Interpretation

The practice expresses a belief that social hierarchy continues after death and demonstrates the extraordinary power of early rulers. It generally declined as states developed professional administrations, and was condemned and suppressed in most later traditions, though echoes survived in practices such as sati in India.
