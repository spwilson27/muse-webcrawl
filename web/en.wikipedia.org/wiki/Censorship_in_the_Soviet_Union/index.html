Censorship in the Soviet Union

Censorship in the Soviet Union was pervasive and strictly enforced. Cultural control was exercised through a system of state institutions, principally Glavlit, the Main Directorate for the Protection of State Secrets in the Press, which censored all printed matter, broadcasts, and public performances from 1922 until the late 1980s. Soviet censorship combined prior restraint, ideological control by the Communist Party, and self-censorship by editors and authors, and it extended to libraries, foreign literature, maps, statistics, and even weather forecasts.

Origins and institutions

The Bolsheviks closed opposition newspapers within days of the October Revolution of 1917, and the Decree on the Press authorised suppression of "bourgeois" publications. Glavlit was established by decree on 6 June 1922 and issued an expanding list of prohibited information — the notorious index of topics that could not be mentioned publicly. Glavlit representatives were stationed in every publishing house and printing plant, and no item could be printed without a censor's authorisation number.

Scope

Censorship suppressed religious and anti-communist material, information about disasters, crime, suicide, industrial accidents, and military matters, and details of leaders' private lives. It also enforced ideological orthodoxy: works by purged figures were withdrawn and physically destroyed, encyclopaedia subscribers were sent replacement pages (most famously for the article on Lavrentiy Beria), and photographs were retouched to remove disgraced officials. Statistics that reflected badly on the regime, such as harvest failures and famine, were concealed; the results of the 1937 census were suppressed entirely.

Samizdat and dissent

Despite controls, uncensored literature circulated through samizdat — hand-typed and carbon-copied manuscripts passed from reader to reader — and through tamizdat, works published abroad and smuggled back in. Writers such as Boris Pasternak, Alexander Solzhenitsyn, and Joseph Brodsky became emblematic of the struggle between literature and the censor.

Glasnost and abolition

Under Mikhail Gorbachev's policy of glasnost after 1986, censorship was progressively relaxed; Glavlit's powers were curtailed, banned works were published in mass editions, and in 1990 the USSR Law on the Press formally prohibited censorship. The system ended with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.
