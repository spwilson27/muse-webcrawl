Impostor syndrome

Impostor syndrome
Also called: impostor phenomenon
Coined by: Clance & Imes, 1978
Not a formal diagnosis in DSM-5

Impostor syndrome, also known as the impostor phenomenon, is a psychological experience in which a person doubts their skills, talents or accomplishments and persistently fears being exposed as a fraud — despite external evidence of competence. Success is attributed to luck, timing or deceiving others, rather than to ability.

History and research

Psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described the phenomenon in 1978 from studies of high-achieving women, and Clance later developed the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale. Subsequent research found it affects men and women across professions — estimates suggest around 70% of people experience it at some point — with higher reported rates among ethnic minorities and in academia and medicine. It is associated with anxiety, depression, perfectionism and burnout, but is not classified as a mental disorder.

Types and coping

Valerie Young popularised subtypes including the perfectionist, the expert, the soloist and the superperson. Recommended coping strategies include recognising the pattern, documenting achievements, mentoring, normalising discussion of failure, and cognitive-behavioural techniques. Critics note that framing the issue purely as individual insecurity can overlook workplace cultures and structural bias that make people feel like outsiders. See Dunning–Kruger effect, the near-opposite distortion.
