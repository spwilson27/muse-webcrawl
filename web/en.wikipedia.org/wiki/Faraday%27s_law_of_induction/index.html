Faraday's law of induction

Faraday's law of induction (or simply Faraday's law) is a law of electromagnetism predicting how a magnetic field will interact with an electric circuit to produce an electromotive force (EMF). This phenomenon, known as electromagnetic induction, is the fundamental operating principle of transformers, inductors, electric motors, generators and solenoids.

Statement

Michael Faraday discovered induction in 1831 (independently matched by Joseph Henry in 1832). The law states that the induced EMF in a closed loop equals the negative of the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop: EMF = −dΦB/dt. The negative sign, expressing Lenz's law, indicates that the induced current opposes the change in flux that produces it. For a coil of N turns, the EMF is multiplied by N.

Maxwell–Faraday equation

In differential form, incorporated into Maxwell's equations, the law reads ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t: a time-varying magnetic field creates a spatially varying, circulating electric field. Faraday's law was the experimental basis for the unification of electricity and magnetism and, ultimately, for technologies of electrical power generation.
