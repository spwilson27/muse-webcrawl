Mole (Spanish: [ˈmole], from Nahuatl mōlli, "sauce") is a generic name for a number of sauces originally used in Mexican cuisine, as well as for dishes based on these sauces. Outside Mexico, it often refers specifically to mole poblano, the dark, complex sauce of Puebla containing dried chillies, nuts, seeds, spices, and a small amount of chocolate, traditionally served over turkey or chicken.

History

Pre-Hispanic Mesoamerican cooks ground chillies, tomatoes, squash seeds, and herbs into sauces on the metate; the conquest added Old World ingredients — almonds, cinnamon, cloves, bread, lard, and sugar — producing the baroque moles of the colonial convents. A famous legend credits the nuns of Puebla's Santa Rosa convent with improvising mole poblano for a visiting bishop (or viceroy). Oaxaca, the "land of the seven moles," preserves the widest family of regional variants, from the nearly black, chilhuacle-based mole negro to the fruit-sweetened manchamantel.

Preparation

Making mole is laborious: chillies are seeded, toasted, and soaked; nuts, seeds, spices, charred tortillas, and aromatics are fried and ground to a paste, then fried again in lard and thinned with broth to a sauce that may simmer for hours. Recipes routinely exceed twenty ingredients, and moles are made in quantity for festivals, weddings, and Day of the Dead offerings.

Cultural significance

Mole is a centrepiece of Mexican celebratory cooking, and the traditional cuisine of Michoacán and the mole traditions surrounding it were recognised when Mexican cuisine was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. Like other great layered sauces — compare the long-simmered sauce tomate tradition of France or the pounded heat of harissa — mole embodies a cuisine built from patient technique and local abundance.
