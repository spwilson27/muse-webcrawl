Bar and bat mitzvah

Bar mitzvah (Hebrew: בַּר מִצְוָה) and bat mitzvah (בַּת מִצְוָה) are Jewish coming-of-age rituals marking the point at which a child becomes accountable for their own actions under Jewish law. A boy reaches bar mitzvah at age 13; a girl reaches bat mitzvah at age 12 in Orthodox and Conservative practice, or 13 in Reform practice. "Bar mitzvah" literally means "son of the commandment" and "bat mitzvah" means "daughter of the commandment".

Before this age, parents bear responsibility for the child's observance; afterwards, the young person may participate in all areas of Jewish community life, be counted in a minyan (in denominations that count women), read from the Torah in synagogue, and is traditionally expected to observe the commandments personally.

History

The age of thirteen for boys is derived from rabbinic tradition: the Mishnah (Pirkei Avot 5:21) lists thirteen as the age for the commandments. The formal ceremony of being called up to the Torah developed in the Middle Ages, and elaborate celebrations grew in subsequent centuries. The first recorded bat mitzvah ceremony in the United States was that of Judith Kaplan, daughter of Rabbi Mordecai Kaplan, in 1922; bat mitzvah celebrations spread widely in the later twentieth century.

The ceremony

The celebrant is commonly called to the Torah (an aliyah) on the Shabbat nearest their birthday, chanting the blessings and often reading the Torah portion or haftarah. Many also lead parts of the service or deliver a d'var Torah (a short teaching). Training typically begins a year or more in advance, including Hebrew study and learning the cantillation marks (trope).

Celebration

A festive meal (seudat mitzvah) usually follows the service, and in contemporary practice celebrations may range from modest family gatherings to large parties. Gifts are customary; money given in multiples of 18 (chai, "life") is a common tradition. In Israel and elsewhere, some families mark the occasion with a trip or a charitable project (tzedakah).

Alternatives and variations

Some congregations hold adult bar and bat mitzvah ceremonies for those who did not have one as a child. Secular and Humanistic Jewish movements have developed alternative coming-of-age programmes, and some families mark the occasion with a simchat bat or other ceremonies for infants or adolescents.
