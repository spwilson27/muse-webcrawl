Yield spread

In finance, the yield spread (also called the credit spread when the comparison is to a risk-free benchmark) is the difference between the yields of two debt instruments, usually expressed in basis points. It measures the additional compensation an investor demands for bearing the extra risk — credit, liquidity, or otherwise — of the lower-quality instrument.

Common spreads

  • Corporate spreads: the yield of a corporate bond minus the yield of a comparable-maturity government bond. Widening corporate spreads signal deteriorating credit conditions.
  • TED spread: formerly the difference between LIBOR and the US Treasury bill rate, a gauge of perceived risk in the interbank market.
  • Mortgage spreads: the difference between mortgage rates and Treasury yields.
  • Yield curve spreads: differences between maturities of the same issuer class, such as the 10-year/2-year Treasury spread, whose inversion has historically preceded recessions.
  • Option-adjusted spread (OAS): the spread after accounting for embedded options, standard for mortgage-backed securities.

Interpretation

Spreads are dynamic: they widen in crises, when investors flee to safety — as in 2008, when investment-grade corporate spreads reached record levels — and narrow in calm or euphoric markets. The high-yield (junk bond) spread over Treasuries is one of the most closely watched indicators of financial stress and correlates strongly with subsequent default rates. Spread changes also drive bond returns independently of changes in benchmark rates, which is why fixed-income managers distinguish "rates risk" from "spread risk."
