Film score

A film score is original music written specifically to accompany a film. The score forms part of the film's soundtrack, which also usually includes pre-existing music, dialogue and sound effects, and comprises a number of orchestral, instrumental, or choral pieces called cues, which are timed to begin and end at specific points during the film in order to enhance the dramatic narrative and the emotional impact of the scene in question. Scores are written by one or more composers under the guidance of the director or producer and are usually performed by an ensemble of musicians, most often a full orchestra, in a process called scoring or spotting-to-picture.

History

Music accompanied films from the earliest public screenings in the 1890s, when pianists and theatre organs improvised or compiled accompaniments for silent films; studios soon issued cue sheets and published photoplay music libraries. The first notable original feature scores include Camille Saint-Saëns's music for L'Assassinat du duc de Guise (1908) and the large compiled-composed scores of the silent era. With the coming of sound, the Hollywood studio system of the 1930s produced the lush symphonic style of Max Steiner (King Kong, 1933; Gone with the Wind, 1939), Erich Wolfgang Korngold, and Alfred Newman. Later generations diversified the idiom: Bernard Herrmann's psychological strings for Hitchcock, Ennio Morricone's eclectic westerns, John Williams's revival of the grand orchestral score with Star Wars (1977), and the electronic and hybrid scores of Vangelis, Hans Zimmer and others from the 1980s onward.

Composition process

After a "spotting session" at which composer and director decide where music is needed, the composer writes cues to exact timings, often working with an orchestrator who expands sketches into full parts. Recording takes place on a scoring stage with the musicians synchronised to picture via click tracks and streamers. The score is then mixed with dialogue and effects by the dubbing (re-recording) mixer. Composers may use the leitmotif technique — recurring themes for characters, places, or ideas — inherited from Wagnerian opera.

Diegetic and non-diegetic music

Music whose source is visible or implied in the story world (a radio, an on-screen band) is diegetic or source music; the score proper is non-diegetic, heard by the audience but not by the characters. Films also make use of pre-existing songs ("compilation soundtracks"), and the boundary is sometimes deliberately blurred.

Recognition

The Academy Award for Best Original Score has been presented since the 1930s; notable multiple winners include Alfred Newman (9), John Williams (5), and Alan Menken (4). Film scores are also widely released as soundtrack albums and performed in concert.
