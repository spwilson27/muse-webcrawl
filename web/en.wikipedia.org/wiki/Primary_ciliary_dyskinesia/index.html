Primary ciliary dyskinesia

Primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD) is a rare inherited disorder characterised by abnormal structure and function of cilia, the microscopic hair-like organelles that move mucus and fluids across cell surfaces. Defective ciliary motion impairs mucociliary clearance in the airways, leading to chronic respiratory infections and progressive lung damage.

Signs and symptoms

Many affected newborns have respiratory distress despite being full-term. Children develop a year-round daily wet cough, chronic rhinosinusitis, recurrent ear infections (otitis media) that can cause hearing loss, and recurrent pneumonia. Without treatment, bronchiectasis — permanent widening and scarring of the airways — develops. Because cilia also determine left–right body orientation during embryonic development, about half of patients have situs inversus (mirror-image arrangement of the internal organs); the combination of situs inversus, chronic sinusitis, and bronchiectasis is called Kartagener syndrome. Male infertility is common because sperm flagella are structurally related to cilia, and female fertility may be reduced.

Genetics

PCD is genetically heterogeneous: mutations in more than 50 genes encoding ciliary components — including the dynein arm proteins DNAH5 and DNAI1 — have been identified. Most cases are autosomal recessive. Defects may remove the outer or inner dynein arms of the ciliary axoneme, disrupt the central microtubule pair, or prevent normal cilia assembly.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines clinical features with specialised testing: measurement of nasal nitric oxide (characteristically very low in PCD), high-speed video microscopy of ciliary beat pattern, transmission electron microscopy of ciliary ultrastructure, immunofluorescence, and genetic testing. PCD is frequently confused with cystic fibrosis, asthma, and immunodeficiency.

Management

There is no cure; treatment aims to preserve lung function through airway clearance physiotherapy, prompt antibiotics for infections, management of sinus and ear disease, and influenza and pneumococcal vaccination. Care is usually coordinated at specialist centres, and lung transplantation is an option in end-stage disease.
