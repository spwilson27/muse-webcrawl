Active intellect

The active intellect (Latin: intellectus agens), also called the agent intellect, is a concept in classical and medieval philosophy, first described by Aristotle in De Anima (Book III, chapter 5). Aristotle distinguished between the passive intellect, which receives intelligible forms, and the active intellect, which makes potential knowledge actual — as light makes potential colours actually visible.

[Image: Aristotle, philosopher of the active intellect]

Aristotle's account

Aristotle wrote that the active intellect is "separate, impassible, and unmixed, being in its essential nature activity," and compared it to an efficient cause that acts upon the passive intellect. Because he also suggested it alone is immortal and eternal, commentators have debated for two millennia whether the active intellect is part of the individual human soul or a separate, divine substance.

Later interpretations

Alexander of Aphrodisias identified the active intellect with God, while Al-Farabi, Avicenna and Averroes developed elaborate emanationist accounts in Islamic philosophy: Averroes held that both active and passive intellects are single and shared by all humanity, a doctrine known as monopsychism. Aquinas, by contrast, argued that each person has his or her own agent intellect as a power of the individual soul. The debate shaped medieval scholastic psychology and the reception of Aristotle in the Latin West.
