Palace of Fontainebleau

The Palace of Fontainebleau (French: Château de Fontainebleau) is a royal palace in the town of Fontainebleau, some 55 km southeast of Paris, set beside the forest of Fontainebleau. Occupied by French sovereigns from the twelfth century to the nineteenth — longer than any other French royal residence — it was transformed under Francis I into a masterpiece of French Renaissance art, enlarged by Henry IV, and favoured by Napoleon, who called it "the true home of kings, the house of ages". It is a national museum and, since 1981, a UNESCO World Heritage site.

History

A fortified hunting lodge stood on the site by the twelfth century; Saint Louis (Louis IX) enlarged it and founded a monastery nearby, calling Fontainebleau his "desert". The medieval castle was largely rebuilt from 1528 by Francis I, who created the Gallery of Francis I — decorated by the Italian artists Rosso Fiorentino and Primaticcio with frescoes and high-relief stucco that became the hallmark of the first School of Fontainebleau and a fountainhead of French Mannerism.

Henry IV added the great courtyards and a canal, and had his heir — the future Louis XIII — baptised here. Louis XIV and Louis XV altered the apartments; the Revolution emptied the palace, but Napoleon refurnished it, established his military school in it, kept Pope Pius VII confined here in 1812–14, and signed his first abdication at Fontainebleau on 6 April 1814 before taking leave of his Old Guard in the Cour d'Honneur — thereafter called the Cour des Adieux ("Court of Farewells"). A second School of Fontainebleau flourished under Henry II, and the palace saw the Edict of Fontainebleau (1685), by which Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes.

The palace today

The museum preserves the state apartments, the Francis I Gallery, the ballroom, the chapel of the Trinity, Napoleon's apartments and throne room — the only surviving Napoleonic throne room in France — and formal gardens including the great parterre designed under André Le Nôtre. Together with the forest, the palace remains one of France's most visited historic monuments.
