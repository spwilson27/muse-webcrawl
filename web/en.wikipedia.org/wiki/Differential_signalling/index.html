Differential signalling

Differential signalling
TypeElectrical signalling method
PrincipleSignal transmitted as the difference between two complementary voltages
AdvantagesCommon-mode noise rejection, low EMI, low voltage swings
Used byUSB, Ethernet, HDMI, LVDS, RS-485, CAN bus
A differential pair carries equal and opposite signals; the receiver responds only to the difference between them.

Differential signalling is a method for electrically transmitting information using two complementary signals. The technique sends the same electrical signal as a differential pair of signals, each in its own conductor. The pair of conductors can be wires in a twisted-pair or ribbon cable, or traces on a printed circuit board. The receiving circuit responds to the difference between the two signals, which results in a signal with a magnitude twice as large.

Advantages

Because external interference — crosstalk, power-supply noise, electromagnetic pickup — tends to affect both conductors equally (as common-mode noise), the receiver, which measures only the voltage difference, largely cancels it. Differential pairs also radiate far less electromagnetic interference than single-ended signals, since the opposing fields cancel, and they tolerate lower voltage swings, enabling faster data rates and lower power consumption. Twisting the pair keeps the two wires' exposure to interference nearly identical, preserving the rejection.

Applications

Differential signalling underlies most modern high-speed serial links: USB, Ethernet over twisted pair, HDMI and DisplayPort, PCI Express, SATA, and LVDS signalling inside laptops and instruments, as well as robust industrial standards such as RS-485 and the CAN bus and balanced audio connections in professional sound.
