Tarrafal camp

The Tarrafal camp (Portuguese: Campo do Tarrafal), officially the Colónia Penal do Tarrafal, was a concentration camp established in 1936 by the Estado Novo regime of António de Oliveira Salazar near the village of Tarrafal on the island of Santiago in Portuguese Cape Verde. Nicknamed the "camp of slow death" (campo da morte lenta), it held political prisoners — first Portuguese anti-fascists, and after its reopening in 1961, independence fighters from Angola, Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde.

History

The camp was created by decree in April 1936, months before the Spanish Civil War, to remove "undesirable" opponents of the dictatorship — communists, anarchists, republicans and striking workers — to a remote colony. Prisoners endured tropical disease, malnutrition, forced labour, torture and a notorious punishment cell, the frigideira ("frying pan"), a small unventilated concrete cell that became lethally hot in the sun. At least 32 prisoners died in the first phase before the camp closed in 1954 under international pressure.

Reopened in 1961 by minister Adriano Moreira during the Portuguese Colonial War, Tarrafal held members of the MPLA, PAIGC and other liberation movements, including figures of the Angolan and Guinea-Bissau independence struggles. The camp was finally closed after the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974.

Legacy

The site is preserved as a museum of resistance (Museu da Resistência) and is one of Cape Verde's most important memorials to the anti-colonial struggle.
