Mudéjar art

Mudéjar art, or the Mudéjar style, is a type of ornamentation and decoration in the Iberian Christian kingdoms of the late Middle Ages, strongly influenced by Islamic taste and workmanship. It arose from the living conditions of the Iberian Peninsula, where Muslim craftsmen — the Mudéjares, Muslims permitted to remain under Christian rule — continued to work for Christian and Jewish patrons long after the Reconquista advanced southward. The style is not a unified artistic movement so much as a synthesis: Islamic techniques such as carved stucco, glazed tile (azulejos), brick patterning, horseshoe and polylobed arches, and wooden artesonado ceilings were applied to Romanesque, Gothic and Renaissance buildings.

Development

The style developed from the 12th century in Castile (the brick Mudéjar of Sahagún, Toledo and Segovia) and reached its most refined expression in 14th-century Aragón and Andalusia. Masterpieces include the Alcázar of Seville, rebuilt for Pedro I of Castile by Muslim craftsmen from Toledo and Granada in the 1360s; the Aljafería palace in Zaragoza, originally an Islamic taifa palace; the Mudéjar towers of Teruel; and the synagogues of Santa María la Blanca and El Tránsito in Toledo. Craftsmen worked across religious lines: the same decorative language served churches, synagogues and palaces alike.

Characteristics

Hallmarks of Mudéjar architecture are the use of brick as both structure and decoration, minaret-derived bell towers, geometric interlace (sebka) ornament, Arabic calligraphy — sometimes pseudo-Kufic, sometimes carved by craftsmen for patrons who could not read it — and intricate wooden ceilings. The style persisted into the 16th and 17th centuries, and was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries as Neo-Mudéjar in bullrings, railway stations such as Madrid Atocha, and exhibitions.

The Mudéjar architecture of Aragón was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986 (extended 2001). The term mudéjar comes from the Spanish adaptation of Arabic mudajjan, "tamed" or "domesticated", referring to Muslims who submitted to Christian rule without converting.
