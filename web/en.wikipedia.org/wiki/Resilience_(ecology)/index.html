Resilience (ecology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In ecology, resilience is the capacity of an ecosystem to respond to a perturbation or disturbance by resisting damage and recovering quickly. Such perturbations and disturbances can include stochastic events such as fires, flooding, windstorms, insect population explosions, and human activities such as deforestation, fracking of the ground for oil extraction, pesticide sprayed in soil, and the introduction of exotic plant or animal species.

Definitions

The concept was introduced into ecological literature by the Canadian ecologist C. S. Holling in his 1973 paper "Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems". Holling distinguished engineering resilience — the speed of return to equilibrium after disturbance, related to resistance and stability near a single equilibrium — from ecological resilience, which emphasizes the magnitude of disturbance an ecosystem can absorb before it shifts to a different regime of processes and structures, an alternative stable state.

Disturbances of sufficient magnitude or duration can profoundly affect an ecosystem and may force it to reach a threshold beyond which a different regime predominates. Such regime shifts — for example, the transition of clear lakes to turbid states, coral reefs to algae-dominated systems, or grasslands to shrublands — can be difficult or impossible to reverse.

Factors affecting resilience

Resilience is generally thought to be promoted by biodiversity, particularly functional diversity and response diversity among species; by connectivity that allows recolonization; and by the presence of slow-changing variables and feedbacks that stabilize the current regime. Conversely, human pressures such as nutrient loading, overharvesting, habitat fragmentation, and climate change can erode resilience and increase the risk of abrupt shifts. Management approaches informed by resilience thinking focus on maintaining the processes that keep ecosystems within desirable states, monitoring early-warning signals such as critical slowing down, and building adaptive capacity in coupled social–ecological systems.

Applications

Resilience thinking underlies much modern conservation and natural-resource management, the work of organizations such as the Resilience Alliance and the Stockholm Resilience Centre, and assessments of planetary boundaries. It has also been extended beyond ecology to urban planning, disaster risk reduction, and public health.
