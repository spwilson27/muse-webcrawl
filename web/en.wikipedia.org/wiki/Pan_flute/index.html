A pan flute (also called panpipes or syrinx) is a musical instrument based on the principle of the closed tube, consisting of multiple pipes of gradually increasing length (and occasionally girth). Multiple varieties of pan flutes have been popular as folk instruments around the world. The pipes are typically made from bamboo, giant cane, or local wood, and are bound together in a raft or bundle.

The instrument is named for Pan, the Greek god of the wild, who in myth fashioned the first syrinx from the reeds into which the nymph Syrinx had been transformed.

Acoustics

Each tube is stopped at one end; the player blows across the open top, setting the enclosed air column vibrating. Pitch is determined by tube length — halving the length raises the pitch an octave — so the pipes are arranged in graduated order like the bars of a xylophone. Overblowing and tilting the instrument allow additional notes and semitones.

Regional varieties

  • Greece — the syrinx of antiquity, typically of seven reeds joined with wax.
  • Romania — the nai, a curved fan of about 20 tubes, brought to virtuoso prominence by Gheorghe Zamfir.
  • Andes — the siku (Aymara), antara and Spanish zampoña, often played in interlocking pairs (ira and arca) by two musicians sharing one melody.
  • Solomon Islands and Oceania — large bamboo panpipes used in ceremonial ensembles.
  • China — the paixiao, an ancient panpipe attested from early imperial times.

History

Panpipes are among the world's oldest instruments: Neolithic examples have been claimed, and the instrument is depicted in Cycladic art from the third millennium BC. Its independent invention in Europe, Asia, the Americas, Africa and Oceania reflects the simplicity of the closed-tube principle.
