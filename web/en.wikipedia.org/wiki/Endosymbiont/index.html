Endosymbiont

An endosymbiont or endobiont is any organism that lives within the body or cells of another organism, in a relationship that may be mutualistic, commensal, or parasitic. The most famous endosymbionts are the ancestors of mitochondria and chloroplasts, which began as free-living bacteria taken up by early eukaryotic cells.

Symbiogenesis

The endosymbiotic theory, championed by Lynn Margulis in the 1960s, holds that mitochondria descend from an alphaproteobacterium and chloroplasts from a cyanobacterium engulfed by ancestral eukaryotes. Evidence includes their double membranes, circular genomes, and bacterial-type ribosomes. Over evolutionary time most endosymbiont genes moved to the host nucleus, turning the symbiont into an organelle.

Living endosymbionts

Many insects depend on bacterial endosymbionts for nutrition: the bacterium Buchnera aphidicola supplies essential amino acids to aphids; Wigglesworthia aids tsetse flies; and Wolbachia, widespread in arthropods, manipulates host reproduction. Corals host photosynthetic dinoflagellates (zooxanthellae) whose loss causes coral bleaching. Legumes house nitrogen-fixing rhizobia in root nodules.

Transmission

Endosymbionts are often transmitted vertically from mother to offspring through the egg, leading to co-evolution and highly reduced symbiont genomes — among the smallest known in cellular life.
