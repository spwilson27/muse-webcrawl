Fictional universe

A fictional universe (also called a fictional world) is the internally consistent fictional setting for one or more works of fiction, generally consisting of a body of imaginary characters, locations, events, objects, and rules that differ from those of the real world. A fictional universe may be shared by many works, authors, and media, forming the basis of a media franchise. The process of constructing such a world is known as worldbuilding.

Characteristics

A fictional universe typically differs from reality in its geography, history, languages, physical laws, biology, or social structures, but maintains internal consistency: events in the world follow rules established by its creators, and audiences expect later stories to respect earlier ones. The agreed body of authoritative facts about a universe is its canon. Well-developed fictional worlds often include invented languages, maps, calendars, genealogies, and mythologies that may never fully appear in the published works but give the setting depth.

Examples

  • Middle-earth — the setting of J. R. R. Tolkien's The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings, with constructed languages such as Quenya and Sindarin and a history spanning thousands of years.
  • The Star Wars galaxy — setting of the Star Wars films and their expanded media.
  • The Star Trek universe — a future history of the United Federation of Planets shared by numerous television series and films.
  • The Marvel and DC universes — shared superhero settings built up by many writers and artists over decades of comics, later adapted into film franchises.
  • The Cthulhu Mythos — a shared setting originating with H. P. Lovecraft and extended by many other authors.
  • Westeros and Essos — the world of George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire.
  • The Wizarding World — the setting of the Harry Potter books and films.

Shared universes and continuity

When multiple authors or works contribute to one universe, questions of continuity arise: stories may contradict each other, and publishers manage this through canon policies, editorial oversight, and occasionally "reboots" or retcons (retroactive continuity changes). Fictional universes also extend beyond their original medium through tie-in novels, games, and fan fiction, in which fans create unofficial new material set in the world.

Multiverses

A multiverse is a collection of fictional universes linked by the idea that each is a parallel reality; comic-book publishers and franchises use the device to reconcile contradictory continuities and to allow alternate versions of characters to meet.
