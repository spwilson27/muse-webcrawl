Stalker (1979 film)

Directed by: Andrei Tarkovsky
Screenplay by: Arkady and Boris Strugatsky
Based on: Roadside Picnic
Starring: Alexander Kaidanovsky, Anatoly Solonitsyn, Nikolai Grinko
Release date: 1979
Running time: 162 minutes
Country: Soviet Union

Stalker (Russian: Сталкер) is a 1979 Soviet science fiction film directed by Andrei Tarkovsky, loosely based on the 1972 novel Roadside Picnic by Arkady and Boris Strugatsky, who also wrote the screenplay. It stars Alexander Kaidanovsky as the Stalker, a guide who leads two clients — a Writer and a Professor — through a mysterious forbidden area called the Zone.

Plot

In a bleak, unnamed country, a meteorite or visitation has created the Zone, a place sealed off by the military where the laws of physics are said to be unreliable. Within the Zone lies a Room said to grant the innermost wishes of anyone who enters it. The Stalker, one of the smugglers who illegally guide people into the Zone, leads the cynical Writer and the rational Professor on the perilous journey, throwing metal nuts tied with strips of cloth ahead to test for gravitational traps. On reaching the threshold of the Room, however, neither man dares enter, and the three return home.

Production

The production was troubled. The first version of the film, shot in Tajikistan and Estonia, was ruined when the experimental film stock was improperly developed, forcing Tarkovsky to reshoot almost the entire film with cinematographer Georgy Rerberg replaced by Alexander Knyazhinsky. Some filming took place beside chemical plants and a hydroelectric station in Tallinn, Estonia, and several members of the production, including Tarkovsky himself, later died of cancers that associates attributed to toxic conditions on location, though the connection has never been proved.

Style and reception

The film is famous for its extreme duration per shot — fewer than 150 shots in over two and a half hours — contrasting sepia-toned scenes outside the Zone with colour within it. It won the Prize of the Ecumenical Jury at Cannes and is regularly ranked among the greatest films ever made, placing in critics' polls for the British Film Institute's Sight and Sound. It has influenced numerous films and video games, including the S.T.A.L.K.E.R. game series.
