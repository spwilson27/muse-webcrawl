British Malaya

Period1786–1957
ComponentsStraits Settlements, Federated Malay States, Unfederated Malay States

British Malaya is the name loosely applied to the set of states on the Malay Peninsula and the island of Singapore that were brought under British control between the late eighteenth and the early twentieth centuries. It was never a single unified colony: it comprised the crown colony of the Straits Settlements (Singapore, Penang, Malacca and dependencies), the Federated Malay States (Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan and Pahang), and the Unfederated Malay States (Johor, Kedah, Kelantan, Perlis and Terengganu), each with different constitutional arrangements under local rulers who retained nominal sovereignty.

History

British involvement began with the acquisition of Penang in 1786 and Singapore, founded by Stamford Raffles in 1819. The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 divided the Malay world between British and Dutch spheres. From the Pangkor Treaty of 1874, British Residents were installed in the tin-rich western Malay states, and the Federated Malay States were created in 1895. The northern states were transferred from Siamese suzerainty by the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909.

Economy and society

British Malaya was one of the world's great export economies, producing much of the world's tin and, after the early 1900s, rubber. Large numbers of Chinese and Indian migrants were brought in to work the mines and plantations, creating the plural society that shaped modern Malaysia. Japanese forces conquered Malaya in 1941–42, and British rule was restored in 1945. The post-war Malayan Union (1946) provoked Malay opposition and was replaced by the Federation of Malaya (1948). A communist insurgency, the Malayan Emergency, began in 1948. The Federation achieved independence on 31 August 1957, and in 1963 combined with Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak to form Malaysia (Singapore separated in 1965).
