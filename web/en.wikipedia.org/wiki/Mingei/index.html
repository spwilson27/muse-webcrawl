Mingei

Mingei (民芸, "folk arts" or "arts of the people") is the Japanese folk-craft movement that developed in the late 1920s and 1930s. The term was coined in 1925 by the philosopher and art historian Yanagi Sōetsu (1889–1961), together with the potters Hamada Shōji and Kawai Kanjirō, to describe the beauty found in ordinary, utilitarian objects made by anonymous craftspeople.

Philosophy

Yanagi's theory held that humble everyday wares — bowls, jars, textiles, tools — made by hand, in quantity, from local natural materials, and intended for daily use possess an honest, unselfconscious beauty that self-conscious fine art often lacks. Mingei objects are characterized by being inexpensive, functional, representative of their region, and produced without the maker's individual ego. Yanagi drew on Buddhist thought and on the example of Korean Joseon-dynasty crafts, which he had championed earlier.

Legacy

In 1936 Yanagi founded the Japan Folk Crafts Museum (Nihon Mingeikan) in Tokyo. The movement influenced studio pottery worldwide, notably through the English potter Bernard Leach, who worked closely with Hamada. Mingei ideas about anonymous craftsmanship, natural materials, and beauty in use continue to shape contemporary craft and design in Japan, where the related tradition of Japanese pottery remains highly valued.
