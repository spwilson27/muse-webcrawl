In mathematics, differential topology is the field dealing with the topological properties of differentiable functions on differentiable manifolds. It is closely related to differential geometry, and together they make up the geometric theory of differentiable manifolds. Unlike pure topology, differential topology exploits the smooth structure of manifolds, allowing the use of calculus — derivatives, tangent spaces and transversality.

Core ideas

Fundamental tools include Sard's theorem on the measure of critical values, the theory of immersions and embeddings developed by Hassler Whitney, and transversality, which guarantees that generic maps intersect in well-behaved ways. Morse theory reconstructs the topology of a manifold from the critical points of a smooth function defined on it, and cobordism classifies manifolds up to being boundaries of higher-dimensional ones.

Milestones

The field came of age in the 1950s and 1960s: John Milnor's discovery in 1956 of exotic spheres — smooth manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard 7-sphere — showed that smooth and topological classification diverge; Stephen Smale's h-cobordism theorem and proof of the higher-dimensional Poincaré conjecture, and René Thom's work on cobordism and singularities, followed, earning Fields Medals. Results in differential topology feed into surgery theory, gauge theory and mathematical physics.
