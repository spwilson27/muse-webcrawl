Fast Ethernet

In computer networking, Fast Ethernet physical layers carry traffic at the nominal rate of 100 Mbit/s. The prior Ethernet speed was 10 Mbit/s. Fast Ethernet was standardized as IEEE 802.3u in 1995 and was the fastest version of Ethernet for three years before being superseded by Gigabit Ethernet. Devices are commonly capable of falling back to 10 Mbit/s for compatibility with older equipment.

Variants

The most common Fast Ethernet standard is 100BASE-TX, which runs over two pairs of Category 5 twisted-pair cable and dominated the market. Other variants include 100BASE-T4 and 100BASE-T2 for older cabling, and fiber-optic versions 100BASE-FX and 100BASE-SX. The competing 100VG-AnyLAN standard (IEEE 802.12) saw little adoption.

Technology

Fast Ethernet preserved the Ethernet frame format, MAC protocol, and CSMA/CD access method, while reducing the bit time from 100 nanoseconds to 10 nanoseconds. 100BASE-TX uses 4B5B encoding with MLT-3 signalling at 125 Mbaud. Full-duplex operation, standardized in 1997, allowed simultaneous 100 Mbit/s transmission in both directions.

Adoption

Fast Ethernet became the standard for local area networks in the late 1990s, used in office networks, switches, and early broadband routers. It has since been largely replaced by Gigabit Ethernet and faster standards, though 100 Mbit/s ports remain common in embedded and low-cost equipment.
