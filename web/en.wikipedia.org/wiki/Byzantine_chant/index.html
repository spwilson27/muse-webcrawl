Byzantine chant

Byzantine chant is the sacred chant of the churches following the rite of Constantinople: the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Eastern Catholic Churches of the Byzantine Rite. Developed in the Byzantine Empire from early Christian and Greek musical traditions, it is a monophonic (single-melody) vocal music, traditionally sung a cappella with a sustained drone called the ison.

Modal system

Like other early chant traditions, Byzantine music is organized into eight modes (echoi), the Octoechos, each with characteristic melodic formulas used to set hymns of the liturgical cycle. Hymn types include troparia, kontakia (once long poetic sermons in verse, pioneered by Romanos the Melodist), stichera, and canons.

Notation and reform

Medieval Byzantine notation used neumes written above the text. In 1814–1821 the "Three Teachers" — Chrysanthos of Madytos, Gregorios the Protopsaltis, and Chourmouzios the Archivist — reformed the notation into the simpler system still used today, enabling mass printing of chant books.

Practice today

Chant is sung by solo chanters (psaltai) and choirs in Greece, Cyprus, the Balkans, the Middle East, and the diaspora, with regional styles and related traditions in Slavic languages. Mount Athos remains a major center of the art. In 2019 UNESCO inscribed Byzantine chant on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
