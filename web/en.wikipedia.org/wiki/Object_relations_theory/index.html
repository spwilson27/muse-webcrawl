Object relations theory

Object relations theory is a school of psychoanalytic thought that emphasises the role of early relationships — especially with caregivers in infancy — in shaping the personality and later interpersonal relationships. In psychoanalytic usage, an "object" is a person (or part of a person) toward whom feelings and drives are directed; the theory holds that templates formed from these earliest relationships persist throughout life.

Development

The theory grew out of the work of Melanie Klein, who argued that infants relate to part-objects, such as the mother's breast, from the beginning of life. W. R. D. Fairbairn proposed that the libido is fundamentally object-seeking rather than pleasure-seeking, and Donald Winnicott introduced concepts such as the "good-enough mother", the holding environment and transitional objects. Margaret Mahler described the separation–individuation process, and Otto Kernberg later developed a systematic theory of borderline personality organization and techniques of transference-focused psychotherapy.

Concepts

Key ideas include internalisation of object representations, splitting of objects into "good" and "bad" part-objects, and projective identification. Object relations theory has strongly influenced attachment theory, relational psychoanalysis and contemporary psychodynamic psychotherapy.
