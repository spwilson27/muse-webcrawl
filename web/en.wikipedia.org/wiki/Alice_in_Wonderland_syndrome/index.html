Alice in Wonderland syndrome

Alice in Wonderland syndrome (AIWS), also known as Todd's syndrome or dysmetropsia, is a disorienting neurological condition that affects human perception. People with the syndrome may experience distortions of size (metamorphopsia): objects appear smaller (micropsia) or larger (macropsia) than they are, distances seem altered, and the perceived size of one's own body parts may change. The condition is named after Lewis Carroll's novel Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, in which the heroine experiences dramatic changes in size.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes are typically brief, lasting seconds to minutes. Besides micropsia and macropsia, sufferers may perceive objects as farther away (teleopsia) or closer (pelopsia), experience distorted body schema — hands or head feeling enlarged or shrunken — and altered time perception. Symptoms are usually not frightening to children once explained, and vision itself remains normal: the disturbance lies in perceptual processing, probably involving the temporo-parietal junction and occipital cortex.

Causes

AIWS is most often associated with migraine, particularly in children, and may occur as a migraine aura equivalent without headache. Other reported associations include Epstein–Barr virus infection and infectious mononucleosis, epilepsy, brain lesions and certain medications. The psychiatrist John Todd coined "Alice in Wonderland syndrome" in 1955, building on earlier descriptions by Coleman (1933) and Lippman (1952); Lewis Carroll himself suffered migraines, prompting speculation that his experiences inspired the novel's size changes.

Diagnosis and treatment

There is no specific test; diagnosis is clinical, after excluding ophthalmological and serious neurological causes with examination and, where indicated, imaging or EEG. No dedicated treatment exists: management targets the underlying condition, and reassurance is the mainstay, since the syndrome in children frequently resolves with age. Migraine prophylaxis may be considered when attacks are frequent.

Epidemiology

The syndrome is thought to be under-reported rather than truly rare; surveys of schoolchildren suggest lifetime prevalence of isolated symptoms in the range of several percent, with full syndromic presentations considerably less common.
