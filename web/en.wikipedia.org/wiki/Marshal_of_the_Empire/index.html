Marshal of the Empire

Marshal of the Empire (French: Maréchal d'Empire) was a civil dignity during the First French Empire. It was created by Sénatus-consulte on 18 May 1804 and largely resurrected the formerly abolished title of Marshal of France. During the French Revolutionary Wars the title had been abolished by the National Convention in 1793. A total of 26 men were created Marshals by Napoleon between 1804 and 1815.

Creation

The first promotions were announced on 19 May 1804, the day after Napoleon proclaimed himself Emperor of the French. Eighteen generals received the dignity, of whom four were honorary (François Christophe Kellermann, Jean Mathieu Philibert Sérurier, Joseph Lefebvre and Catherine-Dominique de Pérignon) and fourteen were active commanders. The marshals became the fourth estate of the Empire, after the Emperor, the great dignitaries, and the Grand Dignitaries.

Privileges and insignia

Marshals were addressed as "Monsieur le Maréchal", saluted with cannon fire, received plumes of white, and carried a blue velvet baton covered in golden eagles — the famous bâton de maréchal. The baton bore the Latin inscription Terror belli, decus pacis ("Terror in war, ornament in peace"). Marshals also enjoyed substantial salaries, pensions, and in some cases foreign princedoms, duchies, and estates granted by the Emperor.

The 26 marshals

The marshals included such famous names as Louis-Alexandre Berthier, Joachim Murat, André Masséna, Michel Ney, Jean Lannes, Nicolas Soult, Louis-Nicolas Davout, Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte (who became King Charles XIV John of Sweden), and Claude-Victor Perrin. Eight more were created after 1804, the last being Emmanuel de Grouchy in 1815, during the Hundred Days.

Later use

After the Bourbon Restoration, the title reverted to "Marshal of France". The dignity of Marshal of the Empire ended with the fall of Napoleon, though the memory of the original 26 marshals endures in the names of Paris boulevards — the Boulevards des Maréchaux — that ring the city.
