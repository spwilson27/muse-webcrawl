Daytime is the period of time each day during which a given location on a rotating planet experiences naturally illuminated conditions from direct sunlight. On Earth, daytime occurs on the half of the planet currently facing the Sun, and for any particular place it lasts from sunrise to sunset, varying in length with latitude and season.

Characteristics

As Earth rotates on its axis, each point on its surface (away from the polar regions) passes through the sunlit hemisphere (day), then across the terminator into darkness (night). Because of Earth's axial tilt of about 23.4° relative to its orbital plane, the length of daytime changes through the year: days are longer than nights in the summer hemisphere and shorter in winter. At the equator day and night are each roughly twelve hours year-round; at the summer solstice, locations inside the polar circles experience 24-hour daylight (the "midnight sun"), while the opposite pole endures polar night.

Length of daytime

Several conventions define the day's edges. Conventionally, daytime begins and ends when the upper edge (limb) of the Sun crosses the horizon; atmospheric refraction bends sunlight so that the Sun appears on the horizon when it is actually about 0.83° below it, lengthening the apparent day by several minutes at temperate latitudes. Twilight — civil, nautical and astronomical — extends the period of useful or detectable light before sunrise and after sunset. The exact day length can be computed from the sunrise equation, a function of latitude and the solar declination.

Daytime on other planets

Every rotating planet has a daytime on its sunward side, but lengths vary enormously: Mercury's solar day lasts about 176 Earth days; Venus rotates so slowly and retrogradely that its solar day is about 117 Earth days; Mars's day (a "sol") closely matches Earth's at 24 hours 39 minutes. Tidally locked exoplanets may have permanent daytime on one hemisphere and permanent night on the other.

Ecology and human life

The alternation of day and night structures nearly all life on Earth. Photosynthesis powers ecosystems from the sunlit zone; animals are adapted as diurnal, nocturnal or crepuscular; and humans' circadian rhythms, sleep, agriculture, working hours and religious observances (such as fasts defined from dawn to sunset) are keyed to daytime. Artificial lighting has progressively blurred the boundary, a change studied under headings such as light pollution and shift-work health effects.
