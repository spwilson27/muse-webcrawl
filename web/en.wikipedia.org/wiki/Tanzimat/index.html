Tanzimat

The Tanzimat (Ottoman Turkish: تنظيمات, "reorganisation") was a period of reform in the Ottoman Empire that began with the Edict of Gülhane in 1839 and concluded with the adoption of the first Ottoman constitution in 1876. The reforms aimed to modernise the empire, centralise its administration, and secure its territorial integrity against internal nationalism and external — especially European and Russian — pressure.

Reforms

The Tanzimat introduced sweeping changes: guarantees of security of life, honour and property for all subjects; regularised taxation and the abolition of tax farming; conscription on a universal basis; secular commercial, penal and procedural codes partly modelled on French law; new secular schools and a ministry of education; provincial administration reorganised under the Vilayet Law of 1864; and the Imperial Ottoman Bank and a modern census. The Reform Edict of 1856 proclaimed the legal equality of Muslim and non-Muslim subjects, a cornerstone of the ideology of "Ottomanism."

Assessment

The reforms modernised the Ottoman state and created the bureaucratic and educational foundations inherited by the Turkish Republic, but their implementation was uneven, hampered by fiscal weakness, foreign capitulations and conservative resistance. The period ended when Sultan Abdul Hamid II suspended the constitution in 1878, two years after its promulgation.
