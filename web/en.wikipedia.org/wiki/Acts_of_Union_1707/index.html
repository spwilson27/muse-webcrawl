The Treaty of Union, ratified by the Acts of 1707.

The Acts of Union, passed by the parliaments of England and Scotland in 1706 and 1707, united the two kingdoms — which had shared a monarch since the Union of the Crowns in 1603 — into a single Kingdom of Great Britain, with effect from 1 May 1707.

Background

Motivations included England's desire to secure the Protestant succession under the Act of Settlement 1701, which Scotland had refused to guarantee, and Scotland's economic distress after the failure of the Darien scheme colonial venture. English pressure, including the Alien Act 1705, combined with financial incentives — Scottish commissioners and investors were compensated, including for Darien losses, through the payment known as the Equivalent.

Terms

The Treaty of Union's 25 articles provided for a single Parliament of Great Britain at Westminster, a shared flag and coinage, free trade between the countries, and preservation of Scotland's separate legal system and courts, its Presbyterian Church settlement, and its universities and burgh privileges. Scotland sent 45 MPs and 16 representative peers to Westminster.

Reception

The Union was unpopular with much of the Scottish public, prompting riots, and Jacobite attempts to overturn it followed in 1715 and 1745. Nevertheless it endured, and was extended to Ireland by the Acts of Union 1800.
