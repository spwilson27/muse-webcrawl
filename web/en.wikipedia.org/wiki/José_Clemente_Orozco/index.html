José Clemente Orozco (23 November 1883 – 7 September 1949) was a Mexican painter and one of the three great figures of Mexican muralism, alongside Diego Rivera and David Alfaro Siqueiros. His large public murals explored political corruption, human suffering and the violence of history with a darker, more pessimistic vision than many of his contemporaries.

Detail of Orozco's fresco work, characterized by monumental figures and somber tones.

Early life

Orozco was born in Zapotlán el Grande (now Ciudad Guzmán), Jalisco. As a young man he damaged his left hand in a gunpowder accident and lost it, an injury that marked his life. He studied at the Academy of San Carlos in Mexico City, worked as a political cartoonist during the Mexican Revolution, and held his first solo exhibition in 1916.

Mural work in Mexico and the United States

In the 1920s Orozco painted murals at the National Preparatory School in Mexico City, including the controversial cycle later partially destroyed and repainted. From 1927 to 1934 he worked in the United States, producing frescoes at Pomona College (Prometheus, 1930, considered the first major fresco in the US by a Mexican artist), the New School in New York, and Dartmouth College, where The Epic of American Civilization (1932–1934) covers some 3,200 square feet.

Later work in Guadalajara

Returning to Mexico, Orozco executed some of his most celebrated works in Guadalajara, including the murals of the University of Guadalajara, the Government Palace, and the Hospicio Cabañas (1936–1939), whose dome fresco The Man of Fire is often called his masterpiece. He died in Mexico City in 1949 while working on another mural.
