Back-to-back house

Back-to-back houses are a form of terraced housing in England, built from the late 18th century through the early 20th century, in which pairs of houses share a rear wall so that each dwelling has only one external front wall, with no rear windows or yard of its own. They were built in large numbers in the industrial cities of northern and midland England, including Leeds, Birmingham, Liverpool, and Nottingham, to house rapidly growing working-class populations cheaply.

Design

A typical back-to-back had one room on each floor — often a cellar, a living room/kitchen, and one or two bedrooms — entered directly from the street or from a communal court. Because the houses shared three walls with neighbours, they were economical to build but suffered from poor ventilation and sanitation: windows existed only on the front, privies and water supplies were shared in the court, and through-draughts were impossible.

Health and reform

The type became notorious for overcrowding and disease, and medical officers of health campaigned against it. Building of new back-to-backs was effectively halted by the Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act 1909, which prohibited their construction. Leeds, the city most associated with them, was unusual in continuing to build an improved design with through-ventilation into the 1930s.

Survivals

Most back-to-backs were demolished in the slum clearances of the 20th century. Preserved examples include the Birmingham Back to Backs, a court of houses in Birmingham maintained by the National Trust as a museum illustrating working-class life from the 1840s to the 1970s.
