Young–Laplace equation

In physics, the Young–Laplace equation is an algebraic equation that describes the capillary pressure difference sustained across the interface between two static fluids, such as water and air, due to the phenomenon of surface tension or wall tension, although use of the latter is only applicable if assuming that the wall is very thin. The Young–Laplace equation relates the pressure difference to the shape of the surface or wall and is fundamentally important in the study of static capillary surfaces.

The equation is:

Δp = −γ ∇·n̂ = 2γHf = γ(1/R1 + 1/R2)

where Δp is the Laplace pressure (the pressure difference across the fluid interface), γ is the surface tension, n̂ is the unit normal pointing out of the surface, Hf is the mean curvature, and R1 and R2 are the principal radii of curvature.

History

The equation is named after Thomas Young, who developed the qualitative theory of surface tension in 1805, and Pierre-Simon Laplace, who completed the mathematical description in the years that followed. It also explains why the pressure inside a small spherical droplet is greater than the pressure outside: for a sphere, the equation reduces to Δp = 2γ/R.

Applications

The Young–Laplace equation governs the shape of liquid droplets, soap films and bubbles, capillary rise in narrow tubes, and the behaviour of menisci. In medicine it describes the pressure balance in the alveoli of the lungs, where pulmonary surfactant lowers surface tension; in engineering it underlies tensiometry methods that infer surface tension from drop shapes.
