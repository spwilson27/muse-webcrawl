Pontoon bridge

A pontoon bridge (or ponton bridge), also known as a floating bridge, uses floats or shallow-draft boats to support a continuous deck for pedestrian and vehicle travel. Its buoyancy limits the maximum load it can carry. Pontoon bridges are usually temporary, constructed in wartime or emergencies, though some permanent examples cross sheltered waters.

History

Floating bridges are ancient: the Persian king Xerxes I crossed the Hellespont in 480 BCE on bridges of lashed ships during his invasion of Greece. Chinese forces used iron-chain pontoon bridges as early as the 10th century CE. Armies have prized pontoon bridges ever since for rapidly crossing rivers where permanent bridges were absent or destroyed; the purpose-built bridging trains of the Napoleonic era and the ribbon bridges and Bailey bridges of the Second World War are famous examples.

Design

A typical design consists of a series of purpose-built watertight floats (pontoons), often of steel or inflatable construction, anchored against the current and connected by transverse beams carrying a roadway. Permanent civilian examples include several long crossings in the Seattle area, such as the Evergreen Point Floating Bridge on Lake Washington, among the longest floating bridges in the world.

Uses and limitations

Besides military use, pontoon bridges serve at festivals, in disaster relief, and as low-cost crossings. They are vulnerable to floods, floating debris, ice, and heavy seas, and their decks ride close to the water, restricting navigation.
