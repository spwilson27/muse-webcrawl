Bandenbekämpfung

Bandenbekämpfung (German for "bandit fighting" or "combating of bandits") was the doctrine and practice of anti-partisan warfare employed by Nazi Germany in the occupied territories during the Second World War, above all on the Eastern Front. By officially designating partisans, resistance fighters, and frequently entire civilian populations as "bandits" (Banden), the regime placed them outside the laws of war and used the doctrine to justify mass reprisals, village burnings, deportations, and mass murder.

Organization

Responsibility for rear-area security was divided between the Wehrmacht's rear army area commands and the SS and police apparatus of the Higher SS and Police Leaders. In the summer of 1942, Heinrich Himmler was given overall responsibility for Bandenbekämpfung in the civilian-administered occupied territories, and SS-Obergruppenführer Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski was appointed chief of the anti-partisan units (Chef der Bandenkampfverbände) in October 1942, coordinating operations from a dedicated staff.

Practice and crimes

Large-scale "dead zone" operations — such as Operation Cottbus in Belarus in 1943 — were nominally directed against partisan bands but in practice killed overwhelmingly unarmed civilians, including Jews who had escaped ghettos and peasants working in fields. Reported "enemy dead" routinely exceeded captured weapons by an order of magnitude, a discrepancy that historians cite as evidence that the victims were mostly non-combatants. Villages suspected of aiding partisans were burned and their inhabitants murdered or deported for forced labor.

Legacy

Historians regard Bandenbekämpfung as an integral part of the Nazi war of annihilation (Vernichtungskrieg) in the East, inseparably linked with the Holocaust and the Hunger Plan. Bach-Zelewski later testified at the Nuremberg trials about the doctrine's criminal character, while comparatively few of its practitioners were ever prosecuted.
