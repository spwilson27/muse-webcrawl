Parabolic antenna

TypeReflector antenna
Invented1888 (Heinrich Hertz)
Frequency rangeMicrowave, typically above 300 MHz
UsesSatellite communications, radio telescopes, radar, point-to-point links

A parabolic antenna is an antenna that uses a parabolic reflector, a curved surface with the cross-sectional shape of a parabola, to direct radio waves into a narrow beam or to collect them from a distant source. It is the most common form of high-gain antenna used at microwave frequencies, where its large electrical aperture produces high directivity. Familiar examples include satellite television dishes, radio telescopes, and radar antennas.

A parabolic reflector antenna with feed supported at the focal point.

Principle of operation

The paraboloid has the geometric property that rays arriving parallel to its axis are reflected to a single point, the focus, and conversely a source placed at the focus radiates a collimated beam parallel to the axis. A feed antenna (often a horn antenna) is placed at the focus, supported by struts. Because of this focusing action, the gain of a parabolic antenna increases with the size of the dish relative to the wavelength; the beamwidth is approximately inversely proportional to aperture diameter.

The maximum gain of an ideal parabolic antenna is determined by its aperture area and efficiency: G = η (πD/λ)², where D is the dish diameter, λ the wavelength, and η the aperture efficiency (typically 0.55–0.70).

History

Parabolic reflectors were first used for radio by Heinrich Hertz in 1888, in his experiments demonstrating the existence of electromagnetic waves; he used cylindrical parabolic reflectors for both transmitting and receiving spark-gap oscillators. In the 1930s, Grote Reber built one of the first parabolic radio telescopes. During the Second World War, parabolic dishes became standard for radar. In 1946, the first large steerable radio telescopes appeared, and dishes such as the 76-metre Lovell Telescope at Jodrell Bank (1957) and the 64-metre Parkes telescope (1961) followed. Consumer satellite television dishes became widespread in the 1980s and 1990s.

Types

  • Prime focus: the feed sits at the focus in front of the dish.
  • Cassegrain: a convex secondary reflector bounces the signal to a feed behind the main dish.
  • Offset: an off-axis section of a paraboloid, avoiding feed blockage; used for most home satellite dishes.
  • Shaped reflectors: modified surfaces for customised coverage patterns, common on communications satellites.

Applications

Parabolic antennas are used for satellite communications (both ground stations and spacecraft), deep-space communication networks, radio astronomy, weather and military radar, terrestrial microwave relay links, and satellite television reception.
