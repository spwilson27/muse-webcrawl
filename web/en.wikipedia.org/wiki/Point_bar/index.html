Point bar

A point bar is a depositional feature made of alluvium that accumulates on the inside bend of streams and rivers below the slip-off slope. Point bars are found in abundance in mature or meandering streams, and form where the flow is slower along the inner bank of a meander, allowing sediment carried by the river to settle out. They are the depositional counterpart of the cut bank, the eroded steep bank on the outside of the bend.

Formation

In a meandering channel, helicoidal flow carries the fastest current toward the outer bank, eroding it, while slower water near the inner bank deposits its load of sand and gravel. As the meander migrates outward, the point bar grows by lateral accretion; the sloping surfaces produced are called epsilon cross-bedding or inclined heterolithic stratification in the rock record. Point bar deposits typically fine upwards, from coarse gravel lag at the base to fine sand and silt near the top, reflecting the decrease in flow energy upward and toward the floodplain.

Related features

Point bars must not be confused with braid bars or mid-channel bars, which form in the middle of braided channels, or with the natural levee that borders a channel. As meander migration continues, a point bar may become vegetated and incorporated into the floodplain; the swales between successive accretion ridges are sometimes preserved as meander scrolls. Repeated deposition on point bars was historically a favourable setting for placer deposits of gold and other heavy minerals.
