Anatomy Act 1984

The Anatomy Act 1984 (c. 14), which governed anatomical examination in the United Kingdom until 2006.

The Anatomy Act 1984 (c. 14) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that regulated the use of human bodies and body parts for anatomical examination, medical education, and research. It replaced the earlier Anatomy Act 1832, modernising the licensing of anatomy teachers and premises, and setting out the conditions under which a person could bequeath their body for dissection after death, or under which relatives could authorise such use.

Provisions

The Act required persons carrying out anatomical examinations to hold a licence from the Secretary of State, and premises used for dissection to be licensed and open to inspection by Her Majesty's Inspectors of Anatomy. It provided that a person's written request, made before death, that their body be used for anatomical examination should be honoured, and dealt with the lawful possession, retention, and eventual disposal of bodies and parts, including imported material. Unlawful possession of anatomical specimens was made an offence.

Repeal

Following the organ-retention scandals at Alder Hey and elsewhere, the law governing human tissue was overhauled. In England, Wales, and Northern Ireland the Act was repealed and replaced by the Human Tissue Act 2004, which came into force in 2006 and placed consent at the centre of the lawful use of bodies and tissue, administered by the Human Tissue Authority. Scotland legislated separately through the Human Tissue (Scotland) Act 2006.
