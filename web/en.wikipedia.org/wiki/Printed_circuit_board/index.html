Printed circuit board

A multilayer PCB with components mounted.

A printed circuit board (PCB), also called a printed wiring board (PWB), is a flat laminated structure of conductive and insulating layers that mechanically supports and electrically connects electronic components. Conductive traces, pads, and other features are etched or printed onto a non-conductive substrate — usually fibreglass-reinforced epoxy laminate (FR-4) — replacing earlier point-to-point wiring.

History

Albert Hanson patented printed conductors on insulating boards in 1903, and Charles Ducas proposed electroplated patterns in 1925. Austrian engineer Paul Eisler, working in London, made the first functioning printed circuit boards in the 1930s–40s; they entered mass production after the Second World War through the US Army's auto-assembly process, and were released for commercial use in 1948.

Structure

A PCB alternates copper layers with insulating "prepreg" and core material. Holes called vias connect traces between layers. A coloured polymer solder mask (usually green) insulates the board and prevents solder bridges; a printed silkscreen legend marks component positions. Surface finishes such as HASL or gold plating (ENIG) protect exposed copper.

Manufacture

The standard subtractive process images the circuit pattern onto copper-clad laminate with photoresist, etches away unwanted copper, drills holes, plates the barrels, applies solder mask and legend, and routes the board outline. Design is done in EDA software, producing Gerber files for fabrication. Components are attached by through-hole soldering or surface-mount technology (SMT), typically with reflow soldering.

Types

  • Single-sided and double-sided boards
  • Multilayer boards with internal signal and power planes
  • Flexible ("flex") and rigid-flex circuits on polyimide film
  • HDI (high-density interconnect) boards with microvias
