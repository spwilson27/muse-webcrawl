Rayleigh criterion

The Rayleigh criterion is a conventional measure of the angular resolution of an optical instrument. Proposed by Lord Rayleigh (John William Strutt) in the 19th century, it states that two point sources are just resolvable when the central maximum of the diffraction pattern (Airy disk) of one image coincides with the first minimum of the other. At this separation the combined intensity dips by about 26% between the peaks (for incoherent sources of equal brightness), which the eye can just detect.

Formula

For a circular aperture of diameter D illuminated by light of wavelength λ, the minimum resolvable angular separation is

θ ≈ 1.22 λ / D  (radians)

The factor 1.22 comes from the first zero of the Bessel function governing circular-aperture Fraunhofer diffraction. For a slit or rectangular aperture the factor is 1.0. The corresponding concept in microscopy is the Abbe diffraction limit, d = λ/(2 NA).

Notes

The criterion is a convention rather than a hard physical limit: skilled observers and image-processing can sometimes separate sources below it (the empirical Dawes limit for telescopes is slightly tighter), while detector pixelation and noise can worsen practical resolution.
