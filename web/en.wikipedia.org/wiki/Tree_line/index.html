Tree line

The tree line is the edge of a habitat at which trees are capable of growing and beyond which they are not. It is found at high elevations and high latitudes. Beyond the tree line, trees cannot tolerate the environmental conditions (usually low temperatures, extreme snowpack, or associated lack of available moisture). The tree line is sometimes distinguished from a lower timber line, below which trees form forest with a closed canopy.

Causes

The primary limiting factor is temperature: tree growth generally requires a growing season with mean temperatures above roughly 5–7 °C. At similar climates, the tree line occurs at progressively lower elevations towards the poles: near the equator it can lie above 4,000 m (as in the Andes and on Mount Kenya), while in northern Siberia and Scandinavia it descends to sea level. Wind exposure, snow accumulation, soil conditions, and in some places aridity (a "desert" tree line) or human land use can also set local limits.

Types

  • Alpine tree line — occurs in mountains due to decreasing temperature with altitude;
  • Arctic tree line — the northern limit of tree growth in the Northern Hemisphere, bordering the tundra;
  • Antarctic tree line — the southern limit in the Southern Hemisphere;
  • Desert tree line — set by insufficient rainfall;
  • Exposure tree line — set by wind and salt spray, e.g. on coasts and islands.

Transition zone

Above the forest limit, trees often continue in stunted, deformed growth forms known as krummholz (German for "crooked wood"), shaped by wind and ice abrasion, before giving way to alpine tundra. Tree lines are sensitive climate indicators: many have advanced upslope and polewards with recent warming, though responses vary with local conditions.
