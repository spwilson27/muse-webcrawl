Ski-jump (aviation)

Ski-jump
TypeAircraft carrier takeoff ramp
First usedRoyal Navy, 1970s (HMS Hermes trials; Invincible class)

Typical angle7–14 degrees

In aviation, a ski-jump is an upward-curved ramp on an aircraft carrier flight deck that allows aircraft to take off from a short runway with a heavier load than a flat deck permits. As the aircraft travels up the ramp it is launched at a positive angle of attack, gaining altitude and time for its engines (and, for STOVL types, lift nozzles) to accelerate it to flying speed. The technique underpins STOBAR (short take-off but arrested recovery) and STOVL carrier operations.

History

The ski-jump was developed by the Royal Navy in the 1970s for the Sea Harrier, following studies by Lt Cdr Doug Taylor. HMS Hermes tested a temporary ramp, and the Invincible-class carriers were built with 7-degree (later 12-degree) ramps. The system proved cheaper and simpler than catapults and allowed operations in rough seas.

Users

Ski-jumps are used on the British Queen Elizabeth-class, the Russian Admiral Kuznetsov, China's Liaoning and Shandong, India's Vikramaditya and Vikrant, and STOVL carriers of Spain, Italy and other navies operating Harriers and F-35Bs. Compared with catapult launch, a ski-jump limits maximum takeoff weight and precludes most fixed-wing airborne early warning aircraft, but avoids the cost and complexity of catapult systems.
