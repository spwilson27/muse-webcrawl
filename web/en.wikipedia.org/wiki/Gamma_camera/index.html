Gamma camera

A gamma camera (also called a scintillation camera or Anger camera) is a device used in nuclear medicine to image the distribution of a gamma-emitting radiotracer within the body. It produces images called scintigrams and is the detector at the heart of planar scintigraphy and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT).

Construction and operation

The camera was developed by American physicist Hal Anger in 1957–58 at the Donner Laboratory. Gamma photons emerging from the patient pass through a collimator — a lead plate pierced by thousands of parallel holes that accepts only photons travelling in a chosen direction — and strike a large sodium iodide (NaI(Tl)) scintillation crystal. Each absorbed photon produces a flash of light, which is detected by an array of photomultiplier tubes (or, in modern cameras, silicon photomultipliers and semiconductor detectors such as CZT). Anger's position logic computes the (x, y) location of each event from the relative signals, and pulse-height analysis rejects scattered photons outside the photopeak energy window of the tracer — most commonly technetium-99m at 140 keV.

Clinical use

Gamma cameras image the function rather than the anatomy of organs: bone scans, myocardial perfusion studies, thyroid and renal scans, hepatobiliary (HIDA) studies, lung ventilation–perfusion scans and sentinel-node localisation. Rotating one or two camera heads around the patient and reconstructing the projections yields SPECT tomograms; hybrid SPECT/CT systems add anatomical imaging for attenuation correction and localisation.

Trade-offs

Spatial resolution is limited by collimator geometry (typically 7–15 mm), and the collimator absorbs the great majority of emitted photons, so sensitivity is low and examinations are slow compared with PET. The technique nevertheless remains widespread because technetium-99m tracers are inexpensive and produced on site from molybdenum generators.
