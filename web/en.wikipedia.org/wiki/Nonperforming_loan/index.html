Nonperforming loan


Labelled placeholder: bank facade

A nonperforming loan (NPL) is a bank loan that is subject to late repayment or is unlikely to be repaid by the borrower in full. Nonperforming loans are a major problem for banks: they reduce profitability, tie up capital, and, if large enough relative to a bank's holdings, can threaten its solvency. International financial institutions typically classify a loan as nonperforming when payments of interest and principal are past due by 90 days or more, or when interest payments equal to 90 days or more have been capitalized, refinanced or delayed by agreement.

Definitions

The International Monetary Fund defines a nonperforming loan as one where "payments of interest and principal are past due by 90 days or more, or interest payments equal to 90 days or more have been capitalized, refinanced, or delayed by agreement; or payments are less than 90 days overdue, but there are other good reasons — such as a debtor filing for bankruptcy — to doubt that payments will be made in full." The European Central Bank and the European Banking Authority apply similar criteria across the euro area.

Management and resolution

Banks manage NPLs through restructuring, foreclosure on collateral, write-offs, sale of loan portfolios to distressed-debt investors, or transfer to asset management companies ("bad banks"). High NPL levels were a central concern during the European sovereign debt crisis, particularly in Greece and Italy, and in the aftermath of banking crises elsewhere.

Economic significance

An elevated NPL ratio signals stress in the banking system and can constrain new lending, slowing economic recovery. Supervisors monitor the ratio closely and may require banks to raise provisions or capital when it rises.
