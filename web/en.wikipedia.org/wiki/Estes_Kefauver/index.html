Carey Estes Kefauver (July 26, 1903 – August 10, 1963) was an American politician from Tennessee. A member of the Democratic Party, he served in the U.S. House of Representatives from 1939 to 1949 and in the United States Senate from 1949 until his death. He became a national figure as chairman of the Senate Special Committee to Investigate Crime in Interstate Commerce — the Kefauver Committee — whose televised hearings in 1950–51 exposed organized crime to a mass audience.

The crime hearings

The committee travelled to cities across the country, questioning mobsters such as Frank Costello — whose hands, trembling under the television lights, became an iconic image — and Virginia Hill. The broadcasts were among television's first great public-affairs spectacles and made Kefauver a household name; he appeared on the cover of Time magazine and the committee's work helped spur federal action against the Mafia, though J. Edgar Hoover had long denied a national crime syndicate existed.

Presidential ambitions

Kefauver sought the Democratic presidential nomination in 1952 and 1956, campaigning in a coonskin cap as a symbol of independence from party bosses. In 1952 he won most primaries but lost the nomination at the convention to Adlai Stevenson. In 1956 the convention chose him as Stevenson's running mate; the ticket lost to Dwight D. Eisenhower. In the Senate he later chaired the Antitrust and Monopoly Subcommittee, investigating administered prices in industries such as pharmaceuticals and steel, and co-authored the Kefauver–Harris Drug Amendments of 1962, which required proof of drug efficacy. He opposed his region's party establishment on civil rights, refusing to sign the Southern Manifesto.

Death and legacy

Kefauver died of a heart attack in 1963 while still in office. The federal courthouse in Nashville and other institutions bear his name.
