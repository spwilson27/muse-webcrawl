Artur Immanuel Hazelius (30 November 1833 – 27 May 1901) was a Swedish teacher, scholar, and folklorist best known as the founder of the Nordic Museum and of Skansen, the world's first open-air museum, in Stockholm. His motto for the museum—"know yourself" (känn dig själv)—expressed his conviction that Swedes should understand their own cultural heritage.

Life and career

Hazelius was born in Stockholm, the son of Johan August Hazelius, an army officer and publicist. He studied at Uppsala University, earning a doctorate in philology in 1860 with work on Swedish language reform, and participated in the movement for Scandinavian spelling reform, publishing proposals for a more phonetic orthography. He worked as a teacher of Swedish language and literature, and wrote textbooks, while Scandinavianism—the movement for Nordic cultural unity—shaped his outlook.

Collecting and the Nordic Museum

On a journey through Sweden in the summer of 1872, Hazelius was struck by how rapidly industrialisation was sweeping away traditional peasant culture—costumes, furnishings, tools, and customs. He began buying objects on the spot and devoted the rest of his life to collecting. His Scandinavian-Ethnographic Collection opened to the public in Stockholm in 1873 and developed into the Nordic Museum, formally established as a foundation in 1880. Hazelius raised funds tirelessly, including through a famous series of lotteries and by displaying furnished room interiors and costumed figures that evoked entire environments rather than isolated specimens.

Skansen

In 1891 Hazelius opened Skansen, on the island of Djurgården in Stockholm, as an open-air extension of the museum. Historic farmhouses, cottages, a church, workshops, and other buildings from across Sweden were dismantled, transported, and re-erected there, staffed and furnished to recreate ways of life, with domestic and later wild Nordic animals kept on site. Skansen became the model for open-air museums worldwide—the word "skansen" is used generically in several languages—and remains one of Sweden's most-visited attractions, known for festivals such as Midsummer and its Christmas market.

Death and legacy

Hazelius worked to exhaustion and died in 1901; he is buried at Skansen. The Nordic Museum's great purpose-built home on Djurgården, completed in 1907, houses the collections he assembled, numbering hundreds of thousands of objects. His son Gunnar Hazelius succeeded him as Skansen's director but died young in 1905.
