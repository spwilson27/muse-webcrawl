Vigilantism

Vigilantism is the act of preventing, investigating, and punishing perceived offenses and crimes without legal authority. A practitioner is a vigilante, from the Spanish word for "watchman." Vigilantes typically justify their actions as a response to the inability or unwillingness of the state to enforce the law — a perceived failure of the legal system to deliver justice.

History

Organized vigilance committees were a recurring feature of the American frontier, where formal law enforcement was absent or weak. The San Francisco Committees of Vigilance of 1851 and 1856 hanged alleged criminals after summary proceedings; the Montana Vigilantes of 1863–64 executed suspected road agents, and the lynching of accused persons by mobs — particularly the racial terror lynchings of African Americans in the post-Reconstruction South — represented vigilantism's darkest form. The term "Lynch law" derives from this era of extralegal punishment.

In the modern era, vigilantism ranges from neighborhood watch excesses and "paedophile hunter" groups to cyber-vigilantism (doxing, hacker activism) and border militias. The 1984 Bernhard Goetz subway shooting in New York City became a national debate about self-defense and vigilantism. Fictional vigilantes, from Zorro to Batman, are a staple of popular culture, romanticizing the figure while real-world vigilante violence is treated by scholars as a threat to the rule of law and due process.

Analysis

Criminologists distinguish vigilantism from legitimate self-defense and citizen's arrest by its punitive, premeditated character and its claim to a policing role. It tends to flourish where confidence in police and courts is low, and its targets are frequently members of marginalized groups, making it a persistent subject of legal and ethical concern.
