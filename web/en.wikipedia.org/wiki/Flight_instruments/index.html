Flight instruments are the instruments in the cockpit of an aircraft that provide the pilot with data about the flight situation of that aircraft, such as altitude, airspeed, vertical speed, heading and much more other crucial information in flight. They improve safety by allowing the pilot to fly the aircraft in level flight, and make turns, without a reference outside the aircraft such as the horizon. Visual flight rules (VFR) require an airspeed indicator, an altimeter, and a compass or other suitable magnetic direction indicator. Instrument flight rules (IFR) additionally require a gyroscopic pitch-bank (artificial horizon), direction (directional gyro) and rate of turn indicator, plus a slip-skid indicator, adjustable altimeter, and a clock. Flight into instrument meteorological conditions require radio navigation instruments for precise takeoffs and landings.

The term is sometimes used loosely as a synonym for cockpit instruments as a whole, in which context it can include engine instruments, navigational instruments, and radios. In many modern aircraft, electronic flight instrument systems replace mechanical instruments.

The basic six

Most aircraft are equipped with a standard set of six primary flight instruments, arranged in a standard pattern that became known as the "basic six" or "six pack":

  • Airspeed indicator (ASI) – shows the speed of the aircraft relative to the surrounding air.
  • Attitude indicator (AI), or artificial horizon – shows the aircraft's orientation relative to the horizon.
  • Altimeter – shows the aircraft's altitude above sea level.
  • Turn coordinator (TC) – shows the rate and direction of turn and whether the turn is coordinated.
  • Heading indicator (HI), or directional gyro – shows the aircraft's heading relative to magnetic north.
  • Vertical speed indicator (VSI) – shows the rate at which the aircraft is climbing or descending.

Pitot-static instruments

The airspeed indicator, altimeter, and vertical speed indicator operate from the pitot-static system, which measures ram air pressure from the pitot tube and static pressure from a static port. Blockage of either source can cause erroneous readings on all three instruments.

Gyroscopic instruments

The attitude indicator, heading indicator, and turn coordinator traditionally use gyroscopes driven by vacuum or electrical power. Rigidity in space allows the attitude indicator to display pitch and bank, while the heading indicator provides a stable heading reference that must periodically be aligned with the magnetic compass.

Electronic flight instrument systems

Modern airliners and general aviation aircraft increasingly use an electronic flight instrument system (EFIS), in which a primary flight display (PFD) combines the functions of the basic six on a single screen, supplemented by a multifunction display (MFD). Air data computers and attitude and heading reference systems (AHRS) supply the data digitally.
