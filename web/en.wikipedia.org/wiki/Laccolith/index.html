Laccolith

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A laccolith is a sheet-like intrusive igneous body (a pluton) that has been injected between layers of sedimentary rock, forcing the overlying strata upward into a dome. Laccoliths have a flat floor and a domed roof, and are typically fed by a vertical dike. They are distinguished from sills, which spread between layers without doming them, and from lopoliths, which are saucer-shaped and sag downward.

Formation

Laccoliths form when relatively viscous magma, often of intermediate to felsic composition, is intruded at shallow depth and spreads laterally; its pressure lifts the roof rocks. They range from a few hundred metres to several kilometres across. The concept was developed from the work of Grove Karl Gilbert in the Henry Mountains of Utah in 1877, where erosion has exposed classic examples. Well-known laccolithic mountains include the La Sal and Abajo ranges in Utah, the Black Hills intrusions of South Dakota and Wyoming (including the phonolite of Devils Tower, whose form is debated), and Navajo Mountain.

Related forms

When erosion strips away the soft overlying sedimentary layers, the resistant igneous core of a laccolith may stand out as a mountain massif. Multiple stacked laccoliths fed from a common source are sometimes termed a laccolith complex or, informally, "Christmas-tree" laccoliths.
