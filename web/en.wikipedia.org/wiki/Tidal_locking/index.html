Tidal locking

A tidally locked body rotates exactly once per orbit, keeping one face toward its partner.

Tidal locking is the gravitational phenomenon by which an astronomical body comes to have the same rotational period as its orbital period around its partner, so that it always presents the same hemisphere to that partner. Earth's Moon is tidally locked in this way: although it rotates on its axis, it does so exactly once per orbit, so from Earth only one side of the Moon is ever visible (apart from small wobbling effects called libration). The same state, also called synchronous rotation or captured rotation, is found in most of the large moons of the Solar System.

Mechanism

The partner's gravity raises tides on the orbiting body, deforming it into a slightly elongated shape. If the body rotates faster than it revolves, friction carries the tidal bulge ahead of the line joining the two bodies; the partner's gravity pulls back on the offset bulge, exerting a torque that slows the rotation. If the body rotates more slowly, the bulge lags and the torque speeds it up. Either way the rotation is driven toward synchronism. The timescale for locking is extremely sensitive to distance — it grows with the sixth power of the orbital separation — so close satellites lock quickly while distant planets may never lock within the age of the universe.

Examples and variants

  • All the major moons of Jupiter and Saturn, and Earth's Moon, are synchronously rotating.
  • Pluto and its moon Charon are mutually locked: each keeps the same face toward the other, the end state of tidal evolution for a close binary.
  • Mercury sits in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance rather than a 1:1 lock, rotating three times for every two orbits — a stable alternative made possible by its eccentric orbit.
  • Many close exoplanets — especially planets in the habitable zones of red dwarfs — are expected to be tidally locked, with a permanent day side and night side, although thick atmospheres or oceans may redistribute heat and soften the contrast.

Consequences

Before locking is complete, tides dissipate energy as heat inside the body (tidal heating), which can drive volcanism or maintain subsurface oceans. After locking, tidal evolution nearly stops for a circular orbit. For Earth, the Moon's tides are still slowing our planet's rotation: the day lengthens by roughly two milliseconds per century, and the Moon recedes about 3.8 cm per year, measured by laser ranging to retroreflectors left on its surface.
