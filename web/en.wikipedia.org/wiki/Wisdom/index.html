Wisdom
GreekSophia (σοφία); practical wisdom: phronesis
Related conceptsKnowledge, intelligence, prudence, sagacity, insight

Wisdom is the ability to think and act using knowledge, experience, understanding, common sense and insight. Wisdom is associated with attributes such as unbiased judgment, compassion, experiential self-knowledge, self-transcendence and non-attachment, and virtues such as ethics and benevolence. It has been regarded as one of the cardinal human virtues across cultures and religious traditions, and is often personified—as the Greek goddess Athena, the figure of Sophia, or the biblical Lady Wisdom of the Book of Proverbs.

In philosophy

Greek philosophy distinguished sophia (theoretical wisdom, contemplation of truth) from phronesis (practical wisdom or prudence, knowing how to act well). For Plato, wisdom was one of the four cardinal virtues; for Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics, phronesis—the capacity to deliberate well about what is good and advantageous—is indispensable to all the virtues, while sophia is the highest intellectual virtue, directed at the most honourable objects. The very word philosophy means "love of wisdom": Socrates, as portrayed by Plato, claimed wisdom began in recognizing one's own ignorance.

In religion

In the Hebrew Bible, wisdom (hokmah) is celebrated in the wisdom literature—Proverbs, Job, and Ecclesiastes—and is personified as a woman calling in the streets; the "fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom". In Christianity, wisdom is counted among the gifts of the Holy Spirit and identified with Christ in some Pauline writings. In Buddhism, prajñā (insight into the true nature of reality) is one of the perfections and, with compassion, a central aim of practice. Hindu, Taoist, and Confucian traditions likewise prize the wise person's harmony with the order of things.

Psychological study

Psychologists have operationalized wisdom for empirical study. The Berlin wisdom paradigm defines it as expert knowledge in the fundamental pragmatics of life, assessed through responses to difficult life dilemmas, and finds wisdom-related performance peaks in later middle age rather than increasing indefinitely. Other models emphasize humility, perspective-taking, emotion regulation, and reflection; scales such as Monika Ardelt's three-dimensional wisdom scale measure cognitive, reflective, and affective components. Research generally finds wisdom only weakly related to intelligence and years of education, and more strongly to experience, openness, and reflective habits.
