Photometry (optics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Photometry is a branch of optics that deals with the measurement of light in terms of its perceived brightness to the human eye. It is distinct from radiometry, which measures radiant energy in absolute physical terms. Photometry weights measured optical power at each wavelength by the luminosity function, a standard model of the sensitivity of the human eye to different wavelengths.

Quantities and units

The SI base unit is the candela (cd), the luminous intensity of a source. Derived quantities include luminous flux in lumens (lm), illuminance in lux (lm/m²), luminance in cd/m², and luminous efficacy in lumens per watt. By definition, monochromatic radiation at 540 THz (about 555 nm, where the eye is most sensitive) has a luminous efficacy of exactly 683 lm/W. The photopic luminosity function V(λ), standardised by the CIE in 1924, peaks at 555 nm; a separate scotopic function V′(λ), peaking near 507 nm, describes dark-adapted vision.

Measurement

Classical photometers compared an unknown source with a standard by eye; modern instruments use silicon photodiodes filtered to approximate V(λ), or spectroradiometers that weight a measured spectrum computationally. Photometric measurements govern lighting design for workplaces and roads, display and projector specifications, and standards for vehicle lamps and signal lights.
