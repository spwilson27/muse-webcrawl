The aye-aye (Daubentonia madagascariensis) is a long-fingered lemur, a strepsirrhine primate native to Madagascar with rodent-like, ever-growing incisor teeth and a special thin middle finger. It is the world's largest nocturnal primate and is characterised by its unusual method of finding food: it taps on wood with its elongated middle finger, listening for hollow chambers made by insect larvae — a technique called percussive foraging — then gnaws holes in the wood and extracts the insects with the same finger. The aye-aye is the only extant member of the genus Daubentonia and family Daubentoniidae.

Description

Adults weigh about 2–2.7 kg, with a head-body length of 36–43 cm and a bushy tail longer than the body. The coat is coarse, dark brown to black, tipped with white guard hairs. The aye-aye has large, membranous ears, forward-facing eyes adapted to nocturnal life, and continuously growing incisors unique among primates. Its third finger is thin and ball-and-socket jointed — the only primate with such a joint — while the fourth finger is the longest and is used for hooking out food.

Behaviour and ecology

Aye-ayes are solitary, nocturnal foragers that spend the day sleeping in spherical nests built high in tree forks. Their diet includes insect larvae, seeds, nectar, fungi, and fruit such as coconuts and mangoes, which they gnaw open. Males range over large home ranges (up to about 300 hectares) that overlap those of several females. The aye-aye fills an ecological niche similar to that of a woodpecker, absent from Madagascar.

Conservation

The aye-aye is listed as Endangered by the IUCN owing to habitat loss from slash-and-burn agriculture and logging. It is also killed on sight by some local people who regard it as an omen of death and evil (fady), a belief that has historically caused heavy persecution. Captive breeding programmes exist at institutions including the Duke Lemur Center. The species is protected under Malagasy law and listed on CITES Appendix I.

Taxonomy

When first described in the 18th century, the aye-aye was classified variously as a rodent or a marsupial because of its unusual teeth. Molecular studies confirm it as a highly divergent lemur, possibly the earliest-branching lineage of the group. A second, much larger species, the giant aye-aye (Daubentonia robusta), became extinct within roughly the last millennium.
