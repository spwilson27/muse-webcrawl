Screw thread

A machine screw showing a helical V-thread wrapped around its cylindrical shaft.

A screw thread, often shortened to thread, is a helical structure used to convert between rotational and linear movement or force. A screw thread is a ridge wrapped around a cylinder or cone in the form of a helix, with the former being called a straight thread and the latter a tapered thread. Screw threads are the essential feature of the screw as a simple machine, and of threaded fasteners and lead screws.

Structure and terminology

The mechanical advantage of a screw thread depends on its lead, the distance the screw advances in one complete turn. Key parameters include the major and minor diameters, the pitch (distance between adjacent thread crests), the thread angle of the profile, and the flank, crest, and root of the ridge. Most threads are V-shaped; square, trapezoidal (Acme), and buttress profiles are used where high loads or efficient power transmission are needed. Threads may be right-handed — tightening clockwise, the default — or left-handed, used where rotation would otherwise loosen the fastener.

Standards

The ISO metric screw thread is the most commonly used system worldwide, designated by the letter M followed by major diameter and pitch in millimetres. Imperial systems include the Unified Thread Standard (UNC, UNF, UNEF) used in the United States, and the older British Whitworth thread, the first national standard, introduced by Joseph Whitworth in 1841. Pipe threads such as NPT and BSP are tapered or parallel profiles designed to seal fluid connections.

Manufacture

Threads are produced by cutting with taps and dies, single-point turning on a lathe, thread milling, grinding, or by rolling between dies, which cold-forms the profile and leaves a stronger, smoother thread with favourable grain flow. Internal threads are usually tapped; external threads on mass-produced fasteners are usually rolled.
