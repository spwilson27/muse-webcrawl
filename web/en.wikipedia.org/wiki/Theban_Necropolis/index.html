The Theban Necropolis is a necropolis on the west bank of the Nile, opposite Thebes (modern Luxor) in Upper Egypt. It was used for ritual burials for much of the Pharaonic period, especially during the New Kingdom, and contains some of the most famous tombs and mortuary temples of ancient Egypt.

Components

The necropolis comprises several distinct areas. The Valley of the Kings holds the rock-cut tombs of New Kingdom pharaohs, including Tutankhamun (KV62). The Valley of the Queens contains the tombs of royal wives and children, most famously the richly painted tomb of Nefertari. Deir el-Medina was the village of the craftsmen who built the royal tombs, and the surrounding hills—Sheikh Abd el-Qurna, Dra' Abu el-Naga', el-Assasif, el-Khokha, and Qurnet Murai—hold hundreds of private tombs of nobles and officials, many celebrated for their wall paintings of daily life. Along the edge of the cultivation stand the great mortuary temples, including those of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari, Ramesses II (the Ramesseum), and Ramesses III at Medinet Habu.

History

Burials on the Theban west bank began in the late Old Kingdom and Middle Kingdom, but the necropolis reached its height after Thebes became the capital of a reunified Egypt. Robbery was a problem even in antiquity; royal mummies were re-hidden by priests in caches at Deir el-Bahari and in the tomb of Amenhotep II. The area forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site inscribed in 1979.
