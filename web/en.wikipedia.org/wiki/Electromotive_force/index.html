Electromotive force

Electromotive force (emf, denoted ℰ) is the electrical action produced by a non-electrical source, such as a battery or an electric generator. It is defined as the work done per unit charge in moving charge around a complete circuit, and is measured in volts. Despite its name, emf is not a force but an energy per unit charge, analogous to a voltage.

Sources

Emf arises from several mechanisms: chemical action in batteries and fuel cells, electromagnetic induction when the magnetic flux through a circuit changes (Faraday's law of induction), the photovoltaic effect in solar cells, and thermoelectric effects. In a generator, emf is produced as conductors move through a magnetic field.

Emf and terminal voltage

A real source of emf has internal resistance, so the voltage available at its terminals under load is less than the emf by the voltage dropped across that resistance: V = ℰ − Ir. The emf equals the terminal voltage only when no current flows. The concept of back emf describes the opposing emf generated in motors and inductors, which limits current.
