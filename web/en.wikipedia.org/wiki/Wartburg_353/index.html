The Wartburg 353 was a medium-sized family car produced by the East German manufacturer Automobilwerk Eisenach (AWE) in Eisenach from 1966 to 1988. It succeeded the Wartburg 311 and was in turn replaced by the four-stroke Wartburg 1.3. In total, well over a million examples of the 353 and its variants were built, making it the mainstay of the Wartburg marque through most of the period of the German Democratic Republic.

Development and design

The 353 was developed from the transitional 312 model, which had already received the new body shell planned for the 353 combined with the older chassis. The production 353 used a separate ladder-type frame with a modern, angular four-door saloon body designed in-house. A distinctive feature of the car was its engine: a 993 cc water-cooled three-cylinder two-stroke unit derived from pre-war DKW designs, producing about 45 PS (later uprated to 50 PS). Drive was to the front wheels through a four-speed gearbox with a freewheel device, a carry-over from DKW practice that allowed clutchless gear changes on overrun but required engine braking to be used with care.

Variants

Alongside the saloon, AWE offered the 353 Tourist estate (Kombi) and, mainly for export and commercial users, the Trans pickup. The car was sold in several Western markets, including the United Kingdom (where it was marketed as the Wartburg Knight), Belgium, Finland and Greece, where its low price made it attractive despite its smoky two-stroke engine.

Revisions

The model received a series of detail changes during its long production life. In 1975 the 353W ("W" for Weiterentwicklung, further development) introduced disc front brakes, a revised interior and other safety improvements. A more substantial facelift in 1985 brought a new front panel with rectangular headlamps and a revised grille, together with an electronically controlled carburettor. Production ended in 1988 when the 353 gave way to the Wartburg 1.3, which used a Volkswagen-designed four-stroke engine built under licence.
