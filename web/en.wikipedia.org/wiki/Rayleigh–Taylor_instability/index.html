Rayleigh–Taylor instability

The Rayleigh–Taylor instability is an instability of the interface between two fluids of different densities that occurs when the lighter fluid pushes into the heavier one — equivalently, when a heavy fluid is accelerated into, or supported against gravity above, a lighter fluid. Perturbations at the interface grow into characteristic rising bubbles and falling spikes that eventually produce turbulent mixing.

Development and theory

The phenomenon was described by Lord Rayleigh and given a full mathematical treatment by G. I. Taylor in 1950. For two fluids of densities ρ1 and ρ2 under acceleration g, the growth rate of a perturbation of wavenumber k is governed by the Atwood number A = (ρ1 − ρ2)/(ρ1 + ρ2); growth is fastest for long wavelengths and is opposed by surface tension. The instability is closely related to the Richtmyer–Meshkov instability (impulsive acceleration, as by a shock) and the Rayleigh–Plateau and Kelvin–Helmholtz instabilities.

Examples

Rayleigh–Taylor mixing appears in supernova explosions — the "fingers" of heavy elements seen in the Crab Nebula and in simulations of SN 1987A — in inertial confinement fusion, where it threatens capsule compression; in salt domes and mantle plumes in geology; in volcanic and atmospheric flows; and in everyday settings such as the mushroom cloud of an explosion or water suspended above oil.
