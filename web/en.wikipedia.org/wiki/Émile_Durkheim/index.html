Émile Durkheim

David Émile Durkheim (French: [emil dyʁkɛm] or [dyʁkajm]; 15 April 1858 – 15 November 1917) was a French sociologist. Durkheim formally established the academic discipline of sociology and is commonly cited as one of the principal architects of modern social science, along with Karl Marx and Max Weber.

Émile Durkheim
Born: 15 April 1858, Épinal, France
Died: 15 November 1917 (aged 59), Paris, France
Known for: Social facts, collective consciousness, anomie, division of labour, sacred and profane
Notable works: The Division of Labour in Society (1893), The Rules of Sociological Method (1895), Suicide (1897), The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912)

Biography

Born in Épinal into a rabbinical family, Durkheim studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris. He taught at the University of Bordeaux, where he founded the first French department of sociology, and later at the Sorbonne in Paris. He founded the journal L’Année sociologique in 1898. Much of his family and circle were lost in the First World War, and he died in Paris in 1917.

Thought

Durkheim argued sociology should study social facts — ways of acting external to the individual and capable of exercising constraint. In The Division of Labour in Society he contrasted mechanical solidarity in traditional societies with organic solidarity based on interdependence. Suicide used statistics to show suicide rates vary with social integration and regulation, distinguishing egoistic, altruistic, anomic, and fatalistic types. In The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, based on studies of Aboriginal Australian religion, he defined religion through the sacred and the profane and argued religion expresses society worshipping itself, generating collective effervescence.

Legacy

Durkheim’s concepts — anomie, collective consciousness, and social integration — remain foundational in sociology, anthropology, education, and criminology.
