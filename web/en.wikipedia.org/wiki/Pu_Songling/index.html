Pu Songling (Chinese: 蒲松齡; pinyin: Pú Sōnglíng; 5 June 1640 – 25 February 1715) was a Chinese writer of the Qing dynasty, best known as the author of Liaozhai zhiyi (Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio), a collection of nearly five hundred supernatural tales written in classical Chinese. Although he aspired to an official career, Pu repeatedly failed the higher civil-service examinations and spent most of his life as a private tutor in Shandong; his fame is entirely posthumous and literary.

Life

Pu was born in Zichuan (now Zibo), Shandong, into a family of modest means with literary traditions. A precocious student, he passed the county-level examination at nineteen with first-place honours, but thereafter failed the provincial examination again and again — a humiliation he dramatised in tales about examination candidates driven mad or consoled by ghosts. From 1670 he served for decades as secretary and tutor in the household of the local gentry family Bi, which gave him leisure to write. He only received a nominal senior licentiate degree, granted by seniority, at the age of seventy-one.

Strange Tales

The Liaozhai zhiyi, completed in draft around 1679 and revised until late in Pu's life, collects some 494 tales of fox spirits, ghosts, flower fairies, corrupt officials and luckless scholars, framed in lapidary classical prose and often closed with the author's own commentary signed "the Historian of the Strange". Legend holds that Pu set up a roadside tea stall to gather stories from travellers. The collection was first printed in 1766, decades after his death, and has been continuously reprinted, adapted into plays, operas and films (notably the Hong Kong film A Chinese Ghost Story, 1987) — its fox-spirit romances such as "Nie Xiaoqian" and "Yingning" are touchstones of Chinese popular culture. Pu also wrote vernacular plays, songs and a treatise on agriculture.

Legacy

Pu Songling is regarded as the supreme master of the classical Chinese short tale (zhiguai and chuanqi traditions), combining fantasy with sharp satire on the examination system and official corruption. His former residence in Zichuan is preserved as a museum, and the Liaozhai has been translated into many languages, including John Minford's Penguin Classics selection.
