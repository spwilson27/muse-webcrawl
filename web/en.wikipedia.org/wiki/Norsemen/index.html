Norsemen

The Norsemen (or Northmen) were a North Germanic ethnic group of early medieval Scandinavia who spoke Old Norse. During the Viking Age (roughly 793–1066 AD) they established themselves through trade, raiding, exploration and settlement across wide areas of Europe and the North Atlantic, founding settlements in Iceland, Greenland and briefly in North America, and states including the Kievan Rus', the Danelaw in England and Normandy in France.

Terminology

The word "Norseman" first appears in Old English and Old Frankish sources and originally referred to Norse-speaking people generally. In modern usage, "Norsemen" is often used interchangeably with "Vikings", though víkingr in Old Norse referred more specifically to a person engaged in seaborne expeditions, while most Norsemen were farmers, craftsmen and traders. The related term "Varangians" describes Norsemen active in the east, in the lands of the Rus' and in Byzantine service.

Society and culture

Norse society was organised around kin groups and chieftains, with free farmers (karls), nobles (jarls) and enslaved people (thralls). Assemblies called things settled legal disputes. Norse religion centred on gods such as Odin, Thor and Freyja before the gradual Christianisation of Scandinavia between the 8th and 12th centuries. The Norsemen left a rich literary legacy in the sagas and skaldic poetry, recorded mainly in medieval Iceland, and their language influenced English, French and the Celtic languages of the British Isles.

Exploration and settlement

Norse sailors reached the Faroe Islands, Iceland (settled from about 874), Greenland (from about 985 under Erik the Red) and, around 1000 AD, Vinland in North America — the settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland is confirmed archaeologically. In the east they founded trading centres and polities along the river routes to the Black and Caspian Seas.
