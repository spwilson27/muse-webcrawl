Herd behavior

Herd behavior is the behavior of individuals in a group acting collectively without centralized direction. The term can refer to the behavior of animals in herds, packs, bird flocks, fish schools and swarms, and by extension to human conduct in demonstrations, riots, sporting events, religious gatherings and financial markets.

In animals

Groups of animals — from schooling fish to flocking starlings — coordinate their movements through simple local rules followed by each individual, such as aligning with neighbours and maintaining separation. Such collective behavior can confuse predators, improve foraging and reduce the chance that any one individual is taken.

In humans

Human herd behavior arises when people follow the actions of a larger group, sometimes disregarding their own information or judgement. Economists describe information cascades, in which rational individuals imitate predecessors because they infer that earlier actors possessed useful knowledge. Herding is frequently invoked in explaining speculative bubbles and market crashes, stock-market momentum, bank runs and the rapid spread of fads and fashions.

Mechanisms and criticism

Proposed mechanisms include conformity, social proof, reputational incentives (for example, fund managers who fail conventionally rather than succeed unconventionally, a point made by John Maynard Keynes) and simple informational dependence. Critics note that not all coordinated action is mindless imitation: apparent herding may reflect common responses to shared information rather than copying as such.
