The Black Consciousness Movement (BCM) was a grassroots anti-apartheid movement in South Africa that emerged in the late 1960s, filling the political vacuum after the African National Congress and Pan Africanist Congress were banned and their leaders jailed or exiled following the Sharpeville massacre. Its central figure was the medical student Steve Biko.

Philosophy

Drawing on thinkers such as Frantz Fanon and the American Black Power movement, Black Consciousness held that liberation must begin psychologically: black people (defined by the movement to include Africans, Coloureds, and Indians oppressed by apartheid) had to reject the inferiority instilled by white racism, take pride in their identity, and lead their own struggle rather than depend on white liberals. Biko's writings, collected in I Write What I Like, argued that "the most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed."

Organisations and repression

The movement's first organisation was the South African Students' Organisation (SASO), founded in 1968 after black students broke from the multiracial but white-led NUSAS. The Black People's Convention (1972) served as an umbrella political body, and community programmes promoted self-reliance. BCM ideas strongly influenced the school students who rose up in the Soweto uprising of 1976. The state responded harshly: Biko died in police custody in September 1977 after severe head injuries, and the following month the government banned the movement's organisations wholesale.

Legacy

Though suppressed, Black Consciousness reshaped South African resistance, injecting a new generation of activists into the struggle and leaving an enduring imprint on the country's political culture and debates about race and identity.
