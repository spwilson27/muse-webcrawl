Black Belt (U.S. region)

The Black Belt is a region of the Southern United States, named originally for its rich, dark, fertile soil — a crescent of chalky prairie running through central Alabama and Mississippi — and later for the large African American population descended from enslaved people brought to work its cotton plantations. In the context of the Black Consciousness Movement and Black nationalist thought, the Black Belt acquired a political meaning: its Black-majority counties were seen as the heartland of a distinct African American nation within the United States.

Political significance

In 1928 the Communist International adopted the "Black Belt thesis", holding that African Americans in the region constituted an oppressed nation entitled to self-determination. Later movements, including the Republic of New Afrika in the late 1960s, claimed the Black Belt states as a national territory. More practically, the region was a centre of civil rights organising — including SNCC's voter-registration work in Lowndes County, Alabama, where the Lowndes County Freedom Organization adopted the black panther as its symbol — and remains one of the most reliably African American voting regions in the country, though also one of its poorest.
