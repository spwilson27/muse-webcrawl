Vopěnka's principle

Vopěnka's principle is a large cardinal axiom in set theory, named after the Czech mathematician Petr Vopěnka. Intuitively, it states that the universe of sets is so large that in any proper class of structures of the same type, one structure can be embedded into another. It is stronger than the existence of most standard large cardinals short of inconsistency, and it has many equivalent formulations in set theory, category theory, and model theory.

Statement

One formulation: for every proper class C of structures in a fixed first-order language, there exist distinct members A and B of C and an elementary embedding from A into B. Another formulation states that for every proper class of ordinals there is a proper class of cardinals beyond them, and the principle is equivalent to the assertion that for every natural number n, there exists a C(n)-extendible cardinal.

Consequences

Vopěnka's principle implies the existence of a proper class of supercompact, huge, and many other large cardinals. In category theory it implies that every locally presentable category has strong completeness properties and that no large discrete full subcategory exists in accessible categories. In graph theory, a consequence is that for any proper class of graphs, one graph is a subgraph (or minor) of another.

History

Vopěnka originally advanced the principle in the context of his work on alternative set theory and large cardinals in the 1960s. It was initially proposed semi-seriously — Vopěnka reportedly doubted its consistency — but it has since been found to follow from very strong (though still not proven inconsistent) large cardinal hypotheses and has become a standard hypothesis in some areas of category theory.
