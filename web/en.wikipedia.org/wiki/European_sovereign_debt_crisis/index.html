European sovereign debt crisis

European debt crisis
Period2009 – c. 2015 (acute phase 2010–2012)
Countries most affectedGreece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Cyprus, Italy
Key responsesBailout programmes, EFSF/ESM, ECB bond purchases, fiscal austerity

The European sovereign debt crisis, often called the Euro area crisis or Eurozone crisis, was a multi-year debt and banking crisis that took place in the European Union from 2009. Several eurozone member states — notably Greece, Portugal, Ireland, Spain, and Cyprus — were unable to repay or refinance their government debt or to bail out over-indebted banks without the assistance of third parties such as other eurozone countries, the European Central Bank (ECB), and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

Origins

The crisis had its origins in the global financial crisis of 2007–08, which exposed large fiscal deficits, high public debt, and fragile banking systems in several member states. In late 2009, Greece revealed that its budget deficit was far larger than previously reported, triggering a loss of market confidence and a sharp rise in borrowing costs. Because eurozone members share a currency but not a treasury, they could not devalue or print money to manage their debts, and investors began to fear defaults and even exits from the euro.

Bailouts

In May 2010 the eurozone countries and the IMF agreed a €110 billion loan package for Greece, followed by rescue programmes for Ireland (2010), Portugal (2011), a second and third programme for Greece (2012 and 2015), assistance for Spanish banks (2012), and a programme for Cyprus (2013). The loans were conditional on fiscal consolidation and structural reforms, commonly described as austerity, which deepened recessions in the affected countries and provoked widespread protests. Greece's 2012 private-sector involvement deal imposed the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history.

Policy responses

The crisis led to the creation of the European Financial Stability Facility and its permanent successor, the European Stability Mechanism, and to reforms of EU economic governance, including the Fiscal Compact. A turning point came in July 2012, when ECB president Mario Draghi pledged to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro; the ECB's Outright Monetary Transactions programme, and later quantitative easing from 2015, sharply reduced borrowing costs. Steps were also taken toward a banking union, with the Single Supervisory Mechanism and Single Resolution Mechanism.

Consequences

The crisis caused severe recessions, mass unemployment — especially youth unemployment in southern Europe — and political upheaval, contributing to the rise of anti-austerity and eurosceptic movements. Although the acute phase subsided by the mid-2010s, public debt levels in several countries remained well above pre-crisis levels.
