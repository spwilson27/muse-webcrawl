Mathematical olympiad

A mathematical olympiad is a competition in which young mathematicians solve challenging mathematical problems under timed examination conditions. Unlike routine school exercises, olympiad problems require ingenuity and proof-based reasoning rather than heavy computation. The best-known event is the International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO), held annually since 1959.

History

Mathematical competitions for school students have roots in 19th-century Hungary, where the Eötvös competition began in 1894. National olympiads spread through Eastern Europe in the early 20th century. The first IMO took place in Romania in 1959 with seven participating countries; it now draws teams from over one hundred countries, each sending up to six pre-university students.

Format

The IMO consists of six problems spread over two consecutive days, three problems per day in a 4.5-hour session. Each problem is worth seven points, for a maximum of 42. Problems span algebra, combinatorics, geometry and number theory, avoiding calculus and other university topics. Gold, silver and bronze medals are awarded to roughly the top half of contestants in an approximate 1:2:3 ratio.

National and regional olympiads

Most countries run selection pyramids of regional and national competitions feeding into their IMO teams, such as the British Mathematical Olympiad and the United States of America Mathematical Olympiad. Regional events include the Balkan Mathematical Olympiad, the Asian Pacific Mathematics Olympiad and the European Girls' Mathematical Olympiad, founded in 2012 to encourage female participation.

Training and impact

Olympiad training emphasises problem-solving heuristics and proof techniques. Many prominent research mathematicians, including numerous Fields Medal winners such as Terence Tao and Grigori Perelman, competed in olympiads as students.
