Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (CPTSD, or c-PTSD) is a stress-related mental disorder that may develop in response to prolonged or repeated exposure to traumatic events from which escape is difficult or impossible, such as childhood abuse or neglect, domestic violence, trafficking, torture, or being a prisoner of war. It was formally recognised as a distinct diagnosis in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, released in 2018, though it is not a separate diagnosis in the DSM-5.

Symptoms

CPTSD includes the three core symptom clusters of PTSD — re-experiencing the trauma, avoidance of reminders, and a persistent sense of current threat — plus three clusters of "disturbances in self-organisation": severe problems regulating emotions, a persistently negative self-concept (for example beliefs of worthlessness accompanied by shame and guilt), and persistent difficulties in forming and sustaining relationships.

History of the concept

The concept was proposed by psychiatrist Judith Herman in her 1992 book Trauma and Recovery, which argued that survivors of prolonged trauma often showed a clinical picture broader than PTSD alone. Subsequent research groups, including Bessel van der Kolk and the ICD-11 working group on stress-related disorders, developed field trials that supported distinguishing CPTSD from PTSD and from borderline personality disorder.

Diagnosis

In ICD-11, a diagnosis of CPTSD requires meeting the criteria for PTSD plus the three self-organisation disturbances, with significant functional impairment; PTSD and CPTSD are mutually exclusive diagnoses. The International Trauma Questionnaire is a commonly used self-report measure aligned with the ICD-11 criteria.

Treatment

Expert consensus guidelines generally recommend phase-based treatment: an initial phase of safety, stabilisation and skills building (including emotion regulation), followed by trauma-focused processing such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy or EMDR, and then reintegration. Clinical trials suggest standard PTSD treatments can help, but people with CPTSD may benefit from longer, sequenced interventions addressing the self-organisation symptoms.
