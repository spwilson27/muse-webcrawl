Disability rights movement

The disability rights movement is a global social movement that seeks to secure equal opportunities and equal rights for all people with disabilities. Its goals include accessible public facilities, transportation and information; freedom from discrimination in employment, housing and education; independent living; and the full participation of disabled people in public life, under the slogan "Nothing about us without us."

History

Organised activism by disabled people dates to the early twentieth century, including organisations of blind and deaf people and, after the world wars, veterans' groups. The modern movement crystallised in the 1960s and 1970s, influenced by the civil rights movement. At the University of California, Berkeley, Ed Roberts and other students with severe disabilities founded the independent living movement, leading to the first Center for Independent Living in 1972. In Britain, the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) articulated the "social model of disability", distinguishing impairment from the disability imposed by social barriers.

Key campaigns and law

In the United States, activists pressed for regulations implementing Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, culminating in the 1977 sit-ins — including a 25-day occupation of a federal building in San Francisco. The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) prohibited discrimination and mandated accessibility in public accommodations. Comparable measures followed elsewhere, including the UK's Disability Discrimination Act 1995 (later folded into the Equality Act 2010) and the European Union's accessibility directives. Internationally, the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) codified a rights-based approach and has been ratified by the great majority of states.

Ideas and continuing issues

The movement reframed disability from a medical or charity problem to a question of civil rights, design and social organisation — seen in curb cuts, step-free access, captioning and accessible websites, which frequently benefit non-disabled people too. Debates continue over sheltered workshops and subminimum wages, institutional versus community living, assisted dying, and the representation of disability in media.
