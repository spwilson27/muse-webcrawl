Powered exoskeleton

A powered exoskeleton (also known as a power suit, exoframe or exosuit) is a wearable mobile machine consisting of an external framework worn by a person, with motors, pneumatics, levers or hydraulics that augment the wearer's strength, endurance or mobility. Applications range from medical rehabilitation — enabling people with spinal cord injuries to stand and walk — to industrial support and military load carriage.

A rehabilitation exoskeleton allows a user with lower-limb paralysis to walk with crutches for balance.

History

Concepts date to the nineteenth century, but the first serious engineering effort was General Electric's Hardiman (1965–1971), a full-body hydraulic exoskeleton that proved too heavy and uncontrollable to walk. Research revived in the 2000s with projects such as Berkeley's BLEEX and Sarcos's XOS. Commercial medical devices followed: the Israeli-designed ReWalk, Ekso Bionics' Ekso, and Cyberdyne's HAL (Hybrid Assistive Limb), which reads bioelectric signals from the skin. ReWalk received US FDA clearance in 2014 for personal use by people with spinal cord injury.

Applications

  • Medical: gait rehabilitation after stroke or spinal cord injury; personal mobility aids.
  • Industrial: passive and active suits that support lifting and overhead work, used in automotive plants, shipyards and logistics to reduce fatigue and injury.
  • Military: load-carriage prototypes such as Lockheed Martin's HULC and ONYX; no full powered armour has entered service.

Limitations

Power supply remains the principal constraint: batteries limit untethered operation to a few hours. Other challenges include actuator weight, control latency and safety, cost (medical devices have retailed in the tens of thousands of dollars), and the need for the device to adapt to varied terrain and users.
