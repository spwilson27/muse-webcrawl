Intrinsic value (finance)

In finance, intrinsic value is the perceived or calculated value of a company, including tangible and intangible factors, using fundamental analysis. It is distinct from the current market price and need not equal book value. Investors compare intrinsic value estimates with market price to identify under- or overvalued securities, the central practice of value investing as developed by Benjamin Graham and David Dodd.

Value investors seek a "margin of safety" between price and intrinsic value.

Estimation methods

  • Discounted cash flow (DCF) — the present value of projected free cash flows, discounted at a rate such as the weighted average cost of capital, plus a terminal value.
  • Dividend discount model — values equity as the present value of expected dividends (Gordon growth model).
  • Relative valuation — multiples such as P/E, EV/EBITDA, and price-to-book compared with peers; strictly a pricing rather than intrinsic method.
  • Asset-based valuation — adjusted net asset value of tangible and intangible assets minus liabilities.
  • Residual income model — book value plus discounted earnings in excess of the cost of equity.

Options

For an options contract, intrinsic value has a precise meaning: the difference between the underlying's price and the strike price that is in the holder's favour—max(S − K, 0) for a call and max(K − S, 0) for a put. Any remaining premium is time (extrinsic) value, reflecting volatility and time to expiry.

Limitations

Intrinsic value estimates are highly sensitive to assumptions about growth, margins, and discount rates, and depend on the quality of forecasts; two analysts can arrive at materially different values. Graham's "margin of safety"—buying only at a substantial discount to estimated value—is intended to compensate for such uncertainty.
