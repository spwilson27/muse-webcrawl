Reciprocal altruism

In evolutionary biology, reciprocal altruism is a behaviour whereby an organism acts in a manner that temporarily reduces its fitness while increasing another organism's fitness, with the expectation that the other organism will act in a similar manner at a later time. The concept was developed by Robert Trivers in 1971 as an explanation for the evolution of cooperation between unrelated individuals.

Conditions

For reciprocal altruism to evolve, several conditions are generally required: the individuals must interact repeatedly, so that today's donor can be tomorrow's recipient; they must be able to recognise one another (or at least remember past interactions); the cost to the donor must be smaller than the benefit to the recipient; and cheaters who accept help without reciprocating must be detectable and punished, typically by withdrawal of future help.

Examples

Classic examples include blood-sharing among common vampire bats, in which bats that failed to feed regurgitate blood to roost-mates that have fed them before, and grooming and alliance support among primates. Cleaner fish servicing large predatory clients without being eaten are another frequently cited case. In game-theoretic terms, the repeated prisoner's dilemma shows how strategies such as tit for tat can stabilise conditional cooperation.

In humans

In humans, reciprocal altruism is invoked to explain friendship, favour exchange, moral emotions such as gratitude, guilt and moralistic aggression, and the widespread norm of reciprocity. It is distinguished from kin selection, in which altruism is directed toward genetic relatives, though in practice the two mechanisms often overlap.
