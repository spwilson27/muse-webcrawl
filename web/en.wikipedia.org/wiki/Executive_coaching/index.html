Executive coaching

Executive coaching
FieldManagement development, applied psychology
ClientsSenior managers and executives
Typical tools360-degree feedback, psychometric assessment, goal-setting

Executive coaching is a form of one-to-one developmental intervention in which a coach works with a senior manager or executive (the coachee) to improve leadership performance, develop specific skills or behaviours, support transitions into new roles, or address derailing tendencies. It is usually commissioned and paid for by the coachee's organisation, creating a three-way relationship between coach, client, and sponsor. Coaching is distinguished from therapy by its focus on work performance and future goals rather than on mental health treatment.

Practice

Engagements typically run for six to twelve months and begin with assessment — often including interviews, psychometric instruments, and 360-degree feedback from colleagues — followed by agreement of development goals and a series of confidential sessions in which the coach uses questioning, feedback, and reflection to help the client generate insight and change behaviour. Progress is reviewed against the agreed goals with the sponsor while preserving the confidentiality of session content.

History and professionalisation

Executive coaching grew rapidly from the 1980s and 1990s, drawing on humanistic psychology, sports coaching, management consulting, and organisation development. Professional bodies such as the International Coaching Federation (ICF, founded 1995) and the European Mentoring and Coaching Council established competency frameworks, accreditation, and codes of ethics. Research on coaching effectiveness, including meta-analyses, reports moderate positive effects on performance, self-efficacy, and well-being, though the field continues to debate standards, since coaching remains unregulated in most jurisdictions and practitioners enter from widely varying backgrounds.
