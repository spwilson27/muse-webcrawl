Individualized Education Program

An Individualized Education Program (IEP) is a legal document under United States law that is developed for each public school child in the US who needs special education. It is created through a team of the child's parents and district personnel who are knowledgeable about the child's needs.

IEPs are reviewed and revised at least once a year.

Legal basis

IEPs are mandated by the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), which guarantees eligible children a free appropriate public education (FAPE) in the least restrictive environment. Children from infancy to age three receive services under an Individualized Family Service Plan, while students aged three through twenty-one are covered by an IEP.

Contents

By law, an IEP must include a statement of the child's present levels of academic achievement and functional performance; measurable annual goals; a description of how progress toward the goals will be measured and reported; the special education, related services and supplementary aids to be provided; an explanation of the extent to which the child will not participate with nondisabled children; any accommodations needed for state and district assessments; and, beginning no later than age sixteen, transition services to help the student move to post-school life.

Process

A child is referred for evaluation, and with parental consent is assessed in all areas of suspected disability. If the team finds the child eligible under one of the IDEA disability categories and in need of special education, an IEP is drafted, typically within thirty days. Parents are members of the IEP team and have procedural safeguards, including the right to request mediation or a due process hearing if they disagree with the school's decisions.
