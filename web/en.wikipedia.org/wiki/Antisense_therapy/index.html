Antisense therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Antisense therapy is a form of treatment that uses antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) — short, chemically modified strands of DNA or RNA — to alter the expression of a target gene. Because the sequence of an ASO is complementary ("antisense") to a specific messenger RNA, it can bind to that mRNA and prevent the production of a disease-causing protein, modify splicing, or promote its degradation by RNase H.

Approved drugs

Fomivirsen (1998), for cytomegalovirus retinitis, was the first antisense drug approved. Later approvals include mipomersen for homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia; nusinersen (Spinraza, 2016), which corrects SMN2 splicing in spinal muscular atrophy; inotersen and patisiran (the latter an siRNA) for hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis; eteplirsen and related exon-skipping drugs for Duchenne muscular dystrophy; and tofersen for SOD1-associated ALS.

Mechanisms and chemistry

ASOs are chemically modified — for example with phosphorothioate backbones and 2′-O-methoxyethyl sugars — to resist nucleases and bind targets tightly. "Gapmers" trigger RNase H cleavage of the target mRNA, while steric-blocking oligonucleotides redirect pre-mRNA splicing. Delivery remains a central challenge, especially to tissues outside the liver and central nervous system.
