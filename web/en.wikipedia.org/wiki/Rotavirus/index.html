Rotavirus

FamilySedoreoviridae (formerly Reoviridae)
GenomeDouble-stranded RNA, 11 segments
DiameterApprox. 76.5 nm
DiseaseSevere infantile gastroenteritis

Rotaviruses are a genus of double-stranded RNA viruses named for their wheel-like appearance under the electron microscope (from Latin rota, "wheel"). They are the most common cause of severe diarrhoeal disease in infants and young children worldwide. Nearly every child has been infected by the age of five; before widespread vaccination, rotavirus infections caused an estimated several hundred thousand deaths of children under five each year, overwhelmingly in low-income countries, as well as millions of hospitalisations.

Discovery and structure

The virus was identified in 1973 by Ruth Bishop and colleagues in Melbourne, Australia, who observed the particles in duodenal biopsies from children with acute gastroenteritis. The virion is a non-enveloped, triple-layered particle about 76.5 nm across, containing 11 segments of double-stranded RNA that code for six structural proteins (VP1–VP4, VP6, VP7) and six non-structural proteins. The outer proteins VP7 (G type) and VP4 (P type) define the serotypes/genotypes used in classification, and evoke neutralising antibodies.

Disease and transmission

Rotavirus spreads mainly by the faecal–oral route and infects the mature enterocytes lining the small intestine, blunting the villi and causing malabsorptive, watery diarrhoea, vomiting, fever, and potentially life-threatening dehydration. Immunity builds with repeated infections, so disease is most severe at the first infection, typically between 6 and 24 months of age. Treatment is supportive, centred on oral rehydration therapy; there is no specific antiviral drug.

Vaccines

The first licensed rotavirus vaccine, RotaShield, was withdrawn in 1999 after an association with intussusception was found. Two live oral vaccines — Rotarix (monovalent) and RotaTeq (pentavalent) — were licensed in the mid-2000s and have since been introduced into the national immunisation programmes of more than 100 countries, producing dramatic falls in rotavirus hospitalisations and deaths. Additional vaccines have been developed in India, China, and Vietnam.
