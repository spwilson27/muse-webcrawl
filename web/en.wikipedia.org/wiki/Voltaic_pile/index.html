Voltaic pile

The voltaic pile was the first electrical battery that could continuously provide an electric current to a circuit. It was invented by the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta, who published his experiments in 1800 in a letter to Sir Joseph Banks, president of the Royal Society of London. The pile was the first source of continuous current electricity, transforming the study of electricity from static phenomena to electrochemistry and electromagnetism.

Construction

Volta's device consisted of a stack of alternating discs of two dissimilar metals — zinc and copper (or silver) — separated by pieces of cloth or cardboard soaked in brine or dilute acid as an electrolyte. Each metal pair with its electrolyte-soaked separator forms a single electrochemical cell producing roughly a volt; connecting the top and bottom of a stack of thirty or more such pairs yielded a substantial voltage. Volta also developed the "crown of cups" (couronne de tasses), a chain of cups of salt water linked by metal arcs.

Background and reception

Volta developed the pile in the course of his dispute with Luigi Galvani, who attributed the twitching of frog legs to "animal electricity". Volta argued the electricity arose from contact between dissimilar metals, and the pile was his decisive demonstration. Although the contact theory was incomplete — the energy in fact comes from chemical reactions — the device worked reliably.

Impact

Within weeks of the announcement, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle used a voltaic pile to perform the first electrolysis of water. Humphry Davy used large piles to isolate potassium, sodium, calcium, and other elements in 1807–1808. The steady current the pile provided enabled Ørsted's discovery of electromagnetism (1820) and Ampère's electrodynamics. The SI unit of electric potential, the volt, is named in Volta's honour.
