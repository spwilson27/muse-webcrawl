SOS is a Morse code distress signal (··· ––– ···), used internationally as a signal of distress. It consists of three dots, three dashes and three dots, sent as a single continuous prosign without pauses between letters. The signal was introduced by the German government in 1905, adopted by the International Radiotelegraph Convention of 1906 and became the worldwide standard on 1 July 1908, replacing earlier signals such as the Marconi company's CQD.

Origin and rationale

SOS was selected not as an abbreviation but because its pattern is simple, unmistakable and easy to send and recognise even under poor conditions or by operators with failing equipment: three short, three long, three short. The popular expansions "Save Our Souls" and "Save Our Ship" are backronyms invented after the fact. The signal is transmitted as the single procedural character "SOS" with the elements run together, conventionally written with an overbar.

Use

SOS became famous through maritime disasters of the early wireless era, including the sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912, whose operators sent both the older CQD and the new SOS. In radiotelegraphy the signal demanded immediate silence and assistance; misuse was an offence. With the introduction of the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS), mandatory Morse watchkeeping at sea ended on 1 February 1999, and satellite and digital systems took over maritime distress alerting. SOS nevertheless remains universally recognised: it is still used in visual signalling (for example, flashed with a torch or mirror, or spelled out on the ground), in emergency beacons, and as a general symbol of needing rescue. The related voice distress call is "Mayday".
