Sclerotome

In vertebrate embryology, the sclerotome is the part of a somite that gives rise to the axial skeleton — the vertebrae, ribs and much of the skull — along with associated cartilage and connective tissue. The word derives from the Greek sklēros (hard) and tomē (a cutting or section).

Development

Somites are paired blocks of paraxial mesoderm that form along the neural tube in the early embryo. Each somite differentiates into three regions: the dermatome (which contributes to the dermis), the myotome (which forms skeletal muscle) and the sclerotome, which occupies the ventromedial part of the somite, nearest the notochord and neural tube floor plate.

Signals from the notochord and floor plate — chiefly the signalling molecule Sonic hedgehog — induce the sclerotome and maintain expression of the transcription factors Pax1 and Pax9 in its cells. Sclerotomal cells lose their epithelial organisation, become mesenchymal and migrate around the neural tube and notochord. There they condense and undergo chondrogenesis, laying down the cartilage models of the vertebrae.

Resegmentation

Through the process of resegmentation, each vertebra is formed from the caudal (rear) half of one sclerotome combined with the cranial (front) half of the sclerotome behind it. This offset arrangement allows the segmental spinal nerves and muscles to span the joints between vertebrae, so that muscles crossing a joint can move the vertebral column.

Clinical significance

Disruption of sclerotome development contributes to congenital vertebral malformations, including hemivertebrae, fused vertebrae and the segmentation defects seen in spondylocostal dysostosis. The term "sclerotome" is also used in pain medicine for the pattern of deep, referred pain arising from a given vertebral segment's bone and ligament structures, analogous to the dermatome for skin sensation.
