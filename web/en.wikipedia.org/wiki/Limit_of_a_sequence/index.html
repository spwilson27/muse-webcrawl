Limit of a sequence

In mathematics, the limit of a sequence is the value that the terms of a sequence "tend to", and is often denoted using the lim symbol (e.g., limn→∞ an). If such a limit exists, the sequence is called convergent; a sequence that does not converge is said to be divergent. The limit of a sequence is said to be the fundamental notion on which all of calculus and mathematical analysis are based.

Real numbers

A real number L is the limit of the sequence (xn) if the terms become and remain arbitrarily close to L as n grows. Formally (the (ε, N) definition): for every ε > 0 there exists a natural number N such that for all n > N, |xn − L| < ε. For example, the sequence 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … has limit 0, while the alternating sequence 1, −1, 1, −1, … diverges by oscillation.

Properties

Limits of sequences are unique when they exist, and they respect arithmetic operations: the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, and similarly for products and (where defined) quotients. The squeeze theorem, monotone convergence theorem, and the notion of a Cauchy sequence — in which terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another — characterize convergence in complete spaces such as the real numbers. Every bounded monotone sequence of reals converges.

Generalizations

The concept extends to metric spaces and topological spaces: a sequence converges to L if every neighbourhood of L contains all but finitely many terms. Sequences indexed by nets generalize the idea further, and in hyperreal numbers the limit can be expressed through the standard part function.

History

Ideas of limits appear in the Greek method of exhaustion and in Zeno's paradoxes. The rigorous ε–N formulation was developed in the 19th century by Bernard Bolzano, Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass.
