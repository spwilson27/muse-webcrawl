Natural language processing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A chat window showing a conversation with an AI assistant]

Natural language processing (NLP) is a subfield of artificial intelligence and linguistics concerned with the interactions between computers and human language: enabling machines to read, interpret, generate, and respond to text and speech.

Early NLP (1950s–80s) was rule-based (machine translation projects, ELIZA 1966). Statistical methods took over in the 1990s (hidden Markov models, n-gram language models); neural methods followed (word embeddings, 2013), and the transformer architecture (2017) led to large language models (BERT 2018; the GPT series) that transformed the field.

Tasks

Core tasks include tokenisation and parsing, part-of-speech tagging, named-entity recognition, sentiment analysis, information extraction, machine translation, summarisation, question answering, and dialogue. Evaluation uses benchmarks with human-curated test sets and, increasingly, human preference comparisons.

Modern large language models are pre-trained on vast text corpora to predict the next token, then fine-tuned with human feedback (RLHF) for instruction-following, giving rise to general-purpose chatbots and coding assistants.

Challenges

NLP systems can inherit biases from training data, hallucinate facts, struggle with low-resource languages, and raise concerns about misinformation, labour, and energy use. Understanding — versus fluent pattern matching — remains philosophically contested.
