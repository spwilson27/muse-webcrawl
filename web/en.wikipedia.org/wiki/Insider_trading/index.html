Insider trading

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Insider trading is the trading of a public company's securities (stock, bonds, options) by individuals with access to material, nonpublic information about the company — lawful when insiders report trades and abstain from exploiting secret information (most insider transactions are routine and disclosed), illegal when the trading breaches a duty of trust and confidence in possession of such information, or when tips pass to others who trade.

Doctrine and enforcement

The United States built the paradigmatic regime without ever defining the offence by statute: under the classical theory, corporate insiders breach duties to shareholders by trading on company information; under the misappropriation theory (United States v. O'Hagan, 1997), outsiders who steal information in breach of a duty to its source are liable; tippee liability requires a personal benefit to the tipper (Dirks, 1983; Salman, 2016). The EU's Market Abuse Regulation defines and bans the conduct directly, including attempted and cross-market abuse. Famous prosecutions — Ivan Boesky and Michael Milken in the 1980s, Raj Rajaratnam's wiretap-built case (2011), SAC Capital, and persistent cases against political figures' trading — sustain a debate economists keep open: whether insider-trading bans help markets (integrity, trust, cost of capital) or harm them (Manne's classic defence: insider trading moves prices to truth and compensates entrepreneurs) — with most jurisdictions having long since voted for bans.
