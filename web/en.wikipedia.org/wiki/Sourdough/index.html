Sourdough

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A round sourdough loaf with a scored, crusty top, sliced to show open crumb]

Sourdough is a bread made by fermenting dough with a "starter" — a live culture of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria — rather than with commercial baker's yeast. The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acids, giving the bread its tangy flavour, chewy crumb and thick crust, and improving keeping quality. Sourdough is among the oldest forms of leavened bread, used for thousands of years before packaged yeast existed.

The starter

A starter is maintained by regular "feedings" of fresh flour and water, discarding part each time; some bakeries claim starters many decades old. The culture's microbes come from the flour, the air and the baker's hands, and vary by place — the celebrated tang of San Francisco sourdough is attributed to its local strain, Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis. Dough is typically bulk-fermented for hours, shaped, proofed (often overnight in the cold) and baked hot, frequently in a Dutch oven.

History

Leavened breads appear in ancient Egypt by around 3000 BCE, and sourdough was the standard leavening through the Middle Ages until brewer's and then commercial yeast displaced it in the 19th–20th centuries. Prospectors in the Klondike and Alaska prized their starters so highly that "sourdough" became a nickname for the miners themselves.

Baking and popularity

Artisan bakeries revived sourdough from the 1980s, and home sourdough baking surged during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns of 2020, when starters were widely shared and named. The long fermentation also partially breaks down gluten and phytates, which enthusiasts cite — with some scientific support — for digestibility.
