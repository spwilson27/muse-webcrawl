Alevism

Alevism (Turkish: Alevilik) is a local Islamic tradition, followed primarily by Turkish and Kurdish communities in Anatolia, that combines elements of Shia Islam — particularly devotion to Ali and the Ahl al-Bayt — with Sufi mysticism and pre-Islamic Anatolian beliefs. Alevis are estimated to number between 10 and 25 million, concentrated in Turkey, with diaspora communities in Europe; estimates are contested because many Alevis are wary of official identification.

Beliefs and practice

Alevis worship in a cemevi (assembly house) rather than a mosque, in a ceremony called cem led by a dede (religious elder), in which men and women participate together. The ritual includes the semah, a sacred dance accompanied by the bağlama (long-necked lute) and sung poetry. Central teachings emphasise the inner, mystical meaning of the Quran, love of humanity, tolerance, and the maxim "master your hands, your tongue, and your loins." Alevis commemorate the martyrdom of Husayn during a twelve-day fast in Muharram, and venerate the saint Haji Bektash Veli and the rebel poet Pir Sultan Abdal.

History

Alevi communities, known in Ottoman records as Kızılbaş ("red heads") for their distinctive headgear, were repeatedly persecuted under the Ottoman Empire, which aligned with Sunni orthodoxy against Safavid Persia. Persecution recurred in the Republic of Turkey, including the Dersim massacres of 1937–38 and attacks at Sivas in 1993. Since the late 1980s an "Alevi revival" has seen public assertion of Alevi identity and ongoing campaigns for legal recognition of cemevis as houses of worship.
