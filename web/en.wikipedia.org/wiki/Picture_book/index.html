Picture book

A picture book is a book, typically for young children, in which illustrations play an integral role alongside a short text: the story is told jointly by words and pictures, and neither is complete alone. Standard picture books are 32 pages long, with artwork on every spread, and are generally aimed at readers from infancy to about eight years old.

History

Precursors include early illustrated children's books such as Orbis Pictus (1658) by Comenius and the wood-engraved books of Randolph Caldecott and Kate Greenaway in the 19th century, after whom major awards are named. The modern picture book flowered in the 20th century with artists such as Beatrix Potter (The Tale of Peter Rabbit, 1902), Wanda Gág, Leo Lionni, Maurice Sendak (Where the Wild Things Are, 1963) and Eric Carle (The Very Hungry Caterpillar, 1969).

Forms

Related forms include board books printed on thick card for babies and toddlers, wordless picture books told entirely in images, concept books teaching numbers or colours, and interactive books with flaps, pop-ups or textures. Picture-book texts are usually under 1,000 words and are designed to be read aloud by an adult.

Awards

The principal honours are the Caldecott Medal (United States, for illustration), the Kate Greenaway Medal (United Kingdom) and the international Hans Christian Andersen Award.
