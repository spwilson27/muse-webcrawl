Fisher's principle

Fisher's principle
FieldEvolutionary biology
Proposed byRonald Fisher
Year1930

Fisher's principle is an evolutionary model that explains why the sex ratio of most species that produce offspring through sexual reproduction is approximately 1:1 between males and females. It was famously outlined by Ronald Fisher in his 1930 book The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection, though sometimes incorrectly credited to Fisher as original.

Explanation

Fisher argued that if one sex were less common than the other, parents genetically predisposed to produce more of the rarer sex would have greater reproductive success, because the rarer sex would have better mating prospects. This frequency-dependent selection pushes the population sex ratio back toward equality of parental investment in the two sexes. The equilibrium is an example of an evolutionarily stable strategy.

Exceptions

Many species deviate from a 1:1 ratio, including species with environmental sex determination, local mate competition, and sex-biased costs of rearing. W. D. Hamilton's work on extraordinary sex ratios extended Fisher's reasoning to such cases.
