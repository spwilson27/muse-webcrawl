Sigurd

Sigurd (Old Norse: Sigurðr) is a legendary hero of Germanic heroic legend, best known as the slayer of the dragon Fáfnir and the central figure of the Old Norse Völsunga saga and several poems of the Poetic Edda. In the continental German tradition he appears as Siegfried, the hero of the Middle High German Nibelungenlied.

Legend

Sigurd was the posthumous son of Sigmund of the Völsung line. Raised in the court of King Hjálprekr and fostered by the smith Regin, he avenged his father, reforged Sigmund's broken sword Gram, and killed the dragon Fáfnir, who lay on a hoard of cursed gold at Gnitaheiðr. Tasting the dragon's heart blood gave Sigurd the speech of birds, which warned him of Regin's treachery; he killed Regin and took the treasure. He then rode through a ring of fire to wake the valkyrie Brynhildr (Sigrdrífa), to whom he was betrothed.

Death

At the court of the Gjúkungs Sigurd married Guðrún, but — having drunk a potion of forgetfulness — helped King Gunnar win Brynhildr by exchanging shapes with him. Brynhildr, discovering the deception, demanded Sigurd's death, and he was murdered, in the Norse version by Gunnar's brother Gutthorm in his sleep. His death sets in motion the fall of the Burgundians, the Norse counterpart to the events of the Nibelungenlied.

Depictions

Scenes from Sigurd's story, especially the dragon-slaying and the birds' warning, are carved on the Ramsund runestone in Sweden, on Norwegian stave church portals such as Hylestad, and on Manx crosses — evidence of the legend's wide circulation in the Viking Age.
