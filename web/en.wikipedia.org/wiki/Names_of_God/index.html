Names of God

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

There are various names of God, many of which enumerate the various qualities of a Supreme Being. The English word "god" (and its equivalent in other languages) is used by multiple religions as a noun to refer to different deities, or specifically to the Supreme Being, as denoted by various names or attributes. Ancients sometimes considered names to have a magical or spiritual significance, and the names of deities often carried great weight in ritual and theology.

In monotheistic thought, God is usually regarded as the supreme being, creator, and principal object of faith. Different religious groups and cultures attach different names, titles, and epithets to God, reflecting doctrinal teaching about the divine nature, God's relationship to humanity, and scriptural usage.

In Judaism

The most important name of God in Judaism is the Tetragrammaton, the four-letter name YHWH (יהוה), traditionally not pronounced aloud and replaced in reading by Adonai ("my Lord") or, in ordinary speech, HaShem ("the Name"). Other names and epithets include Elohim, El, El Shaddai, Ehyeh-Asher-Ehyeh ("I Am that I Am", revealed to Moses in Exodus 3:14), Tzevaot ("of hosts"), and Adonai. The prohibition on pronouncing the Tetragrammaton is associated with the commandment against taking God's name in vain and reverence for its sanctity.

In Christianity

Christians commonly refer to God simply as "God", "the Lord", or "the Father", reflecting the doctrine of the Trinity: one God in three persons — Father, Son (Jesus Christ), and Holy Spirit. Names carried over from the Hebrew Bible include Yahweh (rendered "Jehovah" in some traditions) and Elohim. Jesus is given numerous titles, including Christ (Messiah), Son of God, Son of Man, Lamb of God, and the Word (Logos). In many languages the generic word for God is used without a proper name.

In Islam

In Islam, God is called Allah (الله), the Arabic word for "the God", used by Arab Christians and Jews as well as Muslims. Tradition holds that God has 99 names (asma'ul husna, "the most beautiful names"), each describing an attribute, such as ar-Rahman (the Most Gracious), ar-Rahim (the Most Merciful), al-Malik (the King), and al-Quddus (the Most Holy). Many of these names appear in the Quran, and reciting them is a common devotional practice.

In other traditions

In Hinduism, deities bear countless names; the Vishnu Sahasranamam lists a thousand names of Vishnu, and Shiva, Devi, and other deities likewise have sahasranamas. The ultimate reality, Brahman, is described by epithets such as Sat-Chit-Ananda (being, consciousness, bliss). Sikhs refer to God as Waheguru and by names in the Guru Granth Sahib including Akal Purakh and Ik Onkar. In the Baháʼí Faith, God is invoked by names such as the All-Merciful and the Ancient of Days; Baháʼís regard "Allah-u-Abha" ("God is Most Glorious") as a greeting and use the Greatest Name as a symbol. Zoroastrians call God Ahura Mazda ("Wise Lord").

Theological considerations

Theologians debate whether any name can properly describe God. Apophatic (negative) theology holds that God can only be described by what God is not. Others argue that names reveal genuine attributes. The question of whether "God" functions as a proper name or a title has occupied philosophers of religion from Maimonides and Aquinas to modern analytic theology.
