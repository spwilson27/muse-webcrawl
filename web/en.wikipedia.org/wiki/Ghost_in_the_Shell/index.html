Ghost in the Shell (Japanese: 攻殻機動隊, Kōkaku Kidōtai, "Mobile Armored Riot Police") is a Japanese cyberpunk media franchise created by Masamune Shirow. It follows Major Motoko Kusanagi, a full-body cyborg field commander of Public Security Section 9, an intelligence and counterterrorism unit in the fictional mid-21st-century Japanese city of Niihama (New Port City), as she hunts hackers and confronts questions of identity in a world where consciousness — the "ghost" — can inhabit artificial "shells."

The 1995 film

Mamoru Oshii's 1995 animated film, produced by Production I.G with a score by Kenji Kawai, adapts Shirow's Puppet Master storyline: a sentient computer program, Project 2501, seeks political asylum and ultimately union with Kusanagi, proposing reproduction and death — true life — as an escape from the limitations of a copied network existence. Kusanagi accepts the merger, departing in a child's cyborg body with the words that the net is vast and infinite. The film's meditative pacing, rain-soaked cityscapes, and philosophical dialogue made it a landmark of animated cinema; it was a formative influence on The Matrix.

Other versions

The franchise branches into parallel continuities. Ghost in the Shell: Stand Alone Complex (2002–05), directed by Kenji Kamiyama, is a television continuity in which Section 9 investigates the Laughing Man, a hacker-terrorist born of corporate crime, and later the Individual Eleven and refugee crisis — generally praised as a sophisticated political thriller. Oshii's film sequel Innocence (2004) competed at Cannes. The Arise prequel series (2013) and the CGI series SAC_2045 (2020) rework the premise again, while a Hollywood live-action film starring Scarlett Johansson appeared in 2017 to mixed reception and debate over casting.

Themes

Across versions the franchise asks what remains human when bodies are replaceable, memories editable, and minds networked: the boundary between person and program, the politics of information, and the state's monopoly on cybernetic force. Its vocabulary and imagery — cyberbrains, thermoptic camouflage, Tachikoma think-tanks — extend the cyberpunk tradition that begins in print with Neuromancer, and with Akira it forms the twin pillar of cyberpunk anime's global influence.
