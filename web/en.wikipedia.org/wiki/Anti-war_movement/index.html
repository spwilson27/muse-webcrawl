Anti-war movement

An anti-war movement (also called antiwar) is a social movement, usually in opposition to a particular nation's decision to start or carry on an armed conflict. The term anti-war can also refer to pacifism, which is the opposition to all use of military force during conflicts, or to anti-war books, paintings and other works of art. Many activists distinguish between anti-war movements and peace movements: anti-war activists work for the withdrawal of troops from a particular conflict, while peace movements aim at ending war in general.

History

Organised opposition to war developed in the 19th century with the first peace societies. Major anti-war movements accompanied the First World War, the Vietnam War — when mass protests in the United States and worldwide became a defining feature of the era — and the 2003 invasion of Iraq, preceded on 15 February 2003 by coordinated protests in hundreds of cities that were among the largest demonstrations in history. Anti-war activism has employed marches, vigils, draft resistance, conscientious objection, civil disobedience and cultural protest.

Arguments

Anti-war movements typically argue that a given war is immoral, illegal under international law, unwinnable, or waged on false premises, and they point to civilian casualties and economic costs. Opponents of anti-war movements have at times accused them of aiding enemies or undermining troops in the field.
