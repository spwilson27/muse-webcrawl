Ale

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ale is a type of beer brewed using a warm fermentation method, resulting in a sweet, full-bodied, and fruity taste. Historically, the term referred to a drink brewed without hops, in contrast to beer, but today it commonly denotes beer brewed with top-fermenting yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) at relatively warm temperatures (about 15–24 °C), as opposed to lagers, which are cold-fermented with bottom-fermenting yeast.

History

Ale was a staple drink of medieval England, safer than much drinking water and a significant source of nutrition; it was brewed both domestically, often by women known as alewives, and in monasteries and commercial breweries. Before hops became standard in the 15th–16th centuries, ale was flavoured and preserved with gruit, a mixture of herbs. The Industrial Revolution brought pale ales from Burton upon Trent, and in the 1970s the consumer organisation CAMRA championed "real ale" — cask-conditioned, unpasteurised ale — against mass-produced keg beer, helping spark the modern craft beer movement, in which ale styles such as India pale ale (IPA) became dominant in the craft sector.

Styles

  • Pale ale – amber, hop-forward styles including bitter, American pale ale, and IPA.
  • Brown ale – mild, malty English and American versions.
  • Porter and stout – dark ales made with roasted malts.
  • Barley wine – very strong ales, often 8–12% alcohol.
  • Belgian ales – including Trappist ales, saison, and lambic (spontaneously fermented).
  • Wheat beer – German weissbier and Belgian witbier styles.
  • Mild – low-alcohol, lightly hopped English ale.

Fermentation

Top-fermenting ale yeast works quickly at warm temperatures and produces more esters, giving ales their fruity, complex flavours. Many traditional British ales undergo secondary fermentation in the cask ("cask conditioning"), producing a gentle natural carbonation, while most modern craft ales are force-carbonated and packaged in bottles, cans, or kegs.
