Coffea

Ripe coffee cherries on a Coffea shrub.

Coffea is a genus of flowering plants in the family Rubiaceae, native to tropical Africa, Madagascar, the Comoros, Mauritius and Réunion in the Indian Ocean, and tropical Asia. Its seeds, commonly called coffee beans, are used to make the beverage coffee, one of the most widely traded agricultural commodities in the world.

Description

Coffea species are shrubs or small trees growing up to about 5–10 m tall when unpruned. The leaves are opposite, glossy and dark green, and the fragrant white flowers are followed by oval fruits ("cherries") about 1.5 cm long that ripen from green through yellow to bright red or purple. Each fruit normally contains two seeds.

Species and cultivation

More than 120 species have been described. Two dominate world production: Coffea arabica (Arabica), accounting for roughly 60–70% of production and prized for flavour, and Coffea canephora (Robusta), which is hardier, higher in caffeine and widely used in espresso blends and instant coffee. Coffea liberica is grown on a small scale in parts of Asia and Africa.

Ecology

Wild coffee plants grow as understorey shrubs in moist tropical forests. Cultivated plants may be grown under shade trees or in full sun; shade-grown systems retain more biodiversity, while sun-grown plantations give higher yields but require more inputs. Major threats include coffee leaf rust (Hemileia vastatrix) and the coffee berry borer beetle.

History

Coffee drinking originated in Ethiopia and Yemen and spread through the Ottoman world to Europe and the Americas in the early modern period. Today Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia and Ethiopia are leading producers, and coffee is a livelihood for tens of millions of smallholder farmers.
