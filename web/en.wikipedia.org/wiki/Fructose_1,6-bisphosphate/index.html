Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate

IUPAC name1,6-Di-O-phosphono-β-D-fructofuranose
FormulaC6H14O12P2
Molar mass340.116 g/mol
Other namesHarden–Young ester; FBP; F-1,6-BP

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is fructose sugar phosphorylated on carbons 1 and 6 (that is, a fructosephosphate). The β-D-form of this compound is common in cells. Upon entering most cells, fructose is quickly converted to fructose 1-phosphate by fructokinase, whereas fructose 1,6-bisphosphate itself is formed and consumed in the central pathway of glycolysis.

Role in glycolysis

In glycolysis, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is produced from fructose 6-phosphate by the enzyme phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), in the pathway's principal committed and regulated step, consuming one ATP. The six-carbon bisphosphate is then split by fructose-bisphosphate aldolase into two interconvertible three-carbon sugars, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate, which proceed through the energy-yielding phase of the pathway.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is also an allosteric activator: it feed-forward activates pyruvate kinase, the enzyme catalysing the final step of glycolysis, and in mammals it regulates PFK-1 indirectly through fructose 2,6-bisphosphate, a related signalling metabolite.

History

The compound was one of the earliest glycolytic intermediates identified: in 1906 Arthur Harden and William Young showed that inorganic phosphate became incorporated into a fructose diphosphate — the "Harden–Young ester" — during alcoholic fermentation by yeast juice.
