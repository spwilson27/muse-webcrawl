Density functional theory (DFT) is a computational quantum mechanical modelling method used in physics, chemistry and materials science to investigate the electronic structure (or nuclear structure) of many-body systems, in particular atoms, molecules, and the condensed phases. Using this theory, the properties of a many-electron system can be determined by using functionals of the electron density, which depend on only three spatial variables rather than the 3N variables of the full many-body wavefunction.

Overview

DFT rests on the two Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964), which prove that the ground-state properties of a many-electron system are uniquely determined by its electron density, and that the true ground-state density minimises the total energy functional. The practical scheme used in nearly all calculations is the Kohn–Sham approach (1965), which maps the interacting problem onto a fictitious system of non-interacting electrons moving in an effective potential that reproduces the same density.

The exchange–correlation functional

The only unknown ingredient in DFT is the exchange–correlation energy functional, which must be approximated. Common approximations form a hierarchy sometimes called "Jacob's ladder": the local-density approximation (LDA), generalised gradient approximations (GGA) such as PBE, meta-GGAs, hybrid functionals mixing in exact Hartree–Fock exchange (such as B3LYP), and beyond. The choice of functional strongly influences accuracy for reaction energies, band gaps and weak interactions.

Applications

DFT is the workhorse of computational materials science and quantum chemistry. It is used to predict crystal structures, reaction mechanisms, catalytic activity, electronic band structures, and spectroscopic properties. Walter Kohn shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for its development. Limitations include the systematic underestimation of semiconductor band gaps and difficulties describing strong electron correlation and van der Waals dispersion, the latter addressed by dispersion corrections.
