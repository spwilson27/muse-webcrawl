Hydrogen bond

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hydrogen bond
Field: Chemistry
Topic: Bonding
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

A hydrogen bond is a weak electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (usually nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine) and a lone pair on another such atom. Written D–H···A, it is much weaker than a covalent bond (roughly 5–30 kJ/mol) but strong enough to shape the physical world.

Hydrogen bonding explains water's anomalously high boiling point, why ice floats (an open hydrogen-bonded lattice), the double helix of DNA (base pairs held by hydrogen bonds), protein folding, and the strength of nylon and wool.

[Image: Diagram of hydrogen bonds between water molecules, dashed lines linking oxygen and hydrogen]

In water

Each water molecule can donate two and accept two hydrogen bonds, forming a flickering three-dimensional network; this is why water has high specific heat, surface tension and solvent power for ionic and polar substances.

History

The idea emerged in the 1910s–20s (Latimer and Rodebush's 1920 paper is a standard citation) and Linus Pauling made it central to structural chemistry in The Nature of the Chemical Bond (1939).
