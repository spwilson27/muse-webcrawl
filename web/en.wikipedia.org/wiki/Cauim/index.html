Cauim

Cauim is a traditional alcoholic beverage of the Indigenous peoples of Brazil. Dating back to pre-Columbian times, it is a fermented drink made from cassava (manioc), maize or rice, sometimes combined with fruit juices. Traditionally, the starchy ingredients were chewed by women and spat into a vessel, so that enzymes in saliva converted starches to fermentable sugars before fermentation — a method also found in other traditional beverages such as the Andean chicha de jora and Japanese kuchikamizake.

Preparation and consumption

Cauim is typically prepared for communal festivities and ceremonies. The drink is usually slightly sour, cloudy and low in alcohol, and is consumed in quantity during feasts, where it plays an important social and ritual role. Variations are made by peoples including the Tupí-Guaraní and other Amazonian groups, using ingredients such as sweet potato, peanut or pumpkin in some regions.

History

European chroniclers of the 16th century, such as Hans Staden and Jean de Léry, described cauim drinking in detail, noting its central place in Tupi social life, diplomacy and ritual. Production by chewing has largely been replaced in modern practice by malting or other sources of enzymes, though traditional methods persist in some Indigenous communities.
