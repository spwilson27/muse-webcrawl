Daylighting

Daylighting is the practice of placing windows, skylights and other openings and reflective surfaces so that sunlight can provide effective internal lighting in buildings. It also refers, in urban planning and hydrology, to uncovering a previously buried or culverted waterway (see below), and in road safety to improving visibility at intersections.

In architecture

Good daylighting reduces artificial lighting energy, improves occupants' comfort and circadian health, and is credited in standards such as LEED and WELL. Design tools include light shelves, clerestories, atria, tubular daylight devices, heliostats and daylight modelling metrics such as spatial Daylight Autonomy. Excess gain and glare must be controlled with shading, orientation and glazing choice—historically, north-facing sawtooth roofs lit factories before electric light.

Daylighting streams

Stream daylighting restores rivers buried in culverts during urbanisation—examples include the Cheonggyecheon in Seoul, the River Roch in Rochdale, England, and Saw Mill River in Yonkers, New York—aiming to improve ecology, flood management and amenity, though at high cost and with debate over ecological authenticity.

Intersection daylighting

In traffic safety, daylighting means clearing parking and obstructions near crosswalks and corners—typically 6 metres / 20 feet—so drivers and pedestrians can see each other, a measure adopted in many U.S. cities and in California law effective 2024–2025.
