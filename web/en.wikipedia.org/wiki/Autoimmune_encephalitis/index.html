Autoimmune encephalitis

Autoimmune encephalitis (AIE) is a group of inflammatory brain disorders in which the immune system attacks the central nervous system, often through antibodies directed against neuronal cell-surface or synaptic proteins. Patients typically develop a subacute syndrome of memory loss, confusion, psychiatric symptoms, and seizures. Many forms respond to immunotherapy, so early recognition is important.

A separate group of antibodies against intracellular antigens (e.g., Hu, Ma2, GAD65) is associated with paraneoplastic syndromes and T-cell–mediated injury, and tends to respond less well to treatment.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical syndrome plus supportive findings: MRI changes in the medial temporal lobes, inflammatory cerebrospinal fluid, EEG abnormalities, and detection of specific antibodies in serum and CSF. Infectious causes, especially herpes simplex encephalitis — which can itself trigger anti-NMDAR encephalitis — must be excluded.

Treatment

First-line therapy combines high-dose corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, and/or plasma exchange; associated tumours should be removed. Patients who do not improve receive second-line agents such as rituximab or cyclophosphamide. Recovery may take months, and relapses occur in a minority of patients.

History

The modern era began with the description of limbic encephalitis in the 1960s and the identification of anti-NMDAR encephalitis by Josep Dalmau and colleagues in 2007, which revealed that some patients previously thought to have viral encephalitis or primary psychosis had a treatable autoimmune disease.
