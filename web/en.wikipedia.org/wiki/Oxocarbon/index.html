Oxocarbon

An oxocarbon or oxide of carbon is a chemical compound consisting only of carbon and oxygen. The simplest and best-known oxocarbons are carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO2). Many other stable or metastable oxides of carbon are known, but they are rarely encountered, such as carbon suboxide (C3O2) and mellitic anhydride (C12O9).

Many other oxocarbons have been predicted or detected but are unstable. Examples include carbon tetroxide (CO4), which has been detected at low temperatures, and various cyclic and polymeric oxides. The chemistry of oxocarbons also extends to the oxocarbon anions, such as squarate (C4O42−) and croconate (C5O52−), which form stable salts.

Overview

Carbon dioxide is notable as the product of respiration and combustion and a key participant in the carbon cycle. Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless, and highly toxic gas produced by incomplete combustion, but also an important industrial feedstock and signalling molecule in biology.

Linear oxides

One family of oxocarbons has the general formula CnO2, a linear chain of carbon atoms capped by oxygen atoms at both ends. Members include carbon dioxide (n = 1), carbon suboxide (n = 3), and higher cumulene diones that become progressively less stable.

Cyclic oxides

Cyclic oxocarbons and their anions (oxocarbon dianions such as deltate, squarate, croconate, and rhodizonate) are notable for their aromatic character and symmetric structures, and have been studied since the 19th century.
