A várzea forest is a seasonally flooded forest found along the whitewater rivers of the Amazon Basin. Each year, as rivers such as the Amazon (Solimões), Madeira and Purus rise with Andean meltwater and rainfall, they spill sediment-rich, nutrient-laden water across vast floodplains, inundating the várzea for months at a time. The term várzea is also used for the floodplain habitat itself.

Ecology

Because whitewater carries fertile Andean sediments, várzea forests grow on comparatively rich soils and are among the most productive ecosystems in Amazonia. Their trees are adapted to prolonged immersion: many species time their fruiting to the flood season, so that seeds are dispersed by fruit-eating fish — such as tambaqui — which swim through the flooded forest. Várzea supports distinctive assemblages of plants, fish, birds and mammals; areas known as restingas (natural levees) host the tallest forest and are favoured for settlement and agriculture.

Human use

Ribeirinho (riverine peasant) communities have long farmed the várzea's fertile soils during the low-water season, growing manioc, maize, beans, bananas and vegetables, and harvesting fish, açaí and timber in rhythm with the flood pulse. Commercial logging and cattle ranching have expanded in the várzea, raising conservation concerns; sustainable-development reserves such as Mamirauá, near Tefé, were created to protect both the ecosystem and traditional livelihoods.
