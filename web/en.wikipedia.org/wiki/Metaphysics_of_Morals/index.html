The Metaphysics of Morals

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Metaphysics of Morals (German: Die Metaphysik der Sitten) is a major work of philosophy by Immanuel Kant, published in 1797, when Kant was 73. It is the mature statement of his moral, legal, and political philosophy, applying the critical moral theory of the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) and the Critique of Practical Reason (1788) to a systematic doctrine of right and virtue.

Structure

The work is divided into two parts:

  • Part I: Metaphysical First Principles of the Doctrine of Right (Rechtslehre) — Kant's legal and political philosophy. It develops the universal principle of right — the conditions under which the freedom of each can coexist with the freedom of all under universal law — and treats private right (property, contract, and domestic relations) and public right (the state, punishment, international right, and cosmopolitan right). Kant argues that property in external things is possible only within a civil condition, defends a republican constitution, and argues for a federation of free states as the path to perpetual peace.
  • Part II: Metaphysical First Principles of the Doctrine of Virtue (Tugendlehre) — Kant's ethics proper. It treats duties of virtue, which prescribe ends as well as actions: one's own perfection and the happiness of others as ends that are also duties, and duties toward oneself (against suicide, self-deception, avarice, and servility) and toward others (beneficence, gratitude, sympathy, respect), concluding with discussions of conscience, moral feeling, and the method of moral education and ascetics.

Key ideas

The distinction between right (juridical duties, externally enforceable and concerned only with outer freedom) and virtue (ethical duties, which cannot be externally legislated because they require adopting ends) structures the whole work. Notable doctrines include Kant's retributive theory of punishment, his rejection of a right of revolution, his account of marriage and parental rights (long criticised), his defence of freedom of speech ("the pen") within obedience to law, and the idea of an innate right to freedom as the only original right belonging to every person.

Reception

Because of Kant's age and the haste of publication, the text contains obscurities and was long considered less rigorous than the three Critiques; the Doctrine of Virtue in particular was neglected in favour of the Groundwork. Since the late 20th century, scholars have substantially re-evaluated the work, and it is now central to Kantian approaches to law, political theory, and virtue ethics.
