Hesiod (Greek: Ἡσίοδος, Hēsíodos) was an ancient Greek poet generally thought to have been active between 750 and 650 BCE, around the same time as Homer. He is regarded as the first written poet in the Western tradition to regard himself as an individual persona and to name himself in his works. Two complete epics survive under his name: the Theogony and Works and Days.

Life

Almost everything known of Hesiod's life comes from his own poems. His father emigrated from Cyme in Aeolis to Ascra in Boeotia, a village he describes as "bad in winter, sultry in summer, and good at no time". Hesiod worked as a shepherd on Mount Helicon, where, he says, the Muses appeared to him, breathed divine voice into him and gave him a staff of laurel. After his father's death he quarrelled with his brother Perses over the inheritance, a dispute that colours much of Works and Days.

Works

Theogony

The Theogony ("Birth of the Gods", 1,022 lines) is a systematic genealogy of the Greek gods, from Chaos, Gaia and Eros through the Titans to the triumph of Zeus and the Olympians. It became the canonical account of Greek theogony and a foundational text of Greek religion.

Works and Days

Works and Days (828 lines) is a didactic poem addressed to Perses, combining moral exhortation — the dignity of labour, the rule of justice (dikē) under Zeus — with practical farming advice, a calendar of lucky and unlucky days, and myths including Prometheus and Pandora and the Five Ages of Man.

Other attributed works

Antiquity credited Hesiod with many more poems, including the Catalogue of Women and the Shield of Heracles, of which substantial fragments survive; most are now considered works of his school rather than of Hesiod himself.
