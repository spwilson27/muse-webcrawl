Lighting design

Lighting design is the craft of planning and creating lighting for built environments and performances, combining technical knowledge of light and equipment with artistic judgment. It is practised in two main fields: architectural lighting design for buildings and public spaces, and theatrical lighting design for stage productions, concerts and events.

Architectural lighting design

Architectural lighting designers — an independent profession pioneered by figures such as Richard Kelly in the mid-20th century — shape how buildings are seen and used. Their work layers ambient, task and accent lighting; integrates daylight; considers energy use, glare, colour rendering and the well-being of occupants; and produces calculations, fixture schedules and controls strategies. Professional bodies include the International Association of Lighting Designers (IALD).

Theatrical lighting design

The stage lighting designer creates a lighting plot specifying every instrument's position, colour, focus and intensity, and cues the changes of state that follow the action. The classic functions of stage lighting — visibility, selective focus, modelling form, and mood — were systematised in the McCandless method, which lights an acting area with two lights at roughly 45-degree angles, typically one warm and one cool. Designers such as Jean Rosenthal and Tharon Musser established lighting design as a distinct artistic discipline in American theatre.

Process

In both fields the process involves analysing the project, developing a concept, calculating and modelling light levels (increasingly with software such as DIALux or rendering tools), selecting equipment, documenting the design, and commissioning the final installation.
