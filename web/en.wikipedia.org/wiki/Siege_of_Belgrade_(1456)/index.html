Siege of Belgrade (1456)

The siege of Belgrade, or the Battle of Belgrade, was a successful attempt by Hungarian and Serbian forces under the command of John Hunyadi to defend the city of Belgrade (Nándorfehérvár) against an assault by the Ottoman Empire in the summer of 1456. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, Sultan Mehmed II rallied his resources to subjugate the Kingdom of Hungary. His immediate target was the border fort of Belgrade, the key to the Danube frontier. The victory at Belgrade halted Ottoman expansion into Central Europe for some seventy years, and the noon bell rung in Catholic churches worldwide commemorates it to this day.

Background

Belgrade stood at the confluence of the Danube and Sava rivers and had been ceded to Hungary in 1427. After conquering Constantinople, Mehmed II marched north with a huge army, artillery including great bombards, and a river fleet to blockade the fortress. Hunyadi, regent of Hungary, prepared the defence with his brother-in-law Michael Szilágyi commanding the garrison, while the Franciscan friar John of Capistrano preached a crusade that drew thousands of peasant volunteers.

The siege

The Ottoman fleet blockaded the Danube until Hunyadi's flotilla broke through on 14 July 1456, relieving the city. Ottoman cannon bombarded the walls for days, and on 21 July Mehmed launched a general assault that penetrated the town. The defenders repelled it in fierce street fighting; the following day, in an episode later embroidered by legend, Capistrano led a counter-charge of crusaders across the Sava that overran the Ottoman camp. Mehmed, wounded, ordered a retreat that night.

Aftermath

The victory saved Hungary from Ottoman conquest for a generation. However, plague broke out in the camp: Hunyadi died on 11 August 1456 and Capistrano on 23 October. Pope Callixtus III ordered church bells to be rung at noon as a call to prayer for the defenders — a custom still observed. Belgrade finally fell to Suleiman the Magnificent in 1521.
