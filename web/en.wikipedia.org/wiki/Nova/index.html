A nova (plural novae or novas) is a transient astronomical event that causes the sudden appearance of a bright, apparently "new" star (Latin: nova stella) that slowly fades over weeks or months. Novae are caused by a thermonuclear explosion on the surface of a white dwarf star in a close binary system.

Mechanism

In a cataclysmic binary, a white dwarf accretes hydrogen-rich gas from a companion star. The gas accumulates in a degenerate layer on the white dwarf's surface until pressure and temperature at its base trigger runaway hydrogen fusion (the CNO cycle). The layer explodes, ejecting 10−4 to 10−5 solar masses at hundreds to thousands of km/s and brightening the system by 7–16 magnitudes. Unlike a Type Ia supernova, the white dwarf survives intact, so the process can repeat: on timescales of roughly 10,000–100,000 years for classical novae, or mere decades for recurrent novae such as T Pyxidis and RS Ophiuchi (last eruption 2021).

Classes and light curves

Novae are classified by the speed of their decline (fast, slow, very slow) and include classical novae, recurrent novae, and the entirely different dwarf novae (brightness changes caused by accretion-disk instabilities, with no nuclear explosion). "Neon novae" ejecta show strong neon lines, indicating an oxygen–neon–magnesium white dwarf. Naked-eye novae appear a few times per century at most; historically famous examples include Tycho's "new star" of 1572 (in fact a supernova), Nova Cygni 1975, and Nova Delphini 2013. About 30–50 novae are estimated to occur in the Milky Way each year, though dust hides most from view.

Importance

Novae contribute processed material — including lithium-7 and isotopes such as 13C, 15N and 17O — to the interstellar medium. The maximum-magnitude/rate-of-decline relation once made them extragalactic distance indicators before being superseded by Cepheids and supernovae. Systems in which the white dwarf gains net mass despite eruptions are candidate progenitors of Type Ia supernovae.
