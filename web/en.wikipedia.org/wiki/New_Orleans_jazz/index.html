New Orleans jazz, also called Dixieland or traditional jazz, is a style of jazz that developed in New Orleans, Louisiana, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It grew out of brass bands, ragtime, blues, Creole music and parade traditions, and is characterised by collective improvisation.

Origins

New Orleans, a port city with African American, Creole, French, Spanish and Caribbean influences, had a rich culture of brass band parades, funeral processions with music, dances and the gatherings once held in Congo Square. Musicians blended marches, ragtime, blues and French quadrilles into a new improvisational style.

Cornetist Buddy Bolden is often credited as the first jazz bandleader in the 1890s–1900s, though he never recorded. The city's Storyville district provided work for pianists such as Jelly Roll Morton until its closure in 1917.

Spread and recordings

As musicians left New Orleans — many for Chicago during the Great Migration — the style spread nationally. The Original Dixieland Jass Band, a white group, made the first jazz recordings in 1917; Black New Orleans bands such as King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band, featuring the young Louis Armstrong, recorded in the early 1920s and defined the idiom.

Style and legacy

The classic New Orleans front line of cornet (later trumpet), clarinet and trombone improvises collectively around the melody over a rhythm section keeping a two-beat feel. Armstrong's Hot Five and Hot Seven recordings of the 1920s transformed the style toward solo improvisation and laid foundations for all later jazz.

Traditional jazz remains central to New Orleans culture, heard in Preservation Hall, at second line parades and during the New Orleans Jazz & Heritage Festival.
