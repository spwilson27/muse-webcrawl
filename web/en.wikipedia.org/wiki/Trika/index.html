Trika

Trika ("triad") is a school of nondual Shaivism that flourished in Kashmir from roughly the 9th to the 13th century CE and forms the doctrinal heart of what is often called Kashmir Shaivism. Its name refers to several triads, most fundamentally Shiva, Shakti, and the individual soul (aṇu), or the three energies of Shiva: will (icchā), knowledge (jñāna), and action (kriyā).

Doctrine

Trika teaches that ultimate reality is the self-luminous consciousness of Shiva, who freely manifests the universe as his own play through Shakti. Bondage is ignorance of one's identity with Shiva, and liberation is pratyabhijñā, "recognition" of that identity, rather than the attainment of something new. The system integrates the disciplines of the earlier Shaiva traditions, organising practice into four means (upāyas), from effortful ritual and meditation up to the "no-means" of direct recognition. Its metaphysics describes 36 tattvas, expanding the 25 of Samkhya with categories proper to Shaivism. Key teachers include Vasugupta, Kallata, Somananda, Utpaladeva, and the polymath Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016), whose Tantrāloka systematised the tradition; his disciple Kshemaraja wrote important summaries such as the Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam.

Legacy

After the decline of the tradition in Kashmir, Trika texts were preserved in manuscripts and revived by modern scholars and practitioners; Swami Lakshman Joo was a notable 20th-century teacher.
