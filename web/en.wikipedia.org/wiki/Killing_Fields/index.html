Killing Fields

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The memorial stupa at Choeung Ek, filled with the skulls of Khmer Rouge victims]
The memorial stupa at Choeung Ek, filled with the skulls of Khmer Rouge victims

The Killing Fields are a number of sites in Cambodia where collectively more than a million people were killed and buried by the Khmer Rouge regime (the government of Democratic Kampuchea) between 1975 and 1979. The term was coined by the Cambodian journalist Dith Pran; the best known site is Choeung Ek outside Phnom Penh, where prisoners from the Tuol Sleng (S-21) prison were executed. The mass graves became the emblem of the Cambodian genocide.

Background

After winning the civil war in April 1975, the Khmer Rouge under Pol Pot evacuated the cities, abolished money, markets, religion and private property, and set out to build an agrarian utopia in 'Year Zero'. Enemies multiplied by paranoia: former officials, soldiers, monks, teachers, the educated (even wearers of glasses), ethnic Vietnamese, Cham Muslims and Chinese, and finally the party's own cadres in cascading purges. Starvation, overwork and disease in the cooperatives killed as many as execution did.

The killing sites

Some 20,000 mass-grave sites have been mapped by the Documentation Center of Cambodia. At Choeung Ek, trucks brought blindfolded prisoners at night; they were killed with hoes, axes and palm-leaf knives to save bullets, their bodies thrown into pits — including a 'killing tree' against which infants were smashed. Estimates of the dead under the regime range from 1.5 to 2 million (some say 3 million) out of a population of about 8 million — with Tuol Sleng's archive of prisoner photographs its most harrowing record.

Memory and justice

Vietnam's invasion ended the regime in January 1979. The Cambodian-led tribunal (ECCC, from 2006) convicted three senior figures, including Tuol Sleng's commandant Duch and ideologue Nuon Chea, amid criticism of cost, delay and political interference. Choeung Ek and Tuol Sleng are memorials and museums; the genocide's memory shapes Cambodian politics still — and the term 'killing fields' entered global language after the 1984 film of that name.
