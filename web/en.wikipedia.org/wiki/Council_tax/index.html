Council Tax

Council Tax is a local taxation system used in England, Scotland and Wales. It is a tax on domestic property, introduced in 1993 by the Local Government Finance Act 1992, replacing the unpopular Community Charge ("poll tax"), which in turn replaced the system of rates. Council Tax is collected by local authorities to help fund local services such as refuse collection, libraries, police and fire services.

Banding

Each dwelling is allocated to one of eight valuation bands, A (lowest) to H (highest), based on its capital value as of 1 April 1991 in England and Scotland (2003 in Wales, which has nine bands). Bills are set relative to Band D: Band A households pay two-thirds of the Band D amount and Band H households pay twice as much. A discount of 25% applies where only one adult lives in a property, and various exemptions and reductions exist, such as for full-time students and people on low incomes through Council Tax Reduction schemes.

Administration

Billing authorities (district and unitary councils in England) collect the tax, distributing shares to precepting authorities including county councils, police and crime commissioners and fire authorities. Non-payment is pursued through magistrates' courts by way of a liability order, and enforcement may include attachment of earnings or, historically, committal to prison in some jurisdictions.

Criticism

Council Tax is often criticized as regressive: bills are based on outdated property values and the ratio between the top and bottom bands is capped, so the tax takes a smaller share of income and property value from wealthy households than from poorer ones. Successive governments have considered but avoided revaluation in England.
