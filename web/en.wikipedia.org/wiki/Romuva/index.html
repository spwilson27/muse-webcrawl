Romuva

Romuva is a modern revival of the traditional ethnic religion of the Baltic peoples, principally Lithuanians, reconstructing the pre-Christian faith practised before Lithuania's Christianization in the late fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries. Its name comes from Romuva (Romowe), the legendary pagan sanctuary in Prussia described in chronicles, said to house images of the gods and an eternally burning sacred fire tended by a high priest, the krivis.

History

The movement traces its roots to the nineteenth-century Lithuanian national revival and to the philosopher Vydūnas, while its modern organization emerged in the late Soviet period: Jonas Trinkūnas founded the Ramuva cultural movement in 1967, and after Lithuanian independence the community registered as a religious society in 1992, taking the name Romuva in 2001. Trinkūnas served as its high priest (krivis) until his death in 2014, succeeded by his wife Inija Trinkūnienė.

Beliefs and practice

Romuva centres on reverence for nature (gamta), the sacred balance of the world (darna), and the pantheon of Baltic deities including Dievas, Perkūnas (thunder), Žemyna (earth) and Laima (fate). Worship takes place at altars (aukurai) with fire rituals, folk songs (sutartinės), and seasonal festivals such as Rasos (summer solstice) and Vėlinės (feast of the dead). Ancestor veneration is central, and ethics emphasize harmony with the natural and human world.

Recognition

Romuva is one of Lithuania's recognized "traditional" religious communities; in 2019 the Seimas declined to grant it the higher status of state recognition, a decision the European Court of Human Rights later found discriminatory in 2021. Related revival movements include Latvian Dievturība and Prussian groups.
