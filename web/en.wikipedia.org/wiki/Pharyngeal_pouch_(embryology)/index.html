Pharyngeal pouch (embryology)

In the embryonic development of vertebrates, pharyngeal pouches (also called branchial pouches) are endodermal outpocketings that form on the internal side of the pharyngeal arches, between them in sequence. They develop in a rostral-to-caudal sequence and give rise to a number of important structures, including glands of the neck and components of the ear. Humans have four well-defined pairs of pouches, with a rudimentary fifth sometimes described.

Derivatives

The first pharyngeal pouch expands into the tubotympanic recess, forming the tympanic cavity of the middle ear and the mastoid antrum; its endodermal lining becomes the inner surface of the tympanic membrane, and its stalk persists as the Eustachian (auditory) tube connecting the middle ear to the nasopharynx.

The second pouch largely obliterates as the palatine tonsil develops; part of its lumen persists as the tonsillar fossa, and its endoderm forms the surface epithelium and crypts of the palatine tonsil.

The third pouch differentiates into the inferior parathyroid glands (from its dorsal wing) and the thymus (from its ventral wing). The thymic primordia migrate caudally and medially to fuse in the midline, which explains ectopic parathyroid tissue found along the descent path.

The fourth pouch gives rise to the superior parathyroid glands and the ultimobranchial body, which contributes the calcitonin-producing parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid gland.

Clinical significance

Abnormal development of the third and fourth pouches underlies DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion syndrome), in which thymic and parathyroid hypoplasia cause immunodeficiency and hypocalcaemia. Persistent pouch remnants can form branchial cleft cysts, sinuses, or fistulae along the neck.
