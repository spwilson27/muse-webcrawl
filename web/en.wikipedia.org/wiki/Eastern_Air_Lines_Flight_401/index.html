Eastern Air Lines Flight 401 was a scheduled flight from John F. Kennedy International Airport in New York to Miami International Airport, Florida. On December 29, 1972, the Lockheed L-1011 TriStar operating the flight crashed into the Florida Everglades, killing 101 of the 176 people on board. The National Transportation Safety Board determined that the entire flight crew had become preoccupied with a faulty nose-gear indicator light and had failed to notice that the autopilot had been inadvertently disengaged, allowing the aircraft to descend gradually until it struck the ground — a classic case of controlled flight into terrain and a landmark in the study of crew resource management.

Accident

The flight departed New York at 9:20 p.m. EST under Captain Robert Loft, First Officer Albert Stockstill and Flight Engineer Donald Repo. On approach to Miami, the nose landing gear indicator light failed to illuminate. The crew entered a holding pattern over the Everglades at 2,000 feet while they attempted to diagnose the problem; the flight engineer went down into the electronics bay ("hell hole") below the cockpit to check the gear visually. During this time a slight pressure on the control column — possibly as the captain shifted in his seat — disengaged the autopilot's altitude-hold mode. The aircraft began a slow descent that none of the crew noticed, despite a brief altitude-alert chime that went unheard or unheeded. When the first officer finally remarked on the altitude, it was too late: the TriStar struck the swamp at 11:42 p.m., breaking up across a wide area.

Rescue and survivors

The crash site, in alligator-infested marshland, was reached first by airboat operator Robert "Bud" Marquis, who had seen the fireball and helped rescue survivors. Seventy-five people survived the accident, among them all but two of the cabin crew's complement of flight attendants in some sections; Captain Loft died at the scene and First Officer Stockstill also died, while Flight Engineer Repo survived the impact but died later in hospital. The high survival rate was attributed to the aircraft's low rate of descent, the energy-absorbing marsh, and the largely intact tail section.

Cause and legacy

The NTSB attributed the accident to the flight crew's failure to monitor their flight instruments during the final four minutes, and their failure to detect the unexpected descent soon enough to prevent impact. Flight 401 became a canonical training case in human factors — illustrating fixation, distraction and the breakdown of cockpit monitoring — and contributed to the development of crew resource management (CRM) training across the industry. The accident is also famous in popular culture for persistent (and officially denied) reports that salvaged parts from the wrecked aircraft, installed in other Eastern TriStars, were accompanied by ghostly apparitions of Loft and Repo — a story popularised by John G. Fuller's 1976 book The Ghost of Flight 401 and a 1978 television film.
