Power pop


Labelled image placeholder: Raspberries, archetypal power pop band

Power pop is a form of pop rock based on the early music of bands such as the Who, the Beatles, and the Beach Boys. It typically incorporates strong melodies, clear vocals, economical arrangements, and prominent guitar riffs, combining the energy of rock with concise, hook-filled pop songwriting.

History

The term was coined by Pete Townshend of the Who in 1967 to describe his band's music. The style crystallised in the early 1970s with Badfinger, the Raspberries, and Big Star, all of whom revived the British Invasion sound amid the dominance of progressive and hard rock. Cheap Trick and the Cars brought a harder-edged version to commercial success in the late 1970s, while in Britain the style overlapped with new wave through artists such as Nick Lowe and Elvis Costello.

Later developments

Power pop experienced revivals in the 1990s alternative era with bands such as Teenage Fanclub, Matthew Sweet, the Posies, and Weezer, and again in the 2000s with Fountains of Wayne and OK Go. Although rarely a dominant chart genre, it has maintained a devoted following sustained by specialist labels, festivals, and fanzines.

Characteristics

Defining features include jangling or crunchy guitars, close harmony vocals, handclaps and other 1960s pop devices, and short song lengths. Lyrics tend to concern romance and youthful longing, delivered with sincerity rather than irony.
