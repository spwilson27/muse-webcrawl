Progeria

Progeria
Other names: Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS)
Specialty: Medical genetics
Pronunciation: /proʊˈdʒɪəriə/

Progeria is the condition of premature aging, most commonly referring to Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), an extremely rare genetic disorder in which symptoms resembling aspects of aging are manifested at a very early age. Children with progeria appear normal at birth but within the first two years of life develop growth failure, loss of body fat and hair, aged-looking skin, stiffness of joints, and other features characteristic of advanced age.

Cause

HGPS is caused by a mutation in the LMNA gene, which encodes lamin A, a structural protein of the nuclear envelope. The classic mutation activates a cryptic splice site, producing an abnormal protein called progerin, which remains permanently farnesylated and disrupts nuclear structure. The condition is usually caused by a new (de novo) mutation and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern when transmitted.

Signs and symptoms

Features include short stature, low body weight, a disproportionately large head, prominent eyes, a small chin, delayed tooth formation, hair loss (alopecia), thin and wrinkled skin, and cardiovascular disease. Intelligence is typically normal. Death usually occurs in the early teens, most often from heart attack or stroke resulting from severe atherosclerosis.

Treatment

In 2020 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved lonafarnib (Zokinvy), a farnesyltransferase inhibitor, as the first treatment for HGPS; it has been shown to extend life expectancy. Supportive care addresses cardiovascular complications and other symptoms.

History

The syndrome was first described by Jonathan Hutchinson in 1886 and by Hastings Gilford in 1897. The causative LMNA mutation was identified in 2003. Progeria affects approximately 1 in 4–8 million newborns; a few hundred cases have been identified worldwide.
