Divergence

In vector calculus, divergence is a vector operator that operates on a vector field, producing a scalar field giving the quantity of the vector field's source at each point. More technically, the divergence represents the volume density of the outward flux of a vector field from an infinitesimal volume around a given point.

As an example, consider air as it is heated or cooled. The velocity of a point of air can be modeled as a vector field. If air is heated in a region, it will expand in all directions, such that the velocity field points outward from that region. Therefore, the divergence of the velocity field in that region would have a positive value.

Definition

The divergence of a continuously differentiable vector field F = Ui + Vj + Wk is defined to be the scalar-valued function:

div F = ∇ · F = ∂U/∂x + ∂V/∂y + ∂W/∂z

where ∇ is the del operator. Intuitively, it is the sum of the rates of change of the field in each coordinate direction.

Divergence theorem

The divergence theorem, also known as Gauss's theorem or Ostrogradsky's theorem, relates the flux of a vector field through a closed surface to the divergence of the field in the volume enclosed. It is a special case of the more general Stokes' theorem.

Applications

Divergence appears throughout physics, notably in Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism, where the divergence of the electric field equals the charge density divided by the permittivity of free space, and in fluid dynamics, where for incompressible flow the divergence of the velocity field is zero.
