Mazurkas (Chopin)

The mazurkas of Frédéric Chopin are a set of over fifty solo piano pieces based on the traditional Polish folk dance of the same name, the mazurka. Chopin began composing them in 1825 and continued until 1849, the year of his death; the mazurkas are among his most intimate and introspective works.

Overview

Chopin wrote at least 58 mazurkas, of which 41 were published during his lifetime, in groups (opuses) roughly in chronological order, with some published posthumously and several remaining unpublished or lost. The sets include the five mazurkas of Op. 6, the five of Op. 7 (including the famous Mazurka in B-flat major, Op. 7 No. 1), the four of Op. 17, and the late works Opp. 56, 59 and 63.

Although based on a dance in triple metre, Chopin's mazurkas were not intended for dancing. They elevate the folk form into an art form, employing characteristic features of the original dance:

  • A lively triple metre with emphasis displaced to the second or third beat
  • Dotted rhythms and distinctive "mazurka rhythm" figures
  • Drone-like pedal points imitating folk instruments
  • Use of the Lydian mode and other modal inflections, including raised fourth scale degrees
  • Rubato passages suggesting the improvisatory character of folk performance

Significance

The mazurkas have been described as Chopin's musical diary and a portrait of his exiled Polish homeland. Robert Schumann, reviewing the early sets, famously remarked that there were "cannons buried in flowers" in the music, a reference to its patriotic fervour beneath a lyrical surface. The later mazurkas, such as Op. 59 No. 3 in F-sharp minor and Op. 63 No. 3 in C-sharp minor, are among the most harmonically daring of Chopin's works.
