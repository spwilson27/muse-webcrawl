Structural engineering

Structural engineering is a sub-discipline of civil engineering in which structural engineers are trained to design the 'bones and joints' that create the form and shape of human-made structures. Structural engineers need to understand and calculate the stability, strength, rigidity and earthquake-susceptibility of built structures for buildings and nonbuilding structures. The structural designs are integrated with those of other designers such as architects and building services engineers, and they often supervise the on-site construction by contractors.

History

Structural engineering theory rests on physical laws and empirical knowledge accumulated since antiquity — from the pyramids of Egypt and the Pantheon of Rome to Gothic cathedrals. The science became explicit with Galileo's analysis of beam bending in Two New Sciences (1638), Hooke's law of elasticity (1676), Newton's laws of motion, and Euler's work on columns and buckling in the eighteenth century. The Industrial Revolution brought new materials — cast iron, wrought iron, steel and reinforced concrete — and new typologies such as long-span bridges and skyscrapers. In the modern era the theory of structures was systematised by figures such as Stephen Timoshenko, and computation transformed the field from the 1960s onward.

Principles

A structure must carry loads — dead loads (its own weight), live loads (occupants, furniture, vehicles), and environmental loads from wind, snow and earthquakes — along a continuous load path to its foundations. Members resist load by tension, compression, bending, shear and torsion, chosen from materials such as structural steel, reinforced and prestressed concrete, timber and masonry. Designs are checked against two families of criteria:

  • Ultimate limit states — the structure must not collapse or fail by rupture, buckling or overturning.
  • Serviceability limit states — deflections, vibrations and cracking must remain within tolerable limits in normal use.

Analysis

Classical methods such as moment distribution have largely been superseded by the finite element method, which discretises a structure into elements solved numerically by software. Engineers also design for robustness (resistance to progressive collapse), fatigue, fire and dynamic effects such as seismic response and wind-induced vibration.

Specialties

Specialist areas include bridge engineering, earthquake engineering, offshore structures, dams, tunnels, and the assessment and conservation of historic structures. Structural engineers are licensed in many jurisdictions and carry legal responsibility for the safety of their designs; failures such as the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse (1981) have driven modern codes and ethics of practice.
