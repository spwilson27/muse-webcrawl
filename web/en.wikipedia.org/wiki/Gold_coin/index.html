Gold coin

A gold coin is a coin made wholly or mostly of gold. Gold has been used for coinage since the earliest minted money — the electrum and gold issues of Lydia in the 7th–6th centuries BC under kings such as Croesus — and remained a staple of monetary systems for some 2,500 years. Most gold coins minted since 1800 are 90–92% pure (22 karat), the balance being copper or silver added for hardness.

History

Gold coinage spread through the Greek world (the Persian daric, Philip of Macedon's staters), Rome (the aureus, and Constantine's solidus, which anchored Byzantine currency for centuries), and the medieval revivals of the Italian city-states — Genoa's genovino, Florence's florin (1252) and Venice's ducat (1284) dominated European trade. Early modern empires poured American gold into coins such as the Spanish doubloon. In the 19th century, gold sovereigns and US eagle denominations underpinned the classical gold standard; circulating gold coinage largely ended in the 1930s as states left the standard and, in the United States, recalled gold coins in 1933.

Modern bullion coins

Since South Africa introduced the Krugerrand in 1967, mints have issued bullion coins sold for their metal content, including the Canadian Maple Leaf, American Gold Eagle, Austrian Philharmonic, British Britannia and Chinese Panda, many in 99.99% fine gold. Gold coins are also collected numismatically; rare issues such as the 1933 double eagle — one example of which sold for US$18.9 million in 2021 — are among the most valuable coins in the world.
