Violin Concerto No. 1 (Bruch)

Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26
ComposerMax Bruch
Composed1866; revised 1867
Premiere24 April 1866 (first version); revised version 7 January 1868, Bremen, with Joseph Joachim
Movements3
KeyG minor

The Violin Concerto No. 1 in G minor, Op. 26, by the German composer Max Bruch, is one of the most popular Romantic violin concertos. Completed when Bruch was in his twenties, it eclipsed nearly all his other work during his lifetime—much to his frustration—and remains a staple of the repertoire, a standard audition and competition piece, and usually the first full Romantic concerto young violinists learn.

The opening of the first movement of Bruch's G minor concerto.

Composition and premiere

Bruch completed a first version in 1866, premiered in Koblenz with Otto von Königslöw as soloist. Dissatisfied, he sent the score to the great violinist Joseph Joachim, whose detailed advice shaped a thorough revision; Joachim premiered the revised version in Bremen in January 1868. Because Bruch sold the work outright to his publisher for a one-time fee, he earned nothing from its enormous subsequent success—a sore point compounded when the autograph score he later sold to American collectors was traced during the 20th century.

Structure

  1. Vorspiel: Allegro moderato (G minor) — a dramatic "prelude" rather than a classical first movement, opening with orchestral woodwind chords answered by the soloist, and flowing without pause into the slow movement.
  2. Adagio (E-flat major) — the lyrical heart of the work, built on a broad singing theme, often considered among the most beautiful slow movements in the concerto literature.
  3. Finale: Allegro energico (G major) — a vigorous, dance-like movement in Hungarian-flavored style, with a grand second theme.

Reception

The concerto's popularity rivalled Mendelssohn's; Bruch complained that his other concertos—including the Scottish Fantasy and the Second and Third Violin Concertos—were unjustly neglected, and he reportedly grumbled that the G minor was played "everywhere." Nevertheless its melodic abundance and idiomatic solo writing have kept it at the center of the violin repertoire for over 150 years.
