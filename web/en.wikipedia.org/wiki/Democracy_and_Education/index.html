Democracy and Education: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Education is a 1916 book by the American philosopher and educator John Dewey. It is Dewey's most systematic statement of his educational philosophy and one of the most influential works in the philosophy of education. Dewey argued that education is not merely a preparation for life but a process of living itself, and that a democratic society depends on schools organised as embryonic democratic communities.

Summary

Dewey begins by treating education as a social function: the process by which a society renews itself by transmitting its habits, knowledge, and ideals to the young. Drawing on his pragmatist philosophy, he presents learning as an active process of "doing" and reflective experience rather than passive absorption of facts. The learner reconstructs experience, and growth — the continuous capacity for further growth — is itself the aim of education.

The book develops Dewey's famous definition of democracy as "primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience". A society is democratic insofar as its members share numerous interests in common and interact freely with other groups. Education in such a society should break down class barriers, give all students access to both intellectual and practical studies, and reject the traditional dualism between vocational and academic training.

Key themes

  • Education as growth: the criterion of educational value is the extent to which schooling creates a desire and capacity for continued growth.
  • Experience and thinking: reflective thought arises from genuine problems encountered in activity; subject matter should be connected to the learner's experience.
  • The school as social institution: schools should be communities in miniature, cultivating habits of inquiry, cooperation, and communication.
  • Criticism of dualisms: Dewey attacks divisions between mind and body, theory and practice, culture and utility, interest and discipline.

Influence

Democracy and Education shaped progressive education movements worldwide and remains a foundational text in teacher training and educational theory. Critics have debated its practical implications, but its central claim — that democratic life and educational method are inseparable — continues to frame discussions of the purposes of schooling.
