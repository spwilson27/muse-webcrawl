Rationing

Rationing is the controlled distribution of scarce resources, goods, or services. Governments impose rationing when supply cannot meet demand — most commonly in wartime, during blockades, droughts, or economic emergencies — to ensure equitable access and prevent hoarding and price spirals.

Methods

Rationing is typically administered through coupons, ration books, stamps, or points systems that entitle each person to a fixed quantity of a commodity within a set period. Price controls often accompany rationing, and black markets commonly arise where enforcement is weak.

Wartime rationing

Both world wars saw extensive rationing. In Britain during the Second World War, the Ministry of Food rationed bacon, butter, sugar, meat, tea, cheese, eggs, and other staples from 1940, with food rationing not fully ending until 1954. The United States rationed gasoline, rubber, sugar, coffee, and meat under the Office of Price Administration. Governments promoted rationing as fair — "fair shares for all" — and nutrition under British wartime rationing is often credited with improving the diet of the poorest.

Other applications

Rationing also occurs in peacetime: water rationing during droughts, fuel rationing during the 1970s oil crises, healthcare rationing through triage and waiting lists, and carbon rationing proposals as a climate policy. Economists debate rationing's efficiency against allocation by price, weighing fairness against consumer choice.
