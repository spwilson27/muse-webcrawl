Marinade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Pieces of meat marinating in a bowl of dark, herb-flecked liquid]
Pieces of meat marinating in a bowl of dark, herb-flecked liquid

A marinade is a seasoned liquid, usually acidic, into which food is soaked before cooking to add flavour and — in the case of meat — to modify texture. Marinades typically combine an acid (vinegar, citrus, wine, yogurt), oil, salt and aromatics. The soak is usually brief: despite their reputation, marinades penetrate only a few millimetres into meat, functioning mainly as surface seasoning and a base for crust formation.

Science

Acid denatures surface proteins; left too long (especially citrus on fish, as in Ceviche, or strong marinades beyond a day) it turns meat mushy or chalky. Salt is the true penetrator, seasoning deeper and retaining moisture; oil carries fat-soluble aromatics and prevents sticking; enzymes in pineapple (bromelain), papaya (papain) and kiwi genuinely tenderise — aggressively if unchecked. Marinating also reduces the formation of some carcinogenic compounds (HCAs) in grilled meat, a finding that gave the technique a health argument beyond flavour.

Traditions

Marinade families map the world's cuisines: yogurt-and-spice for tandoori, soy-ginger-sesame across East Asia, adobo's vinegar in the Philippines and Latin America, jerk's Scotch bonnet and allspice in Jamaica, chimichurri as both marinade and sauce in Argentina, and wine-based pots in French country cooking. Food safety rules — refrigerate, never reuse raw marinade as sauce without boiling — are the unglamorous constant.
