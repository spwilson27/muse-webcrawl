Desires are states of mind that are expressed by terms like "wanting", "wishing", "longing" or "craving". A great variety of features is commonly associated with desires. They are understood as attitudes toward propositions: an agent desires that a certain state of affairs obtain. Desires stand in contrast to beliefs, which represent how the world actually is: in the standard terminology, beliefs have mind-to-world direction of fit, while desires have world-to-mind direction of fit — they are satisfied when the world is changed to match them.

Theories

Theories of desire try to state its essential features. Action-based theories define desire by its tendency to produce action toward its object. Pleasure-based theories focus on the pleasure felt when a desire is satisfied, or the displeasure of frustration. Value-based theories link desire to seeing its object as good or valuable. Neuroscientific research, notably by Kent Berridge, distinguishes "wanting" (mediated by dopamine-driven incentive salience) from "liking", showing the two can come apart, as in addiction, where craving persists without pleasure.

Philosophical and religious views

Desire is central to many philosophical traditions. Plato ranked appetites lowest in the tripartite soul; Aristotle analysed orexis as the origin of animal movement. Hume held that reason alone cannot motivate and that desire directs action ("reason is the slave of the passions"). In Buddhism, craving (taṇhā) is identified in the Four Noble Truths as the origin of suffering (dukkha), and liberation involves its cessation; Hindu thought similarly treats desire (kāma) as both a legitimate aim of life and, when uncontrolled, a cause of bondage. Schopenhauer saw the will — endless, unsatisfiable desire — as the essence of the world and the source of suffering.

Classification

Desires are commonly divided into intrinsic desires (for things wanted for their own sake) and instrumental desires (for means to something else), and into occurrent desires and standing (dispositional) desires. Their strength, together with beliefs about how to act, is the basis of decision theory's model of rational choice.
