Hydrodesulfurization

Hydrodesulfurization (HDS), also called hydrotreating, is a catalytic chemical process widely used in petroleum refineries to remove sulfur from crude-oil fractions such as naphtha, kerosene, diesel, and fuel oils. Removing sulfur reduces sulfur dioxide emissions when the fuels are burned and prevents poisoning of the catalysts used in downstream processes such as catalytic reforming.

Process

The feedstock is mixed with hydrogen and passed over a catalyst — typically cobalt and molybdenum oxides on an alumina support (CoMo), or nickel-molybdenum (NiMo) for harder feeds — at temperatures of roughly 300–400 °C and pressures of 30–130 atmospheres. Sulfur compounds such as thiols, sulfides, and thiophenes react with hydrogen to form hydrocarbons and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which is separated and converted to elemental sulfur in a Claus unit. The process also removes nitrogen and oxygen compounds and saturates olefins.

Deep desulfurization

Environmental regulations requiring ultra-low-sulfur diesel (10–15 ppm sulfur) have driven development of more active catalysts and two-stage processes, since sterically hindered compounds such as 4,6-dimethyldibenzothiophene react only slowly under conventional conditions.
