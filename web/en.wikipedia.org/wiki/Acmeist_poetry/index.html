Acmeist poetry

Acmeist poetry was a Russian modernist poetic movement that emerged around 1911–12 as a reaction against the vagueness and mysticism of Russian Symbolism. Its name derives from the Greek akmē, "the highest point" or "bloom". The Acmeists prized clarity, precision and craftsmanship — Nikolay Gumilev called for "beautiful clarity" (sometimes called Clarism) — and celebrated the material world, architecture, and cultural memory.

History and manifestoes

The movement crystallised in the "Poets' Guild" (Tsekh poetov) in St Petersburg, led by Nikolay Gumilev and Sergei Gorodetsky. In 1913 Gumilev published "The Legacy of Symbolism and Acmeism" and Osip Mandelstam wrote "The Morning of Acmeism" (published 1919), which compared poetry to Gothic architecture — a building art in which the word, like stone, is both material and meaning. The journal Apollon served as a platform.

Principal figures

Core Acmeists included Gumilev, Anna Akhmatova, Osip Mandelstam, Gorodetsky, Georgy Ivanov and Mikhail Zenkevich. Akhmatova's early collections Evening (1912) and Rosary (1914) exemplified the movement's concrete, intimate lyricism; Mandelstam's Stone (1913) embodied its classical solidity. Mandelstam's later concept of "nostalgia for world culture" grew from Acmeist roots.

Fate

The movement dissolved amid revolution and repression: Gumilev was executed by the Cheka in 1921, Mandelstam died in a Gulag transit camp in 1938, and Akhmatova was silenced for decades, though her late masterpiece Poem Without a Hero carried the Acmeist tradition forward. Acmeism nonetheless shaped 20th-century Russian poetry through its emphasis on craft and the tangible word.
