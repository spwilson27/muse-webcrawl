Civil service

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The civil service is a collective term for a body of government employees — civil servants — who implement government policy, administer public services and advise ministers, and who are hired on the basis of merit through competitive examination or qualifications rather than political affiliation. In most systems civil servants are expected to be politically impartial, serving governments of any party, in contrast to political appointees.

History

The prototype was imperial China's mandarinate, recruited by examination for over a millennium (605–1905). Modern merit systems displaced patronage ("spoils") in the 19th century: Britain's Northcote–Trevelyan Report (1854) founded the unified, permanent, impartial Home Civil Service; the US Pendleton Act (1883), following President Garfield's assassination by a disappointed office-seeker, began federal merit reform. France's grands corps and India's IAS (successor to the colonial ICS) are elite variants.

Roles and debates

Civil services combine policy advice, delivery (tax, benefits, health systems), regulation and crisis management. Permanent tenure protects impartiality but fuels debates about accountability and responsiveness, the revolving door to the private sector, representativeness (class, gender, region), and reform movements from New Public Management to digital-government units. In Westminster systems, special advisers add a partisan layer formally outside the impartial service.
