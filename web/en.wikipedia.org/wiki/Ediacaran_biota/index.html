The Ediacaran biota (also called the Ediacara fauna or Vendian biota) is the assemblage of mostly soft-bodied, macroscopic organisms that lived in the oceans during the Ediacaran Period, from about 635 to 541 million years ago, immediately before the Cambrian explosion. They represent the earliest known complex multicellular life and are named after the Ediacara Hills of South Australia, where their fossils were recognised by Reg Sprigg in 1946–47.

Discovery and distribution

Similar fossils have since been found worldwide, in three classic assemblages: the Avalon assemblage (deep-water, oldest, c. 575 Ma, including Mistaken Point in Newfoundland), the White Sea assemblage (shallower, most diverse), and the Nama assemblage (youngest, terminal Ediacaran, from Namibia). Because the organisms lacked hard parts, they are preserved as impressions in sandstone, often where microbial mats sealed the sea floor — a preservation style sometimes called the "death mask".

Forms and affinities

Ediacaran organisms show a bewildering range of body plans, many with quilted, tubular or fractal construction unlike any modern animals: frond-like rangeomorphs such as Charnia; disc-shaped forms; the oval, ribbed Dickinsonia (shown by biomarker evidence to be an animal); worm-like bilaterians such as Spriggina; and Kimberella, interpreted as a mollusc-like grazer. Debates continue over whether most were animals, giant protists, lichens, or members of an extinct "failed experiment" in multicellularity — the hypothetical kingdom Vendobionta proposed by Adolf Seilacher. The first skeletonised animals, such as the tube-shaped Cloudina, appear late in the period.

Extinction

Most Ediacaran forms disappear at or before the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary, in one of the earliest mass turnovers in the fossil record, possibly driven by the rise of mobile predators, burrowing animals that disrupted the microbial mats, and changing ocean chemistry. A few lineages may have survived into the Cambrian.
