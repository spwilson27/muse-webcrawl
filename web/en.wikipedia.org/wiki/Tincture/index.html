Tincture


Tinctures are alcoholic extracts of plant or animal material

A tincture is typically an extract of plant or animal material dissolved in ethanol (ethyl alcohol). Solvent concentrations of 25–60% are common, but may run as high as 90%. In chemistry, a tincture is a solution that has alcohol as its solvent. In herbal medicine, alcoholic tinctures are made with various ethanol concentrations for preservation and ease of dosing.

Preparation and use

Tinctures are prepared by steeping the source material — bark, berries, leaves, roots, or resins — in alcohol for days to weeks, then pressing and filtering the liquid. Because alcohol extracts a wide range of compounds and acts as a preservative, tinctures remain potent for years, far longer than dried herbs. They are usually administered in small measured drops under the tongue or diluted in water.

Examples

Well-known tinctures include tincture of iodine, used as an antiseptic; tincture of opium (laudanum), historically a common painkiller; tincture of benzoin, applied to protect skin; and cannabis and herbal tinctures used in traditional and complementary medicine. Non-alcoholic variants use vinegar, glycerol, or propylene glycol as the solvent for those who avoid ethanol.
