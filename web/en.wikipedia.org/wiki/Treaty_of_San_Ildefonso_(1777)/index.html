Treaty of San Ildefonso (1777)

Signed1 October 1777
LocationPalace of La Granja de San Ildefonso, Spain
PartiesSpain; Portugal
EffectFixed colonial boundaries in South America

The border between Spanish and Portuguese America as settled at San Ildefonso.

The First Treaty of San Ildefonso was signed on 1 October 1777 between Spain and Portugal, settling long-running boundary disputes between their South American empires. It should not be confused with the later treaties of San Ildefonso of 1796 and 1800 between Spain and France.

Background

Since the Treaty of Madrid (1750), Spain and Portugal had quarreled over the borders between Brazil and the Spanish colonies, particularly around the Río de la Plata, where the Portuguese settlement of Colonia del Sacramento sat opposite Buenos Aires. A Spanish expedition under Pedro de Cevallos captured Colonia and invaded southern Brazil in 1776–77, prompting negotiations.

Terms

Portugal ceded Colonia del Sacramento and the territory north of the Río de la Plata to Spain, confirming Spanish control of the entire northern bank of the estuary (roughly modern Uruguay). Spain in turn returned the island of Santa Catarina and the Rio Grande do Sul region to Portugal. The boundary in the Amazon and the interior followed watersheds and river lines. In Africa, Portugal ceded the islands of Annobón and Fernando Pó (Bioko), along with rights on the mainland coast between the Niger and Ogooué rivers, to Spain.

Aftermath

The treaty largely stabilized the South American frontier until the Napoleonic era; the Badajoz settlement of 1801 adjusted the far southern border again. The Spanish expedition that took possession of Fernando Pó and Annobón suffered catastrophic losses to disease, and effective Spanish administration of Equatorial Guinea came only much later.
