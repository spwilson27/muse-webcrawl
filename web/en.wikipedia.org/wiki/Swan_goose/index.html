The swan goose (Anser cygnoides) is a large goose native to eastern Asia. Rare and declining in the wild, it breeds in northern China, Mongolia and the Russian Far East, and winters mainly in central and eastern China. It is the ancestor of the domesticated Chinese goose, which is distinguished by the prominent basal knob on its bill.

Description

The swan goose is 81–94 cm long and among the heaviest of the Anser geese, weighing roughly 2.8–3.5 kg. It is brownish-grey with a long black bill, chestnut-brown crown and hindneck contrasting with pale cheeks, and a white band at the base of the bill. The sexes are similar, though males are slightly larger. Its loud, prolonged honking call distinguishes it from other geese of the region.

Distribution and status

Breeding areas include the wetlands of the Amur basin, Lake Khanka, Mongolia's river valleys, and northern China; the great majority of the world population winters around Poyang Lake and the lower Yangtze floodplain. Habitat loss, particularly the drainage and agricultural conversion of wetlands, hunting, and egg collecting have reduced populations, and the species is classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN.

Domestic form

Domestic Chinese geese descend from the swan goose and have been kept in China for centuries for meat, eggs, guarding, and weed control in crops. They differ from wild birds in their larger size, upright posture, and the greatly developed knob on the bill.
