Free surface effect

The free surface effect is a mechanism in naval architecture which can cause a watercraft to become unstable and capsize. It refers to the tendency of liquids — in open containers or incompletely filled compartments such as tanks or flooded spaces aboard ship — to react to the vessel's motion by remaining level relative to the horizon. As a vessel rolls, the liquid shifts toward the low side, moving the vessel's center of gravity in the direction of the heel and reducing its righting moment.

Mechanism

When a tank is only partially filled, the liquid surface is free to move. The shift of liquid effectively raises the ship's virtual center of gravity and reduces the metacentric height by an amount that depends on the second moment of area of the free surface — principally on the width of the tank, not on the quantity of liquid. Because the loss grows with the cube of the tank's breadth, subdividing tanks longitudinally greatly reduces the effect.

Mitigation

Naval architects counter the free surface effect by keeping tanks either completely full or empty where possible, subdividing large tanks with longitudinal bulkheads, and fitting anti-roll tanks with baffling designed to control the movement of liquid. Damage stability regulations account for the free surface effect of flooded compartments and liquid cargoes such as those carried by tankers and livestock carriers.

Examples

The free surface effect has contributed to notable capsizings, including those of ferries flooded through open bow doors, and is a central concern in the stability of ships carrying liquid bulk cargoes. It also applies, in everyday terms, to the carrying of open containers of liquid.
