Neurodegenerative disease

A neurodegenerative disease is caused by the progressive loss of structure or function of neurons, which may ultimately involve cell death. Many neurodegenerative diseases — including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, multiple system atrophy, tauopathies, and prion diseases — occur as a result of neurodegenerative processes. Such diseases are incurable, resulting in progressive degeneration and/or death of neuron cells. As research progresses, many similarities appear that relate these diseases to one another on a sub-cellular level.

Classification

Neurodegenerative diseases are commonly classified according to the protein that aggregates abnormally within or between cells. Alzheimer's disease is characterised by amyloid-beta plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles; Parkinson's disease and other synucleinopathies by alpha-synuclein inclusions (Lewy bodies); Huntington's disease by mutant huntingtin protein with an expanded polyglutamine tract; and prion diseases by misfolded prion protein (PrP), which is uniquely transmissible between individuals.

Mechanisms

Several cellular pathways are implicated across these conditions: protein misfolding and aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired protein clearance by the proteasome and autophagy systems, excitotoxicity, and chronic neuroinflammation driven by microglia. Ageing is the single greatest risk factor for most of these diseases, and both genetic mutations and environmental exposures contribute in varying proportions. In Alzheimer's disease, rare familial forms are caused by mutations in APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2, while the APOE ε4 allele is the strongest common genetic risk factor for the sporadic form.

Signs and symptoms

Depending on the population of neurons lost, symptoms may be dominated by dementia (progressive cognitive decline), movement disorders such as tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia in Parkinson's disease, chorea in Huntington's disease, or progressive muscle weakness and paralysis in ALS, where motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord die.

Treatment

For most neurodegenerative diseases there is no cure and treatment is symptomatic: cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine in Alzheimer's disease, levodopa in Parkinson's disease, riluzole and edaravone in ALS. Recently, monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid, such as lecanemab and donanemab, have shown modest slowing of early Alzheimer's disease. Research directions include antisense oligonucleotides, gene therapy, and stem cell approaches. Compare neurotoxicity, in which neuronal damage is caused by external toxic substances rather than spontaneous degeneration.
