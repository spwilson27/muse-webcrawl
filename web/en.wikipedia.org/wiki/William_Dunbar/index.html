William Dunbar (c. 1460 – c. 1520) was a Scottish poet, a makar at the court of King James IV of Scotland, and one of the greatest poets of the Scots Renaissance. Some eighty poems are attributed to him, ranging over courtly allegory, satire, religious verse, comic invective and elegy, and displaying an unmatched command of register — from aureate Latinity to demotic flyting.

Life

Nothing certain is known of Dunbar's early life. He may have studied at the University of St Andrews, and his poems suggest time spent as a travelling Franciscan novice preacher. By 1500 he was in royal service, receiving a pension from James IV, and in 1501 he accompanied an embassy to England connected with the King's marriage to Margaret Tudor. He appears in the Treasurer's Accounts through 1513 but vanishes from record after the disaster of Flodden; the date of his death is unknown.

Works

The Thrissil and the Rois (1503) is an allegorical celebration of the royal marriage. The Goldyn Targe is a dream-vision in aureate style. His most famous line opens the Lament for the Makaris — "I that in heill wes and gladnes, / Am trublit now with gret seiknes" — whose refrain Timor mortis conturbat me ("the fear of death disturbs me") punctuates a catalogue of dead poets. Comic and satirical pieces include The Tretis of the Tua Mariit Wemen and the Wedo, a frank, Rabelaisian dialogue, and numerous petitions to the King pleading, with wit, for a church benefice that never came.

The Flyting

The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie is a virtuoso contest of poetic abuse between Dunbar and Walter Kennedy, trading extravagant insults in Scots and mock-Gaelic — the supreme example of the flyting genre in Scots literature.

Legacy

Dunbar's poems survive in manuscripts including the Bannatyne Manuscript (1568). He is regarded as the outstanding poet of the court of James IV and a bridge between Chaucerian tradition and the vernacular brilliance of the Scottish Renaissance.
