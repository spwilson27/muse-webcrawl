Higman's lemma

In mathematics, Higman's lemma states that the set of finite sequences over a finite alphabet, ordered by the subsequence (embedding) ordering, is a well-quasi-order. That is, in any infinite sequence of words over a finite alphabet, there exist two words, one appearing earlier and one later, such that the earlier is a subsequence of the later. The result is due to Graham Higman, who published it in 1952.

Statement and proof

More generally, if a set is well-quasi-ordered, then the set of finite sequences over it under the subsequence order is also well-quasi-ordered. The standard proof uses the "minimal bad sequence" argument: assuming an infinite sequence with no good pair exists, one constructs a smaller such sequence, leading to a contradiction. The same proof technique is used for the Kruskal tree theorem.

Applications

Higman's lemma is a foundational result in the theory of well-quasi-orders and has applications in computer science, particularly in the termination analysis of algorithms and the theory of well-structured transition systems.
