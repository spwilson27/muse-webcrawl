Shallow water blackout

Shallow water blackout is a loss of consciousness caused by cerebral hypoxia towards the end of a breath-hold dive in water, typically because the swimmer hyperventilated before diving. It most often strikes strong swimmers and freedivers near the surface during ascent or while practising underwater laps, and it can lead to drowning without any preceding struggle or warning — victims commonly sink silently, even in crowded, lifeguarded pools.

Mechanism

The urge to breathe is driven primarily by rising carbon dioxide (CO2) in the blood, not by falling oxygen. Deliberate hyperventilation expels CO2, delaying the urge to breathe, but does not significantly increase the body's oxygen stores. During the dive, oxygen continues to be consumed; because the CO2 signal remains suppressed, oxygen can fall below the level needed for consciousness before the swimmer feels any need to surface. During ascent the danger peaks, as falling water pressure reduces the partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs and blood still further — hence the traditional name, since blackout often occurs in the shallow water near the surface. The closely related phenomenon of deep water blackout occurs on ascent from deep freedives.

Circumstances and victims

Cases frequently involve competitive swimmers and military trainees doing repeated underwater laps or breath-hold contests, spearfishers, and freedivers. Public awareness campaigns by organisations such as Shallow Water Blackout Prevention — founded after the deaths of several young men, including competitive swimmers — have pushed pools and aquatic organisations to ban prolonged breath-holding games and unsupervised underwater training.

Prevention

  • Never hyperventilate before breath-hold swimming or diving.
  • Never practise breath-holding alone; use the "one up, one down" buddy system, with a dedicated observer.
  • Take adequate surface recovery intervals between dives.
  • Pools should prohibit long underwater laps and competitive breath-hold games.
