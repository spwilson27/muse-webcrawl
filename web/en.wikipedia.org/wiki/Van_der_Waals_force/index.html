Van der Waals force

In molecular physics and chemistry, the van der Waals force (sometimes van der Waals interaction) is a distance-dependent interaction between atoms or molecules. Unlike ionic or covalent bonds, these attractions do not result from a chemical electronic bond; they are comparatively weak and therefore more susceptible to disturbance. The van der Waals force quickly vanishes at longer distances between interacting molecules.

Fluctuating electron clouds create instantaneous dipoles that induce complementary dipoles in neighbours.

Definition

Named after Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals, the term is used in several related senses. In the broadest sense it covers all weak intermolecular attractions other than hydrogen bonds, comprising:

  • Keesom forces — attractions between permanent dipoles (dipole–dipole interactions);
  • Debye forces — attractions between a permanent dipole and an induced dipole;
  • London dispersion forces — attractions between instantaneous, fluctuating dipoles, present in all atoms and molecules, including nonpolar ones.

Alternatively, "van der Waals force" is sometimes restricted to the London dispersion force alone. In the Lennard-Jones potential, used to model intermolecular interactions, the attractive r−6 term represents these dispersion forces, balanced by a steep repulsive term at short range.

Characteristics

Van der Waals forces are weak compared with chemical bonds — typically 0.4 to 4 kJ/mol — but collectively they dominate phenomena such as condensation of gases, the cohesion of molecular solids, the physical adsorption of gases on surfaces, and much of the folding of proteins and the stacking of DNA bases. They are also additive and nonsaturating in a way chemical bonds are not.

Applications and examples

The ability of geckos to climb smooth surfaces is attributed to van der Waals attraction between the millions of microscopic setae on their feet and the surface. Van der Waals forces also stabilise supramolecular assemblies, hold the layers of graphite together, and underpin the design of van der Waals heterostructures — artificial materials assembled from atomically thin layers held together by these forces.
