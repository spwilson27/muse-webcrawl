Witchetty grub

The witchetty grub (also spelled witjuti grub) is a term used in Australia for the large, white, wood-eating larvae of several moths, particularly the larva of the cossid moth Endoxyla leucomochla, which feeds on the roots of the witchetty bush (Acacia kempeana). The term may also apply to larvae of other cossid and hepialid moths and of longhorn beetles found in the roots, trunks, and stems of acacia and eucalyptus trees.

A witchetty grub

As food

Witchetty grubs have long been an important traditional food for Aboriginal Australians, especially in the desert regions of Central Australia. They are rich in protein and fat and were traditionally gathered by women and children, who dug them from the roots of witchetty bushes and river red gums. The grubs were eaten raw or lightly cooked in hot ashes; raw witchetty grubs are said to have a flavor reminiscent of almonds, while cooked ones have crisp skin and a taste compared to roast chicken or scrambled egg.

Culture

The grub features prominently in Aboriginal Dreaming stories and artwork in Central Australia, and "witchetty grub Dreaming" is a recurring subject for artists of the region. The grub's name derives from words in the Adnyamathanha and Arabana languages (wityu, "hooked stick", and varti, "grub") used for the hooked tools used to extract them.
