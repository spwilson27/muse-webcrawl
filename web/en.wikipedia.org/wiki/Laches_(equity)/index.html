Laches (equity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In common-law legal systems, laches is a lack of diligence and activity in making a legal claim, or moving forward with legal enforcement of a right, particularly in regard to equity. It is an equitable defence that bars a claim where the claimant's unreasonable delay has prejudiced the defendant — for example because evidence has been lost, witnesses have died, or the defendant has changed position in reliance on the claimant's inaction. The name derives from the Old French laschesse, slackness.

Elements

A defendant invoking laches must generally show (1) that the claimant delayed unreasonably and without excuse, with knowledge of the facts, and (2) that the delay caused prejudice. Unlike a statute of limitations, which fixes a definite period, laches is flexible and fact-specific: a short delay may bar a claim if prejudice is great, while a long delay may not if the defendant suffered no harm. The old maxim expresses the underlying idea: "equity aids the vigilant, not those who slumber on their rights."

Relation to statutes of limitation

Where a statute of limitations governs the claim, courts often presume that delay within the statutory period is reasonable, and laches applies mainly to equitable claims for which no statute provides — though in the United States the Supreme Court has held that laches cannot bar claims for damages brought within the Copyright Act's limitation period (Petrella v. MGM, 2014), while confirming its availability against unreasonable delay in patent cases was later limited by SCA Hygiene Products v. First Quality Baby Products (2017).
