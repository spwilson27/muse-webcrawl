Spanish Colonial architecture

Spanish Colonial architecture comprises the building styles developed in the Spanish Empire in the Americas and the Philippines from the 16th to the early 19th century. It transplanted Spanish Renaissance, Baroque and Mudéjar traditions into new settings, where they fused with indigenous materials, labour and motifs.

Characteristics

Typical features include thick adobe, stone or brick walls finished in stucco; low-pitched red tile roofs; arched doorways and arcaded courtyards; small windows; and ornate church facades — exuberantly carved in the Mexican Churrigueresque style, or restrained in frontier missions. Towns were laid out on the grid plan prescribed by the Laws of the Indies around a central plaza with church and government buildings, as seen in Mexico City, Lima, Quito and Manila.

Missions and churches

Religious orders built mission complexes from California and Texas to Paraguay: the California missions founded by Junípero Serra, the stone churches of the Jesuit missions among the Guaraní, and great cathedrals such as those of Mexico City, Puebla and Cusco — the latter rising on Inca foundations and blending Spanish forms with local craftsmanship (the Andean mestizo baroque).

Legacy and revival

In the early 20th century the style was romanticised in the United States as Spanish Colonial Revival, shaping homes and public buildings across the Southwest and Florida, and as Mission Revival. Original colonial buildings anchor many UNESCO World Heritage historic centres in Latin America.
