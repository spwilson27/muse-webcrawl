William Louis Veeck Jr. (February 9, 1914 – January 2, 1986), also known as "Sport Shirt Bill", was an American Major League Baseball franchise owner and promoter. Veeck — as owner of the Cleveland Indians, St. Louis Browns, and Chicago White Sox — was a flamboyant showman famous for innovative promotions, fan-friendly ballpark features, and for breaking the American League's color barrier by signing Larry Doby in 1947.

Early life

Veeck was born in Chicago. His father, William Veeck Sr., was president of the Chicago Cubs, and the younger Veeck worked for the Cubs in various jobs, famously planting the ivy on the outfield walls of Wrigley Field in 1937. After serving in the Marine Corps in the Second World War — during which he suffered a leg injury that eventually required amputation and led to his trademark peg leg and hollowed-out wooden leg used as an ashtray — he bought the minor league Milwaukee Brewers and then, in 1946, the Cleveland Indians.

Ownership career

In Cleveland, Veeck signed Larry Doby, the first African American player in the American League, in July 1947, and the veteran pitcher Satchel Paige in 1948; the Indians won the 1948 World Series. In St. Louis, he staged perhaps his most notorious stunt in 1951, sending 3-foot-7-inch Eddie Gaedel to bat for the Browns — Gaedel, wearing uniform number ⅛, walked on four pitches. With the Chicago White Sox, Veeck installed an exploding scoreboard, gave players names on the backs of their jerseys, and won the 1959 American League pennant. His 1979 "Disco Demolition Night" promotion ended in a riot and a forfeited game.

Legacy

Veeck's memoirs, Veeck as in Wreck (1962), co-written with Ed Linn, remain classics of sports literature. He championed fan amenities, giveaways, fireworks, and interactive features that are now standard across professional sports, and was an early advocate of interleague play and revenue sharing. He was posthumously inducted into the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1991.
