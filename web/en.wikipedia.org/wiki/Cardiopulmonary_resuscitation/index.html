Cardiopulmonary resuscitation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A rescuer performing chest compressions on a training manikin]
A rescuer performing chest compressions on a training manikin

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an emergency procedure consisting of chest compressions, often combined with rescue breaths, performed when the heart stops beating (Cardiac arrest) to manually circulate blood and preserve brain function until circulation can be restored. Modern CPR — external compressions (developed in 1960), mouth-to-mouth ventilation and early Defibrillation — roughly doubles or triples survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

Technique

For adults, guidelines (American Heart Association, European Resuscitation Council) call for compressions at 100–120 per minute, 5–6 cm deep, in the centre of the chest, with minimal interruptions; lay rescuers are taught compression-only ('hands-only') CPR for adult collapse. Trained responders add breaths at a 30:2 ratio. The 'chain of survival' — recognition and call, early CPR, rapid defibrillation, advanced care, post-arrest care — frames systems improvement.

History

Precursors include the bellows and tobacco-smoke methods of the eighteenth-century humane societies, Sylvester's arm-lift technique and, decisively, Kouwenhoven's closed-chest massage (1960) joined with Peter Safar's airway work and the external defibrillator. Mass public training since the 1970s, public-access AEDs and dispatcher-assisted CPR have steadily raised bystander intervention rates; survival still varies from under 5% to over 20% between systems.

Outcomes

CPR buys time; defibrillation within minutes is what most often restarts the heart in shockable rhythms. Good neurological survival concentrates in witnessed arrests with bystander CPR and early AED use — the rationale for CPR education in schools, now mandatory in many jurisdictions.
