Iron sight

Iron sights are a system of physical alignment markers (usually made of metallic material) that assist the user in aiming a ranged weapon such as a firearm, crossbow or telescope. They typically consist of two components — a front sight (post or bead) and a rear sight (notch or aperture) — which the shooter aligns with the target.

Types

Open sights use a notched rear element and a front post, as on most pistols. Aperture (peep) sights use a small hole in the rear sight, which the eye centres automatically; they are common on military rifles. Flip-up "back-up iron sights" (BUIS) are frequently fitted to rifles equipped with optical sights.

Adjustment

Sights are adjusted for windage (horizontal) and elevation (vertical) so that the point of aim matches the point of impact at a chosen range, a process known as zeroing. The distance between front and rear sight, the sight radius, affects aiming precision.

Comparison with optics

Iron sights are simple, robust and need no batteries, but lack the magnification and light-gathering of telescopic and reflex sights, which have largely supplanted them as primary sights on many modern service rifles.
