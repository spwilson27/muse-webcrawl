Herne the Hunter is a ghost of English folklore said to haunt Windsor Forest and Great Park in Berkshire. He is depicted as a huntsman wearing antlers — a stag's horns upon his head — riding a black horse, attended by demonic hounds and horned owls, and rattling chains. Herne is associated with a particular oak tree in Windsor Great Park, and his appearance is said to portend misfortune, especially for the royal family.

Origins

The earliest certain reference is in Shakespeare's The Merry Wives of Windsor (c. 1597), where the fairy-costumed characters invoke "Herne the hunter", who "in winter-time, / Even in the dead time of the night... walks round about an oak, with great ragg'd horns; / And there he blasts the tree, and takes the cattle, / And makes milch-kine yield blood, and shakes a chain / In a most hideous and dreadful manner." In the play, Mistress Page explains that Herne was a keeper in Windsor Forest who hanged himself from the oak.

Later antiquarians attached a fuller story: Herne was a huntsman of Richard II, mortally wounded saving the king from a stag, healed by witchcraft, then disgraced by jealous rivals and hanged from his oak. Folklorists have compared Herne with horned figures such as Cernunnos and with the leader of the Wild Hunt, and some connect the name to "Herian", an epithet of Odin as leader of the host of the slain; the historical basis of such links is uncertain.

In popular culture

Herne became a major figure in modern paganism and in fiction: he appears in Harrison Ainsworth's novel Windsor Castle (1842), in the television series Robin of Sherwood (1984–86) as the spirit of the forest guiding Robin, and in works by authors including Susan Cooper and Alan Garner.
