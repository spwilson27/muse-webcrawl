Stobi

Stobi (Greek: Στόβοι) was an ancient city of Paeonia, later conquered by Macedon and finally part of the Roman province of Macedonia. Its ruins lie near the modern village of Gradsko in North Macedonia, at the confluence of the Erigon (Crna) and Axius (Vardar) rivers. Because it stood where the road from the Danube to the Aegean crossed the road from Heraclea Lyncestis to Serdica, Stobi was one of the most important cities of the northern Balkans in antiquity.

History

Stobi was founded in the Archaic or early Hellenistic period and developed under Macedonian rule. Under Rome it became a prosperous municipium, granted the right to mint its own coins, and an important trading centre, partly because of nearby salt works. The Roman historian Livy mentions Stobi as the place where Macedonian forces marshalled during the wars with Rome. In late antiquity Stobi became an episcopal see; its bishops attended councils from the fourth century onward. The city was damaged by an earthquake around 518 and suffered in the Slavic invasions of the late sixth and seventh centuries, after which it was largely abandoned.

Archaeology

Excavations have revealed a large Roman theatre, a synagogue built over an earlier one — one of the oldest known synagogue remains in Europe, associated with a benefactor named Polycharmos — several Christian basilicas including the Episcopal Basilica with celebrated mosaic pavements, baptisteries, baths, a forum, and wealthy private houses such as the House of Peristeria and the Theodosian Palace, the latter named for a possible visit by Emperor Theodosius I. Finds from Stobi are displayed in museums in North Macedonia and Belgrade.

The peacock mosaic

The mosaic of peacocks flanking a kantharos, from the baptistery of the Episcopal Basilica, is among the most famous images of late antique Macedonian art; a stylised version appears on the reverse of the North Macedonian 10-denar banknote.
