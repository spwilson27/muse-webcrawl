Negative symptom

The five "A's" of negative symptoms in schizophrenia.

In psychiatry, negative symptoms are symptoms characterized by the absence or reduction of normal functions, in contrast to positive symptoms, which are excesses or distortions of normal functions (such as hallucinations and delusions). Negative symptoms are a core feature of schizophrenia and are also seen in other psychotic and neurological disorders.

Domains

Negative symptoms are usually grouped into five domains, sometimes called the five A's:

  • Blunted affect — reduced expression of emotion in face, voice and gestures.
  • Alogia — poverty of speech, speaking little or with little content.
  • Anhedonia — reduced ability to experience pleasure.
  • Asociality — reduced interest in social interactions and relationships.
  • Avolition — reduced motivation to initiate and complete goal-directed activities.

Primary and secondary symptoms

Clinicians distinguish primary negative symptoms, considered intrinsic to the underlying illness (in schizophrenia, sometimes grouped as the "deficit syndrome"), from secondary negative symptoms caused by other factors such as medication side effects (sedation, drug-induced parkinsonism), depression, positive symptoms, substance use, or social deprivation. The distinction matters because secondary symptoms may improve when their cause is treated.

Assessment and treatment

Negative symptoms are rated with instruments such as the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS), the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Brief Negative Symptom Scale (BNSS), and the Clinical Assessment Interview for Negative Symptoms (CAINS). They predict poorer functional outcomes than positive symptoms and respond only modestly to antipsychotic medication; psychosocial interventions, cognitive remediation, and treatment of comorbid depression are important components of management.
