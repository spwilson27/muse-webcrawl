Mexican wine

Mexican wine and wine making began with the arrival of the Spanish in the sixteenth century, making Mexico the oldest wine-growing region in the Americas. Although Spanish settlers planted vines widely, colonial authorities restricted production to protect Spanish wine imports, and Mexico's wine industry remained small for centuries. Since the 1980s it has expanded rapidly in quality and output.

Regions

The largest and best-known wine region is the Valle de Guadalupe in Baja California, which together with neighboring valleys produces the great majority of Mexico's wine. Other producing states include Coahuila, home to Casa Madero — founded in 1597 and among the oldest wineries in the Americas — as well as Querétaro, Guanajuato, Aguascalientes, and Zacatecas.

Grapes and styles

Warm, dry climates and cool Pacific breezes shape wines made from Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Tempranillo, Nebbiolo, Syrah, and Grenache for reds, and Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, and Chardonnay for whites. Mexican producers are noted for experimentation, blending varieties less commonly combined elsewhere.

Industry today

Domestic consumption has grown steadily, and Mexican wines now win international awards, though beer and spirits remain far more popular nationally and most wine consumed in Mexico is imported.
