Mound Builders

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monks Mound at Cahokia, Illinois.

The Mound Builders were members of various indigenous cultures of North America who, over a period beginning about 3500 BCE, constructed earthwork mounds for religious, ceremonial, burial, and residential purposes. The first mound-building cultures arose in the lower Mississippi Valley; the tradition later spread through the Ohio and Mississippi river systems and into the Southeast.

Cultures

Major mound-building traditions include the Archaic-period works at Watson Brake and Poverty Point in Louisiana; the Woodland-period Adena and Hopewell cultures of the Ohio Valley, famed for elaborate geometric earthworks and effigy mounds such as Serpent Mound; and the Mississippian culture (c. 800–1600 CE), whose platform mounds supported temples and chiefs' houses at centres like Cahokia — the largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico.

The "lost race" myth

Nineteenth-century American settlers, finding the mounds abandoned, often refused to credit Native Americans with building them, instead inventing a vanished "Mound Builder race" — variously identified with Vikings, Welshmen, Israelites, or Toltecs. Thomas Jefferson, who excavated a burial mound on his Virginia property, argued on the evidence that Native Americans had built them. Systematic work by Cyrus Thomas of the Bureau of Ethnology in the 1880s, published in 1894, conclusively demonstrated that the mounds were the work of the ancestors of living Native peoples.
