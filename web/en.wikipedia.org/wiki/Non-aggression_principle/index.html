Non-aggression principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The non-aggression principle (NAP) is an ethical stance asserting that aggression — the initiation of physical force against persons or their property, or the threat of it — is inherently wrong. It is a foundational axiom of modern libertarian philosophy: legitimate force is only defensive, in response to aggression. From it, libertarians derive opposition to taxation (as coerced taking), conscription, prohibition and most regulation, while accepting voluntary exchange, charity and contract.

The principle has roots in natural-law thought and was codified by Ayn Rand (who rejected the label libertarian) and especially Murray Rothbard, for whom it was the central axiom of a complete legal philosophy: no man may aggress against the person or property of another. Robert Nozick's rights-based theory arrives at similar conclusions through a Kantian side-constraint on using persons as means.

Disputes

Everything turns on definitions: what counts as property (and its just acquisition), what counts as aggression (fraud? pollution? risk imposition?), and what remedies are proportionate. Critics argue the NAP is circular, since property rights themselves require specification and enforcement; that it ignores structural coercion and positive obligations; and that absolute readings yield implausible results on pollution, children and emergencies. Defenders reply that these are problems of application shared with any rights theory, not refutations of the principle itself.
