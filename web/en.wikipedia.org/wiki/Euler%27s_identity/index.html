Euler's identity

FieldComplex analysis
Named afterLeonhard Euler

In mathematics, Euler's identity is the equality

eiπ + 1 = 0

where e is Euler's number, the base of natural logarithms; i is the imaginary unit, satisfying i² = −1; and π is pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. The identity is a special case of Euler's formula from complex analysis, which states that for any real number x, eix = cos x + i sin x; setting x = π gives the identity.

Mathematical beauty

Euler's identity is celebrated as an example of mathematical beauty because it links five fundamental constants — 0, 1, e, i and π — together with the three basic arithmetic operations of addition, multiplication and exponentiation, each exactly once. Richard Feynman called Euler's formula "the most remarkable formula in mathematics".

History

Euler's formula was proved, in a general form, by Leonhard Euler in 1748 in his Introductio in analysin infinitorum, although the identity itself does not appear explicitly as a standalone equation in Euler's published work and may have emerged from the work of earlier mathematicians such as Roger Cotes and Abraham de Moivre.
