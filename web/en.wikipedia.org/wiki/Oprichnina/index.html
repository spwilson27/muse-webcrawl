The oprichnina (Russian: опри́чнина) was a state policy implemented in the Tsardom of Russia by Tsar Ivan IV between 1565 and 1572. Under it, a large portion of the country was carved out as the tsar's separate domain, administered directly by him and policed by a corps of loyal enforcers, the oprichniki, while the remainder — the zemshchina — continued under the traditional boyar institutions. The word derives from the Old Russian oprich, "apart from" or "separate".

Origins

In December 1564 Ivan abruptly left Moscow for Aleksandrov, threatening abdication and blaming treason among the boyars. A delegation begged him to return; he agreed on condition that he be granted absolute power to punish traitors and to create the oprichnina. The policy aimed to break the power of the hereditary aristocracy, confiscating their estates within the oprichnina lands and resettling them elsewhere.

Terror

The oprichniki, dressed in black and riding black horses, with a dog's head and a broom attached to their saddles — symbolising sniffing out treason and sweeping it away — carried out mass arrests, executions and confiscations. The terror culminated in the sack of Novgorod in 1570, in which thousands were killed on suspicion of treasonous contacts with Lithuania. Modern estimates of the total number of victims of the oprichnina range from several thousand to tens of thousands.

Abolition and legacy

The system failed to protect Russia: in 1571 Crimean Tatars burned Moscow while the oprichnina army proved ineffective, and Ivan formally abolished the oprichnina in 1572, forbidding even the use of the word. The policy left the country economically devastated and socially fractured, contributing to the crisis that later erupted in the Time of Troubles.
