Alexandrine

An alexandrine is a line of poetic meter comprising twelve syllables in French verse, or six iambic feet (iambic hexameter) in English verse. The French alexandrine is traditionally divided by a caesura into two hemistichs of six syllables each.

French alexandrine

The alexandrine takes its name from the Roman d'Alexandre, a 12th-century French romance about Alexander the Great written in twelve-syllable lines. It became the dominant meter of French classical tragedy and epic, used by Corneille, Racine, and Molière, and codified in Boileau's Art poétique. The classical alexandrine required a medial caesura after the sixth syllable, strict alternation of masculine and feminine rhymes, and rich rhyme.

Nineteenth-century poets such as Victor Hugo loosened the form, displacing the caesura (the alexandrin ternaire), and the symbolists and modernists treated it ever more freely, though it remains the emblematic line of French poetry.

English alexandrine

In English, an alexandrine is a line of iambic hexameter, most familiar as the ninth, closing line of the Spenserian stanza used by Edmund Spenser in The Faerie Queene. Alexander Pope mocked the form's lumbering quality in his Essay on Criticism: "A needless Alexandrine ends the song, / That, like a wounded snake, drags its slow length along."

Other traditions

Analogous twelve-syllable lines appear in Polish literature (the 13-syllable line of Pan Tadeusz is a near cousin) and in Dutch and German verse, where the alexandrine was the standard line of Baroque drama.
