Grassmann's law

Grassmann's law, named after its discoverer Hermann Grassmann (1809–1877), who published it in 1863, is a dissimilatory sound change in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit. In both languages, if an aspirated consonant (a voiced or voiceless aspirated stop) is followed in a word by another aspirated consonant in the next syllable, the first loses its aspiration.

A textbook example is the Greek word for "hair": nominative thríks versus genitive trikhós. The underlying root had aspirates in both syllables; in the nominative, where the second aspirate was lost before a following s, the first aspirate survives, while in the genitive the first is de-aspirated because the second remains. Similarly, the Greek verb títhēmi "I put" (from reduplicated *thi-thē-mi) shows loss of aspiration in the reduplicating syllable.

Details

In Sanskrit, the law interacts with Bartholomae's law and applies across a similar range of forms, for example in reduplicated perfects and in the verb root dah- "burn". The dissimilation is a classic example of a long-distance phonological process and is frequently discussed in the literature on the Obligatory Contour Principle.

Grassmann's law operated independently in Greek and Sanskrit after the breakup of Proto-Indo-European; parallel innovations in the two branches are not generally taken as evidence of a shared post-PIE development. The discovery of the law famously resolved apparent irregularities in the correspondence of Indo-European aspirates that had troubled earlier comparativists.
