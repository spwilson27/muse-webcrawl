Lobola

Lobola (also lobolo, mahadi, roora, amagugu, or ikhazi) is a customary practice in Southern Africa in which the family of the groom negotiates with the family of the bride on the terms of a marriage, including a payment — traditionally in cattle, nowadays often in cash or a combination — made by the groom's family to the bride's family. It is sometimes loosely translated as "bride price" or "bride wealth", though participants typically reject the idea that it is a purchase: the payment is understood to join the two families, legitimate the marriage and the children of the union, and compensate the bride's family for the loss of her labour and companionship.

Practice

Negotiations are conducted by delegations of elders and are governed by detailed etiquette, fines for breaches of protocol, and set stages: the sending of a letter or emissaries requesting a meeting, the discussion of the amount and composition of the payment, and agreement on payment schedules. The amount varies with region, family status, and the bride's education and employment; it may be paid in instalments, and in customary law a marriage may be recognised before payment is complete. Among the Zulu the related practice is called ilobolo; among the Shona of Zimbabwe, roora; in Botswana, bogadi; in South Africa's Sotho-Tswana groups, mahadi.

Legal status

In South Africa, the Recognition of Customary Marriages Act, 1998 recognises marriages concluded under customary law, of which lobola negotiation is a typical (though not strictly essential) element. Customary marriages must be registered, and spouses obtain equal status and capacity. Similar recognition exists in Zimbabwe and other countries of the region.

Debate

Lobola remains widely practised, including by urban and diaspora families, and is defended as an honoured tradition affirming family bonds and respect. Critics argue that commercialised, inflated amounts can delay marriages, burden couples with debt, and in some interpretations subordinate wives; feminist debate within the region weighs these concerns against the practice's cultural meaning. Surveys show many couples negotiate hybrid forms, and some families set symbolic amounts.
