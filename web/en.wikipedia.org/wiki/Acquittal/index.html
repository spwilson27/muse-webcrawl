Acquittal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An acquittal is a judgment in a criminal trial that the accused is not guilty of the offence charged — a finding that the prosecution failed to prove guilt to the required standard (beyond a reasonable doubt), or that a legal bar to conviction applies. An acquittal is not a declaration of factual innocence; it is a determination that the state's case did not meet the standard the law demands, which is why acquitted defendants may still face civil liability for the same conduct under the lower civil standard (the O. J. Simpson trials, criminal acquittal in 1995 and civil liability in 1997, are the canonical comparison).

Finality and variants

In most common-law systems a true acquittal is absolutely final: the double-jeopardy bar prevents retrial even if new evidence emerges — with narrow modern exceptions (England and Wales since 2003 allow retrial for serious offences on new and compelling evidence, first used in a murder case in 2006; Scotland retains its distinct "not proven" verdict alongside guilty and not guilty, whose reform is periodically debated). Directed acquittals (judgments of acquittal for insufficient evidence, entered by the judge) and partial acquittals on some counts are standard procedural variants. Acquittal rates, and what they signify about charging standards versus jury behaviour, are contested criminological terrain; compensation schemes for the wrongfully accused (distinct from the wrongfully convicted) exist in some jurisdictions.
