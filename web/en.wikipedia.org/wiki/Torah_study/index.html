Torah study

Torah study is the study of the Torah, Hebrew Bible, Talmud, responsa, rabbinic literature, and similar works, all of which are Judaism's religious texts. Although in Judaism the word "Torah" refers to the Five Books of Moses, in this context "Torah" refers to the entire corpus of Jewish religious literature, and the study of the whole is referred to as "Torah study".

Study is traditionally regarded as a religious obligation (mitzvah) in Judaism, derived from verses such as "you shall meditate on it day and night" (Joshua 1:8) and "you shall teach them diligently to your children" (Deuteronomy 6:7). The Talmud states that the study of Torah is equal in weight to all other commandments combined.

Traditional method

The traditional method of study is in pairs, known as chavruta (Aramaic for "friendship"): two students read, translate and debate a text together, sharpening each other's understanding. Study sessions take place in a beit midrash (house of study), and a daily schedule in yeshivot typically divides the day between Talmud in depth (iyun), Talmud survey (bekiut), Jewish law (halakha), and ethical literature (mussar).

Texts studied

  • Chumash — the Five Books of Moses, often with the commentary of Rashi.
  • Tanakh — the whole Hebrew Bible, including Prophets and Writings.
  • Mishnah and Talmud — the core of the Oral Torah; the Babylonian Talmud is the central text of yeshiva study.
  • Midrash — rabbinic homiletic and exegetical works.
  • Halakha — codes such as the Shulchan Aruch and their commentaries, and responsa literature.
  • Jewish philosophy and Kabbalah — works such as Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed and the Zohar.

Annual cycle

The Torah is divided into weekly portions (parashot) read publicly in synagogue over the course of a year, concluding and recommencing on Simchat Torah. Many also follow daily study cycles such as Chitas (Chumash, Psalms, Tanya), the daily Talmud page (Daf Yomi, instituted in 1923, completing the Talmud in about seven and a half years), and the annual cycle of Maimonides' Mishneh Torah.

Modern study

Today Torah study is pursued by Jews of all denominations, in yeshivot, universities (as Jewish studies), adult-education programmes such as Partners in Torah, and online. In recent decades women's Talmud study has become widespread in Orthodox and other communities, with institutions dedicated to advanced Torah study for women.
