Transcendental idealism

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804).

Transcendental idealism is a philosophical system founded by Immanuel Kant in the 18th century. Kant argues that the conscious subject cognises the objects of experience not as they are in themselves, but only the way they appear to us under the conditions of human sensibility. In Kant's words, transcendental idealism holds that "the conditions of the possibility of experience in general are at the same time conditions of the possibility of the objects of experience."

Overview

Kant distinguishes between phenomena (things as they appear to us) and noumena (things in themselves). Space and time are not properties of things in themselves but forms of human intuition; the categories of the understanding (such as causality and substance) structure all possible experience. Objects of knowledge therefore conform to the mind, rather than the mind conforming to objects — a reversal Kant compared to the Copernican Revolution in astronomy.

Kant combined transcendental idealism with what he called empirical realism: within experience, objects are genuinely real and knowable, even though we cannot know them as they are apart from our cognitive constitution. The doctrine was first fully expounded in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781; revised 1787).

Reception

Transcendental idealism became the foundation of German idealism: Fichte, Schelling and Hegel each transformed the doctrine, generally abandoning the unknowable thing-in-itself. Interpretations of Kant's system remain contested, dividing broadly into "two-world" and "two-aspect" readings.

References: Kant, Critique of Pure Reason (1781/1787); Allison, H., Kant's Transcendental Idealism (1983, rev. 2004).
