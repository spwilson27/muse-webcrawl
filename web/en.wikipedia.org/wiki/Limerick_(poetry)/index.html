Limerick (poetry)

Rhyme schemeAABBA
MetreAnapaestic

A limerick is a form of verse, usually humorous and frequently rude, in five lines, predominantly anapaestic metre with a strict rhyme scheme of AABBA, in which the first, second and fifth lines rhyme, while the third and fourth lines are shorter and share a different rhyme. The form appeared in England in the early years of the 18th century and was popularised by Edward Lear in the 19th century.

Form

The standard limerick has long first, second and fifth lines of seven to ten syllables and two shorter middle lines of five to seven syllables. A typical pattern of stresses is da-da-DUM da-da-DUM da-da-DUM. The final line is often a punchline, sometimes repeating the opening line with a twist. A well-known example runs:

There was an Old Man with a beard,
Who said, "It is just as I feared!—
Two Owls and a Hen,
Four Larks and a Wren,
Have all built their nests in my beard!"

History

Verses in limerick form can be found in British literature from the late 18th century, including Mother Goose rhymes. Edward Lear's A Book of Nonsense (1846), written for children, established the form's popularity; Lear's own limericks were gentle and typically ended by repeating the first line. Later writers, among them the anonymous contributors to magazine verse competitions in the early 20th century, developed the witty and bawdy limerick aimed at the last-line surprise. The name of the form is generally taken to derive from the Irish city and county of Limerick, possibly via a parlour game in which such verses were chanted with the refrain "Will you come up to Limerick?"; the precise origin remains disputed.

Variations

Writers such as W. S. Gilbert and Ogden Nash played with the form, and anti-limericks deliberately break its metre for comic effect. The limerick remains a staple of light verse, school playgrounds, and collections of comic poetry.
