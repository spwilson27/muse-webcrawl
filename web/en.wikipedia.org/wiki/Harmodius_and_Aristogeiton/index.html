Harmodius and Aristogeiton

Harmodius (c. 530 – 514 BC) and Aristogeiton (c. 550 – 514 BC) were two ancient Athenian lovers who became known as the Tyrannicides after they assassinated Hipparchus, brother of the tyrant Hippias, in 514 BC. Celebrated in democratic Athens as martyrs of freedom, they were honoured with famous bronze statues in the Agora and with drinking songs (skolia), although the tyranny in fact continued for four more years after their act.

The assassination

Thucydides, correcting the popular tradition, explained that the plot arose from a personal grievance: Hipparchus had courted Harmodius and, rejected, publicly insulted Harmodius's sister by dismissing her from a procession. The conspirators struck during the Panathenaea of 514 BC, killing Hipparchus, but Hippias survived and tightened his rule. Harmodius was killed on the spot; Aristogeiton was captured, tortured, and executed.

Cult and statues

After the expulsion of Hippias (with Spartan help) in 510 BC and the democratic reforms of Cleisthenes, the pair were venerated as founders of Athenian freedom. Antenor made a bronze group of them, the first political statues in the Agora; Xerxes carried it off in 480 BC, and a replacement by Kritios and Nesiotes (477/6 BC) — known today from Roman copies, most famously in Naples — became an icon of democracy. Their descendants received hereditary privileges such as dining rights in the Prytaneum.

Historiography

Thucydides' digression (6.54–59) and the Athenaion Politeia use the episode to correct patriotic myth: the lovers acted from erotic grievance, not political principle, and did not end the tyranny. Modern scholarship treats the cult as a foundational civic myth of the Athenian democracy, whatever the men's motives.
