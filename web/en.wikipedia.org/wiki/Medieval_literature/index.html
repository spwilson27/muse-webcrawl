Medieval literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medieval literature is a broad subject, encompassing essentially all written works available in Europe and beyond during the Middle Ages, roughly the period from the fall of the Western Roman Empire (c. 500) to the beginning of the Renaissance (c. 1500). The literature of this time was composed largely of religious writings, alongside secular works including epic poetry, romance, and chronicle.

Languages

In Western Europe, Latin remained the language of the Church and of scholarship throughout the period, but vernacular literatures flourished: Old English works such as Beowulf; Old Norse sagas and Eddic poetry; French chansons de geste like The Song of Roland and the Arthurian romances of Chrétien de Troyes; German works such as the Nibelungenlied; and Spanish, Italian, Welsh, and Irish traditions. Dante Alighieri's Divine Comedy (c. 1308–1321), written in Tuscan, stands at the transition to Renaissance literature.

Genres

Major genres include saints' lives (hagiography), biblical paraphrase and mystery and morality plays, heroic epic, courtly romance and the lyric poetry of the troubadours and minnesingers, fabliaux, allegory such as the Roman de la Rose, and historical chronicles. In the later Middle Ages, writers such as Geoffrey Chaucer (The Canterbury Tales) and Christine de Pizan achieved wide influence, and devotional works like The Imitation of Christ circulated enormously.

Transmission

Before printing reached Europe in the mid-15th century, texts survived in hand-copied manuscripts, often illuminated, preserved chiefly in monastic and court libraries. Much medieval literature is anonymous, and works were frequently adapted and retold rather than treated as fixed texts.
