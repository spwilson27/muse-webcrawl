Korean Air Lines Flight 007

Korean Air Lines Flight 007 (KE007/KAL007) was a scheduled flight from New York City via Anchorage to Seoul. On 1 September 1983 the Boeing 747-230B was shot down by a Soviet Sukhoi Su-15 interceptor near Moneron Island, west of Sakhalin, after straying deep into prohibited Soviet airspace over the Kamchatka Peninsula and Sakhalin. All 269 people on board were killed, including U.S. Congressman Larry McDonald.

The flight and shootdown

After departing Anchorage, the aircraft deviated progressively north of its assigned route — apparently because the autopilot remained in heading mode rather than following the inertial navigation system — crossing Kamchatka, where Soviet fighters first scrambled, and then Sakhalin. A Su-15 piloted by Major Gennadi Osipovich fired two air-to-air missiles after (he later said) firing warning shots that the airliner could not have seen. The damaged 747 descended and crashed into the sea; no survivors were found.

Aftermath

The Soviet Union initially denied, then admitted the shootdown, claiming the airliner was on a spy mission. The incident sharply worsened Cold War tensions: President Ronald Reagan condemned it as a "crime against humanity" and announced that the Global Positioning System, then military, would be made available for civilian use to prevent future navigational tragedies. After the Cold War, Russia handed over the flight recorders in 1992, and the ICAO's revised 1993 report confirmed the navigation-mode error explanation. The shootdown led to an amendment of the Chicago Convention (Article 3 bis) prohibiting the use of weapons against civil aircraft in flight.
