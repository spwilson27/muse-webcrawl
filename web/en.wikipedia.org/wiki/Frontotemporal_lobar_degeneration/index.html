Frontotemporal lobar degeneration

Frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) is a pathological process characterized by selective atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain. It underlies the clinical syndromes grouped as frontotemporal dementia (FTD), the second most common cause of dementia in people under 65 after Alzheimer's disease. Historically the condition described by Arnold Pick in the 1890s was called Pick's disease, a term now reserved for one specific FTLD subtype.

Classification

FTLD is classified by the abnormal protein found in neuronal inclusions at autopsy. About half of cases are FTLD-TDP, with TDP-43 inclusions; most of the rest are FTLD-tau, including Pick's disease (3-repeat tau Pick bodies), corticobasal degeneration, and progressive supranuclear palsy pathology; a small minority involve FUS and other FET proteins (FTLD-FET).

Clinical presentation

Corresponding clinical syndromes include the behavioural variant of frontotemporal dementia — with personality change, disinhibition, apathy, and loss of empathy — and the primary progressive aphasias, in which language deteriorates first (semantic variant, with loss of word meaning, or nonfluent/agrammatic variant). Onset is typically between 45 and 65 years, noticeably younger than in Alzheimer's disease.

Genetics

Up to about 40% of cases show a family history. The major autosomal-dominant causes are mutations in MAPT (tau), GRN (progranulin, causing FTLD-TDP), and a hexanucleotide repeat expansion in C9orf72, discovered in 2011, which also causes amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

Treatment

No disease-modifying therapy exists; care is symptomatic and supportive, with some use of SSRIs for behavioural symptoms and speech therapy for aphasia. Genetic counselling is offered where a mutation is identified.
