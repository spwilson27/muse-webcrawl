Wave-cut platform

A wave-cut platform, also called a shore platform, wave-cut bench or coastal bench, is the narrow flat area often found at the base of a sea cliff or along the shore of a lake, bay or sea that was created by erosion. Wave-cut platforms are often most obvious at low tide when they become visible as huge areas of flat rock. Sometimes the landward side of the platform is covered by sand, forming the beach, and the platform can only be identified at low tides or when storms move the sand.

Formation

Wave-cut platforms form where waves attack the base of a cliff, cutting a notch through hydraulic action and abrasion. As the notch deepens, the rock above is undercut until it collapses, and the cliff retreats. Repeated collapse leaves behind a gently sloping rock surface, sloping seaward at an angle of usually less than 4 degrees, extending between the high- and low-water marks. Because the platform dissipates wave energy, its growth eventually slows cliff retreat.

Examples

Well-known examples include the platform at Southerndown in South Wales, the platforms around Kaikōura in New Zealand (some uplifted by the 2016 Kaikōura earthquake), and the wide platforms along the coast of south-eastern Australia, including at Kiama in New South Wales. Raised, abandoned platforms above modern sea level — marine terraces — provide evidence of past changes in sea level and land uplift.

Ecology

The flat, intertidal surface of a wave-cut platform provides habitat for algae, limpets, mussels and other shore organisms, and rock pools formed in depressions in the platform support diverse communities exposed at low tide.
