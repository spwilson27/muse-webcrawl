Ferdinand I of Leon (Spanish: Fernando I de León; c. 1015 – 1065), surnamed the Great, was a medieval Iberian monarch of the Jiménez dynasty. Originally count of Castile, he became king of León in 1037 and ruled a combined Leonese-Castilian monarchy that dominated Christian Spain.

Background

Ferdinand was a son of Sancho III, king of Navarre, who had partitioned his dominions among his sons. Ferdinand's marriage to Sancha, heiress of León, and the death of her brother Bermudo III at Tamarón brought him the Leonese throne. His victory over Navarre at Atapuerca (1054) secured Castile's independence from his elder brother's kingdom.

Legacy

Ferdinand extracted tribute from the taifa states, conquered Coimbra, and patronised the church and the pilgrimage road to Santiago de Compostela. His decision to divide his territories among his three sons undid his life's work of unification and led to a decade of fraternal conflict, resolved only when Alfonso VI reunited the crowns in 1072. Ferdinand is buried in the royal pantheon of San Isidoro in León.
