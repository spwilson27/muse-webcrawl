Sex chromosome

A sex chromosome (also called an allosome or heterosome) is a chromosome that participates in sex determination. In humans and most other mammals, sex is determined by the XY system: females have two X chromosomes, males one X and one Y. The presence of the SRY gene on the Y chromosome normally triggers male development. Other groups use different systems — birds and some reptiles and insects use the ZW system, in which females are the heterogametic sex.

The human X and Y

The human X chromosome is about 155 million base pairs long and carries roughly 800–900 protein-coding genes, many unrelated to sex. The Y is much smaller — about 59 million base pairs and some 60–70 genes — including SRY and genes needed for sperm production. The two chromosomes recombine only at small pseudoautosomal regions at their tips. Because males have only one X, recessive X-linked conditions such as haemophilia and Duchenne muscular dystrophy are much more common in males; in females, one X in each cell is largely inactivated (lyonisation), forming a Barr body.

Evolution and disorders

The X and Y evolved from an ordinary pair of autosomes beginning roughly 200–300 million years ago; the Y progressively lost most of its genes. Numerical abnormalities of the sex chromosomes include Turner syndrome (XO), Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), triple X syndrome and XYY syndrome, which are among the commonest chromosomal conditions in humans.
