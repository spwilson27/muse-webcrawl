Lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender (LGBT) rights in the United States have expanded significantly since the late 20th century. Same-sex marriage has been legal nationwide since the Supreme Court's decision in Obergefell v. Hodges (2015), and federal court decisions have established protections against employment discrimination. Many specific policies, however, continue to vary by state, and LGBT rights remain a subject of ongoing political and legal contestation.

History

Early American law criminalised same-sex sexual activity under state sodomy laws. The modern movement is commonly traced to the Stonewall riots of June 1969 in New York City. The American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its list of mental disorders in 1973.

Key legal milestones

  • 2003 — Lawrence v. Texas strikes down remaining state sodomy laws.
  • 2004 — Massachusetts becomes the first state to legalise same-sex marriage.
  • 2011 — "Don't Ask, Don't Tell" repealed; lesbian, gay, and bisexual service members may serve openly.
  • 2013 — United States v. Windsor strikes down section 3 of the Defense of Marriage Act.
  • 2015 — Obergefell v. Hodges legalises same-sex marriage nationwide.
  • 2020 — Bostock v. Clayton County holds that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act protects employees against discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity.
  • 2022 — The Respect for Marriage Act requires federal and interstate recognition of same-sex and interracial marriages.

State variation

States differ widely on issues including adoption, conversion-therapy bans, hate-crime coverage, gender-marker changes on identity documents, access to gender-affirming care for minors, and participation of transgender athletes in school sports. Since 2021, many state legislatures have debated and enacted laws restricting transgender-related healthcare and school policies, several of which have been challenged in court.

Public opinion

Public support for same-sex marriage rose steadily from the 1990s, reaching roughly seven in ten Americans in polls of the early 2020s, though opinion on transgender-related policies is more divided.
