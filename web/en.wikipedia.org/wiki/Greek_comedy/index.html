Greek comedy

Terracotta comic masks from the Greek theatre.

Greek comedy is a genre of ancient Greek drama that, together with tragedy and the satyr play, formed the theatre of classical Athens. Performed at state festivals of Dionysus — the City Dionysia and the Lenaia — comedies entertained audiences with fantasy plots, topical satire, obscenity and slapstick. Ancient scholars divided its development into three phases: Old Comedy, Middle Comedy and New Comedy.

Origins

Aristotle traced comedy to the phallic processions (komoi, revels) of Dionysian festivals and to the iambic invective tradition; the word kōmōidia likely means "song of the revel-band". Comic choruses wearing animal costumes appear on early vases. Comedy was officially added to the City Dionysia in 486 BCE, after tragedy (c. 534 BCE), and to the Lenaia around 440 BCE.

Old Comedy

Old Comedy (c. 486–400 BCE) is embodied by the eleven surviving plays of Aristophanes — The Acharnians, The Knights, The Clouds, The Wasps, The Birds, Lysistrata, The Frogs and others — and by fragments of rivals such as Cratinus and Eupolis. Its typical structure included the agon (contest), the parabasis (in which the chorus addressed the audience directly on the poet's behalf), and fantastic schemes: a private peace with Sparta, a city in the clouds built by birds, a sex strike to end war. Old Comedy attacked named contemporaries — politicians like Cleon, intellectuals like Socrates — with a freedom protected by festival licence and remarkable in a democracy at war.

Middle Comedy

Middle Comedy (c. 400–323 BCE) is known almost entirely from fragments and quotations in Athenaeus. Personal and political satire receded; mythological burlesque, stock characters (the braggart soldier, the cook, the courtesan) and the famous "fish" scenes of the marketplace came to the fore. Poets included Antiphanes and Alexis.

New Comedy

New Comedy (c. 323–263 BCE) shifted to domestic situations: young lovers blocked by stern fathers, clever slaves, lost children and recognitions, ending in marriage. Its master was Menander, whose Dyskolos ("The Bad-Tempered Man") survives complete thanks to a papyrus published in 1958, along with substantial parts of Samia and Perikeiromene. Through the Roman adaptations of Plautus and Terence, New Comedy shaped European comedy of manners, from Shakespeare and Molière to the modern sitcom.

Performance

Comedies required three (later more) actors in padded costumes with grotesque masks and, for male characters, a conspicuous leather phallus, plus a chorus of twenty-four. Competitions awarded prizes to poets and choregoi (sponsors); Aristophanes won first prize at least four times. Plays were performed once, in the open-air Theatre of Dionysus in daylight, before an audience of thousands.
