Emperor Wen of Sui (21 July 541 – 13 August 604), personal name Yang Jian, was the founding emperor of China's Sui dynasty. His reign (581–604), known as the Kaihuang era, reunified China after nearly four centuries of division and laid the institutional foundations inherited by the Tang dynasty.

Rise to power

Yang Jian came from a prominent military-aristocratic family of the northwest. His daughter Yang Lihua was empress to Emperor Xuan of Northern Zhou, and on Xuan's death in 580 Yang Jian became regent for the child Emperor Jing. Within a year he forced the boy's abdication, exterminated the Yuwen imperial clan, and proclaimed the Sui dynasty in 581.

Reunification and reforms

In 589 Sui armies crossed the Yangtze and conquered the Chen dynasty, reuniting north and south for the first time since 316. Wen's government codified law in the Kaihuang Code (583), abolishing many cruel punishments; reformed administration through the Three Departments and Six Ministries system; instituted the equal-field land system and a standardised coinage; and began the examination-based recruitment (the ancestor of the imperial examinations) by ordering officials to recommend candidates for testing. A devout Buddhist, Wen distributed relics and built pagodas across the empire while also patronising Confucian and Daoist establishments. Massive granaries were filled — the surplus he accumulated became proverbial, reportedly lasting decades into the Tang.

Frugal and hardworking, Wen ruled jointly in practice with his formidable wife, Empress Dugu Qieluo; officials called them the "Two Saints."

Death and legacy

Late in his reign Wen deposed his eldest son Yang Yong as crown prince in favour of Yang Guang. He died in 604 at Renshou Palace — later rumours, unproven, blamed Yang Guang, who succeeded as Emperor Yang and squandered the dynasty's strength within fifteen years. Historians nonetheless rank Wen among China's most capable emperors: the Sui reunification, law code, bureaucracy, and early Grand Canal works set the stage for the Tang golden age.
