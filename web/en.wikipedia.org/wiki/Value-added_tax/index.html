Value-added tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A value-added tax (VAT) is a consumption tax assessed on the value added at each stage of production and distribution: businesses charge VAT on sales and reclaim VAT paid on inputs, so the net tax is collected fractionally along the chain while the full burden falls on the final consumer. First implemented nationally in France in 1954 (designed by Maurice Lauré), VAT became the European Community's required indirect tax (first directive 1967) and has spread to more than 160 countries; the United States is the major economy without one, relying on state retail sales taxes instead.

Standard rates range widely (5 per cent in Canada as GST federal component; 17–27 per cent in the EU, where the minimum standard rate is 15 per cent and Hungary's 27 per cent is the world's highest widely-cited rate), with reduced and zero rates for food, books, medicines and exports (zero-rating). Economists praise VAT's revenue-raising efficiency and self-enforcing invoice chain; critics note its regressivity (mitigated in design debates by exemptions or transfers) and fraud vulnerabilities, notably missing-trader (carousel) fraud in the EU.
