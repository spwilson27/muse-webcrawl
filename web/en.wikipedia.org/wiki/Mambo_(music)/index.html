Mambo (music)

Mambo
Stylistic originsSon cubano, danzón, big band jazz
Cultural originsLate 1930s–1940s, Havana, Cuba
Key figuresPérez Prado, Tito Puente, Machito

Mambo is a genre of Cuban dance music pioneered by musicians such as Arsenio Rodríguez, Cachao López and, most famously, Pérez Prado, in the late 1930s and 1940s. The word is of Kikongo origin and the style developed from the nuevo ritmo of the danzón, adding syncopated brass and saxophone riffs over an Afro-Cuban rhythm section driven by clave.

Development

Orestes and Israel "Cachao" López introduced the syncopated final section in the danzón "Mambo" (1938) while playing with Arcaño y sus Maravillas. Pérez Prado, working in Mexico City from 1949, codified the international mambo sound — punchy unison brass, grunted vocal interjections, and hits such as "Mambo No. 5" and "Cherry Pink (and Apple Blossom White)".

The Palladium era

In New York, mambo became the music of the Palladium Ballroom at 53rd and Broadway, the "temple of mambo", where the orchestras of Machito, Tito Puente and Tito Rodríguez — the "Big Three" — played for dancers including a large Jewish, Italian, Puerto Rican and African-American clientele. The mambo craze swept the United States in the early 1950s before giving ground to the cha-cha-chá (which evolved from it) and later to salsa, which drew heavily on mambo arrangements.

Legacy

Mambo rhythms underpin much Latin dance music, and the genre enjoyed revivals through samples and covers, such as Lou Bega's 1999 hit version of "Mambo No. 5". As a dance, ballroom mambo is the parent of modern competitive salsa on-2 dancing.
