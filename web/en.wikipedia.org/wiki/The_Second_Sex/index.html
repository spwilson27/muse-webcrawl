The Second Sex (Le Deuxième Sexe) is a 1949 book by the French philosopher Simone de Beauvoir, one of the founding works of modern feminism and of feminist philosophy. Written in fourteen months and published in two volumes — Facts and Myths and Lived Experience — it asks why woman has been constituted throughout history as man's Other: the inessential, defined relative to man as subject, confined to immanence while men claim transcendence. Its most quoted sentence opens the second volume: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

Argument

Beauvoir surveys biology, psychoanalysis and historical materialism and finds each insufficient: none explains why the female's subordination took the form it did, nor justifies it. Drawing on her existentialism, she argues that no essence fixes woman's lot; rather, civilisation has made femininity — training girls into passivity, narcissism and dependence through childhood, marriage, and the organisation of work and sexuality. The book's second volume follows the female life course from childhood through sexual initiation, marriage, motherhood, prostitution and old age, in prose that mixes philosophy, literature, memoir and reportage. Her conclusion is reciprocal freedom: women's liberation requires economic independence, the transformation of institutions, and men's liberation from the same myths.

Reception and legacy

The book scandalised postwar France (the Vatican placed it on the Index of forbidden books; one critic called its author a nymphomaniac — she replied that the charge told its own story) and sold enormously. The first English translation (1953, by H. M. Parshley) was abridged and philosophically blunted, a distortion corrected only by the complete translation of 2009–2010. The Second Sex supplied second-wave feminism with its analytic framework — the sex/gender distinction it seeded runs through Judith Butler and contemporary theory — and it remains the single most influential feminist book of the twentieth century by most scholarly reckonings, its diagnosis and its limits (its bourgeois French lens, criticised by later and decolonial feminists) alike still debated.
