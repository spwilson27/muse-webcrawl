Rosé

Rosé wine

A rosé (from French rosé, "pink") is a type of wine that incorporates some of the colour from the grape skins, but not enough to qualify it as a red wine. It may be the oldest known type of wine, as it is the most straightforward to make. Rosé wines can be still, semi-sparkling or sparkling, and range from bone dry Provençal styles to sweet blush wines such as White Zinfandel.

Production methods

There are three principal methods. In skin contact (maceration), black-skinned grapes are crushed and the skins remain in contact with the juice for a short period, typically two to twenty hours, before the must is pressed and the skins discarded. In the saignée ("bleeding") method, some pink juice is bled off at an early stage of making a red wine, concentrating the remainder. Blending red and white wine is generally discouraged or prohibited for quality appellations in Europe, though it is permitted for rosé Champagne.

Styles and regions

Provence in southern France is the largest region specialising in dry, pale rosé, mainly from Grenache, Cinsault and Mourvèdre. Tavel produces darker, fuller-bodied rosés. Spanish rosado from Navarra and Rioja, Italian rosato, and New World blush wines broaden the spectrum. Rosé consumption surged in the 2010s, particularly in the United States and France, where it now accounts for a substantial share of still wine sales.
