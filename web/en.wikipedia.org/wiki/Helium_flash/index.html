Helium flash

A helium flash is a very brief thermal runaway nuclear fusion of large quantities of helium into carbon through the triple-alpha process in the core of low-mass stars (between 0.8 and 2.0 solar masses) during their red giant phase. The flash occurs because the helium core is degenerate: its pressure does not increase with temperature, so the usual stellar thermostat that regulates fusion fails, and the reaction rate climbs explosively until temperatures reach roughly 10⁸ K and degeneracy is lifted.

The flash happens deep inside a red giant and is invisible at the surface.

Mechanism

As a star ascends the red giant branch, hydrogen burns in a shell around an inert, contracting helium core. Because the core's electrons are degenerate, contraction heats the core without expanding it. At a temperature near 10⁸ kelvin, triple-alpha fusion ignites. In normal (non-degenerate) matter the heat would expand and cool the gas, throttling the reaction; in degenerate matter the temperature instead soars, the reaction rate (which varies roughly as T⁴⁰) runs away, and within minutes the core's fusion luminosity may briefly rival that of an entire galaxy. The energy released goes into heating and expanding the core, ending degeneracy; the outer envelope actually cools and contracts, and the star leaves the tip of the red giant branch to burn helium steadily in its core on the horizontal branch.

Related events

Analogous degenerate flashes occur in other settings: a "shell helium flash" (thermal pulse) recurs on the asymptotic giant branch, and accreting white dwarfs can undergo carbon flashes en route to Type Ia supernovae. Stars more massive than about 2 solar masses ignite helium gently, without a flash, before degeneracy sets in.
