Moneyball

Moneyball is an approach to building a competitive baseball team based on empirical analysis of baseball statistics — commonly known as sabermetrics — rather than on the conventional wisdom and scouting judgments long used by the sport's establishment. The term entered wide use after the publication of Michael Lewis's 2003 book Moneyball: The Art of Winning an Unfair Game, which chronicled how the Oakland Athletics, one of the poorest teams in Major League Baseball, used the approach under general manager Billy Beane to field a winning team at a fraction of the payroll of rivals such as the New York Yankees.

Background

Under previous general manager Sandy Alderson, the Athletics had already begun exploring objective analysis of in-game activity. When Beane succeeded Alderson in 1997, he inherited a franchise with one of the smallest budgets in baseball. Drawing on the work of statistician Bill James and on analysts such as his assistant Paul DePodesta, Beane emphasized statistics that traditional scouts undervalued — most notably on-base percentage (OBP) — over measures like batting average, stolen bases, and the subjective "tools" grading of amateur players.

The 2002 season

The book's central narrative is the 2002 season. Having lost star players Jason Giambi, Johnny Damon, and Jason Isringhausen to free agency, the A's rebuilt around undervalued players such as Scott Hatteberg, a catcher converted to first base, and relief pitcher Chad Bradford, whose unorthodox submarine delivery scared off other clubs. The team set an American League record with a 20-game winning streak in August and September and won 103 games and the American League West division, before losing in the Division Series to the Minnesota Twins.

Reception and legacy

The 2011 film adaptation Moneyball, starring Brad Pitt as Beane, brought the concept to a mass audience. The term has since been applied well beyond baseball, to any data-driven attempt to find undervalued assets or outperform conventional expert judgment in sports, business, and other fields. Critics noted that the 2002 Athletics also benefited from a star pitching trio — Tim Hudson, Mark Mulder, and Barry Zito — and shortstop Miguel Tejada, whose contributions the book arguably understated.
