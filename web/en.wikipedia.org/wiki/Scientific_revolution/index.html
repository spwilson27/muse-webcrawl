Scientific Revolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Scientific Revolution was a series of events that marked the emergence of modern science during the early modern period, roughly from the mid-sixteenth to the late seventeenth century. Mathematics, physics, astronomy, biology and chemistry were transformed: the Aristotelian and Ptolemaic pictures of nature were displaced by a heliocentric cosmos governed by universal mathematical laws, and new methods — systematic observation, experiment and quantification — became the standards for knowledge of nature. The period is conventionally bookended by Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543) and Isaac Newton's Principia Mathematica (1687).

Background

The Revolution built on medieval foundations: the recovery of Greek and Arabic learning, university scholasticism, and Renaissance humanism, printing, and artisanal knowledge. The printing press spread texts quickly and standardised diagrams and tables, while navigation, artillery and commerce created demand for better astronomy, cartography and mechanics.

Key developments

Astronomy and physics

Copernicus proposed a heliocentric arrangement of the planets. Tycho Brahe's unprecedentedly accurate observations supplied the data from which Johannes Kepler derived his three laws of planetary motion (1609–1619), abandoning circular orbits for ellipses. Galileo Galilei turned the telescope on the heavens in 1609–1610 — finding mountains on the Moon, the satellites of Jupiter, sunspots and the phases of Venus — and laid foundations of the science of motion. René Descartes proposed a mechanical philosophy of matter in motion and analytic geometry. Newton unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics with his laws of motion and universal gravitation, explained Kepler's laws, and co-invented calculus.

Other sciences

Andreas Vesalius reformed anatomy through dissection (1543); William Harvey demonstrated the circulation of the blood (1628). Robert Boyle and others developed experimental chemistry and the air pump; Antonie van Leeuwenhoek and Robert Hooke opened the microscopic world. Francis Bacon articulated a programme of inductive, collaborative inquiry, and new institutions — the Royal Society (1660) and the Paris Academy of Sciences (1666) — together with journals such as the Philosophical Transactions (1665) created a public, cumulative enterprise of research.

Characteristics

Historians commonly emphasise: the mathematisation of nature; the union of theory with hands-on experiment; the "mechanical philosophy" that explained phenomena by matter and motion; and new social forms of science — societies, journals, correspondence networks and priority disputes. The concept itself was popularised by historians such as Herbert Butterfield, who in 1949 called the Revolution the event that "outshines everything since the rise of Christianity."

Historiographical debate

Later scholarship has questioned whether a single, sudden "revolution" occurred: continuity with medieval science, the roles of alchemy, magic and theology in the lives of figures like Newton, and the contributions of artisans, women and non-European knowledge have all been stressed. Many historians nonetheless retain the term for the profound reconfiguration of natural knowledge between Copernicus and Newton, whose consequences fed directly into the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution.
