Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), also commonly referred to as the Steel Seizure Case, is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that limited the power of the President of the United States to seize private property. The Court held that President Harry S. Truman lacked constitutional or statutory authority to seize the nation's steel mills during the Korean War.

Background

During the Korean War, a labour dispute between steel companies and the United Steelworkers threatened a nationwide strike, which the government feared would cripple war production. On 8 April 1952 Truman issued Executive Order 10340, directing Secretary of Commerce Charles W. Sawyer to seize and operate most of the country's steel mills. Unlike earlier wartime statutes, no act of Congress authorised this seizure; Congress had considered and declined to include a seizure provision in the Defense Production Act of 1950.

Decision

By 6–3, the Court affirmed an injunction against the seizure. Justice Black's majority opinion held that presidential power must stem from an act of Congress or from the Constitution itself, and that neither the Commander in Chief Clause nor the executive power authorised the President to make law by taking private property to resolve a labour dispute. The mills were returned to their owners the same day; the steelworkers struck for 53 days before settling.

Jackson's concurrence

The case's most enduring legacy is Justice Robert H. Jackson's concurring opinion, which analyses presidential power in three zones: (1) when the President acts with Congress's express or implied authorisation, power is at its maximum; (2) when Congress has been silent, the President acts in a "zone of twilight" relying on independent powers; and (3) when the President acts against Congress's expressed or implied will, power is at its "lowest ebb" and must be scrutinised closely. This framework has been cited in countless later separation-of-powers cases and is often treated as more influential than the majority opinion itself.
