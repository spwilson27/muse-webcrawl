The Spanish Army (Spanish: Ejército de Tierra, "Land Army") is the land warfare branch of the Spanish Armed Forces, responsible for land-based military operations. It is one of the oldest standing armies in continuous existence, with origins in the forces of the Catholic Monarchs in the late fifteenth century.

History

Under the Habsburg monarchy, Spanish infantry — organised in the famous tercios — dominated European battlefields through the sixteenth and much of the seventeenth century, fighting in Italy, Flanders, France and the Mediterranean, and underpinning a global empire in the Americas, Africa and Asia. Defeats such as Rocroi (1643) marked the gradual eclipse of Spanish land power.

The army fought through the Peninsular War against Napoleon (1808–1814), the Carlist Wars, colonial campaigns in Morocco and the Philippines, and the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), after which it formed the backbone of the Franco regime. The attempted coup of 23 February 1981 prompted sweeping reforms; conscription ended in 2001 and the army became a fully professional force integrated into NATO and EU operations.

Modern structure

Today's army is organised around a headquarters (headed by the Chief of Staff of the Army, JEME) and operational forces including the Land Force command in Seville, with brigades such as the "Rey Alfonso XIII" II Legion Brigade, the "Guadarrama" XII Armoured Brigade and the "Galicia" VII Air Assault Brigade; the Spanish Legion (La Legión), mountain troops, the special operations command and the Regulares remain distinctive components. Spanish soldiers have served extensively abroad, including in the Balkans, Lebanon (UNIFIL), Afghanistan, Iraq, Mali and NATO's enhanced Forward Presence in Latvia.
