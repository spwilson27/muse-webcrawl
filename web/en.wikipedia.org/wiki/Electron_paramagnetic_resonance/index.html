Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) or electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy is a method for studying materials that have unpaired electrons. The basic concepts of EPR are analogous to those of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), but the spins excited are those of electrons instead of atomic nuclei. In a magnetic field, the energy levels of an unpaired electron split (the Zeeman effect); microwave radiation at the resonant frequency is then absorbed, revealing information about the electron's environment.

Theory

Every electron has a magnetic moment and spin quantum number s = 1/2. In an external magnetic field B₀, the electron's magnetic moment aligns either parallel (ms = +1/2) or antiparallel (ms = −1/2) to the field, with an energy difference ΔE = geμBB₀, where ge is the electron's g-factor and μB is the Bohr magneton. Absorption occurs when the microwave photon energy hν matches ΔE. Interactions with nearby magnetic nuclei split the line further (hyperfine structure), providing detailed structural information.

Applications

Because most stable molecules have all electrons paired, EPR is specific to paramagnetic species: free radicals, many transition-metal complexes, defects in crystals, and molecules in triplet states. It is used in chemistry to study reaction mechanisms and radical intermediates, in materials science to characterise defects and dopants, in biology and medicine to probe metalloproteins and oxidative stress, and in radiation dosimetry and archaeological dating.

Instrumentation

A continuous-wave EPR spectrometer consists of a microwave source (often X-band, around 9–10 GHz), a resonant cavity holding the sample, an electromagnet providing a swept field, and a detection system using field modulation and lock-in amplification. Pulsed EPR techniques measure relaxation times and electron–nuclear couplings with high precision, analogous to modern NMR methods.

History

EPR was first observed by the Soviet physicist Yevgeny Zavoisky in Kazan in 1944, working with samples such as copper(II) chloride. The technique was developed independently after the Second World War, aided by wartime advances in microwave (radar) technology.
