Laysan (Hawaiian: Kauō) is one of the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands, located 790 nautical miles (1,460 km) northwest of Honolulu. It comprises a low coral island of about 1,016 acres (4.11 km²) surrounding a shallow hypersaline central lake — one of only a handful of natural lakes in Hawaii. The island is part of the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument and is administered as a wildlife refuge.

Ecology

Laysan is a crucial breeding habitat for seabirds, hosting millions of Laysan and black-footed albatrosses, and is the namesake of the Laysan albatross, the Laysan duck (one of the rarest waterfowl in the world) and the endangered Laysan finch. Hawaiian monk seals haul out on its beaches. The island's native ecosystem was devastated in the early twentieth century by introduced rabbits and by guano mining and feather hunting, which drove the Laysan millerbird and Laysan rail to extinction; restoration efforts since the rabbits' removal in 1923 have allowed much of the native vegetation and birdlife to recover.

History

The island was claimed for the Kingdom of Hawaii in 1857. Guano mining took place from the 1890s, and Japanese feather poachers operated there in the 1900s. In 1909 President Theodore Roosevelt included Laysan in the Hawaiian Islands Bird Reservation. The island is uninhabited except for rotating teams of Fish and Wildlife Service biologists.
