The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is an independent agency of the United States federal government responsible for enforcing federal securities laws, regulating the securities industry and exchanges, and protecting investors. Its mission is to protect investors, maintain fair, orderly, and efficient markets, and facilitate capital formation.

[Image: Headquarters of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission in Washington, D.C.]

History

The SEC was created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 in the aftermath of the Wall Street Crash of 1929, building on the Securities Act of 1933. President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed Joseph P. Kennedy Sr. as its first chairman. Later legislation expanded its authority, including the Investment Company Act and Investment Advisers Act of 1940, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, and the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

Organization

The Commission has five members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for staggered five-year terms; no more than three may belong to the same political party. Divisions cover corporation finance, investment management, trading and markets, economic and risk analysis, and enforcement. Companies offering securities to the public must register and file periodic reports — such as annual Form 10-K and quarterly Form 10-Q filings — available through the EDGAR database.

Enforcement

The Division of Enforcement investigates violations such as insider trading, accounting fraud, and unregistered securities offerings, and can bring civil actions seeking injunctions, disgorgement, and penalties. Criminal cases are referred to the Department of Justice. Notable matters included fallout from the Bernie Madoff Ponzi scheme and cases arising from the 2008 financial crisis.
