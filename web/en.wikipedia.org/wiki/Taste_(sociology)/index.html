Taste (sociology)

[Image: Taste as social distinction: Bourdieu's mapping of cultural preferences to class]

Overview

In sociology, taste is the range of cultural preferences and judgements that individuals and groups display — in art, music, food, dress, manners — and, crucially, the social work those preferences do. The modern study of taste was founded by Pierre Bourdieu's Distinction (1979), a survey-based study of French society arguing that taste is not natural or innocent but acquired through class upbringing (habitus), functions as cultural capital, and classifies the classifier: "taste classifies, and it classifies the classifier".

Theories

Bourdieu distinguished the "taste of necessity" of the working classes, the pretension of the petit bourgeois, and the effortless aesthetic disposition of the dominant class. Earlier, Thorstein Veblen (The Theory of the Leisure Class, 1899) analysed conspicuous consumption; Georg Simmel described fashion's trickle-down. Later research complicated Bourdieu: the "cultural omnivore" thesis (Peterson) found high-status taste marked by breadth across high and low culture rather than snobbery, and digital-era studies examine algorithmic taste-shaping on streaming and social platforms.
