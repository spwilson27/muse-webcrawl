Chemical nomenclature

A systematic name encodes a compound's structure

Chemical nomenclature is the system of rules for naming chemical substances. Ideally, a systematic name unambiguously encodes a substance's composition and structure, so that a chemist anywhere can draw the correct structure from the name alone. The principal authority is the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), whose recommendations are published in reference works such as the "Blue Book" for organic chemistry and the "Red Book" for inorganic chemistry.

History

Early chemical names were unsystematic relics of alchemy — oil of vitriol, aqua fortis, spirit of hartshorn. In 1787 Guyton de Morveau, Lavoisier, Berthollet and Fourcroy published the Méthode de nomenclature chimique, which introduced composition-based names (such as "sulfuric acid") meant to reflect what a substance contained. Berzelius's alphabetic symbols for elements made formulas possible. An 1892 congress in Geneva laid down the first international rules for organic naming, and IUPAC, founded in 1919, has revised and extended the rules ever since.

Types of names

  • Systematic (IUPAC) names — constructed from roots, prefixes and suffixes describing the structure; for example, 2-methylpropane.
  • Retained and trivial names — long-established names kept for convenience, such as water, ammonia and benzene.
  • Compositional names — for inorganic salts and oxides, assembled from the names of the constituents, e.g. sodium chloride, iron(III) oxide.
  • Registry identifiers — CAS Registry Numbers and InChI strings uniquely index substances in databases.

Principles

Organic nomenclature selects a parent structure (usually the longest carbon chain or principal ring), identifies the principal functional group, which determines the suffix (-ol, -one, -oic acid and so on), and adds prefixes and locants for substituents and unsaturation. Stereochemistry is specified by descriptors such as (R)-, (S)- and (E)-/(Z)-. IUPAC distinguishes "preferred IUPAC names" (PINs) for regulatory use from general nomenclature that permits more traditional alternatives. Biochemical and polymer nomenclature are maintained by related commissions, and isotope-modified compounds are named with bracketed labels such as [2H]water.
