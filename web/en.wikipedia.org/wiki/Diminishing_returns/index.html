Diminishing returns

In economics, diminishing returns (also called the law of diminishing marginal returns) is the decrease in the marginal output of a production process as the amount of a single factor of production is incrementally increased, holding all other factors of production equal. According to the law, adding successive units of one input — for example labour — to a fixed amount of another input, such as land or machinery, will at some point yield successively smaller increases in output, and may eventually cause total output to fall.

History

The idea was developed by classical economists including Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot, Thomas Malthus and David Ricardo, largely in the context of agriculture: applying ever more labour and capital to a fixed plot of land produces ever smaller increments of crop yield. Malthus used the principle in his theory of population, arguing that food production grows more slowly than population. The concept became a cornerstone of neoclassical production theory and the theory of the firm's short-run cost curves.

The concept

The law applies in the short run, when at least one input is fixed. As the variable input increases, the marginal product of that input eventually declines — each additional worker, for instance, has less of the fixed capital to work with and may get in the way of others. Three stages are commonly distinguished: increasing marginal returns, diminishing marginal returns (where total output still rises), and negative returns (where total output falls). The point of diminishing returns is where the marginal product curve reaches its maximum.

Examples and applications

Fertiliser applied to a field raises yields strongly at first, then by smaller and smaller amounts, and eventually not at all. In business, the principle explains why adding staff to a late project can delay it further, and why successive rounds of investment in a fixed facility produce declining gains. It is distinct from returns to scale, which describe how output responds when all inputs are increased together, typically over the long run.
