Context principle

The context principle is a principle of the philosophy of language formulated by Gottlob Frege in the introduction to The Foundations of Arithmetic (1884): "never to ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition." Words, on this view, have meaning only as constituents of sentences; the sentence is the smallest unit of language that can be used to say something true or false, and the meanings of its parts are explained by their contribution to the sentences in which they occur.

Role in Frege's philosophy

Frege deployed the principle against psychologism and against asking what numbers "are" in isolation. Instead of seeking a definition of number as an object of intuition, one should fix the sense of sentences in which numerals occur — in particular identity statements of the form "the number of Fs equals the number of Gs" (the strategy later distilled as Hume's Principle). The principle thereby underwrites Frege's logicist claim that truths of arithmetic are analytic: the meanings of numerical terms are determined by the truth conditions of complete propositions.

Influence and debate

The context principle anticipated central ideas of later analytic philosophy: it influenced Wittgenstein's dictum that meaning is use, Quine's holism ("the unit of empirical significance is the whole of science"), and structuralism in the philosophy of mathematics. Philosophers such as Michael Dummett debated whether the principle is compatible with a compositional theory of meaning, in which sentence meaning is built up from word meanings — an apparent tension ("bottom-up" versus "top-down" explanation) that Dummett and others resolved by treating context as explanatory priority rather than temporal or definitional priority.
