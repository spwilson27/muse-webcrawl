Phaedra

In Greek mythology, Phaedra (Ancient Greek: Φαίδρα, Phaídra) was a Cretan princess. She was the daughter of King Minos and his wife Pasiphaë, the wife of Theseus, and the mother of Demophon of Athens and Acamas. She is best known for her doomed love for her stepson, Hippolytus, a story told in Euripides' tragedy Hippolytus and in many later works.

Mythology

Phaedra's brother was the Minotaur, whom Theseus killed in the Labyrinth with the help of her sister Ariadne. Theseus later married Phaedra. In Euripides' Hippolytus (428 BCE), Phaedra falls desperately in love with her stepson Hippolytus, who has devoted himself to Artemis and sworn off love. Aphrodite, offended by Hippolytus's contempt, engineers Phaedra's passion as punishment.

In Euripides' surviving play, Phaedra struggles against her desire and resolves to die rather than dishonour herself; her nurse betrays the secret to Hippolytus, who reacts with horror. Phaedra hangs herself, leaving a letter falsely accusing Hippolytus of assaulting her. Theseus curses his son, calling on Poseidon to kill him; Hippolytus is fatally injured when a monstrous bull from the sea panics his horses. Artemis reveals the truth too late, and father and son are reconciled as Hippolytus dies.

Other versions

In Seneca's Roman tragedy Phaedra, Phaedra openly propositions Hippolytus and, upon rejection, accuses him directly before taking her own life over his corpse. The story was retold by Ovid in the Heroides, by Racine in Phèdre (1677)—considered a pinnacle of French classical tragedy—and in modern adaptations including plays by Sarah Kane (Phaedra's Love) and operas and ballets. In some traditions Phaedra's tomb was shown at Troezen.
