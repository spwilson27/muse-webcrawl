Source rock

In petroleum geology, a source rock is a rock which has generated, is generating, or is capable of generating hydrocarbons. Source rocks are generally fine-grained sedimentary rocks — most commonly organic-rich shales, mudstones, and limestones — that contain sufficient quantities of organic matter (kerogen) which, when buried and heated over geological time, converts to oil and natural gas.

Formation

Source rocks form where abundant organic matter — usually marine or lacustrine plankton and algae — accumulates together with fine sediment under low-oxygen conditions that inhibit decay. During burial, the organic matter is transformed into kerogen, classified in types based on its origin and hydrogen content. With further heating (the "oil window", roughly 60–150 °C), Type I and II kerogens generate liquid oil, and at higher temperatures gas is generated, in processes called catagenesis and metagenesis.

Characterization

Geochemists evaluate a source rock's total organic carbon (TOC), kerogen type, and thermal maturity (commonly measured by vitrinite reflectance). Generated hydrocarbons are expelled from the source rock and migrate upward into porous reservoir rocks, where they accumulate in traps sealed by impermeable cap rocks.

Examples and unconventional resources

Famous source rocks include the Jurassic Kimmeridge Clay of the North Sea, the La Luna Formation of Venezuela, and the Devonian–Mississippian Bakken and Jurassic Haynesville shales of North America. In unconventional "shale oil" and "shale gas" plays, the source rock itself serves as the reservoir, and hydrocarbons are produced directly from it by hydraulic fracturing.
