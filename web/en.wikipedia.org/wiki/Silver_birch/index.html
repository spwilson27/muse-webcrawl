Silver birch

Betula pendula, commonly known as silver birch, warty birch, European white birch, or East Asian white birch, is a species of tree in the family Betulaceae, native to Europe and parts of Asia, though in southern Europe it is only found at higher altitudes. Its range extends into Siberia, China, and southwest Asia in the mountains of northern Turkey, the Caucasus, and northern Iran. It has been introduced into North America, where it is known as the European white birch or weeping birch, and is considered invasive in some states, especially near the Great Lakes.

Description

The silver birch is a medium-sized deciduous hardwood tree that typically reaches 15–25 m (49–82 ft) tall, exceptionally up to 31 m (102 ft), with a slender trunk usually under 40 cm (16 in) in diameter. The bark on the trunk and branches is golden-brown at first, but later turns to white as a result of the cellulose in the outer bark being replaced by betulin; it peels in papery strips. The shoots are rough with small warts, and the triangular leaves have coarsely double-toothed margins. The flowers are wind-pollinated catkins; the small winged seeds (samaras) ripen in late summer.

Ecology and uses

Silver birch is a pioneer species, rapidly colonising open ground, and provides food and habitat for hundreds of insect species. Its timber is used for plywood, furniture, and pulpwood; the sap can be drunk or made into wine and syrup; and the bark has traditionally been used for tanning, writing material, and waterproof coverings. It is widely planted as an ornamental tree, and several weeping and cut-leaf cultivars exist.
