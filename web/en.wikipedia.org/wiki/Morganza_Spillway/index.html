The Morganza Spillway or Morganza Control Structure is a flood-control structure in Pointe Coupee Parish, Louisiana, along the western bank of the Mississippi River, operated by the United States Army Corps of Engineers. It is designed to divert floodwaters from the Mississippi River into the Atchafalaya Basin, protecting Baton Rouge and New Orleans during extreme flood events.

Design

The structure is a concrete weir about 3,906 feet (1,191 m) long with 125 gated bays. When fully opened, it can pass up to 600,000 cubic feet per second (17,000 m³/s) of water westward into the Morganza floodway, which carries the water roughly 35 miles south into the Atchafalaya Basin floodway and ultimately to the Gulf of Mexico. The spillway forms part of the Mississippi River and Tributaries Project, the comprehensive flood-control system authorized after the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927.

Construction and history

Construction of the spillway began in 1939 under the Flood Control Act of 1936 and was completed in 1954. Together with the Bonnet Carré Spillway east of the river and the Old River Control Structure to the north, it gives the Corps of Engineers the ability to regulate how much water continues down the lower Mississippi past Louisiana's major cities.

Operation

The Morganza Spillway has been fully opened only twice in its history. It was first opened during the Mississippi River flood of 1973, when 42 of its 125 bays were raised and it operated for about two months, discharging into the Atchafalaya Basin. It was opened a second time on May 14, 2011, during the record 2011 Mississippi River floods, ultimately using 17 bays and flooding portions of the Atchafalaya Basin to relieve pressure on the levees protecting Baton Rouge and New Orleans. Opening the spillway inundates farmland and some homes in the floodway, whose residents hold flowage easements acknowledging that the land may be flooded when the structure operates.
