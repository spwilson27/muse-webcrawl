Navigability

Navigability describes the extent to which a body of water — a river, lake, canal or strait — can be used for transport by vessels. A waterway is navigable if it is sufficiently deep, wide and free of obstructions such as rapids, shoals, dams or low bridges to allow the safe passage of boats or ships, whether in its natural state or after engineering works.

Physical factors

Key determinants include water depth relative to vessel draft, channel width, current speed, seasonal variation in flow and ice, and hazards such as waterfalls and submerged rocks. Many rivers are navigable only along part of their course: the Nile is interrupted by cataracts, and the Congo River by the Livingstone Falls between Kinshasa and the sea. Engineering — dredging, locks, canals and dams — has made many waterways navigable that were not naturally so; the Saint Lawrence Seaway and the canalised Rhine are major examples.

Legal meaning

"Navigable" also has legal significance. In United States law, navigable waters are subject to federal authority under the Commerce Clause, and the "navigable waters" of the United States are regulated under statutes such as the Clean Water Act and the Rivers and Harbors Act. The classic test, from The Daniel Ball (1871), treats waters as navigable in law when they are used, or susceptible of being used, as highways of commerce. Title to the beds of navigable waters generally passed to the states at statehood under the equal-footing doctrine. Similar public-navigation rights exist in other jurisdictions, often deriving from older public trust principles.
