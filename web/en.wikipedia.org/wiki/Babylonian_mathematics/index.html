Babylonian mathematics

Babylonian mathematics (also known as Assyro-Babylonian mathematics) refers to the mathematics developed by the peoples of Mesopotamia from the time of the early Sumerians through the Hellenistic period, documented mainly on clay tablets in cuneiform script.

Number system

Babylonian numerals used a sexagesimal (base-60) positional system — unique among early civilisations for its place-value character — inherited from Sumerian practice. It underlies the modern division of hours into 60 minutes and circles into 360 degrees. A placeholder for zero appeared only late and a true zero was lacking.

Achievements

Babylonian scribes compiled tables of reciprocals, squares and cubes to perform multiplication and division, solved quadratic equations geometrically, approximated √2 to high accuracy (tablet YBC 7289), and produced "Plimpton 322", a table often interpreted as listing Pythagorean triples over a millennium before Pythagoras. By the Seleucid period, Babylonian astronomers used sophisticated arithmetic schemes to predict lunar and planetary phenomena, later transmitted to Greek astronomy.

Character

The mathematics was strongly problem-oriented, presented through worked examples in scribal schools (the edubba), with general rules embedded in procedures rather than stated abstractly.
