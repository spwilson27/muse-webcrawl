Bioplastic

Utensils made of PLA bioplastic.

Bioplastics are plastic materials produced from renewable biomass sources, such as vegetable fats and oils, corn starch, straw, woodchips, sawdust and recycled food waste. Some bioplastics are also biodegradable, but the terms are not equivalent: "bio-based" describes the origin of the material, while "biodegradable" describes its end-of-life behaviour, and a plastic can be one without the other.

Types

Common bioplastics include polylactic acid (PLA), made by fermenting plant sugars and widely used in packaging, disposable tableware and 3D printing filament; polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs), produced directly by microorganisms; starch blends; cellulose-based plastics such as cellophane and cellulose acetate; and bio-based versions of conventional plastics (bio-polyethylene and bio-PET) that are chemically identical to fossil-based versions but made from renewable feedstocks such as sugarcane ethanol.

Advantages and criticisms

Bioplastics can reduce dependence on fossil resources and may have a lower carbon footprint. Criticisms include competition with food production for agricultural land, the fact that many "biodegradable" plastics degrade only under industrial composting conditions (PLA, for example, requires temperatures near 60 °C), and the risk that bioplastics contaminate conventional plastic recycling streams. Global production capacity remains a small fraction — around one percent — of total plastics production.

Applications

Uses range from food packaging, carrier bags and agricultural films to medical implants (where biodegradability is an advantage) and consumer electronics housings.
