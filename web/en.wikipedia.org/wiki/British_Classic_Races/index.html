British Classic Races

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The British Classic Races are a series of five horse races run during the traditional flat racing season in England. They were inaugurated in the late 18th and early 19th centuries and are restricted to three-year-old thoroughbreds, testing speed, stamina and versatility over distances from one mile to one mile and six furlongs. Restricted by age, each horse can contest the Classics in only one season of its life, which gives the races a unique prestige in the breeding of thoroughbreds.

The St Leger, founded by Anthony St Leger, is the oldest of the five; the Derby, founded by the 12th Earl of Derby with Sir Charles Bunbury — its name settled, legend has it, by the toss of a coin — became the most famous horse race in the world and has given its name to "derby" contests in many other sports and countries.

The Triple Crown

A colt who wins the 2,000 Guineas, the Derby and the St Leger in the same season wins the English Triple Crown. Fifteen colts have achieved the feat, but only one since the Second World War: Nijinsky, in 1970, ridden by Lester Piggott and trained in Ireland by Vincent O'Brien. The fillies' equivalent — the 1,000 Guineas, the Oaks and the St Leger — is the Fillies' Triple Crown, last won by Oh So Sharp in 1985. Since the later 20th century, commercial breeding has favoured speed and early maturity, so few Derby winners now attempt the longer St Leger, and the Triple Crown is rarely contested in earnest.

Influence

The British Classics were widely imitated abroad: many racing nations established their own Guineas, Derby, Oaks and St Leger equivalents, and the pattern of the races remains the standard test for identifying the outstanding stallion prospects of each generation.
