Quirinus

In Roman mythology and religion, Quirinus was an early god of the Roman state. In Augustan Rome, Quirinus was also an epithet of Janus, as Janus Quirinus. His name is thought to derive from the Sabine word quiris, "spear"; he was very likely a Sabine war god absorbed into early Roman religion. Together with Jupiter and Mars, he formed the archaic "Capitoline" precursor known as the Archaic Triad.

Origins and identity

Quirinus was centred on the Quirinal Hill, one of the seven hills of Rome, which bore his name and was originally a Sabine settlement. In historical times Quirinus was identified with the deified Romulus, the founder of Rome, who was said to have been taken up into heaven and revealed to Proculus Julius as the god. This identification is reflected in poetry—Virgil and Ovid treat Romulus-Quirinus as one figure—and in the Julian family's self-presentation: Julius Caesar placed a statue of himself in Quirinus's temple, and Augustus rebuilt the temple magnificently, dedicating it in 16 BC.

Cult

Quirinus was one of only three gods (with Jupiter and Mars) served by his own major flamen, the Flamen Quirinalis, among the most senior priests of Rome. His festivals included the Quirinalia on 17 February, also popularly known as the Feast of Fools, associated with the toasting of spelt grain. As the deity of the Quirites—the Roman citizen body in its civilian capacity—Quirinus represented the community in peace as Mars represented it in war.

Legacy

The name survives in the Quirinal Hill and Rome's Palazzo del Quirinale, long a papal palace and now the residence of the President of Italy. Scholars such as Georges Dumézil interpreted Quirinus as a god of the "third function" (production and the populace) in Indo-European ideology, alongside Jupiter (sovereignty) and Mars (warriors).
