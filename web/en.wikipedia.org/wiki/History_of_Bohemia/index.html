History of Bohemia

The history of Bohemia spans more than a thousand years, from the settlement of Slavic tribes in the 6th century through the Duchy and later Kingdom of Bohemia, Habsburg rule, and the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918. Bohemia, centred on Prague, was one of the most prosperous and politically significant lands of Central Europe for much of the medieval and early modern period.

Early settlement and the Přemyslid duchy

Slavic tribes settled the Bohemian basin around the 6th century, after periods of Celtic (Boii) and Germanic habitation. In the 9th century the region was influenced by Great Moravia. Around 870, Duke Bořivoj I founded the Přemyslid dynasty, accepted Christianity, and built Prague Castle, which became the permanent seat of the Bohemian rulers.

The Kingdom of Bohemia

In 1198 Ottokar I obtained a hereditary royal title, and Bohemia became a kingdom within the Holy Roman Empire. The 13th and 14th centuries were a golden age: silver mining at Kutná Hora enriched the crown, German settlers founded towns, and Charles IV (r. 1346–1378), King of Bohemia and Holy Roman Emperor, made Prague an imperial capital, founding Charles University in 1348 and building the Charles Bridge and much of the New Town.

The Hussite Wars

The execution of the reformer Jan Hus at Constance in 1415 ignited the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), in which Bohemian Hussite armies under commanders such as Jan Žižka repeatedly defeated imperial crusades. The wars ended with the Compactata of Basel, which permitted communion in both kinds in Bohemia.

Habsburg rule

In 1526 Ferdinand I of Habsburg was elected King of Bohemia. Tensions between the Protestant estates and the Catholic dynasty culminated in the Defenestration of Prague in 1618, which began the Thirty Years' War. After the defeat of the Bohemian estates at the Battle of White Mountain in 1620, Bohemia was forcibly re-Catholicised and integrated more tightly into the Habsburg monarchy, remaining there until 1918.

National revival and modern era

The 19th century saw a Czech National Revival, with scholars such as Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann reviving the Czech language and historians such as František Palacký reinterpreting the national past. In October 1918 Bohemia became the core land of the new Czechoslovak Republic. After the Second World War and the communist period (1948–1989), Bohemia became part of the independent Czech Republic on 1 January 1993.
