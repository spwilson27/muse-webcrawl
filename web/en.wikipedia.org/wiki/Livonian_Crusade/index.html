Livonian Crusade
Datec. 1198 – 1290
LocationLivonia (modern Latvia and Estonia)
CombatantsGerman and Danish crusaders; Livonian Brothers of the Sword; Teutonic Order — vs. Livs, Estonians, Latgalians, Semigallians, Curonians and allies
ResultCrusader victory; creation of Terra Mariana

The Livonian Crusade was the conquest and Christianisation of the lands of modern Latvia and Estonia by German and Danish crusaders during the Northern Crusades, conventionally dated from Pope Celestine III's call in 1198 to the suppression of the last pagan resistance around 1290. It resulted in the confederation of ecclesiastical and military-order states known as Terra Mariana.

Background

From the 1180s the Augustinian monk Meinhard of Segeberg missionised among the Livs at the mouth of the Daugava River, building a church and fort at Ikšķile. His successor Berthold was killed in battle in 1198, prompting a crusade proclamation. In 1201 Bishop Albert of Riga founded the city of Riga and in 1202 the military order of the Livonian Brothers of the Sword to provide year-round force.

Conquest

Crusading armies, recruited each summer in northern Germany, subdued the Livs and Latgalians along the Daugava and Gauja rivers, then turned on the Estonian counties. Denmark joined from 1219, when Valdemar II landed at Lindanise and founded Tallinn (the legend of the Dannebrog falling from the sky dates to this campaign). The Sword Brethren were crushed by the Samogitians and Semigallians at the Battle of Saule (1236) and their remnants were absorbed into the Teutonic Order as its Livonian branch.

Resistance and conclusion

Conquest proceeded through cycles of submission, baptism, and revolt — notably the long Semigallian resistance and the Estonian St. George's Night Uprising of 1343, which lay outside the conventional period. Saaremaa (Ösel) resisted into the 1260s. By about 1290 the last Semigallian strongholds had fallen and the crusade was complete, leaving a German-speaking landowning and clerical elite ruling over Latvian and Estonian peasantries for centuries.
