In scientific research and statistical inference, the null hypothesis (often denoted H0) is the claim that there is no relationship between two measured phenomena, or no association among groups. It is the default position that any observed difference or effect is due to random variation alone.

A statistical hypothesis test evaluates the data against the null hypothesis: if the observed data would be sufficiently improbable were the null true (as measured by the p-value relative to a chosen significance level), the null is rejected in favour of an alternative hypothesis. Rejecting the null does not prove the alternative, and failing to reject the null does not prove the null — the test is asymmetric, like a criminal trial's presumption of innocence.

History

The framework was developed in the early 20th century by Ronald Fisher, who introduced significance testing, and by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, who formalised the competing null/alternative structure with Type I and Type II errors. Modern practice is largely a hybrid of the two approaches.

Errors

A Type I error is rejecting a true null hypothesis (a false positive), with probability bounded by the significance level α. A Type II error is failing to reject a false null (a false negative); its probability, β, relates to the statistical power of the test, 1 − β. Choice of the null matters: researchers typically place the claim they hope to establish in the alternative hypothesis, so that the null represents the sceptical position of "no effect".

Criticism

Routine null-hypothesis significance testing has been widely criticised for encouraging binary thinking, for the common misinterpretation of p-values as the probability that the null is true, and for contributing to publication bias and the replication crisis in fields such as psychology.
