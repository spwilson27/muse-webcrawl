Statistical significance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In statistical hypothesis testing, a result is said to be statistically significant when a test statistic is observed beyond the critical value at a pre-specified significance level, meaning the result is unlikely to have occurred under the null hypothesis. The term was popularised by R. A. Fisher and is conventionally associated with a p-value below 0.05.

Concept

A researcher chooses a significance level α — the probability of a Type I error, i.e. rejecting a true null hypothesis — before analysing the data. Common choices are 0.05, 0.01 and 0.001. If the computed p-value is less than α, the result is declared statistically significant. Significance says nothing directly about the magnitude or practical importance of an effect: a trivially small effect can be significant in a huge sample, while a large effect may fail to reach significance in a small sample.

History

The underlying idea can be traced to John Arbuthnot's 1710 analysis of sex ratios at birth. Fisher introduced the term "significance" and the 5% convention in the 1920s. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson developed the alternative decision-theoretic framework of fixed levels, power, and Type I/II errors; textbook practice later merged the two approaches.

Role in science

Significance testing is entrenched in medicine, psychology and the social sciences, where journal publication has historically depended on significant results — a practice implicated in publication bias and the replication crisis. Statisticians increasingly recommend reporting effect sizes with confidence intervals, pre-registration of analyses, and treating significance as one piece of evidence rather than a verdict.
