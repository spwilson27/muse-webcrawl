In statistics, an effect size is a quantitative measure of the magnitude of an experimental effect or relationship. Unlike statistical significance (a p-value), which depends on sample size, an effect size describes the size of a phenomenon itself, and is therefore essential for interpreting practical importance and for meta-analysis.

Measures

Common effect sizes include Cohen's d, the difference between two means divided by a pooled standard deviation; the correlation coefficient r for relationships; the odds ratio and relative risk for binary outcomes; and eta-squared or partial eta-squared for the proportion of variance explained in ANOVA. Jacob Cohen's rough conventions (small 0.2, medium 0.5, large 0.8 for d) are widely quoted but are meant only as last-resort guides.

Interpretation

Reporting standards in medicine, psychology and education increasingly require effect sizes with confidence intervals alongside significance tests, because a trivial effect can be highly significant in a huge sample while a large effect may miss significance in a small one. Meta-analyses combine standardised effect sizes across studies to estimate an overall effect.
