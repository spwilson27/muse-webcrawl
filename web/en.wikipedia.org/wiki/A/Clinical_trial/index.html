Clinical trial

Clinical trial
TypeBiomedical / health-related research study
PurposeEvaluate safety and efficacy of interventions

Clinical trials are prospective biomedical or behavioural research studies on human participants designed to answer specific questions about biomedical or behavioural interventions, including new treatments (such as novel vaccines, drugs, dietary choices, dietary supplements, and medical devices) and known interventions that warrant further study and comparison.

Phases

Trials of new drugs are commonly classified into four phases:

Phase I

First testing in a small group of healthy volunteers (or, for some conditions, patients) to assess safety, dosage range and side effects.

Phase II

The treatment is given to a larger group of people with the target condition to evaluate efficacy and further study safety.

Phase III

Large-scale, often randomised and multicentre, confirmatory studies comparing the intervention with standard treatment or placebo. Regulators typically require successful Phase III results for marketing approval.

Phase IV

Post-marketing surveillance studies conducted after approval to monitor long-term effects and performance in the general population.

Design

The randomised controlled trial — in which participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control groups, often with blinding of participants and investigators — is considered the most reliable form of scientific evidence for evaluating interventions. Trials are governed by a pre-specified protocol, require informed consent, and are monitored by institutional review boards and data safety monitoring boards. Results are reported according to standards such as the CONSORT statement, and most trials must be registered publicly before enrolling participants.

History

James Lind's 1747 scurvy experiment aboard HMS Salisbury is often cited as a prototype controlled trial. Modern regulation followed tragedies such as the thalidomide disaster, which led to requirements for proof of efficacy in the United States in 1962.
