Informed consent

Informed consent
FieldMedical ethics; research ethics; law
Key documentsNuremberg Code (1947); Declaration of Helsinki (1964); Belmont Report (1979)
ElementsCapacity, disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, authorisation

Informed consent is a process by which a person, having received adequate information about a medical treatment or research study — its nature, benefits, risks and alternatives — voluntarily agrees to take part. It is a legal and ethical requirement in medicine and human-subject research, rooted in respect for personal autonomy.

Elements

Valid consent generally requires that the person have decision-making capacity, that material information be disclosed (what a reasonable person would want to know), that the person understand it, and that the decision be voluntary and documented — usually by signing a consent form after discussion with the clinician or researcher. In research, consent is ongoing: participants may withdraw at any time without penalty. Special rules govern children, people lacking capacity, and emergency treatment.

History

The doctrine emerged from early twentieth-century case law on bodily autonomy and was decisively shaped by the Nuremberg Code (1947), written after Nazi medical experiments, and the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964). The US Belmont Report (1979) framed it as the application of respect for persons, and modern regulations — from the Common Rule to the EU's research and data-protection law — specify its content and documentation.
