Replication crisis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Chart summarising replication rates reported by large-scale reproducibility projects]

The replication crisis (also called the reproducibility crisis) is an ongoing methodological crisis in which the results of many scientific studies have proven difficult or impossible to reproduce. Because reproducibility is a defining feature of the scientific method, the failure of published findings to replicate — particularly in psychology, medicine, economics, and other social and life sciences — has prompted widespread concern about the reliability of the scientific literature and debate about how research is conducted, funded, and published.

Background

Concerns about irreproducible results are as old as modern statistics, but the crisis acquired its name in the early 2010s, when several high-profile failures converged. In 2005, John Ioannidis published the influential essay "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False", arguing on statistical grounds that small sample sizes, small effect sizes, flexibility in analysis, and bias combine to make most positive findings likely to be false positives. In 2011, a paper reporting evidence for extrasensory perception passed peer review at a leading social psychology journal, galvanising criticism of standard methods, and in the same year Daryl Bem's critics and others highlighted questionable research practices. Fraud cases, such as that of social psychologist Diederik Stapel, added to the sense of urgency.

Large-scale replication projects

The Open Science Collaboration's 2015 project attempted to replicate 100 studies from three prominent psychology journals. While 97% of the original studies reported statistically significant results, only about 36% of the replications did, and the average effect size was roughly halved. Similar projects in experimental economics and in social science research published in Nature and Science found replication rates of roughly 60%. In preclinical cancer biology, an industry-led effort reported successfully replicating only a minority of "landmark" findings.

Causes

Frequently cited causes include:

  • Low statistical power — underpowered studies both miss real effects and inflate the ones they find (see statistical power).
  • Publication bias — journals preferentially publish positive, novel results, while null results remain in the "file drawer".
  • P-hacking — flexible analytic choices (outcome switching, optional stopping, selective reporting) that manufacture significance, inflating Type I error rates.
  • HARKing — hypothesizing after the results are known, presenting exploratory findings as confirmatory.
  • Observer bias and lack of blinding in measurement.
  • Incentives — career rewards for quantity and novelty of publications rather than rigour.

Responses

Proposed and adopted reforms include pre-registration of study designs and analysis plans, registered reports (peer review before data collection), open data and open materials, larger sample sizes, replication studies funded and published in their own right, and improved statistical education. Some researchers caution that replication failure does not always imply the original finding was false, pointing to hidden moderators and contextual sensitivity.
