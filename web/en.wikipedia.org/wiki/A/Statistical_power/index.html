Statistical power

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Power curves showing probability of detecting an effect as sample size increases]

The statistical power of a binary hypothesis test is the probability that the test correctly rejects the null hypothesis (H0) when a specific alternative hypothesis (H1) is true. It is commonly denoted by 1 − β, where β is the probability of a Type II error — failing to detect an effect that is really there. A study with high power has a low chance of producing a false negative.

Power = P(reject H0 | H1 is true) = 1 − β

Factors affecting power

Four quantities are interrelated: sample size, effect size, the significance criterion (α), and power. Given any three, the fourth can be computed. Power increases when:

  • the sample size increases;
  • the true effect size is larger;
  • measurement precision improves (reducing noise);
  • the significance threshold α is made less strict (at the cost of more Type I errors).

By convention, researchers often design studies for 80% power (β = 0.20) at α = 0.05, although higher power — 90% or more — is recommended where feasible and where missing an effect would be costly.

Power analysis

A power analysis is usually performed before a study (a priori) to determine the minimum sample size needed to detect an effect of a given size with a given degree of confidence. Such calculations are routinely required by funders and ethics boards, since both underpowered studies (which waste resources on uninformative tests) and grossly overpowered studies (which may expose more participants than necessary) raise concerns.

Underpowered studies and the replication crisis

Chronically low power is regarded as one of the systemic causes of the replication crisis. A counterintuitive consequence of low power is that statistically significant results from underpowered studies tend to overestimate the true effect size (the "winner's curse"), and a lower proportion of significant findings from such studies are true positives. Jacob Cohen's surveys of the psychological literature from the 1960s onward repeatedly found median power well below recommended levels.
