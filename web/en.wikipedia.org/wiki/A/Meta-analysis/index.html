Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis is a statistical analysis that combines the results of multiple scientific studies addressing the same question. By pooling data, it can yield a more precise estimate of an effect than any single study, and to investigate whether effects differ across contexts, populations or study designs.

History

Statistical methods for combining results date to Karl Pearson (1904) and later work by William Cochran and Frank Yates. The term "meta-analysis" was coined by psychologist Gene Glass in 1976. The approach became central to evidence-based medicine, and organisations such as the Cochrane Collaboration (founded 1993) have produced thousands of systematic reviews incorporating meta-analyses.

Methods

A meta-analysis typically follows a systematic review: a pre-specified protocol, comprehensive searching, reproducible inclusion criteria, and assessment of each study's risk of bias. Effect sizes from individual studies are combined, commonly either under a fixed-effect model or a random-effects model that allows for between-study heterogeneity, often estimated by the DerSimonian–Laird method or more modern estimators.

Interpretation

Results are usually displayed in a forest plot. Heterogeneity is described with statistics such as Cochran's Q and I², and publication bias — the tendency for positive results to be published more readily — is examined with funnel plots and tests such as Egger's regression. Poorly conducted meta-analyses can mislead, giving rise to the "garbage in, garbage out" criticism; reporting standards such as PRISMA aim to mitigate this.
