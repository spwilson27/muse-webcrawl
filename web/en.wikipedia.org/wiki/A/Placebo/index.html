A placebo (Latin for "I shall please") is a substance or treatment which is designed to have no therapeutic value. Common placebos include inert tablets (such as sugar pills), inert injections (such as saline), sham surgery, and other procedures designed to mimic a genuine medical intervention without delivering its active component.

Despite containing no active medicine, placebos can produce measurable physiological changes in some people — a phenomenon known as the placebo effect. Reported effects include changes in heart rate, blood pressure, pain perception, fatigue, and the symptoms of conditions such as depression, anxiety, irritable bowel syndrome, and Parkinson's disease. The effect is attributed to the patient's belief in the treatment, to classical conditioning, and to the therapeutic context — the ritual of care, attention from clinicians, and expectation of relief — rather than to any pharmacological action of the placebo itself.

Use in research

Placebos are a cornerstone of clinical trial design. In a placebo-controlled trial, participants are randomly assigned to receive either the experimental treatment or an indistinguishable placebo, ideally under double-blind conditions so that neither participants nor assessors know who received what. This controls for the placebo effect, for observer bias, and for the natural course of disease, allowing researchers to isolate the treatment's true effect. An adequately sized trial also depends on statistical power calculations made in advance.

Mechanisms

Research using brain imaging has shown that placebo analgesia involves real neurobiological pathways: expectation of pain relief can trigger release of endogenous opioids and dopamine. Placebo responses in Parkinson's disease have been shown to increase dopamine release in the striatum. Conditioning can produce placebo effects even without conscious expectation, for example when a neutral stimulus has previously been paired with an active drug.

Nocebo effect

The counterpart of the placebo effect is the nocebo effect, in which negative expectations cause a treatment — or an inert substance — to produce harmful or unpleasant effects, such as increased pain or side effects mirroring those a patient has been warned about.

Ethics

The use of placebos raises ethical questions, particularly when effective treatments already exist, and when placebos are prescribed deceptively in clinical practice. Some studies of "open-label placebos" — where patients know they are receiving an inert pill — have nevertheless reported symptom improvements, complicating assumptions that deception is necessary.
