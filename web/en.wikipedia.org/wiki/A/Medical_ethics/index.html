Medical ethics

Medical ethics is an applied branch of ethics which analyses the practice of clinical medicine and related scientific research. It rests on values that practitioners can refer to in cases of confusion or conflict, and is closely related to medical law and to bioethics, the broader study of moral questions arising in the life sciences.

History

Codes of medical conduct date back to antiquity, most famously the Hippocratic Oath of ancient Greece. Modern medical ethics was reshaped by the exposure of Nazi medical experiments and the Nuremberg Code of 1947, followed by the World Medical Association's Declaration of Geneva (1948) and Declaration of Helsinki (1964). Scandals such as the Tuskegee syphilis study further spurred institutional review boards and codified rules for human-subjects research.

The four principles

A widely taught framework, "principlism" associated with Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, identifies four prima facie principles: autonomy (respecting patients' decisions), beneficence (acting in the patient's best interest), non-maleficence ("first, do no harm"), and justice (fair distribution of resources and treatment).

Topics

Major subjects include informed consent, confidentiality, truth-telling, end-of-life care and assisted dying, abortion, allocation of scarce resources, organ transplantation, genetic testing, conscientious objection, and conflicts of interest. Hospital ethics committees and institutional review boards apply these principles in practice and in research oversight.
