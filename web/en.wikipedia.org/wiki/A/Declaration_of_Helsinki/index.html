Declaration of Helsinki

The Declaration of Helsinki is a set of ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects, developed by the World Medical Association (WMA). It was originally adopted in Helsinki, Finland, in June 1964 and has been amended repeatedly, most recently in 2024.

Background

The Declaration built upon the Nuremberg Code (1947), issued after the Nazi medical experiments trials, answering the Code's perceived rigidity with a fuller framework addressed to physicians. It became the foundational international document of research ethics and a reference point for national laws and regulations.

Principles

Core requirements include that research be justified by a careful assessment of risks and benefits; that the welfare of the individual subject take precedence over the interests of science and society; that participation be based on freely given informed consent; that protocols receive prior review by an independent ethics committee; and that special protection be given to vulnerable groups. Later revisions addressed placebo use, post-trial access to interventions, research in resource-poor settings, and the registration and publication of trial results.

Influence and criticism

Although legally non-binding, the Declaration is incorporated by reference into many national regulations and the policies of journals, funders and professional bodies. Debates over successive revisions — especially the standards for placebo-controlled trials and research in developing countries — have occasionally led regulators, such as the US Food and Drug Administration, to refer instead to the ICH Good Clinical Practice guidelines.
