Blind experiment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In a blind or blinded experiment, information which may influence the participants of the experiment is withheld until after the experiment is complete. Good blinding can reduce or eliminate experimental biases that arise from a participants' expectations, observer's effect on the participants, observer bias, confirmation bias, and other sources.

Types

Single-blind

In a single-blind experiment, the participants do not know which group (treatment or control) they belong to, but the experimenters do. This is standard in trials where the treatment cannot easily be disguised from the researchers administering it.

Double-blind

In a double-blind experiment, neither the participants nor the experimenters know who is receiving a particular treatment. This procedure is used to prevent bias in research results, and double-blind studies are regarded as the gold standard of clinical trials, particularly for interventions with subjective outcomes such as pain relief.

Triple-blind

In a triple-blind experiment, in addition to the participants and experimenters, the analysts who interpret the data are also kept unaware of group assignments until the analysis is complete.

Unblinding

Unblinding is the revelation of group assignments. In clinical trials it may occur prematurely for safety reasons — for example, if a participant suffers a serious adverse event — or by accident when a treatment's characteristic side effects reveal its identity. Trials pre-specify procedures for emergency unblinding.

History

Blinded assessment dates to the 1780s, when the French royal commission including Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier used blindfolds to debunk Franz Mesmer's claims about "animal magnetism". Blind wine tastings and double-blind drug trials became standard methodology in the twentieth century.
