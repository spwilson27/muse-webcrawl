P-value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In null-hypothesis significance testing, the p-value is the probability of obtaining test results at least as extreme as the result actually observed, under the assumption that the null hypothesis is correct. A very small p-value means that such an extreme observed outcome would be very unlikely under the null hypothesis.

Definition

Formally, if T is a test statistic and tobs its observed value, the p-value is P(T ≥ tobs | H0) for a one-sided test (with the appropriate tail and two-sided variant depending on the test). If the p-value is below a pre-chosen significance level α — conventionally 0.05 — the result is declared statistically significant and the null hypothesis is rejected.

Interpretation and misinterpretation

The p-value is frequently misinterpreted. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability that the observed result occurred by chance alone, nor a measure of the size or importance of an effect. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a formal statement on p-values, listing six principles for their proper use and warning that a p-value near 0.05 provides only weak evidence against the null hypothesis.

History

The concept was formalised by Karl Pearson in 1900 in connection with the chi-squared test and popularised by R. A. Fisher in the 1920s in Statistical Methods for Research Workers, which promoted the 0.05 threshold. The modern practice combines Fisher's approach with the Neyman–Pearson framework of fixed significance levels and alternative hypotheses.

Criticism

Critics argue that the dichotomisation of results at p < 0.05 contributes to publication bias, "p-hacking", and the replication crisis in psychology and other fields. Alternatives and complements include confidence intervals, effect sizes, and Bayesian methods. In 2017 hundreds of researchers proposed redefining the default threshold for new discoveries to 0.005.
