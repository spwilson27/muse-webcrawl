Nuremberg Code

The Nuremberg Code is a set of research ethics principles for human experimentation created by a US military tribunal as one result of the Nuremberg trials of Nazi war criminals at the end of the Second World War, in particular the "Doctors' Trial" (United States v. Karl Brandt, 1946–47).

Background

Twenty-three Nazi physicians and officials were tried for experiments conducted on concentration-camp prisoners, including freezing, infection and poison experiments. Sixteen were convicted and seven executed. In its judgment of August 1947 the tribunal set out ten points defining "permissible medical experiments".

The ten points

The Code's first and most famous requirement is that the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential. Other points require that experiments yield fruitful results for the good of society, be based on prior animal work, avoid unnecessary suffering, exclude experiments where death or disabling injury is expected, keep risks proportional to the humanitarian importance of the problem, and be conducted only by scientifically qualified persons. Subjects must be free to withdraw, and investigators must stop if continuation seems likely to cause injury.

Legacy

The Code was slow to be adopted in practice and was sometimes dismissed as "a good code for barbarians". It nevertheless laid the groundwork for the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and for modern regulations on informed consent and human-subjects research worldwide.
