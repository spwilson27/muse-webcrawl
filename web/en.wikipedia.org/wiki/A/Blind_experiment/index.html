Blind experiment

Blind experiment
FieldExperimental design; clinical research
VariantsSingle-blind, double-blind, triple-blind
PurposePrevent bias from participants' or experimenters' expectations

A blind experiment (or blinded trial) is a scientific experiment in which information that could bias the participants is withheld ("masked") until after the results are recorded. Blinding is used to prevent the placebo effect, observer bias and demand characteristics from distorting outcomes.

Types

In a single-blind experiment, participants do not know whether they are receiving the experimental treatment or a control (such as a placebo). In a double-blind experiment, neither the participants nor the experimenters interacting with them and assessing outcomes know the assignments, which are held by a third party until analysis. "Triple-blind" designs additionally mask the data analysts. Blinding is a cornerstone of the randomised controlled trial, regarded as the gold standard for testing medical interventions.

Limitations

Blinding is not always feasible — surgical techniques, psychotherapy and interventions with obvious side effects are hard to mask — and can break down ("unblinding") when effects reveal the allocation. Related techniques, such as blind data analysis, are also used in physics, where analysts finalise their methods before seeing the final values in order to avoid tuning them to expectations.
