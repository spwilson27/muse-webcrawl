Christian marriage

Christian marriage is a marriage joined in faith and in accordance with the teachings of Jesus Christ and the Christian church. For most Christians, marriage is a sacred institution instituted by God; the Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church and some others regard matrimony (holy matrimony) as a sacrament, while most Protestants consider it a covenant and ordinance rather than a sacrament.

Biblical foundations

Christian teaching draws on Genesis ("the two shall become one flesh"), the wedding at Cana, and Ephesians 5, which presents marriage as an image of Christ's union with the Church. The purposes traditionally taught include companionship, procreation, mutual sanctification and the prevention of sexual immorality.

Practices and denominational differences

Churches vary widely. The Catholic Church teaches that a valid sacramental marriage is indissoluble and may be ended only by annulment; Orthodox churches permit divorce and remarriage under economia (pastoral discretion); most Protestant churches permit divorce and remarriage on biblical grounds such as adultery or abandonment. Vows, rings, parental consent, the role of clergy versus the couple as ministers of the sacrament, and attitudes toward same-sex marriage all differ among traditions.

History

Early Christians married under Roman law; the church gradually assumed regulation of marriage in the Middle Ages, requiring banns, clerical blessing and registration, a process regularised by the Council of Trent (1563) and, in England, by Hardwicke's Marriage Act (1753). Civil marriage later became the universal legal form, with religious ceremonies often performed alongside.
