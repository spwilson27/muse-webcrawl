Capital punishment in the United Kingdom

Capital punishment in the United Kingdom — the judicial execution of convicted offenders — was abolished for murder in Great Britain in 1965 and in Northern Ireland in 1973, and was removed entirely from statute law in 1998. The last executions in the United Kingdom took place on 13 August 1964, when Peter Anthony Allen and Gwynne Owen Evans were hanged simultaneously at Walton Prison, Liverpool, and Strangeways Prison, Manchester, for the murder of John West.

History

Hanging was the traditional method of execution in Britain for centuries, and under the 18th-century "Bloody Code" more than 200 offences carried the death penalty. Reforms in the 19th century steadily reduced the capital crimes, and the Judgement of Death Act 1823 and subsequent statutes confined the penalty largely to murder and treason. Public executions ended in 1868. In the 20th century the cases of Timothy Evans (executed 1950, later pardoned) and Derek Bentley (executed 1953, conviction quashed 1998) intensified public and parliamentary opposition.

Abolition

The Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965 suspended capital punishment for murder in England, Scotland and Wales for an initial five years; Parliament made abolition permanent in 1969. The death penalty remained theoretically available for treason and a few other offences until the Crime and Disorder Act 1998, and the Human Rights Act 1998, which incorporated the European Convention on Human Rights, completed abolition. Repeated free votes in Parliament have consistently rejected restoring the death penalty.
