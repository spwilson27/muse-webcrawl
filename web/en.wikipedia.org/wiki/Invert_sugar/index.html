Invert sugar

Invert sugar
CompositionEqual parts glucose and fructose (from hydrolysed sucrose)
ProductionAcid or enzymatic (invertase) hydrolysis of sucrose
UsesConfectionery, baking, beverages, brewing

Invert sugar, also known as invert sugar syrup or inverted sugar syrup, is a mixture of the monosaccharides glucose and fructose produced by hydrolysing the disaccharide sucrose. It takes its name from the effect the hydrolysis has on polarised light: a sucrose solution rotates plane-polarised light to the right (dextrorotatory), whereas the resulting glucose–fructose mixture rotates it to the left (levorotatory), so the direction of rotation is "inverted."

Production

Invert sugar is made by heating sucrose in water with a small amount of acid (such as cream of tartar or citric acid), or by treating it with the enzyme invertase, as bees do when producing honey. Partially inverted syrups — most famously golden syrup — retain some sucrose, while fully inverted syrups are used where crystallisation must be avoided entirely.

Properties and uses

Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose, because fructose is perceived as sweeter than either sucrose or glucose. It is hygroscopic, retaining moisture in baked goods, and it resists crystallisation, which makes it valuable in fondants, ganache, ice cream, soft-centred chocolates and sorbets. Because its component sugars are already monosaccharides, it ferments readily and is used in brewing and winemaking. Honey is a natural analogue, consisting largely of glucose and fructose with a similar profile.
