Multiple-input and multiple-output (MIMO) is a method for multiplying the capacity of a radio link using multiple transmission and receiving antennas to exploit multipath propagation. MIMO has become an essential element of wireless communication standards including IEEE 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4), 802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5), HSPA+ (3G), WiMAX, LTE and 5G NR. In recent years MIMO technology has also been applied to power-line communication and other wireline systems.

Principle

In conventional single-antenna systems, multipath — the arrival of a signal over several paths of different lengths — is a source of fading and interference. MIMO turns multipath into an advantage: if the propagation paths are sufficiently different, the channel between each transmit and receive antenna pair behaves semi-independently, allowing several parallel data streams to occupy the same spectrum. With Nt transmit and Nr receive antennas, the potential capacity grows roughly linearly with min(Nt, Nr) at high signal-to-noise ratio, without extra bandwidth or transmit power.

Techniques

  • Spatial multiplexing — different data streams are sent from different antennas simultaneously, increasing throughput.
  • Transmit/receive diversity — the same data is coded across antennas (e.g. the Alamouti space–time block code) to make the link more robust.
  • Beamforming — signals are weighted across antennas so that energy is directed toward the receiver and away from interferers.
  • Precoding — the transmitter uses channel state information to shape the transmitted signals optimally.

Multi-user MIMO and massive MIMO

In multi-user MIMO (MU-MIMO), a base station with many antennas serves several terminals simultaneously on the same time-frequency resource, separating them spatially. Massive MIMO, a key technology of 5G, extends this to arrays of tens to hundreds of antenna elements, yielding large gains in spectral efficiency and energy efficiency through very sharp beamforming and channel "hardening".

History

The foundations were laid by Arogyaswami Paulraj and Thomas Kailath, who proposed spatial multiplexing in the early 1990s, and by Gerard J. Foschini's 1996 paper on layered space–time architectures (BLAST) at Bell Labs, with independent theoretical work by Emre Telatar on the capacity of multi-antenna Gaussian channels. MIMO entered consumer products with draft 802.11n Wi-Fi equipment in the mid-2000s and is now ubiquitous in smartphones and cellular networks.
