Bossa nova

Bossa nova
Stylistic originsSamba, samba-canção, jazz (cool jazz)
Cultural originsLate 1950s, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
Key figuresJoão Gilberto, Antônio Carlos Jobim, Vinícius de Moraes
Signature song"Garota de Ipanema" ("The Girl from Ipanema")

Bossa nova is a style of Brazilian music that developed in the late 1950s in Rio de Janeiro. Literally "new wave" or "new trend", it fuses the syncopated rhythms of samba with sophisticated jazz harmony, soft singing and nylon-string guitar accompaniment. Its defining recordings include João Gilberto's "Chega de Saudade" (1958) and the album Getz/Gilberto (1964), which carried "The Girl from Ipanema" to worldwide fame.

Characteristics

Bossa nova songs typically use extended jazz chords, a distinctive guitar pattern compressing samba rhythm into a repeating two-bar figure, and intimate, understated vocals. Lyrics, often by Vinícius de Moraes and others, dwell on love, the sea and Rio's neighbourhoods such as Ipanema and Copacabana. Composers such as Antônio Carlos Jobim, Luiz Bonfá and Baden Powell defined the repertoire: "Desafinado", "Corcovado" ("Quiet Nights"), "Wave" and "Manhã de Carnaval".

Influence

A 1962 concert at Carnegie Hall and collaborations with Stan Getz and Charlie Byrd launched a US bossa nova craze; the style permanently enriched jazz, lounge and pop music worldwide, and remains a staple of guitar and vocal jazz.
