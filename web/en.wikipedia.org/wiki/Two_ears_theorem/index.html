Two ears theorem

In geometry, the two ears theorem states that every simple polygon with more than three vertices has at least two ears — vertices at which the internal diagonal between the two neighbouring vertices lies entirely inside the polygon, so that the triangle formed by the vertex and its two neighbours is contained in the polygon. The theorem was proved by Gary H. Meisters in 1975.

Significance: polygon triangulation

The two ears theorem is the key lemma behind the fact that every simple polygon can be triangulated — decomposed into triangles by non-crossing diagonals. The proof proceeds by induction: find an ear (the theorem guarantees one, indeed two), cut it off, and apply the same argument to the smaller polygon that remains. A simple polygon with n vertices is thereby divided into exactly n − 2 triangles using n − 3 diagonals. An efficient linear-time triangulation algorithm was later given by Bernard Chazelle (1991).

Related results

Meisters's theorem underlies the art gallery theorem of Václav Chvátal (proved by Steve Fisk using triangulation and 3-colouring of the triangulation graph): ⌊n/3⌋ guards always suffice and are sometimes necessary to see the whole interior of an n-vertex polygon. The ear decomposition also appears in algorithms for polygon decomposition, mesh generation, and computer graphics. A vertex that is an ear tip is necessarily a convex (non-reflex) vertex; polygons in which ears are counted in more refined ways lead to results on "mouths", "pockets", and the ear-decomposition of polyhedra, where the analogue of the theorem fails in general.
