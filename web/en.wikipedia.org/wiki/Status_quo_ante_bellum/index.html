Status quo ante bellum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The term status quo ante bellum is a Latin phrase meaning "the situation as it existed before the war". The term was originally used in treaties to refer to the withdrawal of enemy troops and the restoration of prewar leadership and conditions. When used as such, it denotes that no side gains or loses any territorial, economic or political rights as a result of the conflict.

This principle contrasts with uti possidetis, under which each side retains whatever territory and property it holds at the end of the war.

Historical examples

  • Treaty of Ghent (1814) — ended the War of 1812 between the United States and Britain; all conquered territory was restored, and boundaries reverted to their pre-war state.
  • Korean War armistice (1953) — the Demilitarized Zone was established roughly along the 38th parallel, approximating the pre-war division.
  • Iran–Iraq War (1980–1988) — ended under UN Security Council Resolution 598 with borders essentially unchanged.
  • Eritrean–Ethiopian War (1998–2000) — the Algiers Agreement sought restoration of the pre-May 1998 administrative status, though a boundary commission later awarded some territory.
  • Kargil War (1999) — India and Pakistan returned to the pre-war Line of Control.

Related terms

The shorter expression status quo ante means "the state existing before" any specified change, and status quo simply "the existing state of affairs". A peace on the basis of status quo ante bellum may still leave underlying disputes unresolved, since no party's claims are vindicated.
