Tax credit

A tax credit is an amount of money that taxpayers can subtract directly from the tax they owe to a government. Unlike a tax deduction, which reduces taxable income (and is therefore worth more to taxpayers in higher brackets), a credit reduces the final tax bill dollar-for-dollar (or pound-for-pound), so its value is the same regardless of the taxpayer's marginal rate.

Types

Non-refundable credits can reduce tax liability to zero but not below it; any excess is lost or, for some credits, carried forward. Refundable credits can generate a payment to the taxpayer when they exceed liability, making them an instrument of welfare and income policy as well as tax policy. Common examples in the United States include the Earned Income Tax Credit and the partly refundable Child Tax Credit, the American Opportunity and Lifetime Learning education credits, the foreign tax credit (which relieves double taxation of foreign-source income), and energy credits for items such as electric vehicles and home improvements. In the United Kingdom, tax credits were for two decades the name of the main means-tested working-age benefits (Working Tax Credit and Child Tax Credit), administered by HMRC and now largely replaced by Universal Credit.

Policy use

Governments use credits to subsidise favoured activities — children, work, education, research and development, low-income housing, renewable energy — because credits are visible, targetable and (when refundable) can reach households with little or no tax liability. Economists debate credits versus direct spending on grounds of efficiency, take-up and administrative cost; complex eligibility rules can leave many intended beneficiaries failing to claim.
