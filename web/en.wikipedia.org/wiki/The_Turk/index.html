The Turk

The Turk

Also known as: The Mechanical Turk; the Automaton Chess Player

Creator: Wolfgang von Kempelen

Debut: 1770, Schönbrunn Palace, Vienna

Destroyed: 1854 (fire, Philadelphia)

The Turk, also known as the Mechanical Turk or Automaton Chess Player, was a famous hoax: a chess-playing "machine" constructed in 1770 by Wolfgang von Kempelen to impress the Empress Maria Theresa of Austria. In fact, the cabinet concealed a human chess master who operated the life-size wooden figure — dressed in Ottoman robes and a turban — by means of levers, moving its arm to play pieces on the board.

History

After its debut at Schönbrunn Palace, the Turk toured Europe and the Americas for more than eight decades, defeating most challengers. Its operators included the masters Johann Baptist Allgaier and Aaron Alexandre; among the famous opponents it beat were Napoleon Bonaparte and Benjamin Franklin. After von Kempelen's death it passed to Johann Nepomuk Maelzel, who exhibited it in the United States. Edgar Allan Poe saw it in Richmond and published a celebrated essay in 1836 attempting to expose the secret. The original Turk was destroyed by fire in Philadelphia in 1854.

Secret

The cabinet's doors were opened before each performance to display elaborate clockwork, but sliding compartments and a seat that moved on rails allowed the hidden operator to remain concealed. Candles and a system of magnets and indicators let the operator follow the game from inside. The machinery also allowed the Turk to perform the knight's tour and to "speak" using a letter board.

Legacy

The Turk became a byword for concealed human labour behind apparent automation; Amazon named its crowdsourcing platform Mechanical Turk (launched 2005) after it. A modern reconstruction was built in the 1980s by John Gaughan. The Turk also inspired speculation about artificial intelligence and is discussed by writers from Poe to Walter Benjamin.
