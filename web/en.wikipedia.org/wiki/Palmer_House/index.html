The Palmer House is a historic hotel in the Loop area of downtown Chicago, Illinois, operating today as the Palmer House Hilton. It is one of the oldest continuously operating hotels in the United States and a member of Historic Hotels of America. The hotel was built by businessman Potter Palmer as a wedding present for his wife, Bertha Honoré Palmer.

History

The first Palmer House opened in 1870 at State and Quincy streets, a wedding gift from Potter Palmer to his bride. Only thirteen days after its grand opening, it was destroyed in the Great Chicago Fire of October 1871. Palmer borrowed money — reportedly the largest individual loan then recorded — and rebuilt on a larger scale. The second Palmer House, designed by John M. Van Osdel, opened in 1873 at State and Monroe. It was a seven-storey building of unprecedented luxury, advertised as fireproof, and became a byword for Gilded Age opulence: its barber shop floor was famously tiled with silver dollars embedded in the mortar.

The current, third Palmer House was built in stages between 1923 and 1927 to designs by Holabird & Roche, a 25-storey building occupying a full city block. Its Peacock Alley and the Empire Room supper club hosted generations of entertainers, and its ornate lobby, with a ceiling painted by French muralist Louis Pierre Rigal, remains a landmark. Conrad Hilton bought the hotel in 1945. The Palmer House has hosted numerous U.S. presidents and was a centre of the 1968 Democratic National Convention events in Chicago.

The brownie

The Palmer House is credited with the invention of the chocolate brownie: in 1893 Bertha Palmer asked the hotel's pastry chef for a portable, cake-like dessert suitable for ladies attending the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, and the result — a dense chocolate confection with walnuts and an apricot glaze — is still served at the hotel.
