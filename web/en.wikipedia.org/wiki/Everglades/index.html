Everglades

The Everglades is a natural region of tropical wetlands in the southern portion of the U.S. state of Florida, comprising the southern half of a large drainage basin within the Neotropical realm. The system begins near Orlando with the Kissimmee River, which discharges into the vast but shallow Lake Okeechobee. Water leaving the lake in the wet season forms a slow-moving river 60 miles (97 km) wide and over 100 miles (160 km) long, flowing southward across a limestone shelf to Florida Bay at the southern end of the state. The Everglades is often described as a "River of Grass", a phrase popularised by conservationist Marjory Stoneman Douglas.

Ecosystem

The Everglades supports a distinctive mosaic of habitats: sawgrass marshes, cypress swamps, mangrove forests, pinelands and hardwood hammocks. It is the only place in the world where alligators and crocodiles coexist. Wildlife includes the Florida panther, West Indian manatee, wood stork and numerous wading birds. Invasive species, notably Burmese pythons, have severely reduced mammal populations in parts of the park.

Human impact and restoration

Beginning in the late 19th century, large-scale drainage for agriculture and flood control reduced the Everglades to roughly half its original extent, diverting water to the Everglades Agricultural Area and the urban east coast. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, authorised in 2000, is among the largest ecosystem restoration projects ever undertaken, aiming to restore more natural water flows to the remaining system.

Everglades National Park

Everglades National Park, established in 1947, protects the southern part of the Everglades and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979 and a Wetland of International Importance under the Ramsar Convention.
