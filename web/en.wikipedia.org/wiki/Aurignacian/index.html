The Aurignacian is an archaeological industry of the Upper Paleolithic associated with early modern humans (Cro-Magnons) in Europe and Southwest Asia. Dating to roughly 43,000–26,000 years ago, it is named after the type site at Aurignac in southwestern France, excavated by Édouard Lartet in 1860. The Aurignacian marks a major shift — sometimes called part of the "human revolution" — in tool technology, art and symbolic behaviour.

Aurignacian artefacts: blades, split-base bone points and early figurative art.

Material culture

Characteristic artefacts include endscrapers and burins on long stone blades, split-base points of bone and antler, and personal ornaments such as pierced shells, teeth and ivory beads. Aurignacian sites have yielded some of the world's oldest figurative art: the mammoth-ivory Lion-man of Hohlenstein-Stadel and animal figurines of the Swabian Jura caves (c. 40,000 years old), the Venus of Hohle Fels, and early phases of painting at Chauvet Cave. Bone flutes from Geissenklösterle are among the oldest known musical instruments.

Distribution and people

The industry spread from the Levant and the Balkans across Europe as anatomically modern humans replaced or absorbed Neanderthal populations; early or "Protoaurignacian" phases appear in Italy and southern France. The Aurignacian was succeeded by the Gravettian around 28,000–26,000 years ago.
