Rings of Uranus

Discovered: 10 March 1977 (Elliot et al.)
Known rings: 13 narrow main rings plus dusty rings
Principal rings: ζ, 6, 5, 4, α, β, η, γ, δ, λ, ε
Radius range: about 38,000–98,000 km

The rings of Uranus are a system of thirteen known narrow, dark rings around the planet Uranus, intermediate in complexity between the broad rings of Saturn and the simple systems of Jupiter and Neptune. Because Uranus is tilted on its side, the rings lie roughly perpendicular to the planet's orbit around the Sun.

Discovery

The rings were discovered on 10 March 1977 by James L. Elliot, Edward W. Dunham and Jessica Mink, who observed the star SAO 158687 being repeatedly occulted before and after it passed behind Uranus. Voyager 2 imaged the rings in 1986, and the Hubble Space Telescope later revealed additional faint outer rings.

Composition

The rings are extremely dark — with albedos of only a few percent — and are composed of particles from centimetre to metre sizes, probably water ice contaminated with dark organic material. The brightest, the ε ring, is shepherded by the moons Cordelia and Ophelia.
