Film director

A film director is a person who controls the artistic and dramatic aspects of a film and visualizes the screenplay (or script) while guiding the film crew and actors in the fulfilment of that vision. The director has a key role in choosing the cast members, production design and all the creative aspects of filmmaking.

Responsibilities

Film directors create an overall vision through which a film eventually becomes realized. They direct the performances of actors, decide on camera angles, lighting and the framing of shots in collaboration with the cinematographer, and work with editors in post-production to shape the final cut. On most productions the director is hired by the producer and may have greater or lesser creative control depending on their contract and reputation.

History

In the earliest years of cinema, the roles of director and cameraman were often combined. As narrative filmmaking developed in the 1900s and 1910s, figures such as D. W. Griffith established the director as the central creative authority. In the classical Hollywood studio system, directors were employees assigned to projects; the auteur theory, advanced by French critics of Cahiers du Cinéma in the 1950s, argued that the director is the true "author" of a film, a view that elevated directors such as Alfred Hitchcock, John Ford and Howard Hawks.

Training and career

There is no single route into directing: some directors attend film schools, others rise through assistant-director, screenwriting, acting or cinematography roles, and many direct short films, commercials or music videos first. Directors are represented in the United States by the Directors Guild of America (DGA), founded in 1936.
