Tet

This article is about the Vietnamese New Year festival. For the 1968 military campaign, see Tet Offensive.

Tết Nguyên Đán
Observed byVietnamese people, in Vietnam and the diaspora
TypeCultural, national holiday
DateFirst day of the first month of the Vietnamese lunar calendar (late January–February)
CustomsFamily reunions, ancestral offerings, bánh chưng, lì xì (lucky money), first-footing

Tet, in full Tết Nguyên Đán ("Festival of the First Morning of the First Day"), is the Vietnamese celebration of the Lunar New Year and the most important festival in Vietnamese culture. It marks the arrival of spring and the start of a new year on the Vietnamese calendar, which generally coincides with Chinese New Year, occasionally differing by a day because of time-zone differences in determining the new moon.

Customs

Preparations begin a week ahead, when the Kitchen Gods (Ông Táo) are farewelled to report to heaven. Families clean and decorate their homes with kumquat trees, peach blossom in the north and yellow apricot blossom in the south, make the square sticky-rice cake bánh chưng (and its cylindrical southern counterpart bánh tét), and set out a five-fruit tray on the ancestral altar. On New Year's Eve and the first days of the year, people visit parents, elders and teachers in order of seniority; children receive red envelopes of lucky money (lì xì or mừng tuổi); and the first visitor to enter a home (xông đất, first-footing) is believed to set the household's fortune for the year. Businesses close and Vietnam's cities empty as millions travel home, in what is often called the largest annual migration in the country.

History

Tet's roots lie in the agricultural rituals of the Red River Delta's rice civilisation, later layered with Chinese calendrical and Confucian influences as well as Buddhist and folk customs. In 1968 the festival gave its name to the Tet Offensive of the Vietnam War, launched during the holiday ceasefire.
