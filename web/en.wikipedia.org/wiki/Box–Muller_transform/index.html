Box–Muller transform

The Box–Muller transform, developed by George Edward Pelham Box and Mervin Edgar Muller in 1958, is a random-number sampling method for generating pairs of independent, standard, normally distributed (Gaussian) random numbers from uniformly distributed random numbers.

Method

Given two independent uniform random numbers U1 and U2 on (0,1], the transform computes the radius R = √(−2 ln U1) and angle θ = 2πU2, and outputs the two Gaussian variates Z0 = R cos θ and Z1 = R sin θ. The method exploits the fact that, in polar coordinates, a pair of independent standard normal variables has a uniformly distributed angle and a chi distribution for the radius. The basic form requires trigonometric functions, which can be slow; the polar method of Marsaglia avoids them by rejection sampling within the unit circle, computing sin and cos implicitly.

Usage

The transform is exact (unlike the approximation of summing many uniform variables via the central limit theorem) and simple to implement, and it underlies normal random number generation in many statistical libraries and simulations. Alternatives include the ziggurat algorithm, which is faster on modern hardware, and inverse transform sampling using the error function, which produces one variate per uniform input — a property important for quasi-Monte Carlo methods, where the pair-generation of Box–Muller breaks up low-discrepancy structure.
