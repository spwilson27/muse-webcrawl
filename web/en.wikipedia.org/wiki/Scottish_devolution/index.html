Scottish devolution

Scottish devolution is the transfer of legislative and executive powers from the Parliament of the United Kingdom to the Scottish Parliament and Scottish Government. Although Scotland retained its legal system, church, and education system after the Union of 1707, legislative power sat at Westminster until devolution in 1999.

Holyrood, home of the devolved Scottish Parliament.

History

Demands for Scottish "home rule" date to the late 19th century. Post-war administrative devolution expanded the role of the Scottish Office. A 1979 referendum produced a narrow majority for a Scottish Assembly but failed a statutory threshold requiring support from 40% of the electorate, and the proposal lapsed. The poll tax, introduced in Scotland a year before the rest of Great Britain, and years of Conservative governments Scotland had not voted for, strengthened the devolution movement; the Scottish Constitutional Convention (1989–1995), boycotted by the SNP and Conservatives, drew up a blueprint for a parliament.

The 1997 referendum and Scotland Act 1998

Following Labour's 1997 election victory, a referendum asked two questions: whether there should be a Scottish Parliament (74.3% yes) and whether it should have tax-varying powers (63.5% yes). The Scotland Act 1998 created the Parliament, defining reserved matters and leaving everything else devolved. The Parliament first met in 1999.

Further powers

The Scotland Acts of 2012 and 2016 devolved significant further powers, including control over income tax rates and thresholds, stamp duty and landfill tax, and elements of welfare, implementing recommendations of the Calman Commission and the Smith Commission (convened after the 2014 independence referendum, in which 55.3% voted against independence).
