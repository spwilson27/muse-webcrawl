Anatomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anatomy is the branch of biology concerned with the study of the structure of organisms and their parts — from the gross anatomy visible to the naked eye to microscopic anatomy (histology and cell biology). It is among the oldest scientific disciplines: systematic human dissection began in Hellenistic Alexandria (Herophilus and Erasistratus), was codified by Galen, and was revolutionised in the Renaissance by Andreas Vesalius, whose De humani corporis fabrica (1543) corrected Galen from direct observation.

Anatomy provides the vocabulary and map of the body on which Physiology, Medicine and surgery rest. Its terms — standardised internationally (Terminologia Anatomica) — describe structures by position, region and system.

Methods

Classical anatomy uses dissection of cadavers; modern practice adds medical imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound), endoscopy and 3D reconstruction, plus the digitised Visible Human datasets. Comparative anatomy compares structures across species and, since Darwin, reads them as evidence of Evolution — homologous bones in a human arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper.

Organisation

The body is described at levels from chemical to whole organism, and regionally (head, thorax, abdomen) or systemically (by organ system). Surface anatomy relates internal structures to landmarks palpable in the clinic.
