Band-Aid

An adhesive bandage of the Band-Aid type

Band-Aid is a brand of adhesive bandages distributed by the American company Kenvue (formerly part of Johnson & Johnson). First sold in 1921, Band-Aid has become so dominant in the United States and several other markets that the name is widely used generically for any small adhesive bandage, an example of a generic trademark.

History

The bandage was invented in 1920 by Earle Dickson, a cotton buyer for Johnson & Johnson in Highland Park, New Jersey, whose wife Josephine frequently cut or burned her fingers while cooking. Dickson combined a strip of surgical tape with a small piece of gauze and crinoline so his wife could dress her own wounds. His employer marketed the handmade prototypes from 1921; machine-made versions, sterilised products and the familiar perforated plastic strips followed, and demand soared when the company gave free bandages to Boy Scouts and overseas soldiers. The standard design — adhesive backing, a non-stick absorbent pad, and (in many variants) ventilation holes — has remained recognisable ever since, though materials and skin-tone ranges have been expanded; in 2020 the brand announced bandages in multiple skin tones. Figuratively, a "Band-Aid solution" in English denotes a superficial temporary fix.
