The London Charterhouse is a historic complex in Smithfield, London, standing on the site of a Carthusian monastery founded in 1371 over a Black Death burial ground. Since 1611 it has housed the charitable foundation of Thomas Sutton — an almshouse for gentlemen pensioners ("Brothers") and, until 1872, Charterhouse School. It remains an almshouse today, with a museum open to the public.

The Charterhouse from Charterhouse Square

Monastery

In 1348, during the Black Death, Sir Walter Manny acquired land at Smithfield for an emergency plague cemetery, in which thousands were buried. A Carthusian priory, the London Charterhouse (the name "Charterhouse" is an Anglicization of Chartreuse), was founded on the site in 1371. The monastery prospered until the Reformation: in 1535 its prior, John Houghton, and several monks were executed for refusing to acknowledge Henry VIII's supremacy, among the first martyrs of the English Reformation. The priory was dissolved in 1537.

Sutton's Hospital

After passing through the hands of courtiers, including the Duke of Norfolk (who built the great mansion whose rooms survive), the site was purchased by the wealthy merchant Thomas Sutton. His 1611 foundation — formally the Hospital of King James and Thomas Sutton in Charterhouse — provided a home for up to 80 impoverished gentlemen ("Brothers") and schooling for poor boys. Charterhouse School occupied the site until it moved to Godalming, Surrey, in 1872.

Today

The Charterhouse survived the Great Fire and, with damage, the Blitz. The Brothers, now including women, still live in the almshouse, whose chapel, Great Hall, and Tudor rooms survive. In 2017 the Charterhouse opened a museum and learning centre in partnership with the Museum of London, and the previously private Charterhouse Square gardens were opened to the public.
