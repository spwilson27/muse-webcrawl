Malpighian tubule system

The Malpighian tubule system is a type of excretory and osmoregulatory system found in insects, myriapods, arachnids and tardigrades. The tubules are named after Marcello Malpighi, the 17th-century anatomist who first described them in the silkworm.

Structure

Malpighian tubules are slender, blind-ending tubes that float in the haemocoel (body cavity) and open into the digestive tract at the junction between the midgut and the hindgut. Their number varies widely between species, from just two in some insects to more than a hundred in others. The tubule walls are a single layer of cells thick and are often supplied with fine tracheae.

Function

Unlike the kidneys of vertebrates, the Malpighian tubules do not filter the blood under pressure. Instead, ions — chiefly potassium and sodium — are actively transported into the tubule lumen, and water follows by osmosis, carrying dissolved wastes with it. Nitrogenous waste, mainly uric acid, along with salts and other solutes, moves into the tubules from the haemolymph.

The resulting fluid passes into the hindgut, where useful water and ions are reabsorbed in the rectum, and the nearly dry waste is excreted with the faeces. This system allows terrestrial arthropods to conserve water efficiently, a key adaptation to life on land.

Comparison

The Malpighian tubule system performs functions analogous to the vertebrate kidney, but it arose independently in evolution and works by secretion rather than filtration. Some aquatic species can reverse the flow or modify ion transport to cope with excess water.
