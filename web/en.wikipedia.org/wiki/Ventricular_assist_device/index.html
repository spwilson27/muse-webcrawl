Ventricular assist device

A ventricular assist device (VAD) is an electromechanical pump used to support heart function and blood flow in people with weakened hearts. The device takes blood from a ventricle and helps pump it to the aorta or pulmonary artery. VADs may support the left ventricle (LVAD), the right ventricle (RVAD), or both (BiVAD). The most commonly used is the LVAD.

Uses

VADs are used in advanced heart failure as a bridge to transplantation while a patient awaits a donor heart, as a bridge to recovery, or as destination therapy for patients who are not candidates for transplantation. They are also used for temporary support after cardiac surgery when the heart cannot be weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass.

Designs

First-generation devices used pulsatile, volume-displacement pumps that mimicked the natural heartbeat; they were large and prone to mechanical failure. Second-generation devices use continuous-flow axial pumps, and third-generation devices use centrifugal pumps with magnetically levitated rotors, such as the HeartMate III, which have proved more durable. Blood flows from an inflow cannula in the ventricle through the pump to an outflow graft sewn to the aorta. A driveline exits the skin to connect to an external controller and batteries.

Risks

Complications include bleeding, stroke, infection of the driveline, pump thrombosis, right ventricular failure and gastrointestinal bleeding, linked in part to the need for anticoagulation and to the loss of normal pulsatile flow.
