Unit of measurement

Standard units allow measurements made anywhere to be compared.

A unit of measurement, or unit of measure, is a definite magnitude of a quantity, defined and adopted by convention or by law, that is used as a standard for measurement of the same kind of quantity. Any other quantity of that kind can be expressed as a multiple of the unit. For example, a length is a physical quantity, and the metre is a unit of length representing a definite predetermined length; saying "10 metres" means a length ten times that standard.

Systems of units

Historically, units were based on body parts (the cubit, foot and inch) or local artefacts, and varied from place to place. The metric system, introduced in France in the 1790s, rationalised measurement on decimal lines, and its modern descendant, the International System of Units (SI), is the world's most widely used system, built on seven base units: the second, metre, kilogram, ampere, kelvin, mole and candela. Since the 2019 redefinition, all SI units are defined in terms of fundamental constants of nature. Imperial and US customary units remain in everyday use in the United Kingdom and the United States respectively.

Base and derived units

Within a system, base units for fundamental quantities are chosen by convention, and derived units are formed from them — for example, the newton (force) from kilograms, metres and seconds. Unit conversion between systems is a standard scientific skill, and errors in conversion have caused notable failures, such as the loss of NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999 after a pound-force/newton mix-up.

History

Standardised weights and measures are among the earliest technologies of civilisation, appearing in the Indus Valley, Egypt and Mesopotamia. Magna Carta (1215) already demanded uniform measures for wine, ale and corn in England. The Metre Convention of 1875 established international cooperation in metrology, continued today by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM).
