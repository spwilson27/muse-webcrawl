Desiderius Erasmus Roterodamus (28 October c. 1466 – 12 July 1536), commonly known in English as Erasmus of Rotterdam, was a Dutch Christian humanist, Catholic priest and theologian, considered one of the greatest thinkers of the Northern Renaissance. As a Catholic priest, he was an important figure in classical scholarship who wrote in Latin.

Life

Erasmus was born in Rotterdam, probably in 1466, the illegitimate son of a priest. Orphaned by plague, he was educated by the Brethren of the Common Life at Deventer and Hertogenbosch, and reluctantly became an Augustinian canon at Steyn. Ordained a priest in 1492, he escaped monastic life by becoming secretary to the Bishop of Cambrai, then studied in Paris and travelled to England, where he befriended John Colet and Thomas More. For much of his later life he lived in Basel, working with the printer Johann Froben.

Works

His satirical masterpiece In Praise of Folly (1511), written at More's house, mocked monks, theologians and scholastic pedantry. In 1516 he published the first printed Greek New Testament (Novum Testamentum) with a new Latin translation, exposing errors in the Vulgate and supplying tools the Reformers would use. His Adagia collected thousands of classical proverbs, and his Colloquies became Europe's schoolbook of Latin conversation.

Reformation debates

Erasmus long urged reform of the Church from within and was courted by both sides of the Reformation. In 1524 he broke publicly with Martin Luther in De libero arbitrio ("On Free Will"), defending human free will against Luther's doctrine of bondage of the will, which Luther answered with De servo arbitrio (1525). Erasmus remained Catholic but died in Protestant Basel in 1536; his works were later placed on the Catholic Index of prohibited books, even as his influence on education and biblical scholarship endured.
