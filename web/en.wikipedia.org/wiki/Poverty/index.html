Poverty

[Image: a aid worker distributing food supplies in a rural village]

Poverty is the condition of lacking the material resources — income, food, housing, health, education — needed for a minimum standard of living. It is measured absolutely (a fixed real threshold, such as the World Bank's international poverty line, set at $2.15 a day in 2017 prices) or relatively (a fraction of median income, commonly 50–60% in rich countries).

Scale, causes and remedies

Extreme poverty fell dramatically with globalisation and growth in Asia — from about 36% of world population in 1990 to roughly 8–9% in the early 2020s, with the COVID-19 pandemic causing the first rise in a generation — but remains concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and fragile states. Causes span low productivity, ill health, poor education, discrimination, weak institutions and shocks; remedies span growth itself, cash transfers and welfare states, public health and schooling, microfinance and property rights. Amartya Sen's capability approach reframed poverty as deprivation of basic freedoms, underlying the multidimensional measures (MPI) now published alongside income figures by the UNDP and the World Bank.
