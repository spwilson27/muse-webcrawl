Seasteading

Seasteading is the concept of creating permanent dwellings at sea, called seasteads, in international waters outside the territory claimed by any government. Proponents argue that autonomous floating communities could experiment with new forms of government, law, and social organisation in the same way that frontiers and start-up ventures drive innovation, free from the regulation and taxation regimes of existing states. The term combines "sea" and "homesteading".

History

The idea draws on earlier twentieth-century projects such as the Principality of Sealand (founded 1967 on a disused sea fort off England), the abortive Republic of Minerva (1972), and various libertarian "new country" projects. The modern seasteading movement was institutionalised with the founding of The Seasteading Institute in 2008 by Patri Friedman, a former Google engineer and grandson of economist Milton Friedman, with seed funding from venture capitalist Peter Thiel. The institute promoted the idea of "startup societies" competing for residents.

In 2017 the institute signed a memorandum of understanding with the government of French Polynesia to explore a floating island project in a semi-autonomous "special economic zone" at sea; the agreement lapsed the following year amid local political opposition. In 2019, an American bitcoin trader and a Thai partner occupied a small seastead cabin off the coast of Phuket; Thai authorities charged them with violating Thailand's sovereignty, an offence potentially carrying the death penalty, and the couple fled, the structure being towed away by the Thai navy.

Design and technology

Proposed seasteads range from converted cruise ships and oil-rig-like platforms to modular floating hexagons and spar structures that minimise wave motion. Designs must solve problems of fresh water, waste processing, food and energy supply, station-keeping in storms, and medical care, usually by importing supplies from land. Critics argue that dependence on land-based supply chains undermines claims of independence, and that lifeboat-grade safety on the high seas is expensive.

Legal status

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, states exercise jurisdiction over an exclusive economic zone extending 200 nautical miles from their coasts; beyond that the high seas are open to all but vessels remain subject to the law of their flag state. No seastead has been recognised as a sovereign state, and legal scholars generally hold that artificial islands do not generate territorial rights.

Criticism

Critics describe seasteading as a form of "colonialism" and tax avoidance for the wealthy, question its environmental impact, and note the vulnerability of floating structures to storms, piracy, and the displeasure of nearby naval powers. Supporters respond that experimentation with governance requires physical space outside existing jurisdictions and that early projects are prototypes rather than finished societies.
