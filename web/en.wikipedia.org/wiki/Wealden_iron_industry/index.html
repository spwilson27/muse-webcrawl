Wealden iron industry

The Wealden iron industry was located in the Weald of south-eastern England. It was formerly an important industry, producing a large proportion of the bar iron made in England in the 16th century, and most of the cannon cast in the 18th century. The Weald's geology provided the raw material — iron ore in the Wadhurst Clay — while its extensive woodland supplied charcoal, and its streams provided water power for bellows and forge hammers.

Origins

Irish and Roman bloomeries worked the Wealden ore from prehistoric times, and the industry was revived in the later Middle Ages. The first blast furnace in the Weald was built at Newbridge in 1496, and by the 1540s the industry had become one of cannon founding: the first cast-iron cannon in England were made at Buxted in 1543 by Ralf Hogge.

Heyday

At its peak in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, the Weald had roughly 110 ironworks. Water-powered furnaces smelted ore with charcoal, and finery forges converted the pig iron into bar iron. Wealden ironmasters, many exploiting family estates, exported bar iron and cast ordnance, anchors and firebacks across Europe. Roads in the district were notoriously ruined by the carriage of iron, provoking some of the earliest turnpike legislation.

Decline

The industry declined from the mid-18th century as ironmaking shifted to the coalfields after coke smelting became widespread, and Swedish and Russian bar iron undercut Wealden prices in the forge trade. Cannon founding survived longer — the last Wealden furnace at Ashburnham closed in 1828. The landscape retains many traces: hammer ponds, furnace ponds, slag deposits and place names such as Furnace Wood.
