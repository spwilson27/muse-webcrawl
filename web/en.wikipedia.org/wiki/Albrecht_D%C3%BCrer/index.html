Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 – 6 April 1528) was a German painter, printmaker and theorist of the German Renaissance. Born in Nuremberg, he established his reputation across Europe in his twenties through his exceptionally high-quality woodcut prints and engravings, and is regarded as the greatest artist of the Northern Renaissance.

Early life and training

Dürer was the son of a Hungarian-born goldsmith and was apprenticed first to his father and then to the Nuremberg painter and woodcut designer Michael Wolgemut. Journeys to the Netherlands and Italy exposed him to Italian Renaissance theory, perspective and classical art, which he fused with Northern attention to detail.

Work

His woodcut series, including the Apocalypse (1498), and his engravings — among them Knight, Death and the Devil (1513), Saint Jerome in His Study and Melencolia I (1514) — are landmarks of printmaking. His paintings include the Four Apostles (1526) and sensitive self-portraits; his watercolours and nature studies, such as the Young Hare, are also celebrated. His famous woodcut of a rhinoceros (1515), drawn from a description without ever seeing the animal, remained Europe's standard image of the creature for centuries.

Theoretical writings

Dürer wrote treatises on measurement, geometry and human proportion, including the Four Books on Measurement (1525) and the posthumous Four Books on Human Proportion (1528), helping to ground Northern art in Renaissance science.
