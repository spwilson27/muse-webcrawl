Hybridoma technology

Production of monoclonal antibodies using hybridoma technology.

Hybridoma technology is a method for producing large numbers of identical antibodies, known as monoclonal antibodies. The process starts by injecting a mouse (or other mammal) with an antigen that provokes an immune response. A type of white blood cell, the B cell, produces antibodies that bind to the injected antigen. These antibody-producing B cells are then harvested from the mouse and fused with immortal cancerous cells — myeloma cells. The resulting hybrid cells, called hybridomas, are a fused product of two types of cells: B cells and myeloma cells.

The method was developed by Georges J. F. Köhler and César Milstein in 1975, building on work in the laboratory of Jerne and colleagues at the Basel Institute for Immunology. Köhler and Milstein shared the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Niels Kaj Jerne for the discovery.

Method

Laboratory animals (typically mice) are first exposed to an antigen against which an antibody is desired. Once the animal mounts a response, splenic B lymphocytes are isolated and fused with immortal myeloma cells using agents such as polyethylene glycol or the Sendai virus. The myeloma partner is chosen to lack the enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT), so unfused myeloma cells die in HAT medium (containing hypoxanthine, aminopterin and thymidine), while unfused B cells die naturally after a short lifespan. Only the fused hybridomas survive indefinitely.

Surviving hybridomas are screened for production of the desired antibody and cloned by limiting dilution so that each clone derives from a single cell. The clones secrete a single, defined antibody specificity indefinitely and can be grown in culture or in the peritoneal cavity of mice to yield large quantities.

Applications

Monoclonal antibodies produced by hybridomas underpin modern diagnostic tests such as the ELISA and home pregnancy tests, and many therapeutic antibodies used in cancer, autoimmune disease and transplantation medicine. Although newer methods such as phage display and single-B-cell cloning can produce fully human antibodies, hybridoma technology remains a workhorse of immunology and biotechnology.
