Chavacano or Chabacano is a group of Spanish-based creole language varieties spoken in the Philippines. The name derives from the Spanish word chabacano, meaning "poor quality" or "vulgar". Chavacano is the only Spanish-based creole in Asia, having developed through contact between Spanish colonizers, Mexican soldiers and settlers, and speakers of Philippine languages such as Cebuano, Hiligaynon, Tagalog, and Subanon from the 17th century onward.

Varieties and distribution

The largest variety is Zamboangueño, spoken in Zamboanga City and the surrounding peninsula, where it serves as a lingua franca and language of local media and government; Zamboanga City styles itself "Asia's Latin City". Two older varieties survive in Luzon: Caviteño in Cavite City and Ternateño (Bahra) in Ternate, Cavite, whose Spanish vocabulary shows a strong Mexican and Moluccan connection through the garrison communities moved there in the 17th century. The Ermita variety of Manila is extinct.

Features

Chavacano vocabulary is predominantly Spanish, especially Mexican Spanish, while its grammar reflects Philippine patterns: verb-initial tendencies in some structures, pluralization by reduplication or the preposed markers maga (from mga), and a tense-aspect system using particles such as ya (past/completed) and ay or ele (future). Pronouns mix Spanish forms (yo, tu, ele) with Philippine-influenced forms. Because its grammar differs fundamentally from Spanish, Chavacano is classified as a creole rather than a dialect of Spanish, and it is not generally mutually intelligible with Spanish.

Status

Zamboangueño is vigorous and expanding, used in schools under the Philippines' mother-tongue education policy, while the Cavite varieties are endangered as Tagalog advances. A Chavacano Wikipedia (the "Wikipedia zamboangueña") exists, and standardization efforts for the Zamboanga orthography have been undertaken by local academics and the city government.
