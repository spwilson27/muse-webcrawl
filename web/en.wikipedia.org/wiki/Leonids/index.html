The Leonids are a prolific meteor shower associated with the comet 55P/Tempel–Tuttle and are also known for their spectacular meteor storms. As with all meteor showers, the Leonid meteor shower is named for the constellation in which its radiant lies, Leo. The shower peaks in mid-November, and its meteors are among the fastest of any annual shower, entering the atmosphere at about 71 kilometres per second because they strike the Earth nearly head-on in a retrograde orbit.

Meteor storms

About every 33 years, corresponding to the orbital period of Tempel–Tuttle, the Leonids produce extraordinary storms in which the zenithal hourly rate can reach thousands or even tens of thousands of meteors. The great storm of 1833, in which an estimated 100,000 meteors per hour fell over North America, was pivotal in establishing that meteor showers originate in space rather than in the atmosphere, and it remains one of the most famous celestial events in American history. Other great storms occurred in 1866, 1966—when observers in the southwestern United States reported rates approaching 40 meteors per second for a brief period—and a series of strong outbursts around 1999–2002. Storms have been recorded as far back as 902 AD.

Observing

In ordinary years the Leonids yield a modest rate of roughly 10–15 meteors per hour under dark skies, best seen after midnight when the radiant in Leo rises higher. Because the parent comet next returns to perihelion in 2031, interest surrounds possible enhanced activity in the early 2030s, although no storm comparable to 1966 is confidently predicted.
