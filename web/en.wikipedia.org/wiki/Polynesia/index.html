Polynesia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polynesia is a subregion of Oceania comprising more than a thousand islands scattered across a vast triangle of the central and South Pacific Ocean, whose corners are Hawaii (north), New Zealand/Aotearoa (southwest) and Easter Island/Rapa Nui (southeast). Its peoples share related languages and cultures descended from the Lapita voyagers — the greatest navigators of the pre-modern world.

Peoples and voyaging

Polynesian settlers reached Samoa and Tonga by about 900 BC, paused for nearly two millennia, then — in a final burst of double-hulled canoe voyaging guided by stars, swell patterns and birds — settled the far corners: Hawaii, Rapa Nui and New Zealand (by roughly the thirteenth century AD) among the last habitable places on Earth reached by humans. Their languages form a tight family within Austronesian; their cultures share the marae (ceremonial ground), tapa cloth, tattooing and chiefly mana and tapu — the source of the English word "taboo".

Modern Polynesia

Today the triangle holds independent states (Samoa, Tonga, Tuvalu) and the territories or constituent countries of France (French Polynesia, Wallis and Futuna), New Zealand (the Cook Islands, Niue, Tokelau), the United States (Hawaii, American Samoa) and Chile (Rapa Nui). Low atolls such as Tuvalu face existential sea-level rise (compare Melanesia and Micronesia, Oceania's other great divisions), while a voyaging renaissance — canoes such as Hōkūleʻa circumnavigating by traditional navigation since 1976 — has revived the ancestors' art.
