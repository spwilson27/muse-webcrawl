Kidnapping

Kidnapping
DefinitionUnlawful confinement of a person against their will, usually with abduction
Common motivesRansom, extortion, custody disputes, political coercion, forced marriage
U.S. law18 U.S.C. § 1201; state statutes; Federal Kidnapping Act (1932)

In criminal law, kidnapping is the unlawful confinement of a person against their will, often including transportation (asportation) of the victim from one place to another. The element of abduction distinguishes it from the related offense of false imprisonment. Kidnapping may be committed for ransom or in furtherance of another crime, or in connection with a child custody dispute.

Types

Kidnapping is commonly divided by motive. Kidnapping for ransom seeks money or concessions from the victim's family, employer, or government; its organized variant, "express kidnapping," briefly detains victims to empty their bank accounts, a practice documented in parts of Latin America. Parental kidnapping — taking a child in violation of custody rights — is the most common form involving children and is addressed internationally by the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction. Other categories include bride kidnapping (abduction for forced marriage, practiced in parts of Central Asia and the Caucasus), political kidnapping and hostage-taking by armed groups, and "tiger kidnapping," in which a hostage is held to coerce a relative into committing a crime such as a robbery.

History and law

The word derives from "kid" (child) and "nab" (to snatch), referring to 17th-century thefts of children for labor in the American colonies. In the United States, the 1932 abduction and murder of Charles Lindbergh's infant son prompted the Federal Kidnapping Act (the "Lindbergh Law"), which made transporting a kidnapping victim across state lines a federal offense and facilitated FBI involvement. Under modern statutes penalties are severe, rising to life imprisonment, and aggravated when the victim is injured or killed. The AMBER Alert system, established in 1996, disseminates rapid public notification of child abductions in the United States.

Statistics

Global statistics are unreliable because reporting varies widely, but countries including Mexico, Venezuela, Nigeria, and Iraq have experienced notorious kidnapping-for-ransom economies targeting both wealthy residents and foreign nationals. Stranger abductions of children, though heavily publicized, are statistically rare compared with family abductions.
