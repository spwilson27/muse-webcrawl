Tala (IAST: tāla), from a Sanskrit word meaning "clap" or "palm of the hand", is the term for the rhythmic framework of Indian classical music: a repeating cycle of beats (matras) within which melody and improvisation are organised. Together with raga, the melodic framework, tala structures both Hindustani (North Indian) and Carnatic (South Indian) performance. Performers and listeners track the cycle by clapping, waving, and counting, and improvise against it, returning to the sam—the first beat of the cycle—with effects of rhythmic resolution.

Structure

A tala cycle is divided into sections (vibhag), some clapped (tali, "filled") and some marked by a wave (khali, "empty"). In Hindustani music, common talas include Teental (16 beats, 4+4+4+4), Dadra (6), Rupak (7), Jhaptal (10) and Keherwa (8). The drummer—on tabla in the north—plays a characteristic pattern (theka) that outlines the cycle, elaborating it in solos and accompanying the soloist's improvisations with cross-rhythms (tihai patterns repeated three times to land on sam are a standard flourish).

Carnatic tala system

Carnatic music classifies talas systematically from three elements (angas: anudrutam, drutam, laghu) combined into seven families (suladi sapta tala), yielding, with five varieties of laghu, 35 basic talas, and a theoretical 175 when further subdivisions are counted. Adi talam (8 beats) is the most common. Vocalists often keep tala visibly with hand gestures while singing.

Comparison

Unlike Western metre, which accents a fixed bar, tala is cyclical and additive: cycles range from 3 to over 100 beats (the Simhanandana tala of Carnatic tradition has 128). Rhythm in Indian music is thus measured by return to the cycle's beginning rather than by a continuous pulse of bars.
