Henry George theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Henry George theorem, named after the American political economist Henry George, states that under certain conditions, aggregate land rent in a city or jurisdiction equals the total expenditure on public goods. In other words, the value created by public investment — roads, schools, public safety — is capitalised into land values, so a tax on land rent can finance the public goods that generate the rent without distortion.

Statement and conditions

The result was formalised by economists including Richard Arnott and Joseph Stiglitz (1979). It holds under conditions such as a fixed supply of land, an optimally sized population, and public goods that are pure (non-rival and non-excludable). When city size is optimal, total land rent exactly equals spending on public goods, so the land-value tax is just sufficient to fund them.

Implications

The theorem provides a theoretical foundation for Georgist proposals to fund government from land rents rather than taxes on labour or capital, and it is a standard result in urban economics and local public finance. Critics note that its assumptions are stylised, but it remains an important benchmark in the economics of land taxation and city size.
