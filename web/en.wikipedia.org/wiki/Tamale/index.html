Tamale

A tamale (Spanish: tamal, from Nahuatl tamalli) is a traditional Mesoamerican dish made of masa, a dough made from nixtamalized corn, which is steamed in a corn husk or banana leaf. The wrapping may be discarded before eating or used as a plate. Tamales can be filled with meats, cheeses, fruits, vegetables, herbs, chilies, or any preparation according to taste, and both the filling and the cooking liquid may be seasoned.

History

Tamales originated in Mesoamerica as early as 8000 to 5000 BC. Among the Aztec and Maya they served as portable food for armies, hunters, and travellers, and played a role in ritual offerings. Friar Bernardino de Sahagún documented many varieties of tamales eaten by the Aztecs in the 16th century. From Mexico the dish spread throughout Latin America, where it acquired regional identities: humitas in the Andes, hallacas in Venezuela, and many others.

Preparation

Making tamales is traditionally a communal activity. In Mexico, families gather for a tamalada — a tamale-making party — especially around Christmas and Candlemas (Día de la Candelaria, 2 February), when whoever found the figurine in the Rosca de Reyes cake is expected to provide tamales. The masa is beaten with lard or shortening until light, spread on soaked corn husks, filled, folded, and steamed upright in a large pot (a tamalera).

Regional varieties

Mexican varieties include red chile pork tamales, chicken in salsa verde, sweet tamales with raisins or pineapple, and the large banana-leaf tamales of Oaxaca. In the United States, tamales are a staple of Mexican-American cuisine; a distinct Mississippi Delta hot tamale tradition also developed in the 20th century.
