Concordat of 1801

The Concordat of 1801 was an agreement between Napoleon Bonaparte, as First Consul of France, and Pope Pius VII, signed on 15 July 1801 and promulgated at Easter 1802. It restored the position of the Roman Catholic Church in France after the upheavals of the French Revolution, while preserving many revolutionary changes and subordinating the Church to the French state.

Terms

The Concordat recognised Catholicism as the religion of the "great majority of the French citizens" (though not as a state religion), allowed churches to reopen for worship, and provided for state salaries for bishops and parish priests. In return, the Church renounced its claims to the church lands confiscated during the Revolution. Bishops would be nominated by the First Consul and canonically instituted by the Pope; dioceses were redrawn, and all existing bishops were required to resign. Napoleon unilaterally appended the Organic Articles of 1802, which further restricted papal authority in France, to the Church's dismay.

Duration and legacy

The Concordat governed church–state relations in France until the law of 1905 on the separation of church and state abrogated it. However, it remains in force in Alsace-Moselle, which was part of Germany in 1905, where clergy of recognised religions are still paid by the state.
