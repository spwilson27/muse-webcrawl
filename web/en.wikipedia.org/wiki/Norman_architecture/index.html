The term Norman architecture is used to categorise styles of Romanesque architecture developed by the Normans in the various lands under their dominion in the 11th and 12th centuries, especially Normandy, England and Norman Sicily. It is characterised by massive proportions, round arches, barrel and groin vaults, and relatively sparse ornament.

Characteristics

Norman buildings typically employ thick walls, round-headed windows and doorways (often richly carved with chevron/zigzag moulding in later work), cylindrical or compound piers, and square crossing towers. Castles — keeps such as the White Tower of the Tower of London and Norwich Castle — applied the same vocabulary to military architecture: massive square keeps of several storeys entered at first-floor level.

Norman England

After the Conquest of 1066, the Normans rebuilt England's major churches in stone on an unprecedented scale: Canterbury, Winchester, Ely, Norwich and Durham cathedrals all began as Norman projects. Durham (begun 1093) pioneered the rib vault and pointed arch in its high vaults, pointing the way toward Gothic. Parish churches across England retain Norman doorways, fonts and chancel arches; the style in England is sometimes called "English Romanesque".

Sicily and southern Italy

Norman Sicily fused Romanesque structure with Byzantine mosaic and Islamic ornament — "Arab-Norman" art seen in the Palatine Chapel in Palermo and the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale, now UNESCO-listed.

Transition

In the later 12th century the style gave way to Early English Gothic, though round-arch Norman features persisted in provincial work. A 19th-century "Norman Revival" (Romanesque Revival) reused the vocabulary for churches, town halls and universities.
