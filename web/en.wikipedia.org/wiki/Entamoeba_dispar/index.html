Entamoeba dispar

Entamoeba dispar is an amoeba in the genus Entamoeba that lives in the human large intestine. It is morphologically identical to the pathogen Entamoeba histolytica, the cause of amoebic dysentery and amoebic liver abscess, but E. dispar is a harmless commensal that does not cause disease and requires no treatment. The two species can only be distinguished by molecular or immunological methods, not by ordinary light microscopy of cysts or trophozoites.

History

The distinction was first proposed by Émile Brumpt in 1925, who suggested that there were two species — one pathogenic and one not — but the idea was largely ignored for decades. Biochemical (isoenzyme) studies by Peter Sargeaunt in the 1970s–80s revived it, and DNA-based analyses in the early 1990s confirmed that "non-pathogenic E. histolytica" was a separate species, formally re-described as E. dispar by Diamond and Clark in 1993. The World Health Organization subsequently recommended that diagnoses based on microscopy alone be reported as the "E. histolytica/E. dispar complex".

Clinical significance

E. dispar is considerably more common than E. histolytica worldwide — by a factor often estimated at about ten to one — so most historical "amoebiasis" diagnoses in asymptomatic people probably represented E. dispar. Because it is non-invasive and does not ingest red blood cells or produce the Gal/GalNAc lectin-mediated tissue damage characteristic of its pathogenic relative, infection with E. dispar should not be treated with amoebicidal drugs. Accurate species-specific diagnosis by stool antigen detection or PCR prevents unnecessary treatment and permits correct management of true E. histolytica infection. A third look-alike species, Entamoeba moshkovskii, is also found in human stool and may be associated with mild gastrointestinal symptoms in some studies.
