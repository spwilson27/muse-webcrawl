A Brodmann area is a region of the cerebral cortex, in the human or other primate brain, defined by its cytoarchitecture, or histological structure and organization of cells. The classification was published in 1909 by the German anatomist Korbinian Brodmann, who examined Nissl-stained sections of cortex from many species and divided the human cortex into 52 numbered regions based on differences in the arrangement, density and size of neurons across the cortical layers.

History

Brodmann's 1909 monograph Vergleichende Lokalisationslehre der Großhirnrinde ("Comparative localization studies of the cerebral cortex") presented maps of the cortex of humans and numerous other mammals. Although later anatomists renumbered, split or renamed some regions — for example, von Economo and Koskinas proposed a different parcellation in 1925 — Brodmann's numbering remains the most widely used shorthand for cortical locations in neuroscience, neuroanatomy and clinical neurology.

Important areas

  • Areas 3, 1 and 2 — primary somatosensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus.
  • Area 4 — primary motor cortex in the precentral gyrus.
  • Area 17 — primary visual cortex (V1) around the calcarine sulcus.
  • Areas 41 and 42 — primary auditory cortex in Heschl's gyri.
  • Area 44 and 45 — Broca's area, involved in speech production.
  • Area 22 (posterior part) — Wernicke's area, involved in language comprehension.
  • Areas 9, 10, 11 and 46 — prefrontal cortex, involved in executive function.

Criticism

Brodmann areas were defined purely by microscopic anatomy, and their boundaries do not always correspond to functional boundaries; some areas are now known to contain multiple functionally distinct subregions, while some functions are distributed across several areas. Nevertheless, the scheme remains a common reference framework.
