Calchas (Ancient Greek: Κάλχας, genitive Κάλχαντος) is an Argive mantis, or seer, in Greek mythology, famed as the chief diviner of the Greek army during the Trojan War. He was the son of Thestor and a priest of Apollo, from whom he received the gift of prophecy, including knowledge of past, present and future.

Role in the Trojan War

In Homer's Iliad, Calchas plays a pivotal role at Aulis and at Troy. He interpreted the omen of a snake devouring a sparrow and her eight chicks as foretelling ten years of war. When a plague struck the Greek camp in the first book of the Iliad, Calchas revealed — under Achilles' protection — that Apollo was angry because Agamemnon had refused to ransom Chryseis, the daughter of the priest Chryses. According to other traditions, Calchas also declared that Troy could not fall without Achilles and Philoctetes, and was connected with the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis and the interpretation of omens surrounding the Wooden Horse.

Death

Later tradition held that Calchas died of shame at Colophon after being bested in a contest of prophecy by the seer Mopsus, fulfilling an oracle that he would die when he met a better prophet than himself. Strabo and other sources report variants of the story.

Legacy

Calchas appears in numerous later retellings of the Trojan cycle, including works by Euripides, Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida (as a Trojan defector), and Goethe's Faust.
