The canon law of the Catholic Church is "how the Church organises and governs herself". It is the system of laws and legal principles made and enforced by the hierarchical authorities of the Catholic Church to regulate its external organisation and government and to order and direct the activities of Catholics toward the Church's mission. It was the first modern Western legal system and is the oldest continuously functioning legal system in the West.

History

Canon law developed from the canons of early church councils, decrees of popes, and collections such as Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140), which harmonised a millennium of canons. Medieval canon law profoundly influenced the development of Western civil law, including concepts of marriage, property, and legal procedure. The first comprehensive codification was the 1917 Code of Canon Law promulgated by Pope Benedict XV.

Current law

The Latin Church is governed by the 1983 Code of Canon Law, promulgated by Pope John Paul II, containing 1,752 canons arranged in seven books covering general norms, the people of God, the teaching and sanctifying functions of the Church, temporal goods, sanctions, and processes. The Eastern Catholic Churches follow the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (1990). The supreme legislator is the pope, and diocesan bishops legislate for their dioceses within the bounds of universal law.

Marriage and annulment

Canon law regulates the sacraments, including marriage: because the Church teaches that a valid sacramental marriage is indissoluble, tribunals may declare a marriage null — an annulment — where it is judged that a true marriage never came into existence because of an impediment or defect of consent.
