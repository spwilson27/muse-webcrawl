Sex education

Sex education, also referred to as sexual health education or sexuality education, is instruction on issues relating to human sexuality, including human sexual anatomy, sexual reproduction, sexual activity, reproductive health, contraception, sexually transmitted infections, consent, and relationships. It is commonly provided in schools as part of health curricula, though its content, timing, and compulsory status vary widely between countries and regions and are frequently politically contested.

Approaches

Comprehensive sex education covers contraception and safer sex alongside abstinence, and is advocated by UNESCO and most public-health bodies as part of "comprehensive sexuality education" (CSE), which also addresses gender, rights, and consent. Abstinence-only programmes teach refraining from sex until marriage as the sole or emphasised strategy and typically omit or disparage contraception. Program evaluations — including reviews by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and UNESCO — have generally found that comprehensive programmes do not hasten sexual debut and are associated with reduced risk behaviour, while abstinence-only programmes have not shown sustained effects on behaviour.

Content areas

Curricula typically cover puberty and anatomy; reproduction and pregnancy; contraception; prevention of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections; healthy relationships, consent, and prevention of sexual violence; and, increasingly, online safety and media literacy. Inclusion of LGBTQ+ topics varies by jurisdiction.

By country

Many European countries, led by Sweden (compulsory from 1955), have long-standing school programmes. In the United States, requirements are set by states and districts, producing wide variation; federal funding policy has swung between abstinence-only and evidence-based programmes. In much of the world, provision remains limited, and cultural or religious objections shape what is taught.

History

Organised sex education grew from early-20th-century social hygiene movements and was expanded after the Second World War and again in response to the HIV/AIDS epidemic of the 1980s, which prompted many countries to introduce or strengthen school-based programmes.
