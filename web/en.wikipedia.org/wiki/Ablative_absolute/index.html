The ablative absolute (Latin: ablātīvus absolūtus, "loosened ablative") is a grammatical construction of classical Latin in which a noun or pronoun and a participle — or another noun or an adjective — both stand in the ablative case, forming a phrase grammatically independent of ("absolute" from, i.e. detached from) the rest of the sentence. It typically supplies background circumstances of time, cause, condition or concession, and is one of the most characteristic constructions of Latin prose.

Form

The noun and its modifier agree in the ablative. With a present participle, Caesare duce is not possible — instead one finds patterns such as urbe captā, "the city having been captured", or hīs rēbus gestīs, "these things having been done". Because Latin lacks a perfect active participle of most verbs, the passive perfect participle predominates. Where the verb "to be" is implied, two nouns or a noun and adjective stand alone: Cicerōne cōnsule, "when Cicero was consul".

Meaning and translation

An ablative absolute is usually best rendered in English by a subordinate clause introduced by "when", "after", "since", "because", "although" or "if", according to context: signō datō, mīlitēs impetum fēcērunt — "when the signal had been given, the soldiers charged". By convention, the subject of the phrase does not appear as the subject or object of the main clause; deviations occur in later Latin.

History and parallels

The construction developed from the instrumental-locative uses of the Indo-European ablative. Close analogues exist in other languages: the genitive absolute of Greek, the locative absolute of Sanskrit, and the (historically unrelated) nominative absolute of English ("the weather being fine, we set out").
