Algal bloom

An algal bloom or algae bloom is a rapid increase or accumulation in the population of algae in freshwater or marine water systems. It is often recognised by the discoloration in the water from the algae's pigments. The term algae encompasses many types of aquatic photosynthetic organisms, both macroscopic multicellular organisms like seaweed and microscopic unicellular organisms like cyanobacteria. Algal bloom commonly refers to the rapid growth of microscopic unicellular algae, not macroscopic algae.

Algal blooms are the result of a nutrient, like nitrogen or phosphorus from various sources (for example fertilizer runoff or other forms of nutrient pollution), entering the aquatic system and causing excessive growth of algae. An algal bloom affects the whole ecosystem. Consequences range from the benign feeding of higher trophic levels to more harmful effects like blocking sunlight from other organisms, causing a depletion of oxygen levels in the water, and, depending on the organism, secreting toxins into the water. Blooms that produce toxins are called harmful algal blooms (HABs).

Causes

Blooms result from an excess of nutrients, particularly phosphorus and nitrogen, a process known as eutrophication. Agricultural runoff, wastewater discharge, and atmospheric deposition are major human sources. Warm water temperatures, abundant sunlight, and slow-moving water favour bloom formation. Climate change is expected to increase bloom frequency and intensity in many regions.

Effects

As blooms decay, bacterial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic "dead zones" that can kill fish and invertebrates. Some species, such as Karenia brevis on the Gulf coast of Florida (a "red tide"), produce toxins that accumulate in shellfish and can cause illness in humans and mass mortality of marine life. In freshwater, cyanobacterial blooms can contaminate drinking water supplies; the 2014 Toledo water crisis, caused by microcystin from a Lake Erie bloom, left some 400,000 people without safe tap water for two days.

Monitoring and control

Satellite remote sensing and in-situ sampling are used to detect and forecast blooms. Long-term control depends mainly on reducing nutrient inputs through wastewater treatment, agricultural best practices, and buffer strips along waterways.
