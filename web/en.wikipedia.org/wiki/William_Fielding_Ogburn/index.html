William Fielding Ogburn


Born June 29, 1886, Butler, Georgia, U.S.
Died April 27, 1959 (aged 72)
Known for Cultural lag; Social Change
Field Sociology

William Fielding Ogburn (June 29, 1886 – April 27, 1959) was an American sociologist best known for his concept of cultural lag and for promoting the use of statistics in social science.

Career

Ogburn was educated at Mercer University and Columbia University, where he earned his doctorate in 1912. He taught at Reed College, the University of Washington, and Columbia University before joining the University of Chicago in 1927, where he remained until his retirement in 1951. He served as president of the American Sociological Society in 1929.

Cultural lag

In his 1922 work Social Change with Respect to Culture and Original Nature, Ogburn proposed that material culture — technology and inventions — changes faster than non-material culture, such as laws, customs, and beliefs. The resulting gap, which he called cultural lag, produces social maladjustment until adaptive culture catches up. Ogburn also compiled lists of the social effects of inventions and later directed the research staff behind President Herbert Hoover's report Recent Social Trends in the United States (1933).

Legacy

Ogburn's insistence on quantitative methods helped move American sociology toward empirical, data-driven research. The concept of cultural lag remains widely taught as a framework for understanding the social consequences of technological change.
