Spuyten Duyvil Creek

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spuyten Duyvil Creek is a short tidal waterway in New York City connecting the Hudson River to the Harlem River Ship Canal and the Harlem River, separating the island of Manhattan from the Bronx and the mainland United States. Together with the Harlem River, it forms the northern boundary of Manhattan. The creek's name is Dutch; proposed translations include "Spouting Devil", referring to its treacherous tidal currents, and "Spewing Devil" or "Spite the Devil". The Bronx neighbourhood on its northern shore is also called Spuyten Duyvil.

History

The original creek meandered north of Marble Hill in a winding course. Native Lenape called the area Shorakapkok. In 1895 the Harlem River Ship Canal was cut directly across the base of the peninsula, straightening and deepening the channel for navigation and leaving Marble Hill — politically part of Manhattan — on the mainland side of the canal; the old creek bed was filled in by 1914. The creek is spanned by the Spuyten Duyvil (railroad swing) Bridge, the Henry Hudson Bridge (opened 1936), and the Broadway Bridge.

In folklore

The name is celebrated in Washington Irving's Knickerbocker's History of New York, which tells how the trumpeter Antony Van Corlaer attempted to swim the creek "in spite of the devil" to warn of a British attack and drowned, his ghostly trumpet said to be heard in storms. A variant of the tale later became associated with the Devil's Swimming Pool legend. The creek also appears in accounts of the Revolutionary War, when its crossing was strategically important to operations around Kingsbridge.

Transportation

The creek is the route of Amtrak's Empire Connection into Penn Station and of Metro-North's Hudson Line, whose Spuyten Duyvil station overlooks the water near the site of a fatal 2013 derailment just north of the station. Commercial traffic between the Hudson and Long Island Sound passes through the creek and canal daily.
