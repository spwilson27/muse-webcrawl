The Gulf of Corinth (Greek: Κορινθιακός Κόλπος, Korinthiakós Kólpos) is a deep inlet of the Ionian Sea in Greece, separating the Peloponnese peninsula from the rest of the Greek mainland. It is bounded in the east by the Isthmus of Corinth, which is cut by the Corinth Canal, and in the west by the Strait of Rion, which widens into the Gulf of Patras and connects the Corinthian Gulf with the Ionian Sea.

Geography

The gulf is about 130 kilometres (81 miles) long and between 8.4 and 32 kilometres (5.2 to 19.9 miles) wide. It is bordered by the regional units of Aetolia-Acarnania and Phocis to the north, Boeotia and Attica to the northeast, and Corinthia and Achaea to the south. The port of Corinth lies near its eastern end; other towns on its shores include Itea, Galaxidi, Nafpaktos (the ancient Naupactus, near the site of the Battle of Lepanto of 1571), and Aigio.

The Rio–Antirrio Bridge, one of the world's longest multi-span cable-stayed bridges (2,380 metres), crosses the strait at the gulf's western mouth and was completed in 2004.

Geology

The gulf occupies an active continental rift — the Corinth Rift — where the crust is stretching at rates among the fastest observed on Earth, up to about 15 mm per year in the west. This extension makes the region one of the most seismically active in Europe; significant earthquakes struck in 1981 (Alkyonides) and 1995 (Aigio). The rift is a young analogue of the processes that open ocean basins, and it is intensively studied by geologists.

History

In antiquity the gulf was a strategic waterway: Corinth grew rich controlling the portage across its isthmus, and the Athenian, Spartan, and later Macedonian and Roman fleets contested its waters. In 479 BC the walls at the Isthmus were a line of Greek defence against Persia. The medieval and early-modern period saw Venetian and Ottoman struggles over its ports; the decisive naval Battle of Lepanto was fought near its western entrance in October 1571.
