Church mode

SystemModal theory of Western chant and early polyphony

A church mode is one of the modes of the medieval system of melodic classification, a set of scales and melodic conventions used to organise plainchant and, later, composed polyphony. The system comprised eight modes, each defined by its final (tonic) note, its range (ambitus) and characteristic melodic formulas.

The eight modes

The modes are arranged in four pairs on the finals D, E, F and G. The "authentic" form of each mode spans the octave above its final; the "plagal" (Hypo-) form spans the fourth below to the fifth above:

  • Mode I — Dorian (final D, authentic); Mode II — Hypodorian (plagal)
  • Mode III — Phrygian; Mode IV — Hypophrygian (final E)
  • Mode V — Lydian; Mode VI — Hypolydian (final F)
  • Mode VII — Mixolydian; Mode VIII — Hypomixolydian (final G)

The names were borrowed, by way of a medieval misunderstanding, from the Greek harmoniai. In 1547 Heinrich Glarean's Dodecachordon added four modes, including those on A (Aeolian) and C (Ionian) — the ancestors of the modern natural minor and major scales.

Use

Modes governed Gregorian chant classification (each psalm tone corresponds to a mode) and Renaissance composition, before yielding to the major–minor tonal system in the 17th century. They survive in folk music, modal jazz (e.g. Miles Davis's "So What", in Dorian) and rock, and in modern theory teaching, where the modes are presented as rotations of the major scale.
