The Whigs were a political party in the parliaments of England, Scotland, Great Britain, Ireland and the United Kingdom, active from 1678 to 1859. Alongside their rivals the Tories, they formed one half of Britain's first two-party system.

Origins and beliefs

The Whigs arose during the Exclusion Crisis (1678–1681), seeking to bar the Catholic James, Duke of York, from the throne; their derisive name came from the Scottish whiggamores (cattle-drivers). Whig principles centred on the supremacy of Parliament over the Crown, resistance to absolute monarchy and to Catholicism, and (increasingly) support for Protestant Dissenters. After the Glorious Revolution of 1688, Whig grandees such as the Junto lords dominated government.

Dominance and reform

Under Robert Walpole and his successors the Whigs enjoyed long periods of power in the 18th century, controlling patronage through a landed aristocratic network. In the 19th century, Whig and reforming governments passed the Reform Act 1832, abolished slavery in the British Empire (1833), reformed municipal corporations, and under Lord John Russell and Viscount Palmerston pursued cautious liberal reform and free trade.

Legacy

In 1859 the Whigs merged with Peelites and Radicals to form the Liberal Party. "Whig history" — the progressive interpretation of the past as a march toward liberty — takes its name from the party's outlook, and the American Whig Party borrowed the name directly.
