Liberalism in Canada

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberalism in Canada is one of the country's dominant political traditions, embodied principally by the Liberal Party of Canada, which has governed federally for much of the period since Confederation in 1867 and is often called Canada's "natural governing party". Canadian liberalism combines support for individual rights and freedoms, a market economy with a social safety net, and a strong federal government active in nation-building.

History

The tradition descends from the Reform movements of the 1830s–40s and figures such as Robert Baldwin and Louis-Hippolyte LaFontaine, who achieved responsible government. Under Wilfrid Laurier (prime minister 1896–1911), the first francophone to lead the country, the party consolidated its pan-Canadian, brokerage character. In the twentieth century, governments of William Lyon Mackenzie King, Louis St. Laurent and Lester B. Pearson built much of the modern welfare state, including public health insurance and the Canada Pension Plan, while Pierre Trudeau's governments (1968–79, 1980–84) patriated the constitution and entrenched the Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982).

Ideology

Canadian liberalism is generally centrist to centre-left: fiscally moderate, socially liberal, supportive of bilingualism, multiculturalism and immigration, and committed to federalism. Provincial Liberal parties vary considerably, and in Quebec the tradition has a distinct history intertwined with the province's politics.
