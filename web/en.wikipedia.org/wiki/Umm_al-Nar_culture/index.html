Umm al-Nar culture

PeriodBronze Age, c. 2600–2000 BCE
RegionSoutheastern Arabia (UAE and Oman)
Named afterUmm al-Nar island, Abu Dhabi

The Umm al-Nar culture is a Bronze Age culture of southeastern Arabia, flourishing from roughly 2600 to 2000 BCE in the area of the present-day United Arab Emirates and northern Oman. It is named after the island of Umm al-Nar ("mother of fire") off Abu Dhabi, where Danish archaeologists first excavated its characteristic tombs in the late 1950s.

Characteristics

The culture is best known for its large circular communal tombs, built of dressed stone and divided into multiple chambers, some over 12 metres in diameter, found at sites including Hili, Bat, and Mleiha. Settlements included round towers and well-built houses, and the economy combined oasis agriculture (notably date palms), copper mining and smelting in the Hajar Mountains, fishing, and long-distance trade. Umm al-Nar communities participated in the trade network linking Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley, exporting copper from the land the Mesopotamians called Magan.

Legacy

The culture declined around 2000 BCE and was succeeded by the Wadi Suq culture. The archaeological sites of the Umm al-Nar period at Hili and elsewhere in Al Ain have been inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
