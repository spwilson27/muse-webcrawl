Henry Walter Bates

Henry Walter Bates (8 February 1825 – 16 February 1892) was an English naturalist and explorer who spent eleven years (1848–1859) collecting specimens in the Amazon rainforest and gave the first scientific account of mimicry in animals — the resemblance of a harmless species to a warningly coloured unpalatable one, now called Batesian mimicry in his honour.

Amazon expedition

Bates travelled to Brazil with Alfred Russel Wallace in 1848, funding the expedition by selling specimens. After Wallace returned to England, Bates remained, exploring the tributaries of the Amazon and collecting some 14,712 species, around 8,000 of them new to science, mostly insects.

Mimicry and evolution

His 1862 paper, read to the Linnean Society, showed how edible butterflies of the family Pieridae resembled distasteful Heliconiidae, and interpreted the resemblance as the product of natural selection — striking early support for Darwin's theory, which Darwin praised. His travel book The Naturalist on the River Amazons (1863) remains a classic of natural history writing.

Later life

Bates served for nearly three decades as assistant secretary of the Royal Geographical Society and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1881.
