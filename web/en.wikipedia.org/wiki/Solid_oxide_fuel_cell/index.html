Solid oxide fuel cell

A solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) is an electrochemical conversion device that produces electricity directly from oxidising a fuel. It is distinguished by its use of a solid oxide material as the electrolyte. SOFCs operate at very high temperatures, typically between 600 and 1,000 °C, which allows them to use a variety of fuels and to achieve high electrical efficiencies, but imposes demanding requirements on materials.

Design

The electrolyte is a dense ceramic, most commonly yttria-stabilised zirconia (YSZ), which conducts oxide ions (O2−) at high temperature. The cathode, where oxygen from air is reduced, is usually a perovskite such as lanthanum strontium manganite (LSM); the anode, where fuel is oxidised, is typically a nickel–YSZ cermet. Oxide ions migrate through the electrolyte from cathode to anode, while electrons flow through an external circuit, producing power. Cells are assembled into stacks in planar or tubular geometries.

Fuels and efficiency

SOFCs can run on hydrogen, natural gas, biogas, syngas and other hydrocarbon fuels, which may be reformed internally thanks to the high operating temperature. Electrical efficiencies of 50–60% are achievable, and in combined heat and power systems total efficiencies can exceed 80%. Reversible cells can also operate as electrolysers (SOECs) to produce hydrogen.

Advantages and challenges

Advantages include fuel flexibility, low emissions, and high-grade waste heat. Challenges include long start-up times, thermal cycling stresses, chromium poisoning of cathodes, sulphur intolerance, and the cost and durability of high-temperature materials. Research focuses on lowering operating temperatures to the 500–700 °C range ("intermediate-temperature" SOFCs) using electrolytes such as gadolinia-doped ceria.

Applications

SOFC systems are deployed for stationary distributed generation and combined heat and power for buildings and data centres, and are studied for auxiliary power units and large-scale power plants with carbon capture.
