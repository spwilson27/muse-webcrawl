MHC class I molecules are one of two primary classes of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules (the other is MHC class II) and are found on the cell surface of all nucleated cells in the bodies of vertebrates. They also occur on platelets, but not on red blood cells. Their function is to display peptide fragments of proteins from within the cell to cytotoxic T cells; this will trigger an immediate response from the immune system against a particular non-self antigen displayed with the help of an MHC class I protein. Because MHC class I molecules present peptides derived from cytosolic proteins, the pathway of MHC class I presentation is often called the cytosolic or endogenous pathway.

Structure

Each molecule consists of a polymorphic α heavy chain of about 45 kDa non-covalently associated with a smaller, invariant β2-microglobulin subunit. The α chain has three extracellular domains: α1 and α2 together form a groove with a floor of β-sheet and walls of α-helices that binds a single peptide, while the α3 domain anchors the molecule in the membrane and binds the CD8 co-receptor. In humans the classical class I heavy chains are encoded by the highly polymorphic HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C loci; the polymorphism is concentrated in the peptide-binding groove, so different alleles present different peptide repertoires.

Antigen processing and presentation

Proteins in the cytosol are degraded by the proteasome (or immunoproteasome) into short peptides. These are transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by the TAP (transporter associated with antigen processing) complex. Within the ER, the nascent MHC class I molecule is stabilised by chaperones — calnexin, calreticulin, ERp57 and tapasin — forming the peptide-loading complex. A peptide of suitable affinity binds, the complex is released, and the mature molecule travels through the Golgi to the cell surface. If a cytotoxic T cell's T-cell receptor recognises the peptide as foreign — for example from a virus or a mutated tumour protein — it kills the presenting cell.

Immune evasion and NK cells

Many viruses down-regulate MHC class I to hide from T cells. Natural killer (NK) cells counter this strategy through "missing self" recognition: inhibitory receptors such as KIRs detect the absence of normal class I expression and license the NK cell to attack. Some viruses in turn encode decoy class I mimics, illustrating the evolutionary arms race around this pathway.
