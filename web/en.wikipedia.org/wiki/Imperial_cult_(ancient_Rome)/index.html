The imperial cult of ancient Rome identified emperors and some members of their families with the divinely sanctioned authority of the Roman State. Its framework was based on Roman and Greek precedents, and was formulated during the early Principate of Augustus. It rapidly became established throughout the Empire and its provinces.

Origins

Roman tradition held that Romulus, the legendary founder of Rome, had been deified after death as Quirinus. In the late Republic, Julius Caesar accepted honours that approached divine status in his lifetime: a temple to his clemency, a priest (flamen), and a statue among the gods. After his assassination in 44 BC, the Senate formally deified him as Divus Iulius, and a comet seen during games in his honour was interpreted as his soul ascending to heaven.

Augustus and the Principate

Augustus, Caesar's adopted heir, styled himself Divi filius ("son of the deified one"). He was careful, however, to avoid claiming living divinity at Rome itself: he permitted temples to "Rome and Augustus" in the provinces, and in Italy encouraged the cult of his genius (guardian spirit) and the Lares Augusti at crossroad shrines. After his death in AD 14 the Senate voted him divine honours, and his widow Livia became his priestess.

Development

Subsequent emperors were deified after death by senatorial vote (consecratio), provided their reigns were judged worthy; unpopular rulers suffered damnatio memoriae instead. Living emperors received cult in the provinces, especially in the Greek East, where ruler cult had Hellenistic precedents. Provincial assemblies maintained temples and high priests of the imperial cult, and festivals and games were held in the emperor's honour. Refusal to sacrifice before the emperor's image became a test of loyalty, bringing Christians into conflict with the state during persecutions.

Decline

The cult declined as Christianity gained imperial favour in the 4th century. Constantine restricted some practices, and by the reign of Theodosius I pagan cults, including emperor worship, were suppressed, though the ceremonial aura of divine kingship persisted in Christian imperial ideology.
