The Sunset Strip is the 1.6-mile (2.6 km) stretch of Sunset Boulevard that passes through the city of West Hollywood, California. It extends from West Hollywood's eastern border with Los Angeles to its western border with Beverly Hills. Famous for its boutiques, restaurants, rock clubs and enormous hand-painted billboards, the Strip has been a centre of American popular culture — from the glamour of 1930s nightclubs to the counterculture of the 1960s and the heavy-metal scene of the 1980s.

History

Because West Hollywood was an unincorporated area of Los Angeles County until 1984, the Strip escaped the city's stricter policing, and in the 1920s it attracted gambling clubs, speakeasies and stars from the nearby studios. Glamorous venues such as Ciro's, the Mocambo and the Trocadero hosted Hollywood royalty during the Golden Age. In the 1960s the Strip became a hub of youth culture: the Whisky a Go Go (opened 1964) launched go-go dancing and hosted The Doors as house band, and the November 1966 "curfew riots" — immortalised in Buffalo Springfield's "For What It's Worth" — pitted teenage club-goers against sheriff's deputies.

In the 1970s and 1980s the Strip's clubs — the Roxy Theatre, the Rainbow Bar and Grill, Gazzarri's and later the Viper Room — were the launchpad of glam metal, with bands such as Van Halen, Mötley Crüe and Guns N' Roses. The towering rock-and-roll billboards above the boulevard became a signature of record-label promotion. The area today mixes luxury hotels (the Andaz West Hollywood, the "Riot Hyatt" of rock legend; Chateau Marmont just off the Strip) with comedy at the Comedy Store and Laugh Factory, alongside ongoing debate over development and preservation.

In popular culture

The Strip lent its name to the television series 77 Sunset Strip (1958–64) and the 2012 musical film Rock of Ages, and appears in countless films, songs and novels about Los Angeles nightlife.
