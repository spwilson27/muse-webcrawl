Placebo effect

A placebo (/pləˈsiːboʊ/ plə-SEE-boh) is a substance or treatment which is designed to have no therapeutic value. Common placebos include inert tablets (like sugar pills), inert injections (like saline), sham surgery, and other procedures. The placebo effect is the phenomenon in which a person's condition improves after receiving such a treatment, even though the treatment itself is inert. Conversely, negative effects experienced after an inert treatment are called the nocebo effect.

Overview

Placebo effects are genuine psychobiological events attributable to the overall therapeutic context: the patient's expectations, the ritual of treatment, the clinician–patient relationship, and prior conditioning. They are most prominent for subjective symptoms such as pain, nausea, fatigue and depression, and are generally weak or absent for objective disease progression, such as tumour growth or infection. Placebos can, however, produce measurable physiological changes — for example, placebo analgesia can be partly blocked by the opioid antagonist naloxone, showing that it engages the body's endogenous opioid system.

In drug development, new treatments are routinely tested against placebos in randomised controlled trials, ideally double-blinded so that neither patients nor investigators know who receives the active drug. A drug must outperform its placebo control to be considered efficacious. The apparent size of the placebo response in trials has been reported to grow over recent decades, particularly in the United States, complicating drug development for conditions such as pain and depression.

History

The word placebo is Latin for "I shall please". It entered medical usage in the late 18th century to describe medicines given more to please than to benefit the patient. In 1955, Henry K. Beecher published the influential paper "The Powerful Placebo", claiming that about 35% of patients responded to placebos across a range of conditions. Later re-analyses tempered this claim: much of the apparent effect in early studies reflected natural history of disease, regression to the mean, and reporting biases. A landmark 2001 meta-analysis by Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche found little evidence of large clinical effects overall, though a possible small effect on pain. Subsequent research has confirmed robust placebo effects in specific domains, especially pain, and has identified underlying neurobiology.

Mechanisms

Expectation and conditioning

Two complementary mechanisms are widely accepted. Expectation: when a patient consciously expects relief, brain circuits involved in reward and anxiety modulate symptom perception. Classical conditioning: after repeated pairing of an active drug with a treatment ritual (a pill's taste, a clinic visit), the ritual alone can trigger the drug-like response — including conditioned immunosuppression demonstrated in animals and humans.

Neurobiology

Neuroimaging studies show that placebo analgesia increases activity in prefrontal and brainstem pain-modulating regions and reduces activity in pain-processing areas. Placebo treatment in Parkinson's disease can trigger dopamine release in the striatum. Genetic variation, for example in the COMT gene, may contribute to individual differences in placebo responsiveness (sometimes termed the "placebome").

Nocebo effect

The nocebo effect is the worsening of symptoms or occurrence of side effects after an inert treatment, driven by negative expectations. Patients in the placebo arms of trials frequently report the very side effects listed in the consent form for the active drug. Nocebo effects are a significant cause of discontinuation in clinical trials and of reported intolerance to generic medicines and statins.

Open-label placebos

Strikingly, some trials have found benefits even when patients are told they are receiving a placebo — so-called "open-label" or "honest" placebos — in conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome and chronic low back pain. This suggests deception is not strictly necessary, and that the treatment ritual and positive framing themselves carry therapeutic weight.

Ethics

Deceptive placebo use in routine clinical practice is generally considered unethical because it undermines informed consent and trust. Placebo controls in trials are accepted when no proven effective treatment exists, or when withholding treatment poses no serious risk, under frameworks such as the Declaration of Helsinki. Sham-surgery trials are more controversial but have produced landmark results — for example, showing that arthroscopic surgery for knee osteoarthritis was no better than a placebo procedure.
