Broth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chicken broth served with the vegetables cooked in it (image placeholder)

Broth, also known as bouillon, is a savoury liquid made of water in which meat, fish or vegetables have been simmered for a short period. Unlike stock, broth is seasoned and can be eaten on its own as a light soup, and it serves as a base for soups, sauces, gravies and cooked grains.

Preparation and terminology

A broth is typically made from meaty cuts rather than bare bones, simmered briefly — 45 minutes to two hours for chicken — so it remains light in body, with relatively little gelatin compared with long-simmered stock. The distinction is inconsistently applied: in everyday and commercial usage "broth" and "stock" are often swapped, and products labelled broth are usually salted, while stock is marketed as an unseasoned cooking ingredient.

Varieties

Well-known broths include chicken broth, a home remedy long associated with convalescence and colds; Scotch broth, a filling soup of barley, mutton or lamb and root vegetables; dashi, the Japanese stock-broth of kombu and bonito flakes; and bone broth, a long-simmered, vinegar-assisted preparation popularised in the 2010s for its collagen content. Commercial broths are sold canned, boxed, or as dehydrated cubes and powders.

Nutrition

Broths are low in calories and protein relative to the ingredients simmered in them, but extract minerals and flavour compounds; commercial versions can be high in sodium.
