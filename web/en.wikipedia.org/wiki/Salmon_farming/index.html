Salmon farming, or salmon aquaculture, is the industrial rearing of salmon under controlled conditions for commercial purposes. The farmed species is overwhelmingly the Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar), though smaller quantities of Pacific species such as coho and Chinook salmon are also raised. Farmed salmon accounts for the large majority of salmon consumed worldwide, exceeding the wild catch several times over.

Production cycle

Production begins in freshwater hatcheries, where broodstock are stripped of eggs and milt and the fertilised eggs are incubated until hatching. Fry are raised in tanks through the parr stage and undergo smoltification, the physiological change that prepares them for salt water. Smolts are then transferred to marine grow-out sites, typically floating open-net cages anchored in sheltered fjords and bays, where they are fed pelleted feed for 12–24 months until reaching a harvest weight of roughly 4–6 kg. Increasingly, producers also use land-based recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS), which allow tighter control of water quality and exclude sea lice and escapes.

Feed

Salmon are carnivorous, and their feed historically contained large proportions of fishmeal and fish oil rendered from wild-caught forage fish. Feed conversion ratios of around 1.2:1 are among the best in animal agriculture, and the industry has progressively substituted plant proteins, though this can reduce the omega-3 content of the flesh. The pink colour of farmed salmon flesh is maintained by adding the carotenoid astaxanthin to feed.

Environmental and health concerns

Critics point to several impacts: nutrient pollution beneath cages; outbreaks of parasitic sea lice, which can spill over to wild smolts; escapes of farmed fish that may interbreed with wild populations; and the use of antibiotics and pesticides. Regulators in Norway, Chile, Scotland and Canada have responded with biomass limits, lice thresholds, fallowing requirements and area-based management. On the consumer side, farmed salmon is a major dietary source of long-chain omega-3 fatty acids, though contaminant levels are monitored by food-safety authorities.

History

Modern salmon farming began in Norway in the late 1960s and 1970s, pioneered by the Grøntvedt brothers and others using floating cages in fjords. The industry expanded rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s to Chile, Scotland, Canada, the Faroe Islands and Tasmania, and became highly consolidated, with companies such as Mowi (formerly Marine Harvest), SalMar, Lerøy and Cermaq among the largest producers.
