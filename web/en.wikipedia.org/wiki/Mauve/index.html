Mauve

Mauve () is a pale purple colour named after the mallow flower (French: mauve). It is a muted blend of violet and pink with greyish tones, first used as a colour name in English in the late 18th century. The colour became a cultural phenomenon in the late 1850s after the invention of the first synthetic aniline dye, mauveine.

Mauveine and the "mauve mania"

In 1856 the eighteen-year-old chemistry student William Henry Perkin, attempting to synthesise quinine, accidentally produced a vivid purple dye from coal tar. He patented it as mauveine (also called aniline purple or Perkin's mauve) and built a factory to manufacture it. When Queen Victoria wore a mauve gown to the Royal Exhibition of 1862 — and Empress Eugénie adopted the colour in France — mauve became the height of fashion, a craze the magazine Punch mocked as the "mauve measles".

Symbolism and use

Mauve later acquired associations with mourning (as a half-mourning colour in Victorian etiquette), with the suffragette movement (which used purple, white and green), and with the "Mauve Decade" of the 1890s, a term popularised by Thomas Beer for the artistic and literary culture of that period. The 1890s association with decadence — and with Oscar Wilde, who quipped about the colour — gave mauve a lingering connotation of nostalgia and fading elegance.

In nature

Mauve tones occur in mallow and lilac flowers, in some grape varieties, and in the plumage of certain birds. The colour sits between lavender and orchid in most colour-order systems.
