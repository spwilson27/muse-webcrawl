The short-finned pilot whale (Globicephala macrorhynchus) is one of the two species of cetacean in the genus Globicephala, along with the long-finned pilot whale. Despite its name it is a large oceanic dolphin, recognisable by its bulbous, globe-shaped forehead (melon) and short, sickle-shaped pectoral fins. It is found in warmer waters worldwide, complementing the colder-water distribution of its long-finned relative.

Description

Adults are dark grey to black, often with lighter saddle patches behind the dorsal fin. Males may exceed 6 metres and two to three tonnes, and are much bulkier than females. The species feeds mainly on squid taken at depth, making dives of several hundred metres, partly by night when squid rise toward the surface.

Behaviour and social structure

Short-finned pilot whales are intensely social, living in stable pods of typically 15–30 animals organised around related females; females may live well past their reproductive years, making the species one of the few in which menopause is documented. Pods sometimes gather in aggregations of hundreds. Like other pilot whales they are among the species most prone to mass strandings, in which whole pods beach together — a behaviour linked to their strong social bonds and echolocation in gently shelving waters.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Least Concern, though reliable global population estimates are lacking. It is taken in drive hunts in Japan and sometimes caught as bycatch or targeted elsewhere. A resident population off Tenerife in the Canary Islands is a well-known focus of whale-watching and long-term research.
