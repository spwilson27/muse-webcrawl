Lullaby

A lullaby (or cradle song) is a soothing song or piece of music that is usually played for (or sung to) children, most commonly infants, to lull them to sleep. Lullabies can be found in many cultures and have existed since ancient times. They typically feature slow tempos, simple melodies, and repetitive, rocking rhythms — often in 6/8 time, mimicking the motion of a cradle.

Characteristics

Lullabies are generally simple in structure, with repetitive phrases and a gentle, descending melodic contour. Lyrics often invoke sleep, the night, animals, or promises and warnings — some traditional lullabies contain surprisingly dark themes, such as the falling cradle in "Rock-a-bye Baby".

History

One of the oldest known lullabies is a Babylonian cradle song inscribed on a clay tablet dating to around 2000 BCE. Lullabies appear in ancient Greek and Roman sources, and a large repertoire of folk lullabies developed in every musical culture. In the classical tradition, composers including Brahms ("Wiegenlied", Op. 49 No. 4, 1868), Chopin (Berceuse, Op. 57), and Fauré wrote lullabies or berceuses as concert pieces.

Well-known lullabies

  • "Brahms's Lullaby" ("Guten Abend, gute Nacht")
  • "Rock-a-bye Baby"
  • "Hush, Little Baby"
  • "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star"
  • "Frère Jacques"
  • "All the Pretty Little Horses"
  • "Summertime" (from Gershwin's Porgy and Bess, written in a lullaby style)

Research

Studies in developmental psychology suggest that singing lullabies helps regulate infant arousal, strengthens caregiver–infant bonding, and may support early language development. Live singing has been found to keep infants calm significantly longer than speech or recorded music.
