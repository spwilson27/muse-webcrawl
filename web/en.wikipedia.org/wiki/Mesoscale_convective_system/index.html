Mesoscale convective system

A mesoscale convective system (MCS) is a complex of thunderstorms that becomes organised on a scale larger than the individual thunderstorms and normally persists for several hours or more. MCSs are responsible for a large fraction of warm-season rainfall in the midlatitudes, including the central United States, and can produce severe weather including flash floods, damaging winds, large hail, and tornadoes.

Classification and structure

MCSs take several forms. Linear systems include squall lines, often with a trailing stratiform precipitation region and a rear-inflow jet. Circular or oval systems meeting strict size and duration criteria for their cold cloud shield are called mesoscale convective complexes (MCCs), a category defined by Maddox in 1980. A typical mature MCS consists of a leading line of vigorous convective cells, a broad region of stratiform rain behind it beneath a spreading anvil, and its own mesoscale circulation, including a mid-level mesoscale convective vortex (MCV) that can persist after the convection decays and seed new storms the following day.

Climatology and hazards

In the United States, MCSs occur most frequently in spring and summer, frequently at night, aided by the nocturnal low-level jet that feeds warm, moist air into the system. They are major producers of flash flooding because of their duration and the "training" of cells over the same area; organised wind systems such as derechos arise from long-lived, fast-moving MCSs. Analogous systems occur worldwide, including over West Africa, South America, East Asia (along the mei-yu front), and Australia.
