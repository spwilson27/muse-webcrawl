Exogenous growth theory

Exogenous growth theory, also known as neoclassical growth theory, is a class of economic models in which long-run growth of output per capita is driven by technological progress that is treated as exogenous — that is, determined outside the model rather than explained by it. The canonical versions are the Solow–Swan model, developed independently by Robert Solow and Trevor Swan in 1956, and the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model, which endogenises the saving rate.

The Solow model

In the Solow model, output is produced from capital and labour under diminishing returns to capital. Saving raises the capital stock, but diminishing returns mean capital accumulation alone cannot sustain growth in output per worker: the economy converges to a steady state in which output per capita grows only at the rate of exogenous technological progress. A well-known implication is conditional convergence — poorer economies with the same fundamentals should grow faster as they approach their steady states.

Growth accounting exercises based on the model, beginning with Solow's own 1957 study of the United States, attributed the bulk of long-run growth to the residual — total factor productivity growth — rather than to factor accumulation, prompting Abramovitz's famous description of the residual as "a measure of our ignorance."

Criticism and successors

Because the engine of long-run growth is assumed rather than explained, economists from the mid-1980s developed endogenous growth theory, in which innovation, human capital, and knowledge spillovers are modelled explicitly (Romer, Lucas, Aghion and Howitt). Exogenous models nevertheless remain the workhorse benchmark for growth empirics and policy analysis.
