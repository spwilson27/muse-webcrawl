Coda (music)

In music, a coda (Italian for "tail"; plural code) is a concluding section or passage that brings a piece or movement to an end. Technically, it is an expanded cadence. It may be as simple as a few bars, or it may reach a complexity comparable to a development section, as in the first movement of Beethoven's symphonies. The term derives from the Latin cauda, meaning tail.

Function and history

A coda follows the recapitulation in sonata form, confirming the tonal conclusion of the movement and providing a sense of finality. In the Classical era codas were often brief, but Beethoven greatly expanded them into a "second development", as in the first movement of the Eroica Symphony. Romantic and later composers continued this practice; the coda of the finale of Tchaikovsky's Fourth Symphony, for instance, restates the fate motif triumphantly. A short coda is sometimes called a codetta, a term also used for the closing section of a fugue exposition or of a sonata-form bridge.

In notation and popular music

In sheet music, the coda sign resembling a crosshair (𝄌) marks the point at which a performer jumps to the final section under instructions such as Dal Segno al Coda (D.S. al Coda). In popular music, the equivalent closing passage is often called an outro, and a common device is the repeated chorus fading out, a technique popularized in recording studios from the 1950s onward.
