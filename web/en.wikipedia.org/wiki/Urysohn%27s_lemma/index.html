Urysohn's lemma

Urysohn's lemma

Field: Topology

Named after: Pavel Urysohn

Related: Tietze extension theorem, Urysohn metrization theorem

Urysohn's lemma is a central theorem in topology, proved by Pavel Urysohn in the 1920s. It states that a topological space is normal if and only if any two disjoint closed subsets can be separated by a continuous function: for disjoint closed sets A and B in a normal space X, there exists a continuous function f : X → [0, 1] with f equal to 0 on A and 1 on B.

Proof idea

The standard proof constructs the function through a dyadic rationals argument: one builds a family of open sets indexed by the dyadic rationals in [0, 1], nested so that closures respect the ordering, and defines f(x) as the infimum of the indices of the sets containing x. Normality, via a shrinking argument, supplies the required sets step by step.

Significance

The lemma shows that normal spaces possess a rich supply of continuous real-valued functions, contrary to what one might expect from a purely set-theoretic definition of topology. It is the key ingredient in the proofs of the Tietze extension theorem and the Urysohn metrization theorem, and it underlies the construction of partitions of unity.
