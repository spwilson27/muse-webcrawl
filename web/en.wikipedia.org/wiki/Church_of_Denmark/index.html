The Church of Denmark (Danish: Den Danske Folkekirke, literally "The Danish People's Church"), also known as the Danish National Church, the Evangelical Lutheran Church in Denmark, is the established, state-supported church in Denmark. The reigning monarch of Denmark is the supreme authority in the church, and the Danish Parliament (the Folketing) is its legislative authority; there is no archbishop or single primate, the bishops being equal in rank.

The church is organised into eleven dioceses, each led by a bishop, subdivided into deaneries and some 2,200 parishes with around 2,400 ordained pastors. A majority of Danes — roughly seven in ten — are members, most joining through infant baptism, though regular weekly attendance is low by international standards. Members pay a church tax collected by the state.

History

Christianity was introduced to Denmark in the ninth and tenth centuries; Harald Bluetooth famously proclaimed on the Jelling stones (c. 965) that he had "made the Danes Christian". During the medieval period the Danish church was part of the Roman Catholic Church, with the archbishopric of Lund established in 1103 as the first Scandinavian metropolitan see.

The Reformation was completed in Denmark in 1536, when Christian III arrested the Catholic bishops and confiscated church property. Johannes Bugenhagen, a close associate of Martin Luther, helped organise the new Lutheran church order of 1537/1539. The Church of Denmark thereafter remained the state church through successive constitutions, including the current Constitution of 1849, section 4 of which states: "The Evangelical Lutheran Church shall be the Established Church of Denmark, and, as such, it shall be supported by the State."

Beliefs and practice

The church's confessional basis is the Bible, the three ecumenical creeds, the Augsburg Confession, and Luther's Small Catechism. Denmark is a member of the Lutheran World Federation and the Porvoo Communion. Women were first ordained in 1948, and since 2012 parishes have been obliged to offer wedding ceremonies to same-sex couples. The bishop of Copenhagen often acts as a de facto spokesperson, but formally all bishops are peers.
