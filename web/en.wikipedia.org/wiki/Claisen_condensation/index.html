Claisen condensation

The Claisen condensation is a carbon–carbon bond forming reaction that occurs between two esters, or one ester and another carbonyl compound, in the presence of a strong base, forming a β-keto ester or a β-diketone. It is named after Rainer Ludwig Claisen, who first published work on the reaction in 1887. The reaction requires at least one of the reagents to be enolizable, i.e. to have an α-proton that the base can remove to form an enolate.

The classic Claisen condensation of two ester molecules to a β-keto ester.

Mechanism

In the classic condensation, a strong base such as sodium ethoxide deprotonates the α-carbon of one ester to form an enolate. The enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second ester molecule, forming a tetrahedral intermediate that expels an alkoxide leaving group to give the β-keto ester. Because the product is more acidic than the starting esters, a full equivalent of base is consumed in deprotonating it; acidic work-up then releases the neutral product. The overall reaction is closely related to the aldol condensation, which involves aldehydes or ketones instead of esters.

Types

The classic Claisen condensation joins two identical esters; a crossed (or mixed) Claisen condensation joins two different carbonyl partners and is most useful when one of them cannot form an enolate. The intramolecular version, in which a diester cyclises, is known as the Dieckmann condensation. Related variants use ketones as the nucleophilic partner, and the Stobbe condensation is a modified reaction of esters with succinic esters.

Biochemistry

A biological version of the Claisen condensation is a key step in fatty acid synthesis and polyketide biosynthesis, catalysed by enzymes such as thiolases and ketoacyl synthases using acyl carrier protein thioesters.
