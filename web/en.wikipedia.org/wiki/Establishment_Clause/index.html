Establishment Clause

The Establishment Clause is part of the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, providing that "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion." Together with the Free Exercise Clause, it forms the religion clauses of the First Amendment. The clause prohibits the federal government — and, through the Fourteenth Amendment, state governments — from establishing an official religion or otherwise promoting one religion over others or religion over non-religion in ways the Supreme Court has held unconstitutional.

History

At the founding, several states retained established churches; the clause originally restrained only the federal government and arguably protected state establishments from federal interference. Thomas Jefferson famously described the religion clauses as building "a wall of separation between Church & State" in his 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists. In Everson v. Board of Education (1947), the Supreme Court held the clause incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, while upholding public funding of transportation to parochial schools.

Doctrine

For five decades, courts assessed Establishment Clause claims primarily under the test of Lemon v. Kurtzman (1971), which required that a law have a secular purpose, a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and no excessive government entanglement with religion. The Court also applied an "endorsement" analysis and, in cases such as Lee v. Weisman (1992), a coercion analysis striking down clergy-led prayer at public school graduations. School-sponsored prayer and Bible reading had been invalidated in Engel v. Vitale (1962) and Abington School District v. Schempp (1963).

In Kennedy v. Bremerton School District (2022), the Court stated that it had "long ago abandoned" the Lemon test, directing courts instead to interpret the clause by reference to historical practices and understandings. Earlier decisions had upheld legislative prayer (Marsh v. Chambers, 1983; Town of Greece v. Galloway, 2014) and, in American Legion v. American Humanist Association (2019), a long-standing war memorial cross on public land.
