The Treaty on European Union (TEU) is one of the two principal treaties forming the constitutional basis of the European Union, the other being the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). In its original form — the Maastricht Treaty — it was signed on 7 February 1992 by the members of the European Communities and entered into force on 1 November 1993, formally creating the European Union.

The Maastricht Treaty

The treaty established the European Union on the basis of the existing European Communities, and set out the three-pillar structure of the original Union: the European Communities, the Common Foreign and Security Policy, and cooperation in Justice and Home Affairs. It laid down the timetable and convergence criteria for Economic and Monetary Union, leading to the creation of the euro, introduced the concept of citizenship of the Union, and expanded the powers of the European Parliament through the co-decision procedure.

Ratification proved contentious: Danish voters initially rejected the treaty in a 1992 referendum, approving it only after the opt-outs of the Edinburgh Agreement; French voters approved it narrowly; and in the United Kingdom it was ratified by Parliament amid deep divisions in the governing Conservative Party.

Subsequent amendments

The TEU was amended by the Treaty of Amsterdam (1997), the Treaty of Nice (2001), and most comprehensively by the Treaty of Lisbon (2007), which abolished the pillar structure, gave the Union legal personality, created the permanent office of President of the European Council, and inserted a withdrawal clause (Article 50), invoked for the first time by the United Kingdom in 2017. The current consolidated TEU sets out the Union's values, objectives, democratic principles, competences, institutions and the framework of the Common Foreign and Security Policy.
