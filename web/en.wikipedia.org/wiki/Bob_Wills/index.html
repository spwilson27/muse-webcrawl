James Robert Wills (March 6, 1905 – May 13, 1975) was an American Western swing musician, songwriter, and bandleader. Considered by many to be the founder of Western swing, he was known as the "King of Western Swing." With his band the Texas Playboys, Wills fused fiddle tunes, country, blues, jazz, and big-band swing into a dance music that dominated the Southwest in the 1930s and 1940s, with signature songs including "San Antonio Rose," "Faded Love," "Take Me Back to Tulsa," and "Stay a Little Longer."

Early life

Wills was born near Kosse, Texas, into a family of fiddle players; his father John Wills was a champion fiddler. He grew up in Hall County in the Texas Panhandle, absorbing blues and jazz from Black musicians he worked alongside in the cotton fields, an influence that set his music apart. In the late 1920s he played medicine shows and barbered; a stint in blackface minstrel troupes taught him stagecraft.

The Texas Playboys

After playing with the Light Crust Doughboys in Fort Worth, Wills formed his own band in 1934 in Waco and then Tulsa, Oklahoma, where a daily broadcast on radio station KVOO made the Texas Playboys famous. The band blended twin fiddles, steel guitar (Leon McAuliffe), piano, horns, and Wills's trademark hollers and "ah-ha!" interjections. "New San Antonio Rose" (1940) became a million-seller and a standard, later recorded by Bing Crosby and many others.

Wills appeared in a string of B-Western films in the 1940s, and the band's popularity peaked during World War II. A stroke in 1969 ended his performing career; he recorded a final album, For the Last Time, with former Playboys in 1973. He died in Fort Worth in 1975.

Legacy

Wills was inducted into the Country Music Hall of Fame in 1968 and the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1999 as an early influence. His music shaped artists from Merle Haggard to Asleep at the Wheel, and "San Antonio Rose" remains one of the most recorded songs in country music.
