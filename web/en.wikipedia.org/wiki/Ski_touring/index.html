Ski touring

Ski touring is skiing in the backcountry on unmarked or unpatrolled areas outside of a ski resort's boundaries. It typically involves ascending slopes under one's own power using climbing skins and free-heel bindings, then descending in natural snow conditions. Ski touring may extend over multiple days, travelling between mountain huts or camps.

Techniques and equipment

Tourers use alpine touring (randonnée) bindings, which allow the heel to lift freely for climbing and lock down for the descent, or Telemark bindings with a free heel throughout. Climbing skins — originally made of sealskin, now usually nylon, mohair, or a blend — are attached to the ski base; their angled fibres grip the snow when sliding backward and glide when moving forward. Skiers ascend in a zigzag pattern using switchback "kick turns."

Forms

Day touring, hut-to-hut touring (such as the famous Haute Route from Chamonix to Zermatt), ski mountaineering on steeper and glaciated terrain, and lift-served backcountry access (sometimes called sidecountry) are all forms of the activity. In North America, mechanized forms such as snowcat skiing and heli-skiing provide access to similar terrain without climbing.

Safety

Avalanche risk is the primary hazard. Tourers carry transceivers, probes, and shovels, assess snowpack stability, and favour terrain below roughly 30 degrees on suspect days. Weather, navigation, glacier crevasses, and remoteness add further risk, making training and conservative decision-making central to the sport.
