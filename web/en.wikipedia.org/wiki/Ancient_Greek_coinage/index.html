Ancient Greek coinage

The history of ancient Greek coinage can be divided (along with most other Greek art forms) into four periods: the Archaic, the Classical, the Hellenistic and the Roman. The Archaic period extends from the introduction of coinage to the Greek world during the 7th century BC until the Persian Wars in about 480 BC. The Classical period then began, and lasted until the conquests of Alexander the Great in about 330 BC. The Hellenistic period followed, extending until the Roman absorption of the Greek world in the 1st century BC. Greek cities continued to produce their own coins for several more centuries under Roman rule, the so-called Greek Imperial or Roman provincial coins.

Origins

The first coins were minted in Lydia in western Asia Minor in the late 7th century BC, made of electrum, a natural alloy of gold and silver. Greek cities of Ionia quickly adopted the invention, and coinage spread throughout the Greek world. Early coins were simple punched lumps of metal whose stamp guaranteed weight and purity; by the 6th century BC cities such as Aegina, Corinth and Athens were issuing distinctive silver coinages bearing civic emblems, such as Aegina's sea turtle and Athens' owl of Athena.

Denominations and standards

Greek silver coinage was based on the drachma, divided into six obols; larger denominations included the tetradrachm (four drachmas), which became the standard trade coin of the Classical world, and the decadrachm. Different cities used different weight standards, notably the Attic and Aeginetan. Gold coinage was rarer until Philip II of Macedon and Alexander the Great issued large gold staters, and bronze small change became common in the 4th century BC.

Artistry

Greek coins are celebrated for their artistic quality, from the archaic incuse reverses of Magna Graecia to the portrait heads of Hellenistic kings. Signed dies by artists such as Kimon and Euainetos of Syracuse are among the masterpieces of ancient art. Coin types served as civic propaganda and remain an essential source for ancient history, religion and art.
