Benchmarking

Benchmarking is the practice of comparing business processes and performance metrics to industry bests and best practices from other companies. Dimensions typically measured are quality, time and cost. Benchmarking is used to measure performance using a specific indicator (cost per unit of measure, productivity per unit of measure, cycle time of x per unit of measure or defects per unit of measure) resulting in a metric of performance that is then compared to others.

History

The term derives from the marks surveyors cut into stone to serve as reference points for measuring levels. In management, benchmarking rose to prominence in the late 1970s and 1980s, when the Xerox Corporation systematically compared its warehousing and distribution operations against those of other firms and adopted the practices it found superior. The approach spread widely with the quality-management movement and was popularised by works such as Robert Camp's Benchmarking: The Search for Industry Best Practices that Lead to Superior Performance (1989).

Types

Internal benchmarking compares processes within different units of the same organisation. Competitive benchmarking compares performance directly against competitors. Functional benchmarking compares a specific function, such as logistics or customer service, with that of firms in other industries. Generic benchmarking seeks best practice wherever it may be found, regardless of industry.

Process

A typical benchmarking study follows a cycle: identify what is to be benchmarked; identify comparative companies; determine data collection methods and collect data; determine the current performance gap; project future performance levels; communicate findings; establish functional goals; develop action plans; implement and monitor progress; and recalibrate benchmarks as practices evolve.

Criticism

Critics note that benchmarking can encourage imitation rather than innovation, that comparisons may ignore differences in strategy, scale and context, and that data on competitors can be difficult to obtain or unreliable. Used carelessly, it may anchor an organisation to industry-average thinking.
