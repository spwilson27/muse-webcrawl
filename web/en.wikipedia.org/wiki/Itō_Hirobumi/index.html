Prince Itō Hirobumi (伊藤 博文, 1841–1909) was a Japanese statesman who served as the first Prime Minister of Japan and was the principal architect of the Meiji Constitution of 1889. A samurai of Chōshū Domain, he was a central figure in the Meiji Restoration and one of the genrō, the elder statesmen who guided Japan's modernisation.

Early life

Born Hayashi Risuke in Chōshū, Itō was adopted into the Itō family and rose through domain service. As a young man he studied at the Shōkasonjuku academy under Yoshida Shōin and in 1863 travelled secretly to London with four other Chōshū retainers (the "Chōshū Five"), studying at University College London. He returned in 1864 to urge peace during the foreign bombardment of Shimonoseki.

Statesman of Meiji Japan

After the Restoration of 1868 Itō held a rapid succession of posts, joining the Iwakura Mission to the West (1871–73) and working on finance and public works. In 1882 he led a mission to Europe to study constitutions, spending long periods in Berlin and Vienna with scholars such as Rudolf von Gneist and Lorenz von Stein; the resulting Meiji Constitution (promulgated 1889) combined a strong emperor-centred executive with a bicameral Diet on broadly Prussian lines. As the first head of the cabinet system, he became prime minister in 1885, serving four terms in all, and founded the Rikken Seiyūkai party in 1900.

Korea and assassination

After Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War, Itō became the first Resident-General of Korea in 1906, overseeing its reduction to a protectorate; he resigned in June 1909, favouring a slower path to annexation than the army preferred. On 26 October 1909, arriving at Harbin railway station for talks with a Russian envoy, he was shot and killed by An Jung-geun, a Korean independence activist. His assassination removed a relative moderate on Korea and preceded Japan's formal annexation of the peninsula in 1910.

Legacy

Itō's portrait appeared on the 1,000-yen note from 1963 to 1984. He is remembered in Japan as a founding father of the modern constitutional state, and in Korea chiefly as the architect of its colonisation.
