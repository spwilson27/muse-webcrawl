Curragh incident


Officers of the 3rd Cavalry Brigade at the Curragh

The Curragh incident of 20 March 1914, sometimes called the Curragh mutiny, occurred in the Curragh Camp, County Kildare, Ireland. It happened during the Home Rule crisis, when British Army officers stationed at the Curragh indicated that they would rather resign — and face dismissal — than take part in possible military operations against Ulster unionist opponents of Irish Home Rule.

Background

The Liberal government's Third Home Rule Bill, passed by the Commons and expected to become law in 1914 under the Parliament Act, was fiercely opposed by Ulster unionists, who had formed the paramilitary Ulster Volunteer Force and threatened armed resistance. The government of H. H. Asquith ordered troop movements in Ireland partly to guard arms depots and partly, officers feared, as a prelude to coercing Ulster.

The incident

Brigadier-General Hubert Gough, commanding the 3rd Cavalry Brigade, and most of his officers let it be known that they would choose dismissal if ordered to act against the Ulster Volunteers. Alarmed, the Secretary of State for War, J. E. B. Seely, gave a written assurance — the "peccant paragraphs" added without full cabinet authority — that the army would not be used to crush political opposition to the Bill. Asquith repudiated the added paragraphs; Seely and the Chief of the Imperial General Staff, Sir John French, resigned.

Consequences

No order to move against Ulster had in fact been given, and the officers technically did not disobey a direct order, which is why "incident" is preferred to "mutiny". The affair weakened the government's ability to use the army in Ireland, emboldened unionist and then nationalist gun-running (the Larne and Howth gun-runnings followed within months), and deepened the crisis that was overtaken only by the outbreak of the First World War.
