Bagpipes

Bagpipe

ClassificationAerophone; wind instrument with reservoir
ComponentsBag (reservoir), blowpipe, chanter, drones, reeds
ExamplesGreat Highland bagpipe, uilleann pipes, Northumbrian smallpipes, gaita, cornemuse, duda, zampogna

Bagpipes are a class of wind instruments that use enclosed reeds fed from a constant reservoir of air in the form of a bag. The player inflates the bag — either by blowing through a blowpipe or with bellows — and squeezes it under the arm to maintain steady air pressure, allowing continuous sound without pauses for breath. Most bagpipes have a melody pipe, the chanter, and one or more drones that sound constant accompanying notes.

Construction

The principal parts are the air supply (blowpipe with non-return valve, or bellows), the bag (traditionally leather or sheepskin, now often synthetic), the chanter (a cylindrical or conical bore pipe with finger holes, sounded by a single or double reed), and the drones (long cylindrical pipes, usually with single reeds, tuned to the chanter's keynote). Because the reeds are enclosed and not touched by the lips, the player cannot alter pitch by embouchure; articulation is achieved instead with grace notes and other finger ornaments.

History

Bagpipe-like instruments are ancient: possible depictions occur in the ancient Near East, and the emperor Nero is said by Suetonius to have played a pipe with a bag (the Roman utricularium). Bagpipes spread throughout Europe, North Africa and western and southern Asia, evolving into dozens of regional forms, and became strongly associated with folk, dance and military traditions.

Regional types

British Isles

The Great Highland bagpipe of Scotland — loud, mouth-blown, with two tenor drones and a bass drone — is played solo (including the classical pìobaireachd repertoire) and in pipe bands worldwide. Ireland's uilleann pipes are bellows-blown, quieter, and possess regulators for chordal accompaniment; the Northumbrian smallpipes and Scottish smallpipes are also bellows-blown indoor instruments.

Europe and beyond

Regional types include the Spanish and Portuguese gaita, the French cornemuse and musette, the Italian zampogna, the Breton biniou, the Hungarian duda, Balkan gaida, the Swedish säckpipa, and the Middle Eastern tulum and mizwad.

Music and culture

Bagpipes are used for dance music, ceremonial and military music, weddings and funerals. Scottish pipe bands, graded in international competition, perform marches, strathspeys and reels in medley sets. The instrument's penetrating outdoor sound made it a traditional instrument of war for Highland regiments, a role retained ceremonially today.
