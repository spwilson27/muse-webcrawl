Concept album

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A concept album is an album whose tracks hold a larger purpose or meaning collectively than they do individually. This is typically achieved through a single central narrative or theme, which may be instrumental, compositional, or lyrical. Concept albums range from loose thematic collections — songs united by a mood or topic — to fully plotted rock operas with characters and story arcs.

History

Precursors include Woody Guthrie's Dust Bowl Ballads (1940), Frank Sinatra's mood albums of the 1950s such as In the Wee Small Hours (1955), and themed jazz and country records. In rock, the form crystallised in the late 1960s: the Beach Boys' Pet Sounds (1966) for its unified emotional arc, the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) for its framing device, and the Who's Tommy (1969), a full rock opera. The Kinks, Pretty Things (S.F. Sorrow, 1968), and Small Faces also made early narrative albums.

1970s and beyond

Progressive rock made the concept album a flagship format: Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon (1973) and The Wall (1979), Genesis's The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway (1974), and Jethro Tull's Thick as a Brick (1972). Punk and new wave reacted against its perceived pretension, but the form persisted in heavy metal (concept works by Iron Maiden, Queensrÿche, Mastodon), hip hop (Kendrick Lamar's good kid, m.A.A.d city, 2012), and pop (Beyoncé's Lemonade, 2016, with its accompanying film). Green Day's American Idiot (2004) and My Chemical Romance's The Black Parade (2006) revived the rock opera for a new generation.

Definitions and criticism

Commentators debate how strict the definition should be — whether a recurring theme suffices or a narrative is required. The format has been praised for elevating the album as an art form and mocked (for example in the film This Is Spinal Tap) as a symbol of rock excess. In the streaming era, some artists still sequence albums as unified works, while playlist culture rewards individual tracks.
