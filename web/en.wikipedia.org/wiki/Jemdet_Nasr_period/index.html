Jemdet Nasr period

The Jemdet Nasr period (also spelled Jemdat Nasr) is an archaeological culture in southern Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq) dated approximately to 3100–2900 BC. It is named after the type site of Tell Jemdet Nasr, about 26 kilometres northeast of the ancient city of Kish, first excavated in the 1920s by a team led by Ernest Mackay and later Stephen Langdon. The period follows the Uruk period and precedes the Early Dynastic I period.

Culture

The period is distinguished by distinctive polychrome painted pottery, decorated in red, black and buff geometric patterns, and by a further development of administrative technology. Writing, in the form of early cuneiform on clay tablets, continued from the late Uruk period; the Jemdet Nasr tablets are largely administrative records using numerical signs and logograms. Cylinder seals and stamp seals show characteristic drilled-style designs.

Settlement

Sites of the period include Jemdet Nasr itself, Uruk (levels), Ur, Tell Uqair, Abu Salabikh and Nippur. Evidence suggests that settlement became more regionalised after the wide-ranging Uruk expansion, with the alluvium of southern Iraq supporting irrigated agriculture and increasingly centralised storage and redistribution, possibly centred on temples.

Chronology

Radiocarbon and stratigraphic evidence place the period in the centuries around 3000 BC. Shortly after it, the Early Dynastic period begins, marked by the rise of competing Sumerian city-states.
