Birefringence

Birefringence is the optical property of a material having a refractive index that depends on the polarization and propagation direction of light. These optically anisotropic materials are said to be birefringent (or birefractive). The birefringence is often quantified as the maximum difference between refractive indices exhibited by the material. Crystals with non-cubic crystal structures are often birefringent, as are plastics under mechanical stress.

Explanation

In an anisotropic material, a ray of light splits into two rays taking slightly different paths with different speeds, polarised perpendicular to each other. In a uniaxial material there is one special direction, the optic axis, along which no splitting occurs; the two rays are termed the ordinary ray (o-ray), which obeys Snell's law, and the extraordinary ray (e-ray), whose refractive index varies with direction. Biaxial materials, with lower crystal symmetry, have two optic axes.

The phenomenon was first described in calcite (Iceland spar) by the Danish scientist Rasmus Bartholin in 1669, who observed double refraction; Christiaan Huygens later gave a wave-based explanation.

Sources of birefringence

  • Intrinsic (crystalline) birefringence — arising from anisotropic crystal structure, e.g. calcite (large, negative birefringence) and quartz.
  • Form birefringence — from sub-wavelength structures aligned in a medium.
  • Stress birefringence (photoelasticity) — induced in isotropic materials under mechanical load, used to analyse stress in models and to detect strain in glass and plastics.
  • Flow birefringence — in liquids containing elongated molecules or particles aligned by flow.
  • Electric and magnetic field effects — the Kerr and Pockels effects, and the Cotton–Mouton effect.

Applications

Birefringent materials are used in wave plates (retarders) that alter the polarisation of light, in polarising microscopes for identifying minerals and biological structures, in liquid-crystal displays, in optical modulators, and in photoelastic stress analysis. Between crossed polarisers, birefringent samples produce characteristic interference colours, charted on the Michel-Lévy interference colour chart.
