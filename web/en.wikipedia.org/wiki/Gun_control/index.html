Gun control

Gun control (or firearms regulation) is the set of laws or policies that regulate the manufacture, sale, transfer, possession, modification, or use of firearms by civilians. Most countries have a restrictive firearms regime, under which civilians may own firearms only under specified conditions, usually after licensing. A small number of countries, most notably the United States, have more permissive regimes.

Regulation by type

Regulatory measures commonly include:

  • Licensing of owners, often requiring background checks, safety training, and demonstration of a genuine reason for ownership.
  • Registration of individual firearms.
  • Restrictions on categories of weapons, such as fully automatic firearms, short-barrelled shotguns, or semi-automatic rifles with certain features.
  • Safe-storage requirements and rules for carrying firearms in public, whether openly or concealed.
  • Buyback and amnesty programmes, such as Australia's compulsory buyback following the 1996 Port Arthur massacre.

International comparison

The United Kingdom, Japan, and Australia are frequently cited examples of strict regimes: Japan's law effectively prohibits private handgun ownership, while Britain banned most handguns after the 1996 Dunblane massacre. Switzerland and Canada maintain comparatively high ownership with licensing systems. In the United States, regulation is divided between federal and state law and is shaped by the Second Amendment to the Constitution.

Debate

Scholarly and public debate centres on the relationship between firearm availability and homicide, suicide, and mass shootings. Researchers dispute the magnitude and direction of effects of particular laws; cross-national studies generally find higher firearm homicide rates in countries with higher ownership, while critics of regulation argue that defensive use and deterrence are undercounted and that restrictions disproportionately affect lawful owners.
