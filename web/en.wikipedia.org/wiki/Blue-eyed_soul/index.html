Blue-eyed soul

Blue-eyed soul
Stylistic originsSoul, rhythm and blues, Motown, doo-wop
Cultural origins1960s, United States and United Kingdom
Typical artistsThe Righteous Brothers, Hall & Oates, Dusty Springfield, Steve Winwood, George Michael, Adele

Blue-eyed soul is a term coined in the mid-1960s to describe rhythm and blues and soul music performed by white artists whose sound was heavily influenced by Black American music. Philadelphia disc jockey Georgie Woods is credited with coining the phrase in reference to the Righteous Brothers, whose hit "You've Lost That Lovin' Feelin'" (1964) was initially assumed by many listeners to be the work of a Black duo.

History

Early blue-eyed soul acts included the Rascals, the Spencer Davis Group with Steve Winwood, and Dusty Springfield, whose 1969 album Dusty in Memphis is a landmark of the style. In the 1970s and 1980s, artists such as Hall & Oates—the best-selling duo in music history— Boz Scaggs, and Michael McDonald brought the sound to the top of the pop charts. British acts of the 1980s, including Simply Red and George Michael, carried the genre forward, and in the 21st century singers such as Amy Winehouse, Adele, and Sam Smith achieved global success in a style rooted in blue-eyed soul.

Criticism

The term has been debated: some critics regard it as a useful genre label, while others argue it reflects a racial double standard in the music industry, whereby white performers of Black-derived music received disproportionate airplay and commercial reward. The phrase itself has fallen in and out of use depending on the era.
