Folklore of England
The body of myths, legends, customs and beliefs traditional in England.

English folklore consists of the myths and legends of England, including the region's characteristic legendary creatures, and traditions of recurrent festivities. It has evolved over many centuries: some of its figures and customs are rooted in Germanic and Celtic pagan traditions brought by successive waves of settlers, others in medieval romance, and still others arose in early modern and even Victorian times. Much English folklore was recorded by antiquaries from the 16th century onward, and its study was systematized by the Folklore Society, founded in 1878.

Legendary figures and creatures

  • King Arthur, the once and future king, with Merlin, Camelot, and the Knights of the Round Table
  • Robin Hood, the outlaw hero of Sherwood Forest, robbing the rich to feed the poor
  • Herne the Hunter, the antlered ghost said to haunt Windsor Forest
  • Black dogs such as Black Shuck of East Anglia and the Barghest of Yorkshire — spectral hounds said to portend death
  • Boggarts, household or landscape spirits of the northern counties
  • Spring-heeled Jack, the leaping Victorian apparition
  • Fairies and pixies (especially of the South West), hobgoblins, and giants such as Gog and Magog
  • Dick Whittington, the poor boy who became Lord Mayor of London; Lady Godiva of Coventry; and Guy of Warwick

Customs and calendar traditions

English customary folklore includes morris dancing, mummers' plays, wassailing, May Day celebrations with maypoles and the election of a May Queen, harvest festivals, cheese-rolling at Cooper's Hill in Gloucestershire, the Abbots Bromley Horn Dance, well dressing in Derbyshire, and the burning of the clocks and other local rites. Seasonal customs such as the Padstow 'Obby 'Oss festival and the Lewes bonfire celebrations on Guy Fawkes Night (5 November) remain lively.

Folk tales and song

England shares many tale-types with Europe while cultivating local legends attached to places — holy wells, standing stones, hill figures such as the Cerne Abbas Giant and the Uffington White Horse, and ley lines (a 20th-century notion). Traditional ballads, many collected by Francis James Child and by Cecil Sharp in the folk-song revival, preserve stories of love, murder, war, and the supernatural; sea shanties, carols, and nursery rhymes form part of the same inheritance.

Regional folklore

Each county keeps its own traditions: Cornwall has tales of piskies, knockers (mine spirits), and saints; the Lake District has the Crier of Claife and corpse roads; the Fens have stories of will-o'-the-wisps and flood lore; London has its plague pits, execution grounds, and modern urban legends. English folklore continues to evolve with chain lore, football chants, and internet legends joining the older stock.
