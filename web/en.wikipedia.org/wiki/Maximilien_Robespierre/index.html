Maximilien François Marie Isidore de Robespierre (6 May 1758 – 28 July 1794) was a French lawyer and statesman who became one of the most widely known, influential, and controversial figures of the French Revolution. As a leading member of the Committee of Public Safety, he is associated with the Reign of Terror, during which thousands were executed as enemies of the Revolution.

Rise

Educated at the Lycée Louis-le-Grand in Paris, Robespierre practised law in his native Arras and was elected to the Estates-General of 1789. In the National Assembly he advocateded universal male suffrage, the abolition of slavery in the colonies, and the rights of citizens, earning the nickname "the Incorruptible" for his austere consistency. He opposed the war with Austria in 1792 and, unusually for the period, opposed the death penalty early in his career.

The Terror and fall

Elected to the Committee of Public Safety in July 1793, Robespierre became the dominant voice of the revolutionary government. He argued that terror was "nothing other than justice, prompt, severe, inflexible" and a necessary instrument of virtue in defence of the republic. The execution of the Hébertists and then of Danton and Desmoulins in spring 1794, and the acceleration of executions after the Law of 22 Prairial, alienated the Convention. His Cult of the Supreme Being, inaugurated in June 1794, further divided opinion. On 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) he was arrested after a confrontation in the Convention; the next day he was guillotined along with 21 supporters, including Saint-Just.

Legacy

Robespierre remains deeply disputed: for some, a principled democrat and defender of the poor; for others, the architect of revolutionary tyranny and a precursor of totalitarianism. Historians continue to debate the extent of his personal responsibility for the Terror.
