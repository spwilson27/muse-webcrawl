Citizen science

Citizen science is scientific research conducted, in whole or in part, by amateur (or nonprofessional) scientists. The term encompasses projects in which volunteers gather or analyse data — counting birds, classifying galaxies, monitoring water quality — typically in collaboration with professional scientists. Citizen science is sometimes described as "public participation in scientific research", participatory monitoring, or participatory action research.

History

Amateur contributions to science long predate the term: the Audubon Society's Christmas Bird Count began in 1900, and amateur astronomers and weather observers have supplied data for centuries. The phrase "citizen science" gained currency in the 1990s. The internet transformed the field: the Cornell Lab of Ornithology's eBird (launched 2002) collects millions of bird observations, and the Zooniverse platform (from Galaxy Zoo, 2007) enlists volunteers to classify galaxies, transcribe manuscripts, and identify animals in camera-trap images.

Types and value

Projects range from contributory (volunteers collect data designed by scientists) to co-created and collegial forms in which the public helps define questions. Citizen science can operate at geographic and temporal scales impractical for professionals, and studies show volunteered data can match professional quality for many tasks when protocols and validation are well designed. It is also promoted as a tool for science education and civic engagement, though critics raise questions about data quality, exploitation of free labour, and whose questions get asked.
