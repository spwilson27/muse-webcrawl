The Lidice massacre was the complete destruction of the village of Lidice in the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia (now the Czech Republic) by Nazi German forces on 10 June 1942, in reprisal for the assassination of Acting Reich Protector Reinhard Heydrich by Czechoslovak agents (Operation Anthropoid). Unlike most reprisals, the destruction was publicly announced by the German authorities.

The massacre

On a pretext of links between the village and the assassins, German forces surrounded Lidice on the night of 9–10 June. All 173 adult men were shot in groups at a farm in the village. The women — 203 in number — were deported to Ravensbrück concentration camp, where 60 died. Of the 105 children, most were murdered in gas vans at Chełmno extermination camp; a small number judged suitable for "Germanisation" were given to SS families. In total 340 inhabitants of Lidice were killed. The village was then burned, dynamited and bulldozed, its name removed from maps, and the site ploughed over.

Aftermath and memory

The atrocity provoked worldwide outrage; towns and districts in Britain, the United States, Mexico and elsewhere renamed themselves Lidice, and the British mining community of Stoke-on-Trent raised funds under the slogan "Lidice Shall Live". After the war Lidice was rebuilt beside the original site, which is preserved as a memorial with a museum and the celebrated bronze monument to the 82 murdered children by Marie Uchytilová.
