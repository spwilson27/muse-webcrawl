Ryder Report

The Ryder Report was an official investigation by the United States Army into detention and interrogation operations in Iraq, completed on November 6, 2003. The report was authored by Major General Donald J. Ryder, the Army's Provost Marshal General, who had been tasked with assessing the military police mission and detention procedures across Iraq in the autumn of 2003, during the Iraq War.

Background

By mid-2003, the U.S.-led coalition was holding large numbers of Iraqi detainees in facilities such as Abu Ghraib prison and Camp Bucca, and concerns were rising about overcrowding, unclear procedures, and the mixing of military police guard duties with military intelligence interrogation. Ryder's assessment team visited detention facilities throughout Iraq in October and November 2003.

Findings

The report identified systemic problems: severe shortages of trained military police, inadequate facilities, a lack of clear standard operating procedures, and confusion over the respective roles of military police and military intelligence. It warned that practices in some facilities ran contrary to established doctrine — notably the setting of "favorable conditions" for interrogation by guards — and that certain interrogation techniques being used might violate policy. The report recommended clarifying the chain of command, separating guard and interrogation functions, and improving detainee processing and release procedures.

Relation to the Abu Ghraib scandal

The Ryder Report predated the public exposure of the Abu Ghraib prisoner abuse scandal in April 2004. Along with the International Committee of the Red Cross reporting and the later Taguba Report, it formed part of the documentary record showing that serious problems in Iraqi detention operations had been identified within the Army months before the scandal broke. Subsequent investigations, including the Fay Report and the Schlesinger Report, examined why its warnings had not led to timely corrective action.
