The Royal Air Force (RAF) is the air and space force of the United Kingdom. Formed towards the end of the First World War on 1 April 1918, through the merger of the Royal Flying Corps and the Royal Naval Air Service, it was the first independent air force in the world. The RAF is one of the most capable air forces in the world, operating a fleet of modern combat, transport, surveillance and training aircraft.

History

The RAF was created following the recommendations of the Smuts Report, which recognised air power as a distinct form of warfare. In the interwar period the service policed the British Empire and developed its strategic bombing doctrine under leaders such as Hugh Trenchard, the "father of the RAF". During the Second World War the RAF achieved its most famous victory in the Battle of Britain (1940), when Fighter Command, led by Hugh Dowding, defeated the Luftwaffe's attempt to gain air superiority over southern England — prompting Winston Churchill's tribute to "the Few". Bomber Command conducted a sustained strategic bombing campaign against Germany, and Coastal Command fought the Battle of the Atlantic.

Post-war and modern role

During the Cold War the RAF operated the V bomber force carrying Britain's airborne nuclear deterrent until that role passed to the Royal Navy's submarines in 1969. Since then the RAF has taken part in operations in the Falklands War, the Gulf Wars, Kosovo, Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya and against ISIL in Syria and Iraq. Today its front-line combat aircraft include the Eurofighter Typhoon and the F-35B Lightning, supported by Voyager tankers, Atlas and Globemaster transports, Poseidon maritime patrol aircraft and Reaper and Protector remotely piloted systems.

Structure

The RAF is commanded by the Chief of the Air Staff and administered by Air Command at RAF High Wycombe. Its famous aerobatic display team, the Red Arrows, flies the Hawk T1. See also Royal Navy, British Army and Battle of Britain.
