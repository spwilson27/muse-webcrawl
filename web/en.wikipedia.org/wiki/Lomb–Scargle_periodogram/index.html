Lomb–Scargle periodogram

The Lomb–Scargle periodogram is a method of estimating a frequency spectrum, based on a least-squares fit of sinusoids to data samples, and is commonly used in astronomy for analysing unevenly spaced time-series data — such as measurements of variable stars, exoplanet radial velocities, and other observations that cannot be made at perfectly regular intervals.

A peak in the Lomb–Scargle periodogram indicates a candidate period in unevenly sampled data.

History

The method was developed by Nicholas Lomb in 1976 and refined by Jeffrey Scargle in 1982. Its popularity arises because, for evenly spaced data, it reduces to the classical periodogram, while for uneven data it can be evaluated at arbitrary frequencies and retains a well-understood statistical distribution, allowing false-alarm probabilities to be computed.

Method

At each trial frequency, the periodogram measures how well the data fit a sinusoid of that frequency, fitted by least squares. Scargle's formulation includes a time offset that makes the result invariant to time translation and equivalent to the least-squares spectral analysis. Peaks in the periodogram correspond to likely periodicities. Fast algorithms (such as the fast Lomb–Scargle implementation based on non-uniform FFTs) make it practical for the very large light curves produced by surveys such as Kepler and TESS, where it is a standard tool in exoplanet transit searches and stellar variability studies.

Statistical significance

Under the null hypothesis of Gaussian noise, periodogram values follow a known exponential distribution, so the probability that noise alone produces a peak of a given height — the false alarm probability — can be estimated analytically or by bootstrap resampling.
