Cardiac anatomy

Anterior view of the human heart showing the chambers and great vessels. (Image placeholder)

Cardiac anatomy is the study of the structure of the heart. The human heart is a four-chambered muscular organ, roughly the size of a fist, located in the middle mediastinum of the chest, tilted so that about two thirds lie to the left of the midline. It functions as a double pump: the right side receives deoxygenated blood from the body and sends it to the lungs, while the left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it around the body.

Chambers

The heart is divided by septa into four chambers:

  • Right atrium — receives deoxygenated blood from the superior and inferior venae cavae and the coronary sinus.
  • Right ventricle — pumps blood through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk and arteries.
  • Left atrium — receives oxygenated blood from the four pulmonary veins.
  • Left ventricle — the thickest-walled chamber, pumping blood through the aortic valve into the aorta at systemic pressure.

Valves

Four valves enforce one-way flow. The atrioventricular valves — the tricuspid valve on the right and the mitral (bicuspid) valve on the left — are anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles in the ventricles. The semilunar valves — the pulmonary valve and the aortic valve — each have three cusps and guard the exits of the ventricles.

Wall and coverings

The heart wall has three layers: the inner endocardium, the middle muscular myocardium of cardiac muscle cells, and the outer epicardium. The whole organ is enclosed in the pericardium, a double-walled sac whose serous layers and small amount of fluid allow near-frictionless beating.

Conduction system and blood supply

The heartbeat is coordinated by specialised conducting tissue: the sinoatrial node (the natural pacemaker) in the right atrium, the atrioventricular node, the bundle of His and the Purkinje fibres. The heart muscle itself is supplied by the right and left coronary arteries, which arise from the aortic sinuses just above the aortic valve; venous blood drains mainly via the coronary sinus into the right atrium.

Surfaces and landmarks

Anatomists describe the heart's apex (pointing down, forward and to the left), its base (posterior, formed mainly by the left atrium), and the grooves on its surface — the coronary sulcus and the anterior and posterior interventricular sulci — which mark the internal divisions and carry the coronary vessels.
