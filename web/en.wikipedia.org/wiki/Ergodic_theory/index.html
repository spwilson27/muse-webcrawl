Ergodic theory

Ergodic theory is a branch of mathematics that studies statistical properties of deterministic dynamical systems; it is a combination of measure theory and the theory of dynamical systems. An ergodic system is one for which, loosely speaking, time averages equal space averages: over long times, the trajectory of a typical point samples the whole space according to the invariant measure.

Origins

The theory arose in statistical mechanics, where Boltzmann's ergodic hypothesis asserted that a gas system evolves through all its possible microstates, justifying the use of ensemble averages. The word "ergodic" derives from the Greek ergon (work) and odos (path). The mathematical foundations were laid in the 1930s by George David Birkhoff and John von Neumann.

Main results

Birkhoff's ergodic theorem (1931) states that for a measure-preserving transformation T on a probability space and an integrable function f, the time averages (1/n) Σ f(Tix) converge almost everywhere to the integral of f whenever T is ergodic. Von Neumann's mean ergodic theorem gave the corresponding convergence in L². Other central results include Poincaré's recurrence theorem, the ergodic theorems of Kingman (subadditive) and Lindenstrauss (for amenable groups), Ornstein's isomorphism theory for Bernoulli shifts, and Furstenberg's ergodic proof of Szemerédi's theorem on arithmetic progressions via multiple recurrence.

Applications

Ergodic theory underlies statistical physics, number theory (the dynamics of continued fractions and the Gauss map, geodesic flows and equidistribution), probability theory, information theory (entropy of dynamical systems, following Kolmogorov and Sinai), and the theory of operator algebras. Ergodic methods proved landmark results including Furstenberg–Katznelson's multidimensional Szemerédi theorem and contributed to Green–Tao's theorem on arithmetic progressions in the primes.
