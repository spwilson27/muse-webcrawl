West Coast of the United States

The West Coast of the United States — also known as the Pacific Coast or Pacific Seaboard — is the coastline along which the Western United States meets the North Pacific Ocean. The term most often refers to the contiguous U.S. states of California, Oregon and Washington, and sometimes includes Alaska and Hawaii. By the strictest definition it comprises all land with coastline on the Pacific in those states.

Geography

The coast runs for roughly 1,300 miles (2,100 km) in the contiguous states, from the Mexican border near San Diego to the Strait of Juan de Fuca in Washington. Major coastal cities include San Diego, Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Jose, Portland (via the Columbia River) and Seattle (on Puget Sound). The region is geologically active, bounded by the Cascadia subduction zone in the north and the San Andreas Fault system in California, and its climate grades from Mediterranean in the south to temperate rainforest in the Pacific Northwest.

Population and economy

The three coastal states had a combined population of over 50 million in the 2020s. The West Coast is a centre of technology (Silicon Valley, Seattle), entertainment (Hollywood), shipping through the ports of Los Angeles, Long Beach, Oakland and Seattle–Tacoma, and agriculture. Politically, the coastal states are regarded as a stronghold of the Democratic Party — sometimes called the "Left Coast".
