Treaty of Turin (1860)

The Treaty of Turin was signed on 24 March 1860 between the Kingdom of Sardinia and the French Empire of Napoleon III, transferring the Duchy of Savoy and the County of Nice to France.

Background

At the secret meeting of Plombières in 1858, Cavour had secured French military support against Austria in return for Savoy and Nice. The Second Italian War of Independence followed in 1859; although France stopped short of the completeness Cavour hoped, the acquisition of Lombardy and the subsequent annexation of the central Italian duchies by Sardinia in early 1860 revived the bargain, and the cession was formalized at Turin.

Terms and plebiscites

Sardinia ceded Savoy and Nice, subject to consultation of their populations. Plebiscites held in April 1860 produced overwhelming majorities for union with France—results widely regarded by historians as managed by the authorities. The treaty guaranteed free-trade arrangements and free choice of nationality for inhabitants wishing to remain Sardinian subjects.

Consequences

The loss of Nice embittered Giuseppe Garibaldi, a native of the city, who denounced the cession in parliament. The annexation fixed the modern Franco-Italian Alpine frontier (adjusted slightly in 1947) and remains the legal basis of French sovereignty over Savoy and Nice.
