Fermented milk products

Fermented milk products, also known as cultured dairy foods or cultured milk products, are dairy foods that have been fermented with lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Lactococcus, and Leuconostoc. Fermentation increases shelf life, enhances taste, and can improve digestibility by partially breaking down lactose. Such products have been made for thousands of years and are staples in many cuisines.

Types

  • Yogurt — milk fermented with Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus; regional varieties include Greek yogurt (strained), skyr (Iceland), and dahi (South Asia).
  • Kefir — a fermented milk drink produced with kefir "grains" containing bacteria and yeasts; mildly effervescent and slightly alcoholic.
  • Cultured buttermilk — skim or low-fat milk fermented with Lactococcus cultures.
  • Sour cream and crème fraîche — fermented cream.
  • Cheese — in its many forms, most begin with lactic fermentation followed by coagulation, draining, and aging.
  • Regional products — kumis/airag (fermented mare's milk of Central Asia), filmjölk and viili (Scandinavia), amasi (southern Africa), lassi (India), ayran and doogh (salted yogurt drinks), lebneh (strained yogurt cheese), and tarator-style cultured milks across the Balkans and Middle East.

Production and microbiology

During fermentation, bacteria convert lactose into lactic acid, lowering the pH to about 4.0–4.6, which causes milk proteins (casein) to gel and inhibits spoilage organisms. Industrial production uses defined starter cultures under controlled temperature; traditional products rely on back-slopping or symbiotic cultures such as kefir grains. Some products are marketed as probiotics, containing live cultures claimed to benefit gut health.

History

Fermented milks arose spontaneously wherever milk was stored in warm conditions, likely as early as the domestication of dairy animals around 10,000 years ago. Élie Metchnikoff popularized yogurt consumption in Europe in the early 20th century, linking it to the longevity of Bulgarian peasants.
