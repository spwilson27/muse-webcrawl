E-learning (theory)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

E-learning theory describes the cognitive science principles of effective multimedia learning using electronic educational technology. It brings together research from cognitive psychology, instructional design and human–computer interaction to explain how people learn from words, pictures, sound and interactive activities delivered through computers and networks.

Background

The theory draws heavily on Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, which holds that learners process information through separate visual and auditory channels, each with limited capacity, and that meaningful learning requires active selection, organisation and integration of material. From this framework Mayer and colleagues derived a set of evidence-based design principles, including the coherence principle (remove extraneous material), the signalling principle (highlight essential material), the redundancy principle (avoid presenting identical text and narration together), and principles of spatial and temporal contiguity.

Key principles

Other foundational contributions include John Sweller's cognitive load theory, which distinguishes intrinsic, extraneous and germane load and warns against overloading working memory, and Robert Gagné's nine events of instruction. E-learning theory also incorporates constructivist approaches, in which learners build knowledge through activity and social interaction, and connectivism, proposed by George Siemens and Stephen Downes, which treats learning as the formation of connections within networks of people and digital resources.

Interaction and community

The Community of Inquiry framework, developed by Randy Garrison, Terry Anderson and Walter Archer, argues that a worthwhile online educational experience depends on the interplay of cognitive presence, social presence and teaching presence. Research on e-learning also addresses learner motivation, self-regulation, feedback, assessment integrity and accessibility.

Applications

E-learning theory informs the design of learning management systems, massive open online courses (MOOCs), virtual classrooms, simulations and mobile learning applications. Critics note that much of the evidence base comes from short laboratory studies with university students, and that effects found in controlled settings do not always transfer to authentic courses.
