Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) is a technique used to obtain an infrared spectrum of absorption or emission of a solid, liquid, or gas. An FTIR spectrometer simultaneously collects high-resolution spectral data over a wide spectral range, in contrast to a dispersive spectrometer, which measures intensity over a narrow range of wavelengths at a time.

Method

The instrument is based on a Michelson interferometer. Infrared light from a broadband source is split into two beams; one reflects off a fixed mirror and the other off a moving mirror, and they recombine to produce an interference pattern—an interferogram—that encodes all infrared frequencies at once as the mirror scans. The sample absorbs characteristic frequencies, and a computer applies a Fourier transform to the interferogram to recover the spectrum. Because all frequencies are measured simultaneously, FTIR enjoys the multiplex (Fellgett) advantage, and the use of a laser reference gives it high wavelength accuracy (the Connes advantage).

Applications

Infrared absorption bands correspond to molecular vibrations, so an FTIR spectrum acts as a chemical fingerprint. The method is widely used to identify unknown materials, verify the composition of polymers, pharmaceuticals, and paints, detect contaminants and gases, and study proteins and forensic samples. Variants include attenuated total reflectance (ATR-FTIR), convenient for surface and liquid samples, and FTIR microscopy for imaging small specimens.
