Kilt

TypeKnee-length pleated skirt-like garment
OriginScottish Highlands, 16th century
MaterialWoollen tartan twill
Associated dressHighland dress

A kilt is a garment resembling a wrap-around knee-length skirt, made of twill-woven worsted wool with a tartan pattern, originating in the traditional dress of men and boys in the Scottish Highlands. The modern kilt is pleated at the back and sides, with a flat front apron, and is fastened with straps and buckles. It is the best-known item of Scottish national dress and is worn at formal occasions, weddings, Highland games, and by Scottish regiments.

History

The kilt evolved from the féileadh mór or "great kilt" of the 16th century — a large length of tartan cloth belted at the waist, with the upper part draped over the shoulder or used as a cloak. The smaller tailored kilt (féileadh beag) emerged in the early 18th century. Following the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act 1746 banned Highland dress for civilians (a ban repealed in 1782), though Highland regiments of the British Army kept the kilt as uniform.

During the 19th century, popularised by Sir Walter Scott and the visit of George IV to Edinburgh in 1822, the kilt became a symbol of Scottish identity as a whole, and the system of clan tartans was codified, sometimes called "tartanry".

Construction and accessories

A traditional kilt uses up to eight yards of cloth, hand-sewn, with deep knife pleats stitched to show either the sett (full pattern) or a stripe of the tartan ("military pleating"). Standard accessories include a sporran (a pouch worn at the front), kilt pin, sgian-dubh (a small knife tucked into the hose), knee-length kilt hose with flashes, and Ghillie brogues. A formal outfit adds a Prince Charlie jacket and waistcoat, bow tie, and sometimes a plaid draped over the shoulder.

Modern use

Kilts are worn for formal and ceremonial wear, by pipe bands, and by members of the Scottish diaspora worldwide. Contemporary variants include utility kilts in plain fabrics and kilts in the national traditions of Ireland, Wales (the cilt), and Cornwall.
