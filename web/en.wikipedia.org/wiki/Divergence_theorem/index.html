Divergence theorem
FieldVector calculus

In vector calculus, the divergence theorem, also known as Gauss's theorem or Ostrogradsky's theorem, relates the flux of a vector field through a closed surface to the divergence of the field in the volume enclosed. In words: the total outward flux across a closed boundary equals the sum of all the sources (minus sinks) inside.

Statement

Let V be a compact region of three-dimensional space with piecewise smooth boundary surface S = ∂V, and let F be a continuously differentiable vector field. Then:

∭V (∇ · F) dV = ∯S F · n dS

where n is the outward-pointing unit normal to the surface.

History

Special cases were known to Lagrange and Gauss; Carl Friedrich Gauss used such identities in his work on gravitation and magnetism in the 1810s–1830s, and Mikhail Ostrogradsky gave the first general proof in 1826, publishing in 1831. The theorem is a three-dimensional counterpart of the fundamental theorem of calculus and a special case of the generalised Stokes theorem.

Applications

The divergence theorem is fundamental in physics and engineering: it underlies the integral forms of Gauss's law for electricity and magnetism in Maxwell's equations, derivations of the continuity equation in fluid dynamics, and the analysis of heat conduction and diffusion. It is routinely used to convert difficult volume integrals into simpler surface integrals and vice versa.
