The covered passages of Paris (French: passages couverts de Paris) are a set of early shopping arcades built in Paris, France, mostly in the first half of the 19th century. These pedestrianised galleries, covered by glass roofs and lined with shops, were an early form of the shopping mall and a celebrated feature of Parisian life in the decades before the rise of the department store.

History

The earliest passages — the Galeries de bois of the Palais Royal (1786) and the Passage du Caire (1798) — offered Parisians sheltered, gaslit (later) streets of luxury shops at a time when the city had few pavements and muddy streets. Construction boomed in the 1820s–1840s, producing elegant galleries such as the Galerie Vivienne (1823), the Passage des Panoramas (1799, extended later), and the Galerie Véro-Dodat (1826), many concentrated on the Right Bank near the Grands Boulevards.

Haussmann's renovation of Paris and the arrival of large department stores after 1850 caused the passages to decline; many were demolished. Of roughly 150 passages at their peak, around twenty survive, several — including the Galerie Vivienne — protected as historic monuments and later restored.

Cultural significance

The passages were famously analysed by the philosopher Walter Benjamin in his unfinished Arcades Project (Das Passagen-Werk), where they became emblematic of 19th-century commodity culture and the figure of the flâneur.
