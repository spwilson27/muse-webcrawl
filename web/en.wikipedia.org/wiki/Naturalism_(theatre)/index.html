Naturalism (theatre)

Naturalism is a movement in European theatre that developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, seeking to reproduce reality faithfully on stage. It grew out of the literary naturalism of Émile Zola, whose 1873 essay on the naturalist novel called for art based on scientific observation, heredity, and environment.

Characteristics

Naturalist productions favoured detailed, realistic sets — real furniture, functioning doors, even actual food — and acting that imitated everyday behaviour and speech rather than declamation. Plays typically portrayed ordinary, often working-class people caught in social and biological forces beyond their control. The convention of the "fourth wall" was strengthened: the audience was to feel it was eavesdropping on real life.

Key figures and works

André Antoine's Théâtre Libre in Paris (founded 1887) became the movement's flagship, staging new plays with unprecedented realism. Major naturalist dramatists include Henrik Ibsen, whose later prose plays dissected bourgeois society; August Strindberg, whose preface to Miss Julie (1888) is a naturalist manifesto; Anton Chekhov; and Gerhart Hauptmann, whose The Weavers (1892) dramatised a Silesian uprising. In Russia, Konstantin Stanislavski's Moscow Art Theatre brought psychological realism to the movement, influencing acting worldwide.

Influence

Although naturalism as a strict programme faded, its insistence on truthful behaviour, environmental detail, and socially engaged subject matter shaped modern realism in theatre, film, and television.
