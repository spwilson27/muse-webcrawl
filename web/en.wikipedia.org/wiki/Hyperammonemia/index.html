Hyperammonemia

Hyperammonemia is a metabolic disturbance characterized by an excess of ammonia in the blood. Ammonia, a product of protein catabolism normally converted to urea by the liver, is neurotoxic, and hyperammonemia can cause vomiting, lethargy, irritability, seizures, coma and, if untreated, death.

Causes

Primary hyperammonemia results from inherited defects of the urea cycle enzymes or transporters — the commonest being ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency — or of related pathways. Secondary hyperammonemia arises from liver failure, portosystemic shunting, certain infections with urease-producing organisms, valproic acid therapy, and other conditions that impair hepatic clearance or increase ammonia production.

Pathophysiology

Ammonia crosses the blood–brain barrier; in astrocytes it is combined with glutamate to form glutamine, whose accumulation causes osmotic swelling and cerebral oedema, the main mechanism of acute encephalopathy.

Treatment

Management of acute episodes involves stopping protein intake, providing glucose calories to suppress catabolism, and removing ammonia with scavenger drugs (sodium benzoate, sodium phenylacetate/phenylbutyrate, glycerol phenylacetate), L-arginine, and, in severe cases, haemodialysis. Long-term care includes dietary protein restriction and, for some urea cycle disorders, liver transplantation.
