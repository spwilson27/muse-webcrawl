Laminar flow

In fluid dynamics, laminar flow is characterized by fluid particles following smooth paths in layers, with no disruption between the layers. At low velocities the fluid flows without lateral mixing, adjacent layers sliding past one another like playing cards. It is the opposite of turbulent flow.

Reynolds number

Whether flow is laminar or turbulent is predicted by the dimensionless Reynolds number (Re), the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. For flow in a pipe, laminar flow generally occurs below Re ≈ 2,300, transitions to turbulence between roughly 2,300 and 4,000, and is fully turbulent above that. Laminar flow is governed by the Navier–Stokes equations; for a pipe, the exact solution yields a parabolic velocity profile and the Hagen–Poiseuille relation for pressure drop.

Applications

Laminar behaviour dominates in slow flows of viscous fluids — groundwater movement, blood flow in small vessels, and microfluidics. In aeronautics, "laminar-flow airfoils" are designed to delay boundary-layer transition, reducing drag; in cleanrooms and laminar-flow cabinets, filtered air moves in parallel streams to protect sterile work. The ink-stream demonstrations of Osborne Reynolds in the 1880s first visualized the laminar-to-turbulent transition.
