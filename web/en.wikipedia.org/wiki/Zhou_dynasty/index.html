Zhou dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Zhou dynasty ritual bronze bell (bianzhong) set]

The Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BC) was a royal dynasty of China that followed the Shang and preceded the Qin — the longest-lasting dynasty in Chinese history. Its kings justified their conquest by the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven, which became central to Chinese political thought. The dynasty divides into the Western Zhou (c. 1046–771 BC), ruling from the Wei valley, and the Eastern Zhou (770–256 BC) at Luoyang, whose era of nominal kingship over increasingly independent states — the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods — produced Confucius, Laozi, Mencius, Mozi and Zhuangzi: the Hundred Schools of Thought that defined Chinese philosophy.

Western Zhou

King Wu defeated the Shang at Muye (c. 1046 BC); the Duke of Zhou, regent for the young King Cheng, consolidated the new order, enfeoffing relatives and allies across the north China plain in a kinship-feudal system (fengjian). Bronze inscriptions, ritual reform and the compilation of the earliest strata of the classics (the Book of Documents, Book of Songs) mark the era. In 771 BC, after King You was killed by Quanrong invaders and discontented vassals, the court fled east to Luoyang.

Eastern Zhou: Spring and Autumn, Warring States

Zhou kings became figureheads as great states — Qi, Jin, Chu, Qin, Wu, Yue — competed for hegemony, then annexed the smaller states outright. The Warring States period (475–221 BC) saw mass infantry armies, iron weapons, crossbows, cavalry, bureaucratic reform (Shang Yang's Legalist transformation of Qin) and dazzling intellectual ferment. In 256 BC Qin extinguished the Zhou royal line; in 221 BC it extinguished the remaining states and unified China.
