Percy Williams Bridgman

Percy Williams Bridgman
Born: 21 April 1882, Cambridge, Massachusetts, U.S.
Died: 20 August 1961 (aged 79)
Known for: High-pressure physics, operationalism
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physics (1946)

Percy Williams Bridgman (21 April 1882 – 20 August 1961) was an American physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1946 for his work on the physics of high pressures. He also wrote extensively on the scientific method and on other aspects of the philosophy of science, and advocateded operationalism, the view that a concept is defined by the operations used to measure it.

Biography

Bridgman entered Harvard University in 1900 and studied physics there until earning his PhD in 1908. He taught at Harvard from 1910 until his retirement in 1954, becoming Hollis Professor of Mathematics and Natural Philosophy in 1926 and later Higgins University Professor. His honours included the Rumford Medal and honorary degrees from several universities.

High-pressure work

Bridgman developed apparatus — including the self-tightening seal that bears his name — capable of generating pressures far beyond what had previously been achieved, eventually exceeding 100,000 atmospheres. With this equipment he systematically studied the electrical, thermal and mechanical properties of hundreds of substances under extreme compression, discovering new high-pressure polymorphs of ice and investigating the compressibility of solids, work that founded modern high-pressure physics and underlies techniques such as the diamond anvil cell.

Philosophy of science

In The Logic of Modern Physics (1927), Bridgman argued that physical concepts such as length lose their meaning when the operations used at large scales cannot be carried over to small scales, a stance influenced by Einstein's analysis of simultaneity. His operationalist philosophy influenced psychology and the social sciences as well as physics.
