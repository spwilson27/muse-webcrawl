Hypertrophic scar

Hypertrophic scar
Specialty: Dermatology, plastic surgery
Symptoms: Raised, red, firm scar confined to wound margins
Causes: Excess collagen deposition during wound healing
Treatment: Silicone gel, corticosteroid injections, pressure therapy, laser, surgery

A hypertrophic scar is a cutaneous condition characterized by deposits of excessive amounts of collagen which gives rise to a raised scar, but not to the degree observed with keloids. Like keloids, hypertrophic scars form most often at sites of pimples, body piercings, cuts and burns. They contain nerves and blood vessels and are generally red or pink, firm, and sometimes itchy.

Difference from keloids

The key distinction from a keloid is that a hypertrophic scar remains confined within the boundaries of the original wound, whereas a keloid extends beyond the wound margins into surrounding normal skin. Hypertrophic scars also tend to develop within weeks of injury and may regress spontaneously over time, while keloids rarely regress and can continue growing for years.

Causes and risk factors

Hypertrophic scarring results from an imbalance between collagen synthesis and degradation during the proliferative and remodeling phases of wound healing. Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling is upregulated, and myofibroblasts persist longer than usual. Risk factors include deep burns, wound infection, delayed healing, mechanical tension across the wound, darker skin pigmentation, and younger age. Common sites include the shoulders, chest, elbows, knees, and earlobes.

Treatment

First-line treatments include silicone gel sheeting, pressure garments (especially after burns), intralesional corticosteroid injections such as triamcinolone, and massage. Other options include pulsed-dye laser for redness, cryotherapy, 5-fluorouracil injections, and surgical excision with adjunctive therapy to reduce recurrence. Many hypertrophic scars soften and flatten substantially over 6–12 months even without intervention.
