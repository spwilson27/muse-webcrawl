Siglec

Siglecs (sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins) are a family of cell-surface proteins found primarily on cells of the immune system. They belong to the immunoglobulin superfamily and bind sialic acids — nine-carbon acidic sugars that cap the ends of many glycoproteins and glycolipids — thereby mediating cell–cell interactions and regulating immune responses. Most Siglecs are inhibitory receptors that dampen immune-cell activation, making them important checkpoints in inflammation, infection, autoimmunity and cancer.

Structure and classification

A Siglec molecule has an extracellular portion with an N-terminal V-set immunoglobulin-like domain that binds sialic acid, followed by a variable number of C2-set Ig-like domains, a single transmembrane segment, and a cytoplasmic tail. Most family members carry immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motifs (ITIMs) in that tail, which recruit phosphatases such as SHP-1 and SHP-2 to suppress signalling. In humans the family comprises about 14 functional receptors, Siglec-1 (sialoadhesin) through Siglec-16 in part; they are usually divided into a conserved group (Siglec-1, -2/CD22, -4/MAG and -15) and a rapidly evolving CD33-related group (including Siglec-3/CD33, -5, -6, -7, -8, -9, -10, -11 and -14), many of which exist as paired activating and inhibitory variants.

Biological roles

Because sialic acids are abundant "self" markers on human cells, Siglecs help immune cells distinguish self from non-self. CD22 (Siglec-2) restrains B-cell receptor signalling and is a therapeutic target in B-cell leukaemias and lymphoma. CD33 (Siglec-3) is exploited in acute myeloid leukaemia therapy by the antibody–drug conjugate gemtuzumab ozogamicin. Siglec-8, restricted to eosinophils and mast cells, is a target in eosinophilic disorders; Siglec-15 regulates osteoclasts and tumour immunity; and MAG (Siglec-4) maintains the myelin sheath in the nervous system. Some pathogens, including group B Streptococcus, display sialic acids that engage inhibitory Siglecs to evade immunity, while certain tumours hypersialylate their surfaces to suppress anti-tumour responses — a mechanism now targeted by experimental sialidase and anti-Siglec therapies.
