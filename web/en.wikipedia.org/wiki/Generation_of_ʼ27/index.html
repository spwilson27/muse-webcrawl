Generation of '27

The Generation of '27 (Spanish: Generación del 27) was a group of Spanish poets and writers who rose to prominence in the 1920s and are generally considered the second great flowering of Spanish poetry after the Golden Age. The group took its name from the year 1927, when its members gathered in Seville to commemorate the third centenary of the death of the Baroque poet Luis de Góngora, whose complex, metaphor-rich style they sought to rehabilitate after centuries of neglect.

The Generation of '27 combined a deep respect for Spanish literary tradition — the popular ballad (romancero), the songs of the cancioneros, and the classics of the Siglo de Oro — with an enthusiastic embrace of the European avant-garde, including surrealism, futurism, and the pure poetry advocated by Juan Ramón Jiménez. Its best-known members include Federico García Lorca, Rafael Alberti, Jorge Guillén, Pedro Salinas, Vicente Aleixandre, Luis Cernuda, Dámaso Alonso, Gerardo Diego, Emilio Prados, and Manuel Altolaguirre.

Origins

Most of the group's members met in Madrid during the early 1920s, many of them at the Residencia de Estudiantes, the celebrated student residence that also hosted the filmmaker Luis Buñuel and the painter Salvador Dalí. There they encountered visiting lecturers such as Albert Einstein and Marie Curie, and absorbed the cosmopolitan, experimental atmosphere of interwar European culture. Journals such as Revista de Occidente, founded by José Ortega y Gasset, and little magazines including Litoral, Verso y Prosa, and Carmen provided outlets for their early work.

The name itself derives from the homage to Góngora held at the Ateneo de Sevilla on 16–17 December 1927, organised largely by the bullfighter and cultural patron Ignacio Sánchez Mejías. The participants read Góngora's Soledades and defended his difficult, ornamental style against its detractors, signalling that the new poetry would unite erudition and formal ambition with modern sensibility. The term "Generation of '27" was popularised later by the critic Dámaso Alonso, himself a member.

Characteristics

Unlike many avant-garde movements, the Generation of '27 did not define itself against tradition. Its poets moved fluidly between classical forms — the sonnet, the romance, the décima — and free verse; between popular song and hermetic metaphor. Critics conventionally describe an evolution in three phases: an early period of formal experimentation and "pure poetry" influenced by Jiménez; a middle period, from about 1929, of surrealist influence and growing social and political engagement, marked by works such as Lorca's Poet in New York (written 1929–30) and Alberti's Sobre los ángeles; and a final period overshadowed by the Spanish Civil War.

Recurring features include the elaborate, Góngora-inspired metaphor; an interest in dreams and the unconscious drawn from surrealism; and a Whitmanic or Nerudian breadth in the later, socially committed poems. The group had no single manifesto, and its members' styles diverged considerably: Guillén's luminous affirmation in Cántico contrasts sharply with Cernuda's elegiac explorations of desire and exile in La realidad y el deseo.

Principal members and works

Federico García Lorca (1898–1936), the group's most famous figure, published the Romancero gitano (Gypsy Ballads, 1928), which fused Andalusian folk forms with startling surreal imagery, and the posthumous Poeta en Nueva York. Rafael Alberti (1902–1999) won the National Poetry Prize for Marinero en tierra (1925) before turning to surrealism and committed poetry. Jorge Guillén (1893–1984) spent decades perfecting Cántico. Pedro Salinas (1891–1951) wrote the love trilogy beginning with La voz a ti debida (1933). Vicente Aleixandre (1898–1984) received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1977, an award widely read as recognition of the whole generation. Luis Cernuda (1902–1963), Emilio Prados, Manuel Altolaguirre, Gerardo Diego, and Dámaso Alonso complete the usual canon of ten names.

The group also included women writers, long marginalised in accounts of the generation and now often discussed under the name Las Sinsombrero ("the hatless women"), among them the poet Ernestina de Champourcín, the writer and philosopher María Zambrano, the novelist Rosa Chacel, and the poet Concha Méndez.

The Civil War and exile

The Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) destroyed the generation as a collective. Lorca was murdered by Nationalist militia near Granada in August 1936, an event that became an international symbol of fascist violence against culture. Most surviving members went into exile after Franco's victory: Alberti to Argentina and Italy, Cernuda to Britain, the United States, and finally Mexico, Guillén and Salinas to the United States, Prados and Altolaguirre to Mexico. Aleixandre and Alonso remained in Spain, forming a bridge to younger poets under the dictatorship. The dispersion of the group, and the suppression of much of its work within Spain, delayed full recognition of the generation's achievement until after Franco's death in 1975.

Legacy

The Generation of '27 is now regarded as one of the high points of Spanish literature. Its synthesis of tradition and modernity offered a model distinct from both nineteenth-century realism and the more iconoclastic European avant-gardes. Lorca's works are among the most translated of any Spanish author, and the poetry of Cernuda and Aleixandre strongly influenced later Spanish poets, including the Generation of '50. Anniversaries in 1977 and 2002 brought major exhibitions and scholarly reassessments, including efforts to restore the generation's women to the canon.
