Dengue fever is a mosquito-borne disease caused by the dengue virus, a flavivirus transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti mosquitoes. Symptoms typically begin three to fourteen days after infection and include high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pains — hence the nickname "breakbone fever" — nausea, and a characteristic rash. Most people recover within two to seven days, but a small proportion develop severe dengue, with plasma leakage, internal bleeding, falling platelet counts, and potentially fatal shock.

Cause and transmission

There are four closely related dengue virus serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), with a fifth sylvatic serotype reported in 2013. Infection with one serotype gives lifelong immunity to that serotype but only temporary protection against the others. A second infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe disease, a phenomenon attributed to antibody-dependent enhancement. The virus is maintained in a human–mosquito cycle in urban areas; the daytime-biting Aedes mosquitoes breed in small collections of standing water around homes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by clinical presentation together with laboratory tests detecting viral RNA, the NS1 antigen, or anti-dengue antibodies. There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, with rest, fluids, and paracetamol for fever, while aspirin and NSAIDs are avoided because they increase bleeding risk. Severe dengue requires hospital care with careful fluid management, which reduces mortality from over 20% to under 1%.

Epidemiology and prevention

Dengue is endemic in more than 100 countries in Southeast Asia, the Pacific, the Americas, Africa, and the Caribbean, and its incidence has grown dramatically with urbanisation, travel, and climate change; an estimated 390 million infections occur annually, of which about 96 million are clinically apparent. Prevention centres on mosquito control and bite avoidance. A first vaccine, Dengvaxia, was licensed from 2015 but restricted to people with prior dengue infection because of safety concerns in seronegative recipients; a second vaccine, Qdenga, was later approved in several countries.
