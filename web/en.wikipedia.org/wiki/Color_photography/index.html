Color photography (or colour photography) is photography that uses media capable of capturing and reproducing colours, as opposed to black-and-white or monochrome photography, which records only luminance. Colour images are typically produced by analysing the scene into red, green and blue channels — matching the trichromatic nature of human vision — and recombining them additively or subtractively.

History

The three-colour principle was proposed by James Clerk Maxwell, who in 1861 presented the first durable colour photograph: a tartan ribbon photographed three times through red, green and blue filters and projected in register by Thomas Sutton. Early processes were laborious; the Lumière brothers' Autochrome (1907), using dyed potato-starch grains as filters, was the first commercially successful colour process. In the 1930s, Kodachrome and Agfacolor introduced multilayer films with colour couplers, making colour photography practical for amateurs and professionals alike. Colour became the dominant form of photography after the Second World War.

Digital era

Digital cameras record colour using sensor arrays with Bayer-pattern filters, demosaicing the data into full-colour images. Colour management, white balance and colour spaces such as sRGB govern how colours are captured and displayed.
