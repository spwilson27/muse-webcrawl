Musket Wars

The Musket Wars were a series of as many as 3,000 battles and raids fought throughout New Zealand (and on the Chatham Islands) among Māori iwi (tribes) between about 1806 and 1845, after Māori obtained muskets in trade with Europeans. The wars were among the deadliest in New Zealand's history, with estimates of 20,000 to 40,000 Māori killed, and they profoundly reshaped tribal boundaries (rohe) across the country.

Māori fighters at a pā during the musket era.

Causes

From the early 1800s, northern tribes such as Ngāpuhi traded flax, timber, potatoes, and pork with visiting ships for muskets. Access to firearms created a military imbalance: tribes with muskets could overwhelm those without, who were then driven to acquire muskets themselves, fuelling an arms race. Trade in preserved tattooed heads (mokomokai) also grew with European demand. Potato cultivation, adopted from Europeans, allowed larger war parties (taua) to travel farther by providing portable food supplies.

Course of the wars

The Ngāpuhi chief Hongi Hika, who visited England in 1820 and returned with hundreds of muskets, led devastating campaigns in the 1820s against tribes in the Auckland, Waikato, and Bay of Plenty regions, including the fall of the pā at Mauinaina and Mokoia Island. The Ngāti Toa chief Te Rauparaha led his people on a great migration (heke) from Kāwhia to the Kapiti Coast and, armed with muskets, extended influence over the lower North Island and northern South Island. In 1835, Ngāti Mutunga and Ngāti Tama, having seized a brig, invaded the Chatham Islands and killed or enslaved much of the Moriori population, who were committed to pacifism — an event amounting to genocide.

End and consequences

By the late 1830s, muskets were universal, the military advantage had evaporated, and fortified pā were redesigned to withstand gunfire; exhaustion and growing European presence, followed by the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), brought the era to a close. The wars depopulated some regions, displaced many tribes from their ancestral lands — sowing disputes later heard by the Waitangi Tribunal — and accelerated Māori engagement with European trade and literacy.
