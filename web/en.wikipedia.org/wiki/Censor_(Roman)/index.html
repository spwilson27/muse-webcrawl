The censor was a magistrate of ancient Rome responsible for conducting the census, supervising public morality (regimen morum), maintaining the roll of the Senate, and overseeing certain state finances and public works. Two censors were elected at a time, normally from among former consuls, and the office was regarded as the capstone of the cursus honorum, conferring immense prestige even though the censors lacked imperium and lictors.

History

The censorship was created in 443 BC, when the growing census work was taken from the consuls. Originally patrician-only, the office was opened to plebeians in 351 BC, and by law one censor was eventually required to be plebeian. Censors were chosen every five years by the Centuriate Assembly but held office for at most eighteen months, completing the census and the ritual purification, the lustrum. The dictator Sulla and later the emperors absorbed or curtailed the office's powers; the emperor Domitian assumed the title of censor for life.

Duties

The censors registered citizens and their property, assigning each to a tribe and a property class that determined taxation and military service. In the lectio senatus they revised the Senate roll, enrolling new members and expelling the unworthy. Through the nota censoria they could stigmatise citizens for moral failings — cowardice, neglect of land, luxury — degrading them in status. The most famous holder, Cato the Elder (censor 184 BC), embodied the office's austere moralism. Censors also let state contracts, maintained public buildings and roads — Appius Claudius Caecus built the Appian Way and Rome's first aqueduct during his censorship — and leased public land.

Legacy

The modern word "censorship" derives from this office's supervision of morals, and "census" from its registration function.
