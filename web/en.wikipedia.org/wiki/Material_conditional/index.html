The material conditional (also known as material implication) is a binary operation used in logic and mathematics. The statement "if p then q", written p → q, is false only when the antecedent p is true and the consequent q is false; in all other cases it is true. It is therefore logically equivalent to ¬p ∨ q ("not p, or q") and to its contrapositive, ¬q → ¬p.

Truth table

When p is true and q is true, p → q is true. When p is true and q is false, the conditional is false. When p is false, the conditional is true regardless of q — a feature known as "vacuous truth" when applied to universally quantified statements in mathematics.

Paradoxes of material implication

Because a false antecedent makes the conditional automatically true, and a true consequent does likewise, the material conditional licenses statements that seem irrelevant or misleading in natural language, such as "if the Moon is made of cheese, then Paris is in France." These so-called paradoxes of material implication motivated alternatives including strict implication (C. I. Lewis), relevance logic, and the probabilistic and counterfactual analyses of conditionals. Philosophers such as H. P. Grice defended the material conditional by arguing that the oddity of such statements is pragmatic (conversational implicature) rather than semantic.

Use in mathematics

In mathematics the material conditional underlies standard proof techniques: direct proof of p → q, proof by contraposition, and vacuous cases in induction. It differs from causal, temporal and subjunctive conditionals of ordinary speech.
