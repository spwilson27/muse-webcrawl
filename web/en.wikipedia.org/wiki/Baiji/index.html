The baiji (Lipotes vexillifer), also known as the Yangtze river dolphin, is a freshwater dolphin species endemic to the Yangtze River system in China. It is one of the most endangered cetaceans in the world: an extensive survey in 2006 failed to find a single individual, leading scientists to declare the species functionally extinct. A possible sighting was reported in 2016 but was never confirmed, and the IUCN lists the baiji as Critically Endangered, possibly extinct. It would be the first cetacean species driven to extinction by human activity.

Description

Baiji had a stocky body, a very long, narrow upturned beak, a rounded melon, and small eyes with poor vision, relying heavily on echolocation to navigate and catch fish in the silty river. Adults measured roughly 1.4–2.5 metres in length, with females larger than males, and were bluish-grey above and white below. They lived in small groups and fed on a variety of river fish.

Decline

Once numbering an estimated 6,000 in the 1950s, the baiji declined through hunting during the Great Leap Forward, and then through habitat degradation, collisions with vessels, entanglement in fishing gear, electric fishing, and the damming of the Yangtze, including the Gezhouba and Three Gorges dams. By the late 1990s only a handful remained. Qiqi, a male captured in 1980, lived at the Wuhan Institute of Hydrobiology until his death in 2002 and was the last baiji in captivity.

Conservation

Protected oxbow-lake reserves were established for the baiji, though they ultimately benefited the Yangtze finless porpoise more. A 2006 six-week expedition covering the dolphin's historic range found none and concluded the species was functionally extinct. Unconfirmed reports in 2007 and 2016 briefly raised hopes, but no verified evidence of survival has emerged.
