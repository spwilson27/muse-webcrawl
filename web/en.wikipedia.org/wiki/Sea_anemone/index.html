Sea anemone

Sea anemones are predatory marine invertebrates of the order Actiniaria, class Anthozoa, in the phylum Cnidaria. Named after the terrestrial flowering plant because of their colourful, flower-like appearance, sea anemones are close relatives of corals and jellyfish. Most species attach to hard surfaces on the sea floor with an adhesive basal disc, though some burrow in soft sediment or float near the surface.

Anatomy

A sea anemone consists of a cylindrical column topped by an oral disc ringed with tentacles armed with cnidocytes — stinging cells containing nematocysts that fire harpoon-like threads to capture prey and deter predators. The mouth at the centre of the disc leads to a stomach cavity; it is the animal's only opening, serving for both ingestion and excretion. Anemones lack a medusa stage and spend their lives as polyps, reproducing both sexually and asexually by fission or pedal laceration.

Ecology

More than 1,000 species are known, from polar seas to tropical reefs; the largest exceed one metre in diameter. Many host photosynthetic algae (zooxanthellae) in their tissues. Sea anemones are famous for mutualisms: clownfish live among the tentacles of certain species, protected by a mucus layer, and some hermit crabs carry anemones on their shells. Anemones are also kept in marine aquaria, and species such as Aiptasia can become pests.
