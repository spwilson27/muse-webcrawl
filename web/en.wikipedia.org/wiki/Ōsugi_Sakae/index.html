Ōsugi Sakae (大杉 栄, 17 January 1885 – 16 September 1923) was a prominent Japanese anarchist of the Taishō period. A prolific translator and publisher, he introduced the works of Kropotkin, Bakunin, and European syndicalist thought to Japanese readers and became the central figure of Japan's prewar anarchist movement. He was murdered by military police in the Amakasu Incident, in the chaos following the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake.

Life and activism

Born in Marugame, the son of an army officer, Ōsugi was expelled from military cadet school and turned to socialist circles in Tokyo. He was repeatedly imprisoned — including for the Red Flag Incident of 1908 — and edited journals such as Kindai Shisō ("Modern Thought") and Rōdō Undō ("Labour Movement"). Influenced by Kōtoku Shūsui, he moved from socialism toward anarchosyndicalism, organizing labour unions and advocating direct action and free love.

His personal life was famously turbulent: his relationships with journalist Kamichika Ichiko — who stabbed him in the 1916 Hikage-teahouse incident — and with the feminist writer Itō Noe drew public scandal and fractured the movement, yet Itō became his partner and collaborator in his final years.

Death

On 16 September 1923, days after the Great Kantō earthquake, Ōsugi, Itō Noe, and Ōsugi's six-year-old nephew were arrested by a squad of Kempeitai led by Lieutenant Amakasu Masahiko, beaten to death, and thrown into a well. The Amakasu Incident provoked national outrage; Amakasu was court-martialled and briefly imprisoned, and the murders became a symbol of prewar state violence against dissenters.

Legacy

Ōsugi's translations and essays shaped Japanese radical thought, and his life has been dramatized in film, notably in Yoshida Kijū's Eros + Massacre (1969).
