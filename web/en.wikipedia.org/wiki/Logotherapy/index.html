Logotherapy

Logotherapy is a form of psychotherapy developed by Austrian psychiatrist Viktor Frankl, based on the belief that the primary human motivational force is the search for meaning in life. Frankl presented the approach in his 1946 book Man's Search for Meaning, drawing on his experiences as a Holocaust survivor. The name derives from the Greek logos, here understood as "meaning".

Basic principles

Logotherapy rests on three assumptions: life has meaning under all circumstances, even suffering; the main motivation for living is the "will to meaning"; and people have freedom to find meaning in what they do, experience, or in the attitude they take toward unavoidable suffering. Meaning can be found through creative values (work and deeds), experiential values (love, beauty, nature), and attitudinal values (the stance taken toward suffering).

Techniques

Frankl described techniques including paradoxical intention, in which a patient is encouraged to intend or exaggerate a feared symptom, breaking a cycle of anticipatory anxiety, and dereflection, redirecting attention away from excessive self-monitoring. Socratic dialogue is used to help patients discover their own meanings.

Existential vacuum

Frankl argued that a widespread sense of meaninglessness — the "existential vacuum" — underlies many modern neuroses and can manifest as boredom, depression, aggression, or addiction. Logotherapy addresses these "noogenic neuroses" by helping the patient find concrete meaning and purpose.

Reception

Logotherapy is considered part of existential and humanistic psychology and influenced later meaning-focused therapies. Frankl founded the first logotherapy institute in Vienna; institutes and training programmes now exist worldwide.
