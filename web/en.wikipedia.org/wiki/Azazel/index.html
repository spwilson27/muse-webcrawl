In the Hebrew Bible, the name Azazel (Hebrew: עֲזָאזֵל) represents a desolate place where a scapegoat bearing the sins of the Jewish people is sent during Yom Kippur. In the Book of Enoch, Azazel is a fallen angel who corrupts humanity by teaching forbidden arts. Over time, in Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions, Azazel came to be understood as a demon or a fallen angel in his own right, and in some interpretations as a name associated with Satan.

Hebrew Bible

In Leviticus 16, Aaron is commanded to take two goats for a sin offering on the Day of Atonement. Lots are cast: one goat is designated "for the LORD" and sacrificed, while the other is "for Azazel". The high priest lays both hands on the living goat, confesses over it all the iniquities of Israel, and sends it away into the wilderness bearing the sins of the people. The meaning of "Azazel" has been debated for millennia: suggested readings include a rugged mountain or cliff, a proper name of a wilderness demon, "the goat that departs" (the source of the English word scapegoat), or an abstract noun meaning "complete removal".

Book of Enoch

In the apocryphal Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch), Azazel is one of the chief fallen angels (Watchers) who descend to earth. He teaches men to forge swords, knives, shields and metalwork, and teaches women cosmetics and the beautification of the eyelids, leading humanity into corruption. God orders the archangel Raphael to bind Azazel hand and foot, cast him into darkness in the desert place of Dudael, and place jagged rocks over him until the day of judgement, when he will be thrown into fire. The text declares that "the whole earth has been corrupted through the works of Azazel: to him ascribe all sin."

Later traditions

Rabbinic literature variously treats Azazel as the name of a mountain, a demon, or an embodiment of impurity; the Zohar associates it with fallen powers. In Islamic tradition, Azazel (ʿAzāzīl) appears in some accounts as the original name of Iblis before his fall. In Western occultism and demonology, Azazel appears in grimoires as a powerful demon, sometimes as a standard-bearer of hell's armies. The figure recurs in literature and popular culture, from Milton's Paradise Lost, where Azazel is Satan's standard-bearer, to modern comics, television and film.
