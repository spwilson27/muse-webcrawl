Stanley Baldwin, 1st Earl Baldwin of Bewdley (3 August 1867 – 14 December 1947), was a British statesman and Conservative politician who dominated the government of the United Kingdom between the world wars. He was Prime Minister on three occasions, from May 1923 to January 1924, from November 1924 to June 1929, and from June 1935 to May 1937.

Born into a wealthy Worcestershire iron and steel family, Baldwin entered Parliament in 1908, succeeding his father Alfred. He rose rapidly during the First World War, becoming Chancellor of the Exchequer and helping to oust David Lloyd George's coalition in 1922. As Chancellor he negotiated settlement of Britain's war debt to the United States.

First premiership (1923–1924)

Baldwin succeeded Bonar Law as Prime Minister in May 1923. His call for tariff reform (protection) prompted him to call an election in December 1923, in which the Conservatives lost their majority, enabling Ramsay MacDonald's first Labour government. Baldwin returned to power after the October 1924 election.

Second premiership (1924–1929)

His second government returned Britain to the gold standard (1925), faced down the General Strike of 1926, passed the Trade Disputes and Trade Unions Act 1927, and extended the franchise to women on equal terms with men in 1928. Baldwin projected an image of calm, pipe-smoking moderation that became central to interwar Conservatism.

National Government and third premiership

From 1931 Baldwin served as Lord President of the Council in MacDonald's National Government, effectively the dominant figure in the cabinet, and succeeded MacDonald as Prime Minister in 1935. His final government grappled with the abdication crisis of Edward VIII in 1936, which Baldwin handled in a way widely praised at the time. He retired in May 1937, days after the coronation of George VI, and was created Earl Baldwin of Bewdley.

Legacy

Critics, notably Winston Churchill, blamed Baldwin for failing to rearm Britain against Nazi Germany; his reputation fell sharply during and after the Second World War. More recent historians have offered a more balanced assessment, noting the constraints he faced and his success in preserving democratic stability and marginalising political extremism in Britain.
