TRAPPIST-1 is a cool red dwarf star with seven known exoplanets, located 39 light-years (12 parsecs) from Earth in the constellation Aquarius. The planets, all approximately Earth-sized and probably rocky, were detected by the transit method, principally by the TRAPPIST telescopes and NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope; the seven-planet configuration was announced in February 2017 to worldwide attention.

The planets

The planets are designated TRAPPIST-1b through 1h in order of orbital period, from 1.5 to about 19 days. They form a tightly packed, coplanar system in near-resonant orbits — a resonant chain — reminiscent of Jupiter's moons Io, Europa and Ganymede. Three of the planets (e, f and g) orbit within the star's conventional habitable zone, where liquid water could exist on the surface, and planet e is considered the most Earth-like in size and received flux. Transit-timing variations have allowed unusually precise measurements of the planets' masses and densities, suggesting compositions from rocky to modestly volatile-rich.

The star

TRAPPIST-1 is an ultracool M-dwarf only slightly larger than Jupiter, with about 9% of the Sun's mass, a surface temperature around 2,500 K, and an estimated age of roughly 7–8 billion years. Such stars are extremely long-lived, but their young, active phases and flares raise questions about atmospheric retention on close-in planets.

Observations

The system is a prime target for atmospheric characterisation with the James Webb Space Telescope, which began observing its planets in 2023; early results for the innermost planets were consistent with little or no thick atmosphere, while studies of the habitable-zone planets continue.
