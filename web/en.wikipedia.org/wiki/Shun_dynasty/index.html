Shun dynasty

The Shun dynasty (simplified Chinese: 顺朝; traditional Chinese: 順朝; pinyin: Shùn cháo), officially the Great Shun (大順), was a short-lived Chinese dynasty that existed during the Ming–Qing transition. It was proclaimed in 1644 by Li Zicheng, the leader of a large peasant rebellion, shortly before his capture of Beijing and the fall of the Ming dynasty. Li was proclaimed emperor in Beijing but reigned only about six weeks before being driven out by the invading Qing forces.

Rise

Li Zicheng, a former postal courier and soldier from Shaanxi, joined a peasant rebellion amid the famines, fiscal collapse, and military overstretch that afflicted the late Ming dynasty in the 1630s. By the early 1640s he had become the dominant rebel leader in northern China, promulgating popular policies such as the equalisation of landholdings and the abolition of grain taxes, which won him the slogan "Welcome the Dashing King, pay no grain tax." On 8 February 1644 (the Chinese New Year), Li proclaimed the Great Shun dynasty at Xi'an, which he renamed Xijing.

Capture of Beijing and fall

Shun forces advanced on Beijing in the spring of 1644. On 25 April 1644 the capital fell with little fighting, and the last Ming emperor, the Chongzhen Emperor, hanged himself on Coal Hill behind the Forbidden City. Li Zicheng entered the city and was proclaimed emperor. His regime, however, quickly alienated the capital's officials and population through the extortion of silver from former Ming officials and the indiscipline of his troops.

Meanwhile the Ming general Wu Sangui, guarding Shanhai Pass against the Manchu Qing, defected to the Qing and opened the pass. On 27 May 1644 the combined Qing and Wu forces defeated Li at the Battle of Shanhai Pass. Li fled Beijing after a formal enthronement ceremony on 3 June, and Qing forces under Prince Dorgon entered the capital on 6 June 1644.

Aftermath

Li Zicheng retreated westward and was killed in 1645, probably in Hubei, though accounts vary. Remnants of the Shun armies eventually joined the Southern Ming resistance against the Qing for a time. The dynasty, though it controlled much of northern China and formally ended Ming rule in Beijing, is sometimes dismissed as merely an interregnum between Ming and Qing; official Qing historiography treated Li as a bandit, while the Shun is nonetheless recognised as a dynastic regime in the transition period.
