Psychological trauma (from the Greek τραῦμα, "wound") is damage to the mind that occurs as a result of a distressing event that overwhelms a person's ability to cope, to integrate the emotions involved, or to maintain a sense of safety and predictability. The traumatising event may be a single incident—an assault, accident, disaster, sudden bereavement or combat experience—or a pattern of repeated experiences such as childhood abuse or neglect, domestic violence, war or captivity. Responses vary widely: many people recover with time and support, while others develop persistent conditions including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), complex PTSD, depression, anxiety disorders or substance misuse. In contemporary usage "trauma" names both the event and the lasting response; clinicians distinguish the two, since not everyone exposed to traumatic events is traumatised, and resilience is the most common outcome.

Types

Trauma is commonly categorised as acute (a single overwhelming incident), chronic (repeated or prolonged exposure, as in ongoing abuse or war), and complex (exposure to multiple, often interpersonal, traumatic events, frequently beginning in childhood and perpetrated by caregivers, with effects on attachment, affect regulation and self-concept; ICD-11 recognises complex PTSD as a distinct diagnosis). Related concepts include developmental trauma arising from disrupted early attachment and chronic neglect; secondary or vicarious trauma in caregivers and professionals exposed to others' trauma accounts; historical or intergenerational trauma, the transmission of traumatic effects within families and communities (studied in descendants of Holocaust survivors, enslaved peoples and colonised Indigenous communities, partly through behavioural and possibly epigenetic pathways); and insidious trauma from chronic discrimination. The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) study (Felitti, Anda and colleagues, 1998) showed a graded relationship between the number of categories of childhood adversity and adult risks of physical disease, mental illness and early death, reshaping public-health thinking about early trauma.

Symptoms and mechanisms

Common responses include intrusive re-experiencing (flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive memories), avoidance of reminders, hyperarousal (startle, vigilance, insomnia, irritability), emotional numbing and detachment, negative changes in beliefs about oneself and the world, and somatic complaints. Neurobiological models describe a threat system stuck on high alert: an over-reactive amygdala, under-recruitment of medial prefrontal regulation, altered hippocampal contextualisation of memories (so that reminders trigger responses as if the danger were present), and dysregulation of stress hormones (the HPA axis) and autonomic function. Bessel van der Kolk's influential work emphasised the bodily dimension—trauma stored as sensorimotor and visceral reactions, "the body keeps the score". Memory for trauma is often fragmented and sensory rather than narrative, a feature with implications for testimony and therapy alike.

History of the concept

Nineteenth-century medicine described "railway spine" and traumatic hysteria (Charcot; Janet's work on dissociation); Freud moved from an early seduction theory of hysteria to an emphasis on fantasy. The World Wars produced "shell shock" and "combat neurosis", and Abram Kardiner's The Traumatic Neuroses of War (1941) systematised the syndrome. Advocacy by Vietnam veterans and the feminist movement's work on rape and battering converged in the inclusion of PTSD in DSM-III (1980), the founding moment of modern trauma studies. Judith Herman's Trauma and Recovery (1992) framed complex trauma from captivity and abuse and proposed the now-standard phases of recovery: safety, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection.

Treatment and recovery

First-line treatments for PTSD are trauma-focused psychological therapies: trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (including prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy) and EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing), all endorsed by major guidelines (APA, NICE, VA/DoD). Medications such as SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine) have smaller effects and are used for comorbid depression and anxiety. Phase-based care for complex trauma begins with safety and stabilisation. At the community level, trauma-informed care—services designed around the prevalence of trauma, avoiding retraumatisation—has spread through health care, schools and justice systems, though some researchers warn that the concept of trauma has broadened ("concept creep", in Nick Haslam's term) to cover ordinary adversity, with contested benefits. Post-traumatic growth—the experience of positive psychological change through struggle with trauma—is documented in a substantial minority, alongside recovery without disorder in the majority.
