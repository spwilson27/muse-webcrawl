Mudéjar (Spanish: [muˈðexaɾ], from Arabic مدجن mudajjan, "tamed" or "domesticated") was a term used in medieval and early modern Spain for Muslims who remained in territories conquered by Christian kingdoms during the Reconquista and were allowed to keep their religion, language, and customs in return for taxes and submission to Christian law.

History

As Christian kingdoms expanded southward from the 11th century onward — Toledo fell in 1085, Zaragoza in 1118, Seville in 1248, and Granada in 1492 — large Muslim populations came under Christian rule. Initially, surrender treaties often guaranteed Mudéjars their mosques, Islamic courts (under their own qadis and aljama councils), and property. Conditions varied by kingdom: Aragon and Valencia had large Mudéjar populations concentrated in agriculture and crafts, while in Castile they were fewer and more urban.

From the late 15th century, tolerance eroded. After the fall of Granada (1492), forced conversions in Castile (1502) and the Crown of Aragon (1520s) turned Mudéjars into Moriscos — nominally Christian "New Christians" — who were finally expelled from Spain in 1609–1614.

Culture and legacy

Mudéjar craftsmen were renowned masons, carpenters, and tile-makers. Their fusion of Islamic techniques — brickwork, glazed tile (azulejos), carved stucco, horseshoe and polylobed arches, wooden artesonado ceilings — with Gothic and Romanesque forms produced the celebrated Mudéjar style, exemplified by the towers of Teruel, the Alcázar of Seville, and the synagogues of Toledo. Several Mudéjar monuments are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
