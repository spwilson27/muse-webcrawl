Human herpesvirus 6

Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is the common collective name for two closely related betaherpesviruses, human betaherpesvirus 6A and human betaherpesvirus 6B (genus Roseolovirus). Infection is nearly universal in early childhood; HHV-6B is the cause of the common childhood illness exanthem subitum, also known as roseola infantum or sixth disease.

Infection

HHV-6 is transmitted mainly through saliva. Primary infection, usually before the age of two, often presents with several days of high fever followed by a rash as the fever breaks; febrile seizures are a frequent complication. After primary infection the virus persists latently in lymphocytes and monocytes for life. Uniquely among human herpesviruses, HHV-6 can also integrate its genome into the telomeres of human chromosomes; in about 1% of people this integrated virus (ciHHV-6) is inherited through the germline and present in every cell.

Reactivation and disease

In immunocompromised patients — particularly recipients of haematopoietic stem cell transplants — HHV-6 reactivation can cause encephalitis, bone marrow suppression and graft complications, and is routinely monitored in transplant medicine. Suggested associations with multiple sclerosis, chronic fatigue syndrome and other conditions have been investigated but remain unproven. Antivirals used for serious disease include ganciclovir, foscarnet and cidofovir; no vaccine exists.
