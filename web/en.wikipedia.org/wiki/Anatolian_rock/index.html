Anatolian rock (Turkish: Anadolu rock) is a fusion genre of Turkish folk music and rock music that emerged in Turkey in the mid-1960s, shortly after rock groups such as the Beatles and the Rolling Stones became popular. It combines elements of Turkish folk music — regional melodies, traditional instruments such as the bağlama, and folk lyric themes — with Western rock instrumentation and psychedelic production.

History

The genre's pioneers included Erkin Koray, widely regarded as the father of Anatolian rock, Cem Karaca and his successive bands, Barış Manço and the group Kurtalan Ekspres, the band Moğollar, Edip Akbayram, and Selda Bağcan, whose powerful voice and protest lyrics made her one of the genre's most internationally recognised figures. Newspapers and record labels promoted the style through song contests, most famously the Altın Mikrofon (Golden Microphone) competition, which required entrants to compose original Turkish songs or arrange folk songs with Western instruments.

Anatolian rock flourished through the 1970s, often carrying politically engaged lyrics that reflected Turkey's turbulent decade. The 1980 military coup and the rise of arabesque and pop music pushed the genre underground, though its leading artists continued to record and tour.

Revival and influence

Since the 2000s, international DJs, labels and crate-diggers have rediscovered 1970s Turkish psychedelic rock, with reissues of Selda Bağcan, Erkin Koray and Barış Manço reaching worldwide audiences and samples appearing in hip-hop recordings. Contemporary Turkish bands such as Altın Gün, who perform Anatolian folk-rock repertoire to international acclaim, carry the tradition forward.
