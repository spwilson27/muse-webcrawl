Matthias Ringmann (c. 1482 – 1511) was a German humanist scholar, poet, and cartographer of the Alsatian circle known as the Gymnasium Vosagense at Saint-Dié in Lorraine. He is remembered for one enormously consequential sentence: in the Cosmographiae Introductio of 1507, Ringmann proposed that the newly discovered fourth part of the world be named "America", after Amerigo Vespucci — the name that Martin Waldseemüller's great wall map of the same year spread across the southern continent.

Life and work

Ringmann was born at Eichhoffen in Alsace, studied at Heidelberg and Paris, and earned his living precariously as a teacher, editor, and occasional court poet, moving in the orbit of printers and humanists in Strasbourg, Basel, and Lorraine. At Saint-Dié, under the patronage of Duke René II of Lorraine, Canon Walter Lud gathered a small academy — the Gymnasium Vosagense — to produce a new edition of Ptolemy's Geography. Ringmann edited classical texts, wrote Latin verse, and, importantly, had traveled to Italy, where in Florence he likely encountered accounts and letters attributed to Vespucci, including the sensational Mundus Novus.

Naming America

The Gymnasium's 1507 publications — Waldseemüller's Universalis Cosmographia wall map and globe gores, together with the little Cosmographiae Introductio — introduced the New World to European scholarship. Modern scholarship, notably the work of Franz Laubenberger and Seymour Schwartz, attributes the booklet's text, including the naming passage, to Ringmann rather than Waldseemüller. Its reasoning was characteristic of humanist Latin wordplay: since Europe and Asia took their names from women, the new land should bear the name of its discoverer, Amerigo, in feminine form — America. Ringmann appears to have believed, as the Mundus Novus letters claimed, that Vespucci had reached the mainland in 1497, before Columbus's mainland voyage of 1498. Within a few years Waldseemüller had second thoughts and removed the name from his maps, substituting "Terra Nova", but by then other mapmakers had adopted it; Ringmann himself did not live to see the controversy, dying in 1511 at about twenty-nine, probably of tuberculosis.

Legacy

Ringmann's other works — editions of Caesar and other classics, a Latin grammar in verse, and a German account of the New World (1509), among the first books printed in German on the discoveries — were quickly forgotten. His one coinage, however, named two continents. In Saint-Dié, now styled a "godmother of America", the naming of 1507 is commemorated each year; the town's geography festival keeps Ringmann's role at its center.
