Somatic cell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In cellular biology, a somatic cell (from Ancient Greek σῶμα, sôma, "body"), or vegetal cell, is any biological cell forming the body of a multicellular organism other than a gamete, germ cell, gametocyte, or undifferentiated stem cell. Somatic cells compose the tissues, organs, and other parts of an organism's body. They are usually diploid, containing two sets of chromosomes, and divide by mitosis.

Distinction from germ cells

The defining feature of somatic cells is that genetic changes in them are not passed to offspring: mutations acquired by a somatic cell affect only that individual (though a somatic mutation can cause disease, including cancer, within the individual). Germline cells, by contrast, give rise to gametes and transmit their DNA to the next generation. This division between soma and germline, articulated by August Weismann in the 19th century, is fundamental to genetics and evolutionary theory, though it is less strict in plants, where reproductive structures arise from somatic tissue.

Cloning and reprogramming

Because a somatic cell nucleus contains (nearly) the full genome, it can, in the right environment, direct the development of a whole organism. In somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), the nucleus of a somatic cell is transferred into an enucleated egg cell; this technique produced Dolly the sheep in 1996, the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell. In 2006–2007, Shinya Yamanaka and colleagues showed that somatic cells can be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells by forced expression of a small set of transcription factors (the Yamanaka factors Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc), a discovery with major implications for regenerative medicine.
