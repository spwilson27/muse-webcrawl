French conquest of Algeria

The French conquest of Algeria (1830–1903) began with the invasion of Algiers in 1830 and resulted in the incorporation of Algeria into the French colonial empire. The conquest was marked by prolonged resistance, scorched-earth campaigns, and mass mortality among the Algerian population, and it laid the foundations of French Algeria, which lasted until independence in 1962.

Background and invasion

The immediate pretext was the "fly-whisk affair" of 1827, when the Dey of Algiers, Hussein, struck the French consul Pierre Deval with a fly whisk during a dispute over unpaid debts. France blockaded Algiers for three years, then, seeking a diversion from domestic troubles, the government of Charles X launched an invasion. A French expedition landed at Sidi Fredj on 14 June 1830, won the Battle of Staouéli, and took Algiers on 5 July 1830; the Dey capitulated and went into exile.

Resistance and total war

France initially held only coastal enclaves and debated whether to colonize at all. In the west, Emir Abd al-Qadir built a state and waged guerrilla war from 1832; treaties (Desmichels 1834, Tafna 1837) alternated with renewed fighting. From 1840, Governor-General Thomas Robert Bugeaud pursued "total war": mobile columns, destruction of crops and villages (razzias), and the infamous enfumades, in which Algerian civilians were asphyxiated in caves, as at Dahra in 1845. Abd al-Qadir surrendered in 1847. In the east, Ahmed Bey of Constantine resisted until 1848. Kabylia was subdued by 1857, and the great Mokrani Revolt of 1871–72 was crushed, followed by mass land confiscations.

Consequences

Demographic studies estimate that Algeria's population fell sharply — by up to a third in the decades after 1830 through war, famine, and disease. Land laws such as the Senatus-consulte of 1863 and the Warnier Law of 1873 transferred vast tracts to European settlers (colons). Algeria was legally made part of France in 1848 as three departments, an exceptional status among colonies. Resistance and expansion continued in the Sahara into the early 20th century.
