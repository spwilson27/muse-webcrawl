Pico hydro

Pico hydro is a term used for hydroelectric power generation of under 5 kW. These generators are designed to supply small amounts of electricity to individual households or small communities, particularly in remote areas beyond the reach of an electricity grid. Pico hydro is at the smallest scale of the conventional hydro classifications: pico (<5 kW), micro (5–100 kW), mini (100 kW–1 MW) and small (1–10 MW or higher, depending on definition).


A pico hydro unit in a stream; such systems can power lighting and communications for a household.

Technology

A typical pico hydro scheme diverts part of a stream's flow into a settling tank and down a penstock pipe to a small turbine — often a Pelton, Turgo or crossflow design, or an adapted pump or motor used in reverse — coupled to a permanent-magnet alternator. Because output varies with flow and load, systems use electronic load controllers that keep the generator fully loaded by diverting surplus power to a ballast (dump) load, maintaining stable voltage and frequency. Batteries with an inverter may be added, or the output used directly for lighting and small appliances.

Low-cost designs, notably the "Powerspout" and the village-scale schemes developed in Nepal, Vietnam, Kenya, and the Andean and Himalayan regions, have made sub-kilowatt systems affordable: a 200–500 W unit can provide LED lighting, phone charging, radio and television for a rural home at a fraction of the cost of comparable solar installations where a suitable stream exists.

Advantages and limitations

Pico hydro has a high capacity factor — generating day and night whenever water flows — and low running costs, but it is site-specific: it requires a perennial stream with adequate head or flow, and dry-season reductions can limit output. Civil works are small, and environmental impact is generally minimal because schemes are run-of-the-river with tiny diversions.
