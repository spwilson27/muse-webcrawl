Chinatown

Chinese: 唐人街 (pinyin: Tángrénjiē, "Tang people's street")

Definition: An ethnic enclave of Chinese people outside China

A Chinatown is an ethnic enclave of Chinese people located outside Greater China, most often in an urban setting. Areas known as "Chinatown" exist throughout the world, including in North America, Europe, Asia and Australia. The oldest Chinatown in the world is Binondo in Manila, Philippines, established in 1594.

History

Chinatowns developed as ports of trade and as refuges for Chinese emigrants, who often faced legal discrimination and exclusion — such as under the United States' Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 — and therefore clustered together for mutual support. Community associations, temples, markets and restaurants made these districts self-contained cultural centres. Many Chinatowns were marked by ornamental gateways (paifang) and distinctive signage in Chinese characters.

Notable Chinatowns

  • San Francisco — the oldest Chinatown in North America (established 1848) and one of the largest.
  • New York City — Manhattan's Chinatown is among the largest concentrations of Chinese people in the Western Hemisphere, joined by newer communities in Brooklyn and Queens.
  • London — centred on Gerrard Street in Soho, with origins in the earlier Limehouse settlement.
  • Yokohama — Japan's largest Chinatown, dating to the opening of the port in 1859.
  • Sydney and Melbourne — products of the 19th-century gold rushes in Australia.

Culture

Chinatowns are known for Cantonese and other regional Chinese cuisines, bakeries, herbalists, festivals (notably Lunar New Year parades), and businesses serving both the local Chinese community and tourists. As later waves of migration arrived from different parts of China and Asia, many districts diversified while retaining their identity.
