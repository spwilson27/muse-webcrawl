Honing (metalworking)

Honing — summary
Process typeAbrasive machining / finishing
ToolHone — rotating mandrel with abrasive stones
Typical useFinishing internal cylindrical surfaces (bores)
Achievable finishSurface finishes down to about 0.1 µm Ra
Signature patternCross-hatch (typically ~45°)

Honing is an abrasive machining process that produces a precision surface on a metal workpiece by scrubbing an abrasive grinding stone against it along a controlled path. It is used mainly to finish the internal surfaces of cylinders — such as engine cylinder bores, hydraulic cylinders and bearing races — improving geometric accuracy, surface finish and size control. The combination of rotation and reciprocation of the honing head produces the characteristic cross-hatched pattern on the finished surface.

Process

A honing machine drives a tool called a hone: a mandrel carrying one or more abrasive sticks or stones (commonly aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, diamond or cubic boron nitride) that are expanded outward against the bore wall under controlled pressure. The tool rotates while simultaneously stroking back and forth along the axis of the bore. Because the abrasive contacts the surface at an angle that reverses with each stroke, the resulting scratches form a cross-hatch pattern whose angle is determined by the ratio of rotational to reciprocating speed.

A cutting fluid or honing oil is applied throughout to cool the workpiece, lubricate the cut and flush away swarf. Stones are dressed and wear gradually; automatic sizing systems measure the bore and stop the cycle when the target diameter is reached, holding tolerances on the order of a few micrometres.

Advantages

Honing corrects geometric errors left by previous operations such as boring or drilling, including out-of-roundness, taper and waviness. The cross-hatch valleys in an engine cylinder wall retain oil, reducing friction and wear between piston rings and bore. Because large areas of abrasive contact the work simultaneously, honing removes stock slowly but very uniformly, and it can be applied to hardened steels and coated bores. Related variants include sunnen-style precision honing, flat honing for planar surfaces, and ball honing (flexible "bottle-brush" hones) used to deglaze cylinder walls.

Applications

Typical applications include internal combustion engine cylinders, hydraulic and pneumatic cylinders, compressor bores, gun barrels, gears (gear honing), connecting rods, and fuel-injection components. Honing differs from lapping, which uses loose abrasive between two surfaces, and from grinding, which generally uses a rotating wheel at higher speeds.
