Christian realism

Christian realism is a theological and political philosophy developed chiefly by the American theologian Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971). It holds that the Christian doctrine of original sin gives the most accurate account of human nature in politics, and that ideals of love and justice must be pursued with a sober recognition of self-interest and power.

Core ideas

Niebuhr argued that individuals may act morally, but groups — nations, classes, races — behave more selfishly, a thesis of his Moral Man and Immoral Society (1932). Against both pacifist idealism and cynical realpolitik, Christian realism defends a "proximate" justice: imperfect balances of power that restrain evil, accepting that force may be necessary while remaining under moral judgment.

Influence

The philosophy shaped mid-20th-century American foreign policy thinking and influenced figures across the political spectrum. Its account of sin, irony, and unintended consequences, developed further in The Nature and Destiny of Man and The Irony of American History, has been revived by later commentators reflecting on war and American power.

Criticism

Critics, including Christian pacifists and later liberation theologians, charged that Niebuhr's realism could sanctify the status quo and underestimated the possibilities of nonviolent change.
