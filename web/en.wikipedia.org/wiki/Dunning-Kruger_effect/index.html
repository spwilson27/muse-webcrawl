Dunning–Kruger effect

The Dunning–Kruger effect is a cognitive bias in which people with limited competence in a particular domain overestimate their abilities. It was described by social psychologists David Dunning and Justin Kruger in a 1999 study at Cornell University. Their experiments in humour, grammar, and logical reasoning found that participants scoring in the lowest quartile greatly overestimated their percentile rank, while top performers slightly underestimated theirs.

Original study

Kruger and Dunning proposed that incompetence robs people of the metacognitive ability to recognise their own incompetence — "the skills that engender competence in a domain are often the very same skills necessary to evaluate competence in that domain." The idea was partly inspired by the case of McArthur Wheeler, a bank robber who believed rubbing lemon juice on his face made him invisible to security cameras.

Interpretation and criticism

Later researchers have debated how much of the effect is a genuine cognitive bias versus a statistical artefact combining regression to the mean with the better-than-average effect. Analyses using random simulated data can reproduce similar curves. Nevertheless, studies across many domains — driving, medicine, chess, workplace skills — have found real patterns of miscalibration among novices, and the effect remains a popular concept in discussions of expertise and overconfidence.

Cultural references

The effect is frequently invoked in popular culture and political commentary, and Dunning and Kruger received the satirical Ig Nobel Prize in Psychology in 2000. Popular graphs showing a "Mount Stupid" peak followed by a "Valley of Despair" are simplified extrapolations not drawn directly from the original data.
