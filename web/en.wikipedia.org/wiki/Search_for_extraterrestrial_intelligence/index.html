Search for extraterrestrial intelligence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) is a collective term for scientific searches for intelligent extraterrestrial life, for example by monitoring electromagnetic radiation for signs of transmissions from civilisations on other planets. Scientific investigation began shortly after the advent of radio in the early 1900s, and focused efforts have continued since the 1980s.

History

Early work

In 1959, Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison published a landmark paper in Nature arguing that radio telescopes had become sensitive enough to detect transmissions from other stars, and suggesting the hydrogen line at 1420 MHz as a natural frequency to search. In 1960, Frank Drake conducted Project Ozma at Green Bank Observatory, the first modern SETI experiment, listening to the stars Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani. Drake also formulated the Drake equation in 1961 to organise discussion of the factors determining the number of communicative civilisations in the Milky Way.

The Wow! signal and NASA programmes

In 1977, the Big Ear radio telescope at Ohio State University received a strong narrowband signal, lasting 72 seconds, that bore the expected hallmarks of an artificial extraterrestrial transmission. Astronomer Jerry Ehman wrote "Wow!" in the margin of the printout; the Wow! signal has never been explained or detected again. NASA developed SETI programmes through the 1970s and 1980s, culminating in the High Resolution Microwave Survey, launched on Columbus Day 1992 — but Congress cancelled its funding after less than a year, in 1993.

Private and distributed efforts

After the end of NASA funding, the SETI Institute and other private organisations continued the search. The SETI@home project (1999–2020) let millions of volunteers donate idle computer time to analyse radio data, becoming one of the largest distributed computing projects ever run. In 2015, Yuri Milner launched Breakthrough Listen, a $100 million, ten-year initiative that has conducted the most comprehensive SETI observations to date using the Green Bank Telescope, Parkes Observatory, and optical facilities.

Methods

Most SETI searches scan the radio spectrum for narrowband signals, which are not produced by known natural processes. Optical SETI looks for brief, powerful laser pulses. Researchers also search for technosignatures more broadly: anomalous infrared excesses that might indicate Dyson spheres, industrial pollutants in exoplanet atmospheres, or artificial transits. Modern searches increasingly target nearby stars and known exoplanet systems, and apply machine learning to sift enormous data sets for anomalous signals.

The Fermi paradox

The apparent absence of any detected civilisation, despite the vast number of stars and planets, is known as the Fermi paradox. Proposed resolutions include the rarity of intelligent life, the short lifetime of technological civilisations, the "zoo hypothesis", and the possibility that other civilisations deliberately avoid contact. SETI's continued null results have progressively constrained the prevalence of strong radio transmitters in the nearby galaxy.
