Shielding effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The shielding effect (also called atomic shielding or electron shielding) describes the reduction of the effective nuclear charge experienced by an electron in a multi-electron atom. The term describes the attraction net of the protons in the nucleus and the electrons in the inner shells: electrons in inner shells partially "shield" outer electrons from the full positive charge of the nucleus, so the outer electrons feel a weaker pull than they would in a hydrogen-like atom with the same nuclear charge.

Effective nuclear charge

The effective nuclear charge, Zeff, is the net positive charge experienced by an electron in a multi-electron atom. It is commonly estimated as Zeff = Z − S, where Z is the atomic number and S is a shielding constant representing the average number of electrons between the nucleus and the electron of interest. Because inner electrons repel outer electrons and sit between them and the nucleus, S is positive and Zeff is always less than Z.

Slater's rules

A semi-empirical method for estimating the shielding constant was proposed by John C. Slater. Slater's rules assign contributions to S depending on the electron configuration: electrons in the same group contribute 0.35 each (0.30 for 1s), electrons in the n−1 shell contribute 0.85 each, and electrons in shells n−2 or lower contribute 1.00 each. For d and f electrons the rules are adjusted. These rules give reasonable estimates of Zeff for many atoms and explain trends such as the relatively poor shielding provided by d and f electrons.

Consequences

Shielding helps explain several periodic trends. Across a period, Zeff rises because protons are added faster than shielding electrons, pulling the valence shell inward and shrinking atomic radii. Down a group, new inner shells greatly increase shielding, so valence electrons are held less tightly despite the larger nuclear charge. Shielding also underlies the order of orbital filling and the ionisation-energy anomalies seen across the periodic table, and it is related to the screening effect invoked in X-ray spectroscopy, quantified by Moseley's law.
