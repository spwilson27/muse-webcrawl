The Occupation of Alcatraz (20 November 1969 – 11 June 1971) was a 19-month occupation of Alcatraz Island and its abandoned federal prison by 89 Native Americans acting as the group "Indians of All Tribes". The protest, led by figures including Richard Oakes and LaNada War Jack, claimed the island under the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie, which allowed Native people to claim surplus federal land that had been returned to the government.

Background

By the 1960s, federal "termination" policies had stripped tribes of land and status, and many Native people had been relocated to cities such as San Francisco under the Indian Relocation Act of 1956. A brief symbolic landing on Alcatraz took place in 1964. After the San Francisco Indian Center burned down in October 1969, activists decided on a full occupation.

The occupation

The occupiers issued a satirical proclamation offering to buy Alcatraz for $24 in glass beads and cloth — the sum legend says was paid for Manhattan — and proposed turning the island into a Native cultural and educational centre. At its height the island's population reached several hundred, with a school, clinic, kitchen and the pirate radio station "Radio Free Alcatraz". Celebrities visited and donated, and the action received international media coverage.

Conditions deteriorated after the death of leader Richard Oakes's stepdaughter in a fall in January 1970 and Oakes's departure; utilities were cut and a fire in June 1970 destroyed several buildings, each side blaming the other. On 11 June 1971 federal marshals removed the last 15 occupiers without violence.

Legacy

Although the island was never transferred, the occupation is widely credited with ending the federal termination policy and shifting the United States toward tribal self-determination, culminating in measures such as the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975. Alcatraz remains a symbolic site, and an annual Indigenous Peoples' Day sunrise ceremony is held there.
