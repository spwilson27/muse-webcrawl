Good faith (law)

In contract law, the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is a general presumption that the parties to a contract will deal with each other honestly, fairly, and in good faith, so as not to destroy the right of the other party or parties to receive the benefits of the contract. Good faith (Latin: bona fides) more generally denotes honest intention — the absence of malice or intent to defraud — and it operates across many legal fields, from contract and property to international law, where treaties must be performed in good faith (pacta sunt servanda, Article 26 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties).

Contract law

Civil-law systems broadly impose good faith in the formation, interpretation, and performance of contracts: Article 1104 of the French Civil Code requires contracts to be negotiated, formed, and performed in good faith, and German law's §242 BGB (Treu und Glauben) has been a fertile source of doctrines correcting harsh results. Common-law systems have been more cautious. In the United States, the implied covenant is recognised in most jurisdictions and codified in the Uniform Commercial Code, which imposes an obligation of good faith — "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing" — in the performance and enforcement of contracts for the sale of goods; it is especially prominent in insurance law, where bad-faith denial of claims can support punitive damages. English law long resisted a general duty, preferring piecemeal solutions (implied terms, estoppel, misrepresentation), though cases such as Yam Seng v International Trade Corp (2013) have opened debate about a duty of good faith in "relational contracts". The Supreme Court of Canada recognised an organising principle of good faith and a duty of honest performance in Bhasin v Hrynew (2014).

Other applications

Good faith appears as the bona fide purchaser doctrine in property law (a buyer who takes honestly, for value and without notice of a defect in title is protected), in labour law (the duty to bargain in good faith), in constitutional and administrative law (the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule in US criminal procedure, United States v. Leon), and in international law, where it underpins treaty performance, negotiation obligations, and doctrines of estoppel and abuse of rights.
