Standard time

Standard time is the local time in a region or country when daylight saving time is not in use. More generally, the term refers to the system of synchronising clocks within a geographical area to a single time standard, replacing the older practice in which every town kept its own local mean time based on the sun.

History

Before standardisation, travellers and railways coped with hundreds of local times. British railways adopted "railway time" based on Greenwich Mean Time in the 1840s, and it became the legal standard across Great Britain in 1880. In North America, railroad companies, led by figures such as Charles F. Dowd and William F. Allen, divided the continent into time zones on 18 November 1883 — the "day of two noons" — and the United States made standard time statutory with the Standard Time Act of 1918. The 1884 International Meridian Conference in Washington, D.C. selected Greenwich as the prime meridian and promoted a worldwide system of hourly zones.

Time zones

The Earth is divided into roughly 24 principal time zones, each nominally 15 degrees of longitude wide and offset from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) by a whole number of hours, although political boundaries cause many deviations and some regions use half-hour or 45-minute offsets. "Standard time" for a zone is its winter (non-daylight-saving) offset; many jurisdictions advance clocks by one hour during summer months, in which case daylight time applies instead.
