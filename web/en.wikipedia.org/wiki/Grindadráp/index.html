The grindadráp (Faroese for "grind hunt", from grind, a school of pilot whales) is a type of drive hunting that involves herding long-finned pilot whales and sometimes other dolphin species into shallow bays in the Faroe Islands, where they are beached and killed. Practised for centuries — records date from 1584, and the tradition is thought to go back to Norse settlement — it remains a significant part of Faroese food culture, the meat and blubber being shared out among the local community without commercial sale.

The hunt

When a school of whales is sighted, boats form a semicircle and drive the animals toward a designated authorised beach. Once beached or in shallow water, the whales are killed by licensed men using a spinal lance (mønustingari) designed to sever the spinal cord, replacing the older whaling knife. The catch is then measured, valued in the traditional unit called a skinn, and distributed among participants and residents of the district according to rules administered by the hunt foreman and district sheriff.

Scale and controversy

On average several hundred pilot whales are taken in most years, out of a North Atlantic population estimated in the hundreds of thousands; Faroese and international assessments generally regard the pilot-whale hunt as sustainable, though a controversial catch of more than 1,400 Atlantic white-sided dolphins in September 2021 prompted an internal Faroese review and an annual quota for that species. The grindadráp is strongly opposed by animal-rights organisations, and images of the water reddened with blood have become iconic for campaigners; Faroese defenders argue the hunt is humane, non-commercial, community-based and no different in principle from industrial slaughter of livestock, while Faroese health authorities have also warned about mercury and PCB contamination in whale meat, advising limited consumption.
