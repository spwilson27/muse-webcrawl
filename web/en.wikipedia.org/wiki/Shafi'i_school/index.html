Shafi'i school

The Shafi'i school (Arabic: الشافعية, romanized: al-Shāfiʿiyya) is one of the four major schools of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) within Sunni Islam. It was founded by the Muslim scholar Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i, a pupil of Malik ibn Anas, in the early ninth century CE.

The Shafi'i school affirms, in order, the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus (ijma) and analogical reasoning (qiyas) as the sources of Islamic law. Al-Shafi'i's systematic treatment of these sources in his work Al-Risala is considered foundational to the science of the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh).

History

Al-Shafi'i (767–820 CE) studied in Medina under Malik, the founder of the Maliki school, and later in Baghdad, where he engaged with the Hanafi tradition. His synthesis of the two approaches, developed first in Baghdad (the "old school") and refined in Egypt (the "new school"), spread widely through his students, among them Ahmad ibn Hanbal.

Distribution

The Shafi'i school predominates in East Africa (including parts of Egypt, Somalia, Ethiopia and Kenya), the Levant and Yemen in part, Kurdistan, the North Caucasus (Dagestan and Chechnya), and much of Southeast Asia, including Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei and the southern Philippines, as well as among many Muslims in Sri Lanka and southern India (Kerala).
