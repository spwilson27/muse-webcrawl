Proglacial lake

A proglacial lake is a lake formed by the damming action of a moraine, an ice dam, or glacial erosion during the retreat of a melting glacier, or by meltwater trapped against an ice sheet. Proglacial lakes were widespread during the deglaciation following the Last Glacial Maximum, when meltwater ponded between retreating ice margins and higher ground.

Formation

Proglacial lakes may be impounded by the glacier itself (ice-marginal lakes), by moraine ridges deposited at the ice front, or in basins deepened by glacial erosion and isostatic depression of the crust. Because ice and moraine dams are unstable, such lakes can drain suddenly in events known as glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), or jökulhlaups in Iceland.

Notable examples

During the last deglaciation, enormous proglacial lakes formed in North America, including Lake Agassiz — at times larger than all the modern Great Lakes combined — and Lake Missoula, whose repeated drainage carved the Channeled Scablands of Washington state. Lake Bonneville was the pluvial counterpart in the Great Basin. Modern examples include the lakes forming at retreating glaciers in the Himalayas, the Alps, and Alaska.

Significance

Sediments deposited in proglacial lakes, including annually laminated varves, provide detailed records of past climate and ice-sheet retreat. The catastrophic drainage of proglacial lakes is thought to have disrupted ocean circulation and triggered abrupt climate events such as the Younger Dryas.
