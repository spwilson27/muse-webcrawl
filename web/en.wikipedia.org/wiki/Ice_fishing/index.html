Ice fishing

Ice fishing is the practice of catching fish with lines and hooks or spears through an opening cut in the ice on a frozen body of water. It is practiced during winter in cold-climate regions including Canada, the northern United States, Scandinavia, Finland, the Baltic states, and Russia, targeting species such as walleye, northern pike, perch, lake trout, and crappie.

Methods

Anglers drill or chop holes through the ice using hand or powered augers, then fish with short rods while jigging lures or bait, or set unattended tip-ups — devices that raise a flag when a fish takes the bait. In dark-house spearfishing, practiced especially in Minnesota, the angler sits over a large hole in a darkened shelter and spears fish, often pike lured by a decoy, through the illuminated opening. Modern ice anglers commonly use sonar flashers and underwater cameras to locate fish.

Shelters and culture

Fishermen use everything from buckets and portable flip-over shelters to elaborate wheeled "fish houses" equipped with bunks, stoves, and televisions, which form seasonal villages on popular lakes. Ice fishing derbies and festivals, such as the Brainerd Jaycees Ice Fishing Extravaganza in Minnesota — one of the largest in the world — draw thousands of participants.

Safety

Ice thickness is the principal safety concern; guidelines commonly suggest at least four inches of clear ice for a person on foot, with progressively thicker ice needed for snowmobiles, cars, and trucks. Other hazards include carbon monoxide from heaters in enclosed shelters, frostbite, and thin ice near currents and inlets.
