In biochemistry, a ligase is an enzyme that can catalyse the joining (ligation) of two molecules by forming a new chemical bond. This is typically via hydrolysis of a small pendant chemical group on one of the molecules, typically resulting in the formation of new C–O, C–S, or C–N bonds. Ligases are classified as EC 6 in the EC number classification of enzymes and can be further subclassified according to the type of bond formed.

DNA ligase

The best-known ligase is DNA ligase, which seals nicks in the sugar-phosphate backbone of DNA. During DNA replication it joins Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand; during DNA repair it completes the restoration of damaged strands. Eukaryotes have several DNA ligases (I, III and IV) specialised for replication, repair and recombination respectively. The enzyme from bacteriophage T4, T4 DNA ligase, is a staple of molecular biology laboratories, where it is used to join DNA fragments in cloning.

Other ligases

Other important ligases include the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, which attach amino acids to their cognate transfer RNAs (classified as ligases, EC 6.1); ubiquitin ligases (E3 enzymes), which tag proteins with ubiquitin for degradation or signalling; and carboxylases such as pyruvate carboxylase, which join carbon dioxide to a substrate using ATP.

Mechanism

Ligase reactions generally proceed in steps: the enzyme is first adenylated using ATP (or NAD+ in some bacterial DNA ligases), the AMP group is transferred to one substrate, and the second substrate then attacks to form the new bond, releasing AMP.
