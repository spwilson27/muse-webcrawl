New Objectivity

The New Objectivity (German: Neue Sachlichkeit) was a movement in German art that arose during the 1920s in the Weimar Republic as a reaction against expressionism. Its practitioners turned to unsentimental, sharply observed depictions of contemporary life — portraiture, cityscapes, still lifes, and social satire — executed with precise, often deliberately cool technique. The term was coined by Gustav Friedrich Hartlaub, director of the Kunsthalle Mannheim, for a landmark 1925 exhibition, Neue Sachlichkeit: Deutsche Malerei seit dem Expressionismus ("New Objectivity: German Painting since Expressionism").

Two tendencies

Hartlaub and later critics distinguished two broad tendencies. The verists, such as Otto Dix and George Grosz, produced biting social and political satire exposing the corruption, militarism, and decadence of Weimar society. The classicists or "magic realists," including Alexander Kanoldt and Georg Schrimpf, sought a calm, timeless clarity indebted to early Renaissance painting. Max Beckmann, Christian Schad, and Conrad Felixmüller are also closely associated with the movement.

Beyond painting

The spirit of Neue Sachlichkeit extended to photography (August Sander's typological portraits, Albert Renger-Patzsch), architecture (the functional housing estates of the Neues Bauen), literature (the detached reportage of Erich Kästner and Hans Fallada), film, and design.

Decline

The movement effectively ended with the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. Its leading figures were dismissed from teaching posts, emigrated, or saw their work condemned and exhibited as "degenerate art" in the 1937 Entartete Kunst exhibition. New Objectivity's clinical observation and social critique nevertheless influenced postwar figurative painting and photography worldwide.
