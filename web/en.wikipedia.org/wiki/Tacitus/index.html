Publius Cornelius Tacitus (c. AD 56 – c. 120) was a Roman historian and politician, widely regarded as the greatest historian of ancient Rome, noted for his concise, penetrating prose and bleak analysis of imperial power.

Life

Tacitus rose through the senatorial career, becoming consul in 97 under Nerva and proconsul of Asia later. He married the daughter of Agricola, the conqueror in Britain, whose biography he wrote, and witnessed Domitian's reign of terror, which shaped his view of tyranny.

Works

The Agricola (98) combines biography with ethnography of Britain; the Germania describes Germanic peoples; the Dialogue on Oratory debates rhetoric's decline. His mature histories — the Histories (covering 69–96) and the Annals (covering Tiberius to Nero, 14–68) — survive in part and provide the main narrative for the early Principate, including the fire of Rome and the persecution of Christians under Nero, whom Tacitus names as crucifier of Christus under Pontius Pilate.

Style and influence

Writing 'without anger or partiality' (sine ira et studio), Tacitus influenced political thought from Machiavelli onward and remains a key source for Roman imperial history.
