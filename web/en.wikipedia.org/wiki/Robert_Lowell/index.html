Robert Traill Spence Lowell IV (1 March 1917 – 12 September 1977) was an American poet. He is considered the founder of confessional poetry, and his 1959 collection Life Studies — frank about family, madness and divorce — transformed post-war American verse. He won the Pulitzer Prize twice and served as Consultant in Poetry to the Library of Congress (the post now called Poet Laureate).

Lowell's "For the Union Dead" meditates on the Shaw Memorial in Boston.

Life

Lowell was born into a distinguished Boston family — the "Boston Brahmin" Lowells, descended from the poet James Russell Lowell and counting among relatives the astronomer Percival Lowell and the poet Amy Lowell. After St. Mark's School and two years at Harvard, he studied with John Crowe Ransom at Kenyon College, then worked with Robert Penn Warren. A Catholic convert for much of the 1940s, he was imprisoned in 1943–44 as a conscientious objector who protested Allied area bombing. His life was marked by recurrent manic-depressive illness and hospitalisations. He married the writer Jean Stafford, then the critic and novelist Elizabeth Hardwick, then Lady Caroline Blackwood. At Boston University he taught Sylvia Plath and Anne Sexton, whose work his example liberated.

Poetry

Lord Weary's Castle (1946) won the Pulitzer Prize with dense, formal, Catholic-inflected poems such as "The Quaker Graveyard in Nantucket". The breakthrough came with Life Studies (1959): a prose memoir plus loose, autobiographical poems including "Skunk Hour", "Man and Wife" and "Waking in the Blue", which opened American poetry to raw personal material. For the Union Dead (1964) turned the same intensity on public history. Later, the unrhymed sonnet sequences of Notebook and History (1973), and the valedictory Day by Day (1977), charted a turbulent private and political era. Lowell was also prominent in opposing the Vietnam War, marching on the Pentagon in 1967, an event chronicled in Norman Mailer's The Armies of the Night.
