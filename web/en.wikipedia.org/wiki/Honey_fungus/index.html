Honey fungus

Honey fungus is the common name for several species of fungi in the genus Armillaria, notably Armillaria mellea. They are plant pathogens that cause Armillaria root rot in many species of trees and woody shrubs, and they produce honey-coloured mushrooms in autumn around the base of infected hosts. Honey fungus is among the most destructive fungal diseases of gardens and forests in temperate regions.

Identification

The mushrooms appear in dense clusters in autumn. Caps are honey-brown to yellowish, 3–15 cm across, often with small dark scales near the centre; the gills are white, becoming spotted brown with age, and the stem bears a conspicuous ring. Diagnostic signs below ground include black, bootlace-like cords called rhizomorphs, which the fungus uses to spread through soil from root to root, and white or cream mycelial fans beneath the bark at the base of an infected tree, sometimes smelling strongly of mushrooms.

Life cycle and spread

Armillaria spreads mainly by rhizomorphs growing through the soil and by direct root contact with infected stumps and roots, which can remain a food base for decades. Airborne spores play a smaller role, colonizing fresh dead wood. The fungus can live as a saprotroph on dead wood as well as a parasite on living hosts, which makes eradication difficult: infected plants typically decline over several years, showing poor growth, premature autumn colour, and dieback before death.

Significance

One individual of the related species Armillaria ostoyae in Oregon's Malheur National Forest covers roughly 9.6 km² and is estimated to be thousands of years old, making it one of the largest and oldest known living organisms. In the kitchen, honey fungus is considered edible when thoroughly cooked, though it can cause gastric upset in some people and must never be eaten raw. Gardeners manage it by removing infected stumps, installing root barriers, and choosing resistant plant species, since no reliable chemical cure exists.
