An aqueduct bridge is a bridge constructed to convey water over an obstacle such as a valley or another river. The term covers both the arched arcade sections of ancient water-supply aqueducts and the navigable aqueducts of the canal age, in which a trough of water carries boats across a gap.

Navigable aqueducts

During the Industrial Revolution, engineers such as Thomas Telford built striking navigable aqueducts, including the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct in Wales (1805), whose cast-iron trough stands 38 metres above the River Dee. Modern examples include the Magdeburg Water Bridge in Germany (2003), which carries the Mittelland Canal over the Elbe.

Roman arcade bridges

Roman water aqueducts ran mostly underground, but where a valley had to be crossed the channel was carried on one or more tiers of arches. The Pont du Gard, part of the aqueduct to Nîmes, rises nearly 49 metres in three tiers and is among the best-preserved examples.
