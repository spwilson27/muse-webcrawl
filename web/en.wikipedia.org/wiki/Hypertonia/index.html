Hypertonia

Hypertonia
Specialty: Neurology
Symptoms: Increased muscle tone, stiffness, exaggerated reflexes
Causes: Upper motor neuron lesions, cerebral palsy, stroke, multiple sclerosis
Treatment: Physical therapy, baclofen, botulinum toxin, orthopedic surgery

Hypertonia is a term used in medicine to describe a condition marked by an abnormal increase in muscle tension and a reduced ability of a muscle to stretch. It is caused by injury to the central nervous system, particularly damage to upper motor neurons, which normally suppress the stretch reflex. Hypertonia is the opposite of hypotonia (abnormally low muscle tone).

Presentation

Hypertonia presents as increased resistance to passive movement of a limb. Two major forms are distinguished: spasticity, in which resistance is velocity-dependent and often accompanied by hyperreflexia and the "clasp-knife" phenomenon, and rigidity, in which resistance is uniform throughout the range of motion, as seen in Parkinson's disease ("lead-pipe" or "cogwheel" rigidity). A third form, paratonia, involves involuntary variable resistance and is associated with dementia.

Causes

Hypertonia arises from lesions of the upper motor neuron pathways, including the corticospinal tract. Common causes include cerebral palsy (the most frequent cause in children), stroke, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, and neurodegenerative disorders. In infants, perinatal hypoxic injury to the developing brain is a leading cause.

Pathophysiology

Upper motor neurons exert net inhibitory control over spinal stretch reflex arcs. When this control is lost, alpha motor neurons become hyperexcitable, gamma motor neuron activity increases, and reciprocal inhibition between agonist and antagonist muscles is impaired. The result is sustained muscle contraction, exaggerated tendon reflexes, and clonus.

Management

Treatment aims to reduce tone, preserve range of motion, and improve function. Physical and occupational therapy, stretching, and orthotic bracing form the mainstay. Pharmacological options include oral baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene, and benzodiazepines. Focal spasticity may be treated with intramuscular botulinum toxin injections or phenol neurolysis; severe generalized spasticity may warrant an intrathecal baclofen pump. Orthopedic surgery can correct fixed contractures and deformities.
