Self-interest

Self-interest generally refers to a focus on the needs or desires of the self, and to actions or decisions aimed at one's own advantage. It is a central concept in ethics, economics, psychology, and political philosophy, and is to be distinguished from selfishness, which implies disregard for others: self-interest may be enlightened, long-term, and compatible with social benefit, whereas selfishness need not be.

In ethics and philosophy

Psychological egoism holds that all human action is ultimately motivated by self-interest; ethical egoism holds that people ought to act in their own interest, a position defended most famously by Ayn Rand in The Virtue of Selfishness (1964). Classical moralists were divided: Bernard Mandeville's Fable of the Bees (1714) argued provocatively that private vices produce public benefits, while Joseph Butler argued against reducing all motivation to self-love, insisting on conscience and benevolence as distinct principles.

In economics

Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) gave self-interest its classic economic role: "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." In Smith's account, and in the later concept of the invisible hand, individuals pursuing their own gain in competitive markets unintentionally promote the public good. Modern economics models agents as maximising utility, though behavioural economics and research on altruism, reciprocity, and other-regarding preferences have complicated the picture of homo economicus. In political science, rational-choice and public-choice theories treat voters, officials, and interest groups as self-interested, and constitutional design — from Hume and Madison onward — has aimed to harness or check private interest through institutions.
