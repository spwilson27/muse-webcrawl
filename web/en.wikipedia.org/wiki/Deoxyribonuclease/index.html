A deoxyribonuclease (DNase, for short) is an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolytic cleavage of phosphodiester linkages in the DNA backbone, thus degrading DNA. Deoxyribonucleases are one type of nuclease, a generic term for enzymes capable of hydrolyzing phosphodiester bonds that link nucleotides. A wide variety of deoxyribonucleases are known, which differ in their substrate specificities, chemical mechanisms, and biological functions.

Types

DNases may cut within a DNA strand (endonucleases) or chew inward from the ends (exonucleases). DNase I, secreted by the pancreas and present in serum, cleaves DNA preferentially adjacent to pyrimidine nucleotides and requires Mg²⁺ ions. DNase II acts in lysosomes at acidic pH without divalent cations. Restriction endonucleases, central tools of molecular biology, are bacterial DNases that cut at specific recognition sequences.

Biological roles and applications

DNases participate in digestion of dietary DNA, degradation of neutrophil extracellular traps, defence against foreign DNA, and the controlled fragmentation of DNA during apoptosis. In the laboratory, DNase treatment removes contaminating DNA from RNA preparations, and DNase I footprinting maps protein-binding sites on DNA. Recombinant human DNase I (dornase alfa) is inhaled by cystic fibrosis patients to break down DNA in airway mucus and improve lung function.
