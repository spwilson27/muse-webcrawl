Skeletal formula

The skeletal formula, or line-angle formula, of an organic compound is a shorthand molecular representation in which carbon atoms are implied at the ends and vertices of lines representing bonds, and hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are omitted. It is the standard notation used in organic chemistry for drawing structures quickly and unambiguously.

Conventions

In a skeletal formula, each line represents a covalent bond; double and triple bonds are drawn with two or three parallel lines. Carbon atoms are not labelled: they occur wherever lines meet or end. Hydrogens attached to carbon are assumed to be present in whatever number completes carbon's valence of four. Atoms other than carbon and hydrogen — heteroatoms such as oxygen, nitrogen, and halogens — are drawn explicitly with their symbols, and hydrogens attached to them are shown. Charges, lone pairs, and stereochemistry (using wedge and hashed bonds) are indicated as needed.

History

The skeletal formula developed from the structural formulas introduced in the 19th century as organic chemistry matured, and became the dominant drawing convention as molecules of interest grew larger and more complex. Its economy of notation allows whole natural products and pharmaceuticals to be drawn compactly on a single line.

Related representations

Skeletal formulas complement condensed formulas, full Lewis structures, and three-dimensional models such as ball-and-stick and space-filling models. Pseudoelement symbols such as R, Ph (phenyl), and Me (methyl) are often used as abbreviations within skeletal structures.
