Adjunction space

In mathematics, an adjunction space (or attaching space) is a common construction in topology where one topological space is attached or "glued" to another along a subspace. Specifically, let X and Y be topological spaces, and let A ⊂ Y be a subspace. Given a continuous map f: A → X, the adjunction space X ∪f Y is formed by taking the disjoint union of X and Y and identifying each a ∈ A with f(a) ∈ X, endowed with the quotient topology.

Properties

The adjunction space is characterized by a universal property: continuous maps out of X ∪f Y correspond exactly to pairs of continuous maps out of X and Y that agree on A via f. In categorical language, the adjunction space is the pushout of the inclusion A ↪ Y and the map f: A → X in the category of topological spaces. When the inclusion of A is a cofibration (for example, a closed subspace with well-behaved embedding), the resulting space has a homotopy type that is stable under homotopy equivalences of the ingredients.

Applications

The most important special case is attaching a cell: taking Y to be a closed n-disk, A its boundary sphere, and f an "attaching map" into X. Iterating this construction yields CW complexes, the central objects of algebraic topology, whose skeleta are built one cell at a time by adjunction. Familiar spaces arise this way: a torus can be built from a disk attached along a commutator word in the wedge of two circles, and projective planes and lens spaces have standard cell decompositions of adjunction type. The mapping cone of a continuous map is another special case, formed by attaching a cone on the domain.
