The Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal Galaxy (Sgr dSph), also known as the Sagittarius Dwarf Elliptical Galaxy, is an elliptical loop-shaped satellite galaxy of the Milky Way. It lies about 70,000 light-years from the Solar System and is currently being tidally disrupted and absorbed by the Milky Way — a process that has made it a key object for studying galactic cannibalism and the shape of the Milky Way's dark matter halo.

Discovery

The galaxy was discovered in 1994 by Rodrigo Ibata, Gerry Gilmore and Michael Irwin, who noticed an overdensity of stars moving together in a bulge survey. Despite being one of the Milky Way's nearest neighbours, it had escaped detection because it lies behind the galactic bulge and is heavily obscured by dust and foreground stars.

Tidal stream

Over several orbits of roughly a billion years, the Milky Way's tides have drawn the dwarf into a long stellar stream — the Sagittarius Stream — that wraps around the Galaxy in a polar orbit. Modelling this stream constrains the shape of the galactic dark-matter halo. The dwarf's core hosts four known globular clusters, including Messier 54, which may be its nucleus.

Characteristics

The galaxy's main body is about 10,000 light-years across and contains an older, metal-poor stellar population alongside younger stars; its multiple populations suggest extended star formation before accretion. Some studies have proposed that passages of the Sagittarius dwarf through the Milky Way's disc triggered episodes of star formation in our own galaxy.
