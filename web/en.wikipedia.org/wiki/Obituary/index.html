Obituary

Obituary
MediaNewspapers, magazines, broadcast, online memorials
FormsNews obituary; paid death notice; premature obituary (accidental)

An obituary (often shortened to obit) is a news article that reports the recent death of a person, typically along with an account of the person's life and information about the forthcoming funeral. Larger publications such as The New York Times, The Guardian, and The Economist maintain dedicated obituary desks whose writers prepare essays on notable figures, distinct from the brief paid death notices placed by families and funeral homes in classified advertising sections.

Form and practice

A news obituary generally opens with the death — name, age, place, date, and cause if known or noteworthy — followed by the biographical narrative: birth and upbringing, education, career, major achievements, and survivors. Major newspapers keep advance obituaries on file for prominent living people, written and updated well beforehand so they can be published quickly; only a small number of staff typically have access, to prevent leaks. The New York Times is said to hold well over a thousand such drafts. Because of this practice, style guides govern tense and detail, and obituary writers verify deaths with considerable care.

Premature obituaries

Errors occasionally produce premature obituaries. The most famous case is Alfred Nobel, whose 1888 obituary in a French newspaper — confusing him with his brother Ludvig — condemned him as a "merchant of death" for inventing dynamite; the experience is widely credited with prompting Nobel to endow the Nobel Prizes. Other celebrated cases include Mark Twain ("The report of my death was an exaggeration", 1897) and several mistaken CNN and agency releases in 2003 caused by unedited draft pages.

Cultural significance

Obituaries are a significant historical and literary record: they shape first drafts of reputations and, compiled in annuals and collections, document a society's values. Critics have long noted selection biases — historically favouring men, elites, and certain professions — and major outlets have responded with projects to write belated obituaries for overlooked figures, such as the New York Times "Overlooked" series launched in 2018. Online memorial sites and social media have expanded the form beyond professional journalism.
