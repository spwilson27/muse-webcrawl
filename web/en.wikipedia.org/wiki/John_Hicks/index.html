Sir John Richard Hicks (8 April 1904 – 20 May 1989) was a British economist considered one of the most important and influential economists of the twentieth century. Among his many contributions are consumer demand theory in microeconomics, the IS–LM model — the "Hicks–Hansen synthesis" that long served as the standard graphical interpretation of Keynesian macroeconomics — compensated (Hicksian) demand, and the Hicksian method of welfare comparisons. He shared the 1972 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Kenneth Arrow for pioneering contributions to general equilibrium theory and welfare theory.

Life and career

Hicks was born in Warwick and educated at Clifton College and Balliol College, Oxford, where he read mathematics and then the new school of Philosophy, Politics and Economics. He taught at the London School of Economics (1926–35), Cambridge (1935–38), the University of Manchester (1938–46) and Oxford, where he was Drummond Professor of Political Economy (1952–65) and a fellow of All Souls and later Linacre College. He was knighted in 1964.

Contributions

Microeconomics and Value and Capital

With R. G. D. Allen, Hicks rebuilt demand theory on ordinal utility and indifference curves (1934). His masterpiece Value and Capital (1939) presented a dynamic general-equilibrium framework, distinguishing substitution and income effects, defining Hicksian (compensated) demand alongside Marshallian demand, and introducing notions of temporary equilibrium and elasticity of expectations that shaped a generation of theory.

Keynes and the IS–LM model

In his 1937 review article "Mr. Keynes and the 'Classics'," Hicks distilled The General Theory into the intersecting IS and LM curves — investment–saving equilibrium in the goods market and liquidity-preference/money-supply equilibrium in the money market. The diagram became the central teaching device of macroeconomics; Hicks himself later expressed reservations about its static treatment of time and expectations.

Welfare and other work

The "compensation criteria" of Kaldor and Hicks (1939) underpinned cost–benefit analysis. Later books — A Contribution to the Theory of the Trade Cycle (1950), Capital and Growth (1965), A Theory of Economic History (1969) and Causality in Economics (1979) — ranged across cycles, growth, history and method, showing Hicks's restless intellectual range.
