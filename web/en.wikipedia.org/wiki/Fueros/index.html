Fueros

Fuero (plural fueros) is a Spanish legal term denoting a charter of privileges, a body of local law, or a jurisdiction granted by a sovereign. In medieval Spain, kings and lords granted fueros to towns and regions—often newly settled or reconquered frontier communities—to attract settlers and define municipal rights, taxes, criminal law and self-government.

Medieval fueros

Famous examples include the Fuero de León (1017), the Fuero de Cuenca, which served as a model for many Castilian towns, and the extensive fueros of the Basque provinces, Navarre and Aragon, which embedded institutional self-government (such as the assemblies under the Tree of Gernika) and exemptions into the constitutional fabric of the monarchy. Kings traditionally swore to uphold them on accession.

Abolition and legacy

The Bourbon Nueva Planta decrees of the early 18th century abolished the fueros of the Crown of Aragon; the Basque and Navarrese fueros survived in reduced form until the 19th century, when they were curbed after the Carlist Wars, though Navarre and the Basque Country retained fiscal arrangements (the concierto económico) that persist today. "Fuerismo" became a banner of regional defence, and echoes remain in Spain's modern system of autonomous communities with differing fiscal powers.
