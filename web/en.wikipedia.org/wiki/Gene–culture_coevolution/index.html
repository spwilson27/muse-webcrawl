Gene–culture coevolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gene–culture coevolution, also known as dual inheritance theory, is a branch of theoretical population genetics and evolutionary anthropology that models the interaction between genetic and cultural evolutionary processes. It proposes that human evolution is shaped by two interacting inheritance systems: genes transmitted biologically, and culture transmitted through social learning. The two systems can influence each other's selective environment, so that a culturally learned behavior can create selection pressures that alter gene frequencies, and genetic predispositions can bias which cultural variants are learned and transmitted.

History

The mathematical foundations were laid in the 1970s and 1980s by Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza and Marcus Feldman, whose Cultural Transmission and Evolution (1981) formalized models of cultural inheritance, and by Robert Boyd and Peter J. Richerson, whose Culture and the Evolutionary Process (1985) developed the framework further. Related ideas appeared in the work of William Durham and in Richard Dawkins' concept of the meme, though gene–culture coevolutionists generally treat culture as a population-level phenomenon modeled with quantitative tools rather than as atomistic replicators.

Key example: lactase persistence

The best-documented case is the coevolution of dairying and lactase persistence. Populations that adopted cattle herding and milk consumption experienced selection favoring genetic variants that keep the lactase enzyme switched on into adulthood. Distinct persistence alleles evolved independently in European, Middle Eastern, and African pastoralist populations, each rising to high frequency only after the cultural practice of dairying was in place — a clear instance of culture driving genetic change.

Other proposed cases

Researchers have proposed that agriculture increased selection on amylase copy number (for starch digestion), that cooking altered human gut and tooth morphology, and that culturally transmitted norms such as food taboos and marriage rules shaped genetic variation. The framework has also been applied to the evolution of language capacity, cooperation, and social learning biases such as conformity and prestige bias.
