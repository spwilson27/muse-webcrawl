Reasoning by analogy

Reasoning by analogy (or analogical reasoning) is a method of inference that draws a conclusion about one thing from its similarity to another: if two things are alike in some respects, they may be alike in others. It is a form of inductive reasoning whose conclusions are probable rather than certain, and its strength depends on the relevance of the similarities and the absence of relevant differences.

Structure

An analogical argument typically identifies a source (a familiar case) and a target (the case in question). Shared properties between source and target are mapped across, and a further property known to hold of the source is projected onto the target. Philosophers evaluate analogies by criteria such as the number and diversity of similar instances, the relevance of the shared features, and the presence of disanalogies.

Uses

Analogical reasoning plays a central role in law, where cases are decided by analogy to precedent; in science, where models such as the atom as a miniature solar system or the mind as a computer guide research; and in everyday problem solving and learning. Cognitive scientist Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory describes analogy as the alignment of relational structures rather than surface features. Case-based reasoning in artificial intelligence applies the same idea computationally.

Criticism

Analogies can mislead when superficial similarities conceal deep differences — the "false analogy" fallacy. Philosophers of science have debated whether analogies merely aid discovery or can also justify conclusions.
