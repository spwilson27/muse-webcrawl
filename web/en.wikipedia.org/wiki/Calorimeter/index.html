A calorimeter is an object used for calorimetry, or the process of measuring the heat of chemical reactions or physical changes as well as heat capacity. Common types include differential scanning calorimeters, isothermal micro calorimeters, titration calorimeters and bomb calorimeters.

History

The name was coined by Antoine Lavoisier, who with Pierre-Simon Laplace built an ice calorimeter in the 1780s, measuring heat by the amount of ice melted. Their experiments helped establish the caloric theory and measured the heat of combustion and respiration.

Bomb calorimeter

A bomb calorimeter measures the heat of combustion of a sample burned in pure oxygen inside a strong sealed vessel (the "bomb") immersed in a known mass of water. From the temperature rise of the water, and the calorimeter's calibrated heat capacity, the energy released is calculated. Bomb calorimetry is the standard method for determining the energy content of fuels and, historically, of foods.

Other types

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measures the difference in heat flow between a sample and a reference as temperature changes, revealing transitions such as melting and glass transitions. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measures the heat of binding between molecules in solution and is widely used in biochemistry and drug discovery. In particle physics, a calorimeter is a detector that measures the energy of particles by absorbing them entirely; such detectors are major components of collider experiments.
