Louisiana Purchase Exposition

The Louisiana Purchase Exposition, informally known as the St. Louis World's Fair, was an international exposition held in St. Louis, Missouri, United States, from April 30 to December 1, 1904. Local, state, and federal funds totalling $15 million were used to finance the event. It celebrated the centennial of the Louisiana Purchase (1803), by which the United States acquired a vast territory from France. More than 19 million people attended, and almost every country in the world was represented.

Overview

Originally planned for 1903, the fair was postponed a year to allow fuller international participation. The grounds in Forest Park and the adjoining campus of Washington University in St. Louis comprised some 1,500 buildings connected by miles of walkways. The master plan, overseen by director of works Isaac S. Taylor, followed Beaux-Arts principles, with a central Grand Basin, cascading fountains and the domed Festival Hall on a hill above. Most buildings were temporary, built of "staff", a mixture of plaster and fibre, though a few permanent structures survive.

Exhibits and events

The fair showcased new technologies including wireless telegraphy, early automobiles, X-ray machines and infant incubators, as well as vast displays of agriculture, manufactures and the arts. Controversially, the anthropology exhibits presented living people from colonised and Indigenous communities — including Igorot people from the Philippines, the Apache leader Geronimo, and the Congolese man Ota Benga — in "villages" that reflected the racial hierarchies of the era and have drawn lasting criticism.

The 1904 Summer Olympics, the first held in the United States, took place on the fairgrounds, as did the famous "Anthropology Days" contests. Popular culture credits the fair with popularising the ice cream cone, the hamburger and iced tea, though each has competing origin stories. The fair's theme song, "Meet Me in St. Louis, Louis," became a standard, and the exposition was later commemorated in the 1944 film Meet Me in St. Louis.

Legacy

Unlike many world's fairs, the St. Louis fair ended with a surplus. Its principal surviving building, the Palace of Fine Arts, became the Saint Louis Art Museum. The great bird cage built for the fair remains a centrepiece of the St. Louis Zoo, and Washington University retained several fair buildings. The exposition's archives and many artefacts are held by the Missouri History Museum.
