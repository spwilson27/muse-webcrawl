Longboarding

A rider carving on a longboard

Longboarding is riding on a longboard — a type of skateboard typified by longer decks and wheelbases, larger-diameter and softer (lower-durometer) wheels, and often lower-mounted decks. Longboarding developed from surfing culture in the 1950s, when surfers in Hawaii and California fitted wheels to longer boards to practise on land when there were no waves.

Disciplines

Cruising and carving are the most common uses: travelling moderate distances with flowing, surf-like turns. Downhill riders descend steep roads at high speed, using sliding techniques — drifts and stand-up slides performed with slide gloves — to control speed, and race under bodies such as the International Downhill Federation. Freeride emphasises slides and technical control at moderate speeds, while dancing involves walking and spinning steps along a moving, typically extra-long deck, often combined with freestyle tricks. Long-distance pushing (LDP) covers endurance journeys of hundreds of kilometres.

Equipment

Decks commonly range from about 90 to 150 cm (35–59 in) long and come in shapes such as pintails, drop-throughs and double-drops that lower the rider's centre of gravity. Trucks are wider than standard skateboard trucks, often with reverse kingpins for stability and carve. Wheels are larger (typically 65–85 mm) and softer than skateboard wheels, giving grip and a smooth ride over rough pavement.
