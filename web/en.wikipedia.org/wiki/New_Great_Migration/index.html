New Great Migration

The New Great Migration is the demographic change from 1965 to the present among African Americans, reversing the earlier Great Migration (1916–1970), in which millions of Black Americans left the rural Southern United States for cities in the North, Midwest and West. Since the end of the civil rights movement, a net flow of Black migration has run toward the South, especially to metropolitan areas in Texas, Georgia, North Carolina, Florida, Tennessee and other states.

Causes

Historians and demographers point to several drivers: the decline of manufacturing employment in the Rust Belt; the economic growth of the "New South"; lower costs of living and housing; family and ancestral ties to Southern communities; warmer climate; and, in the view of many returnees, a desire to live in places where Black political and cultural life is strong. Atlanta became a prominent destination and a symbol of Black professional success, while metropolitan areas such as Dallas–Fort Worth, Houston, Charlotte and Raleigh also drew large numbers.

Scale

Census analyses show that the South's share of the nation's Black population rose steadily in the decades after 1970, reversing a century-long decline. The movement has included both retiring older adults returning to home states and college-educated younger professionals — studies have found that a substantial share of Black migrants to the South hold university degrees. Demographer William H. Frey of the Brookings Institution has described the trend as a "reversal of the Great Migration" and a defining feature of twenty-first-century American demography.

Effects

The migration has reshaped Southern politics, contributing to closely contested statewide elections in Georgia and North Carolina, and has altered the cultural geography of Black America, strengthening institutions in Southern suburbs while Northern and Midwestern cities lose population. Researchers also note that much of the movement is to suburban areas rather than to the historic Black urban cores or rural counties migrants' forebears left.
