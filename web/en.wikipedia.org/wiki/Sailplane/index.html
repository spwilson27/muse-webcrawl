Sailplane

A sailplane, or glider, is a type of aircraft designed for sustained unpowered flight, exploiting rising air — thermals, ridge lift and mountain waves — to gain height and travel long distances. Modern sailplanes are sleek composite aircraft with long, slender wings, glide ratios exceeding 40:1 in club machines and approaching 70:1 in the largest open-class designs.

Design and launching

Sailplanes are launched by aerotow behind a powered aircraft, by winch or vehicle tow, or (for motor gliders) under their own power, with retractable engines used only to launch or sustain flight. Water ballast carried in the wings increases wing loading and speed in strong conditions, and is dumped before landing. Pilots compete in cross-country racing tasks of hundreds of kilometres; flights of over 3,000 km have been made in Andean and Patagonian wave lift, and altitudes above 22,000 m have been reached by the Perlan project.

History

Systematic gliding began with Otto Lilienthal in the 1890s, and soaring as a sport flourished in interwar Germany at the Wasserkuppe, where restrictions on powered aviation encouraged glider development. Wooden designs gave way after the Second World War to fibreglass, then carbon-fibre construction, transforming performance. Gliding remains both a recreational sport and an inexpensive route into aviation, governed internationally by the FAI Gliding Commission.
