Semikhah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Semikhah (Hebrew: סמיכה) — the transmission of rabbinic authority by ordination.

Semikhah (Hebrew: סְמִיכָה, "leaning [of hands]") is the traditional Jewish form of ordination by which a rabbi is authorized to teach and to render decisions on matters of Jewish law (halakha). The term derives from the biblical laying on of hands, as when Moses ordained Joshua by placing his hands upon him (Numbers 27:18–23; Deuteronomy 34:9).

Classical semikhah

In ancient Israel, semikhah in its fullest sense could be conferred only by someone who had himself received it in an unbroken chain going back to Moses, and it entitled the holder to sit on the Sanhedrin and to judge cases involving fines and, originally, capital offences. The chain is traditionally held to have been broken during the persecutions of the Roman period, most decisively under Hadrian, when ordaining scholars was punished by death.

Later developments

In the sixteenth century, Jacob Berab of Safed attempted to renew classical semikhah in the Land of Israel, ordaining among others Joseph Karo; the renewal was opposed by Levi ibn Habib of Jerusalem and did not outlast that generation. In modern practice, semikhah is conferred by an individual rabbi or by a yeshiva after years of study and examinations, and the title moreinu ("our teacher") accompanies the ordination document. Ordination requirements and the roles open to ordained rabbis vary among Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist movements, several of which have ordained women since the late twentieth century.
