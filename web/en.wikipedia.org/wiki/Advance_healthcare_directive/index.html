Advance healthcare directive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An advance healthcare directive, also known as a living will, personal directive, advance directive, medical directive or advance decision, is a legal document in which a person specifies what actions should be taken for their health if they are no longer able to make decisions for themselves because of illness or incapacity. In the United States it has a legal status in itself, whereas in some countries it is legally persuasive without being a legal document.

Forms

Advance directives generally fall into several types. A living will specifies preferences about medical treatments, such as resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, tube feeding and organ donation, in defined circumstances such as terminal illness or persistent vegetative state. A durable power of attorney for healthcare (or healthcare proxy appointment) names another person — the agent, proxy or surrogate — to make medical decisions on the maker's behalf. Combined forms exist, and documents such as the "Five Wishes" combine instructions with a proxy appointment. Portable medical orders, such as POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), translate preferences into medical orders for seriously ill patients.

History and law

The concept was proposed by the American lawyer Luis Kutner in 1969. California enacted the first living-will statute, the Natural Death Act, in 1976, and the United States Supreme Court's 1990 decision in Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health recognised that a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing unwanted life-sustaining treatment. The federal Patient Self-Determination Act of 1990 requires healthcare institutions receiving Medicare or Medicaid funds to inform patients of their rights to prepare advance directives. In England and Wales, advance decisions to refuse treatment are given statutory force by the Mental Capacity Act 2005.

Usage and limitations

Despite broad public support, only a minority of adults in most countries have completed an advance directive. Clinicians and ethicists note limitations: preferences may be vague, may not anticipate the actual clinical situation, and documents may be unavailable at the moment of decision. Modern practice therefore emphasises advance care planning — an ongoing conversation among patient, family and clinicians — rather than a single signed form.
