Neo-conceptual art describes art practices that emerged after conceptual art, especially in the United States and the United Kingdom in the late 1970s and 1980s, which revived the idea-centred approach of conceptualism while engaging with objects, images and the commodity form. Neo-conceptual art particularly employs appropriation, text, photography, performance and installation, and frequently critiques the institutions of art and the culture industry.

History

The term came into wide use in the 1980s, particularly in connection with New York artists associated with galleries such as Metro Pictures and Sonnabend. Sherrie Levine's re-photographed Walker Evans images (1981) became emblematic of appropriation art's challenge to originality and authorship. Barbara Kruger combined advertising imagery with confrontational text. Jeff Koons and Haim Steinbach presented consumer products as sculpture, echoing and critiquing Duchamp's readymades for a consumer-capitalist age.

In Britain, the label was applied to some of the Young British Artists of the 1990s as well as to earlier practitioners, though its applicability is debated. Critics such as Peter Bürger's successors questioned whether neo-conceptualism genuinely renewed the avant-garde's institutional critique or merely recycled it as style.

Themes

Common themes include the critique of originality and authorship, the analysis of representation and mass media, feminism, institutional critique, and reflection on the artwork as commodity. Unlike the "dematerialized" works of 1960s conceptual art, neo-conceptual works often exist as durable, marketable objects — a tension that is itself part of their subject.
