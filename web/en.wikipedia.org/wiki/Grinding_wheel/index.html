Grinding wheel

A grinding wheel is a wheel composed of an abrasive compound and used for various grinding (abrasive cutting) and abrasive machining operations. Such wheels are used in grinding machines. The wheels are generally made from a composite material consisting of coarse-particle aggregate pressed and bonded together by a cementing matrix (called the bond) to form a solid, circular shape, with the grit size and bond determining the wheel's cutting behavior.

Characteristics

Five characteristics of a grinding wheel are conventionally specified: the abrasive material (commonly aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, cubic boron nitride, or diamond), the grain size, the wheel grade (hardness of the bond), the structure (spacing of grains), and the bond type (vitrified, resinoid, rubber, metal, or shellac). Standard markings on the wheel identify these properties.

Operation

As the wheel wears, dull grains are released to expose fresh, sharp cutting points — a self-sharpening action controlled by the bond strength. Wheels must periodically be "dressed" to restore their shape and cutting surface. A wheel operated beyond its rated maximum speed can shatter violently, so wheels carry strict speed ratings and must be ring-tested and inspected before mounting.

Uses

Grinding wheels are used for sharpening tools, shaping and finishing metal parts, cutting off stock, removing welds, and precision machining of components in industries from automotive to aerospace. Thin reinforced wheels are used for cutting; diamond wheels for grinding carbides, ceramics, and stone.
