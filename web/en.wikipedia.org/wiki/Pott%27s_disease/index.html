Pott's disease

Pott's disease, also known as tuberculosis spondylitis or spinal tuberculosis, is a form of tuberculosis that occurs outside the lungs, specifically in the vertebrae of the spine. It is named after the English surgeon Percivall Pott, who described the condition in 1779. It is the most common form of skeletal tuberculosis.

Symptoms and progression

The infection, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis spreading through the blood from a primary focus (often the lungs), destroys vertebral bodies, typically beginning in the lower thoracic and upper lumbar spine. As the vertebrae collapse, the spine develops a sharp angular deformity known as gibbus, and cold abscesses can form in surrounding tissues. If untreated, compression of the spinal cord can cause paraplegia (Pott's paraplegia). Symptoms develop slowly and include back pain, stiffness, fever, night sweats, and weight loss.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on imaging (X-ray, CT, and especially MRI) together with biopsy and microbiological culture. Treatment is primarily prolonged anti-tuberculous chemotherapy, usually at least 6–9 months; surgery is reserved for cases with spinal instability, significant deformity, or neurological deficit. With modern treatment the prognosis is generally good.
