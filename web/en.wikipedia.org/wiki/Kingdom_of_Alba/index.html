The Kingdom of Alba (Scottish Gaelic: Rìoghachd na h-Alba) is the name given by historians to the early medieval kingdom that became Scotland, from the late 9th century, when its kings began to be styled in Latin rex Alban ("king of Alba"), until the later Middle Ages, when the kingdom's identity shifted decisively from Gaelic-speaking Alba to the Scots-speaking Scotia.

Origins

Alba emerged from the union of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata and the Pictish kingdoms of northern Britain under the dynasty of Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín, from 843), an amalgamation whose exact mechanism — conquest, dynastic marriage or gradual Gaelicisation — remains debated. The new Gaelic name Alba, originally the Gaelic word for Britain as a whole, was applied to the northern kingdom from about 900, under Donald II and Constantine II, whose long reign (900–943) saw the kingdom weather Viking invasions and define its southern frontier against the Kingdom of Northumbria and the kings of Wessex, notably at the battle of Brunanburh (937).

Development

Over the 10th and 11th centuries Alba's kings absorbed Strathclyde and Lothian, and Malcolm II's victory at Carham (1018) fixed the border near the River Tweed. Macbeth (r. 1040–1057) and Malcolm III Canmore were kings of Alba; after Malcolm III's marriage to the Anglo-Hungarian princess Margaret, and especially under their son David I (r. 1124–1153), Anglo-Norman feudal institutions, burghs and reformed monastic orders transformed the kingdom. Gaelic institutions — the mormaers (great stewards, later earls), the thanes, and inauguration on the Stone of Destiny at Scone — long coexisted with the new order.

Usage of the term

Historians conventionally use "Alba" for the kingdom down to the reign of Alexander III (died 1286) or the Wars of Independence, after which "Scotland" predominates. Alba remains the Scottish Gaelic name for Scotland today.
