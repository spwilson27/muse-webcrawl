Florin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The florin was a gold coin first issued by the Republic of Florence in 1252 — the fiorino d'oro — and it became a standard currency of European trade in the late Middle Ages. Named after the lily (Italian: fiore) of Florence shown on the coin, with Saint John the Baptist, the city's patron, on the obverse, the Florentine florin contained about 3.5 grams of nearly pure gold and famously kept its weight and fineness stable for centuries, making it a trusted international trade coin.

Spread and imitations

Florins were imitated across Europe: Hungary's forint (whose name survives in the modern Hungarian currency), coins in England (where the gold florin of Edward III was issued in 1344), France, the Rhineland (the Rhenish guilder) and elsewhere. The Dutch guilder, known as the florijn, and its abbreviation "fl." descend from the florin, and the symbol ƒ used for guilders and florins derives from it.

Later coins

The name returned in the 19th and 20th centuries: Britain issued a silver florin worth two shillings (one-tenth of a pound) from 1849 until decimalisation, and florins or two-shilling pieces circulated in Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Ireland and other Commonwealth countries. The Aruban florin and the Netherlands Antillean guilder (florin) are modern currencies that retain the name.
