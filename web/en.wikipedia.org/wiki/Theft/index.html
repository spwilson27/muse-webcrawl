Theft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Theft is the act of taking another person's property or services without that person's permission or consent, with the intention of depriving the rightful owner of it. The word theft is also used as a synonym or informal shorthand for some property crimes, such as burglary, embezzlement, larceny, looting, robbery, shoplifting, library theft, and fraud, though in precise legal usage these are distinct offences.

Elements

Definitions vary between jurisdictions, but the classic formulation in England and Wales, in section 1 of the Theft Act 1968, is that a person is guilty of theft if they dishonestly appropriate property belonging to another with the intention of permanently depriving the other of it. The actus reus is thus the appropriation of property belonging to another, and the mens rea has two components: dishonesty (assessed following cases such as Ivey v Genting Casinos) and an intention permanently to deprive.

In the United States, theft law historically distinguished grand larceny from petty (petit) larceny by the value of the goods; many states have consolidated offences into graded theft statutes, often with felony thresholds based on value. Federal law criminalizes specific thefts, including theft of government property and interstate transportation of stolen property.

Related offences

  • Robbery — theft accomplished by force or threat of force.
  • Burglary — unlawful entry into a building with intent to commit theft or another offence.
  • Embezzlement — fraudulent conversion of property by a person lawfully in possession of it, often an employee.
  • Shoplifting — theft of merchandise from a retail establishment.
  • Identity theft — misuse of another person's identifying information; see identity theft.

Prevalence

Larceny-theft is among the most commonly reported crimes in the United States; the FBI's Uniform Crime Reports record millions of larceny-thefts annually, most of low individual value. Motor vehicle theft, metal theft, and cargo theft are tracked separately in crime statistics in many countries.
