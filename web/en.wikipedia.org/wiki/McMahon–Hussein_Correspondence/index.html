The McMahon–Hussein Correspondence was a series of letters exchanged during the First World War between Sir Henry McMahon, the British High Commissioner in Egypt, and Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, from July 1915 to January 1916. In return for Hussein's promise to launch an Arab revolt against the Ottoman Empire, Britain appeared to recognise and support the independence of the Arabs within a large territory of the Ottoman Arab provinces.

The exchange

Hussein demanded recognition of an independent Arab kingdom across most of the Arab Middle East. McMahon, negotiating in the Foreign Office's name, accepted in principle but attached territorial reservations, excluding "portions of Syria lying to the west of the districts of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo" — deliberately ambiguous wording whose meaning was later bitterly contested. Hussein, seeking to avoid a deadlock, agreed to continue negotiations after the war, and proclaimed the revolt in June 1916.

The dispute over Palestine

The letters never mention Palestine by name, which led to a century-long controversy: Arab nationalists argued the pledge covered Palestine (which they considered part of southern Syria), while Britain contended it had been excluded. The 1939 London Conference failed to settle the issue. Historians remain divided over whether the ambiguity was deliberate or the product of haste and poor Arabic translation.

Significance

Together with the conflicting Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916) and the Balfour Declaration (1917), the correspondence became emblematic of Britain's contradictory wartime pledges and a foundational grievance of the Arab nationalist movement. The British government published the letters in 1939, and they remain one of the most cited documents in the historiography of the Middle East's post-Ottoman settlement.
