Militia Acts of 1792

The Acts required every free able-bodied white male citizen aged 18 to 45 to enroll in the militia.

The Militia Acts of 1792 were two statutes enacted by the United States Congress to organize the militia and define the President's authority to call it into federal service. The first act, passed on May 2, 1792, authorized the President to summon the militia when the United States was invaded or in imminent danger of invasion, or when federal laws were opposed by combinations too powerful for ordinary judicial proceedings. The second, the Uniform Militia Act of May 8, 1792, established the enrolled militia.

Provisions

The Uniform Militia Act required the enrollment of every free able-bodied white male citizen between 18 and 45 years of age, required each man to arm himself with a musket or firelock, bayonet, and ammunition, and organized the militia into divisions, brigades, regiments, battalions, and companies under state-appointed officers. Exemptions included federal officials, customs officers, post riders, and ferrymen.

Use and legacy

President George Washington invoked the first act to suppress the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794, personally leading militia forces into western Pennsylvania. The 1792 framework remained the basis of American militia law for over a century until the Militia Act of 1903 (the Dick Act) reorganized the militia into the National Guard and the unorganized militia. The acts remain a reference point in debates over the Second Amendment and military recruitment.
