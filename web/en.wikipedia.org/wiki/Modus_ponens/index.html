In propositional logic, modus ponens (/ˈmoʊdəs ˈpoʊnɛnz/; Latin for "method of putting by placing"), also known as modus ponendo ponens, implication elimination, or affirming the antecedent, is a deductive argument form and rule of inference. It can be summarised as "P implies Q; P is true; therefore Q must also be true".

Modus ponens is a valid rule of inference: if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. It is closely related to the similar valid form modus tollens and to the invalid forms of affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent. The rule was known to the Stoic logicians of antiquity and was discussed by Theophrastus; its Latin name arose in medieval logic.

Form

Premise 1: P → Q  (If P, then Q)
Premise 2: P
Conclusion: ∴ Q

Example: If it is raining, the ground is wet. It is raining. Therefore, the ground is wet. In formal proof systems, modus ponens appears as the rule of →-elimination in natural deduction, and it underlies the "detachment" step in Hilbert-style systems. It is also the rule exploited by forward-chaining inference engines in artificial intelligence: given a rule and its antecedent, the system may assert the consequent.

Justification and discussion

The validity of modus ponens follows from the truth table for the material conditional: the only row in which both "P → Q" and "P" are true has Q true as well. Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" famously showed that the rule cannot itself be added as an extra premise on pain of infinite regress; it must function as a rule that licenses the move from premises to conclusion. In Bayesian and non-monotonic settings, "detachment" can fail for defeasible conditionals, which has prompted study of when real-world conditional reasoning departs from the classical rule.
