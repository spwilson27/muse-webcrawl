Competition (economics)

In economics, competition is a scenario where different economic firms are in contention to obtain goods that are limited by varying the elements of the marketing mix: price, product, promotion and place. In classical economics, competition was thought to drive firms to develop new products, services and technologies, giving consumers greater selection and better products; Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) treated competition as the mechanism by which the "invisible hand" aligns private interest with public benefit.

Forms of competition

Economists classify market structures along a spectrum. In perfect competition, many firms sell an identical product, none can influence the price, and entry is free — a theoretical benchmark in which price equals marginal cost. In monopolistic competition, many firms sell differentiated products. An oligopoly has few dominant firms whose decisions are interdependent, while a monopoly has a single seller. Firms may compete on price, quality, service, branding and innovation, and non-price competition becomes more important as markets move away from the perfect-competition pole.

Effects and policy

Standard theory holds that competition tends to lower prices, raise output and quality, encourage innovation and allocate resources efficiently; lack of competition allows firms to earn rents at consumers' expense. Joseph Schumpeter emphasised dynamic competition through innovation, in which temporary monopolies are continually overturned. Because competition can be undermined by cartels, mergers and abuse of dominance, most economies maintain competition law (antitrust law), enforced by bodies such as the European Commission, the United States Federal Trade Commission and the United Kingdom's Competition and Markets Authority. Critics debate the appropriate scope of intervention, particularly for network industries and digital platforms.
