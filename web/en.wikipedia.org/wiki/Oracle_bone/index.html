An inscribed oracle bone from the Shang dynasty

Oracle bones are pieces of ox scapula and turtle plastron used in pyromancy — a form of divination — in ancient China, mainly during the late Shang dynasty (c. 1250 – c. 1050 BC). The inscriptions carved on them, known as oracle bone script, are the earliest attested corpus of Chinese writing and are of fundamental importance to the study of early Chinese language, history, and religion.

Divination practice

Diviners prepared the bones by boring hollows into them, then applied a heated bronze rod or poker until the bone cracked. The pattern of cracks was interpreted as auspicious or inauspicious answers to questions posed by the king, concerning matters such as warfare, harvests, weather, royal births, illness, and the favor of ancestral spirits. The questions, and sometimes the prognostication and outcome, were carved into the bone itself.

Discovery

Oracle bones first came to scholarly attention in 1899, when the scholar-official Wang Yirong reportedly recognized ancient characters on bones sold in Beijing as "dragon bones" for traditional medicine. Excavations at Xiaotun, near Anyang — the site of Yin, the last Shang capital — recovered enormous quantities of inscribed bones beginning in 1928.

Script and significance

Oracle bone script consists of pictographic and already partly abstract characters, ancestral to modern Chinese characters. Although the script was clearly mature — suggesting an earlier, lost stage of development — the roughly 4,000 distinct graphs identified (of which perhaps a third have been securely deciphered) confirm royal genealogies, rituals, and events recorded in much later historical texts such as Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian.
