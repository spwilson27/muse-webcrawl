Plastination

Plastinated specimens on display

Plastination is a technique for preserving biological tissue in which water and fat are replaced by certain plastics, yielding specimens that are dry, odourless, durable and can be handled and examined without gloves. The process was developed by the German anatomist Gunther von Hagens at the University of Heidelberg in 1977.

Process

Standard plastination involves four main steps: fixation in formaldehyde; dehydration and defatting in an acetone bath; forced impregnation, in which the specimen is placed in a vacuum chamber filled with a reactive polymer (such as silicone rubber, epoxy or polyester resin) so that acetone boils out of the tissue and polymer is drawn in; and curing, in which the polymer is hardened with heat, light or gas. Whole bodies can be preserved, posed and dissected, or cut into thin transparent sheets.

Uses

Plastinated specimens are used in medical education and anatomy teaching, where they offer long-lasting alternatives to formalin-preserved cadavers. Von Hagens' travelling Body Worlds exhibitions have displayed plastinated human bodies to tens of millions of visitors worldwide, prompting both popular fascination and ethical debate about the sourcing and display of human remains.
