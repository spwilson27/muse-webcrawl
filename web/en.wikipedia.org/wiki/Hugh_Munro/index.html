Sir Hugh Thomas Munro, 4th Baronet (1856–1919) was a Scottish landowner and mountaineer best known for compiling Munro's Tables, the list of Scottish mountains over 3,000 feet (914.4 m) that gave rise to the term Munro and to the pursuit of "Munro-bagging".

Life

Munro was born in London, the eldest son of Sir Campbell Munro, 3rd Baronet, of Lindertis in Angus. Educated at Eton, he travelled widely in Europe and South Africa and developed a passion for hill-walking and for the meticulous surveying and listing of peaks. He settled at Lindertis House near Kirriemuir, managed the family estates, and helped to found the Scottish Mountaineering Club in 1889, serving as its president in the mid-1890s.

Munro's Tables

In 1891 Munro published in the Scottish Mountaineering Club Journal his "Tables giving all the Scottish mountains exceeding 3,000 feet in height". The list contained 538 named summits, of which he identified 283 as separate mountains (later termed "Munros") and the remainder as subsidiary "Tops". The tables — compiled from Ordnance Survey maps and his own ascents — astonished contemporaries, who had assumed there were perhaps thirty such peaks.

Munro himself climbed all but a few of his mountains: bad weather and a companion's illness thwarted attempts on the Inaccessible Pinnacle of Sgùrr Dearg on Skye, whose first ascent he missed, and on Càrn Cloich-mhuilinn. He died at Tarbet in 1919, reportedly of pneumonia contracted while serving with the Red Cross in France during the First World War, without formally "compleating" his own list.

Legacy

The Tables have been revised repeatedly by the Scottish Mountaineering Club; the modern list counts 282 Munros. Completing a round of all of them ("compleation") has become one of Britain's great amateur sporting challenges — the first round was finished by the Reverend A. E. Robertson in 1901 — and Munro's name is also commemorated in Munro-bagging culture generally. Other lists — Corbetts, Grahams, Donalds and Furths — follow the model he created.
