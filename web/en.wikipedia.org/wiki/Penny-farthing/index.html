Penny-farthing

The penny-farthing, also known as the high wheel, high wheeler, or ordinary, is an early type of bicycle with a very large front wheel and a much smaller rear wheel. It became popular after the boneshaker and dominated cycling in the 1870s and 1880s. The name comes from the British penny and farthing coins, of very different sizes, which the wheels resemble side by side.

Design and history

The rider sat nearly on top of the front wheel, which grew to diameters of up to about 1.5 metres, sized to the rider's leg length; direct pedals on the front wheel meant larger wheels gave greater speed. French maker Eugène Meyer and English makers such as James Starley developed the design, with tension-spoked metal wheels replacing wooden ones.

Dangers and decline

The high seating position made the machine dangerous: striking an obstacle could pitch the rider headfirst over the handlebars, an accident known as a "header". The arrival of the chain-driven safety bicycle in the mid-1880s made the penny-farthing obsolete, though it remains an iconic symbol of Victorian cycling and is still ridden by enthusiasts.
