Arica is a commune and a port city with a population of roughly 200,000 in the Arica Province of northern Chile's Arica y Parinacota Region. It lies just 18 km (11 mi) south of the border with Peru. Known as the "City of the Eternal Spring" for its mild desert climate, Arica is a major port serving northern Chile and, by treaty, the landlocked nation of Bolivia.

History

The area around Arica has been occupied for more than 10,000 years and was home to the Chinchorro culture, famous for producing some of the world's oldest mummies. The Spanish founded the city in 1541, and silver from the mines of Potosí was shipped through its port. During the colonial period Arica belonged to the Viceroyalty of Peru.

Following Peruvian independence, Arica was a Peruvian city until the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), during which Chilean forces captured it in the Battle of Arica on 7 June 1880, storming the Morro de Arica headland. The 1929 Treaty of Lima confirmed Chilean sovereignty over Arica while returning Tacna to Peru. The treaty also guarantees Peru port facilities in Arica, and a 1952 agreement gives Bolivia free transit of goods through the port.

Geography and climate

Arica is located at the bend of South America's western coast known as the Arica Bend. It is one of the driest inhabited places on Earth, lying at the edge of the Atacama Desert; some weather stations in the area have never recorded measurable rainfall. The city is dominated by the Morro de Arica, a steep hill overlooking the harbour, crowned by a Chilean flag and a museum commemorating the 1880 battle.

Features

Landmarks include the Cathedral of San Marcos, designed in the workshop of Gustave Eiffel and built in the 1870s, and the former customs house, also associated with Eiffel's firm. The valleys of Azapa and Lluta near the city are known for olive and fruit production. Arica is also a popular beach and surfing destination, known for the El Gringo and Alacrán waves.
