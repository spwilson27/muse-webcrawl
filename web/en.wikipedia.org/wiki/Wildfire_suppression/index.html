Wildfire suppression

A hand crew building a control line on a wildland fire.

Wildfire suppression is the range of firefighting tactics used to suppress wildfires. Efforts begin with detection — by lookout towers, aircraft, satellites and public reports — and proceed through initial attack, extended attack, containment, and mop-up, with the aim of protecting human life, property and natural resources.

Methods

  • Direct attack — applying water or suppressant directly at the fire's edge, usually when flames are small.
  • Indirect attack — constructing firelines, roads or backfires at a distance from the main fire, removing fuel ahead of it.
  • Aerial suppression — helicopters with buckets and fixed-wing air tankers dropping water or long-term retardant.
  • Hand crews and engines — ground firefighters cutting line, laying hose and extinguishing hotspots; elite crews include hotshots and smokejumpers.

Strategy and debate

Incident commanders choose between full suppression, point protection, and monitoring strategies depending on values at risk and fire behaviour. Decades of aggressive suppression in some ecosystems have contributed to fuel accumulation and more severe fires, prompting debate about prescribed burning and managed wildfire. Climate change, drought and expansion of the wildland–urban interface have increased suppression costs, which in the United States regularly exceed a billion dollars a year.
