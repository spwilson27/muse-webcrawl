A tailplane, also known as a horizontal stabiliser, is a small lifting surface located on the tail (empennage) of a fixed-wing aircraft. It provides longitudinal (pitch) stability and control: the hinged rear portion, the elevator, is deflected by the pilot to raise or lower the nose. On many large aircraft the entire tailplane is movable (an all-moving tailplane or stabilator) and its incidence can be trimmed to relieve control forces.

Function

The centre of lift of the wing generally lies behind the aircraft's centre of gravity, tending to pitch the nose down; the tailplane counters this by producing a small downward (or in some designs upward) force at the end of a long moment arm, keeping the aircraft in balance. Its size and position are chosen so that the aircraft is statically stable in pitch: a nose-up disturbance increases the tailplane's angle of attack so as to push the nose back down.

Configurations

Tailplanes are usually mounted on the rear fuselage or on the vertical fin. Notable arrangements include the T-tail, where the tailplane sits atop the fin; the cruciform tail, midway up the fin; twin fins with a bridging tailplane; and all-moving stabilators, common on supersonic fighters, where the whole surface pivots. Canard designs place the horizontal surface ahead of the wing instead, and tailless designs omit it entirely, relying on elevons and wing design for pitch control.

Trimmable stabilisers

On airliners and many business jets the entire tailplane is driven by a jackscrew to set pitch trim over a wide range of speeds and loadings. Runaway or mis-set stabiliser trim has figured in several accidents, which is why trim systems have cut-out switches and why incorrect trim setting is a checklist item before take-off.
