Internal energy


Internal energy sums the microscopic kinetic and potential energies of a system

The internal energy of a thermodynamic system is the energy contained within it, measured as the sum of the microscopic kinetic energies of its particles and the potential energies of their interactions. It is the energy necessary to create or prepare the system in its given internal state, and it excludes the kinetic energy of motion of the system as a whole and the potential energy of the system as a whole in any external fields. Internal energy is a state function, usually symbolised by U (or E), and measured in joules.

First law of thermodynamics

The change in internal energy of a system equals the heat supplied to the system minus the work done by the system on its surroundings:

ΔU = Q − W

Because U is a state function, its change depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken, whereas heat and work are path-dependent. In differential form, dU = δQ − δW, or, for reversible changes, dU = T dS − p dV.

Microscopic interpretation

From the standpoint of statistical mechanics, internal energy is the mean total energy of the microscopic degrees of freedom: translational, rotational and vibrational motion of molecules, electronic energy, and intermolecular potential energy. For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature — a monatomic ideal gas has U = (3/2)nRT — because there are no intermolecular forces.

Components and measurement

The absolute value of internal energy cannot be measured directly; only changes can be determined, usually from measurements of heat and work or through calorimetry. By convention, reference states are assigned zero internal energy of formation for elements in their standard states, allowing tables of internal energies of formation for compounds.

Relation to other potentials

Internal energy is related to the other thermodynamic potentials by Legendre transformations: enthalpy H = U + pV, Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS, and Gibbs free energy G = U + pV − TS. Each potential is most convenient for systems held at different combinations of constant temperature, pressure and volume.
