Demesne

A demesne (pronounced /dɪˈmeɪn/) is the land attached to a manor and retained by the lord of the manor for his own use and support, under his own management, as opposed to land sub-enfeoffed to tenants or held by villeins. The word derives from Old French demeine, from Latin dominicus, "belonging to a lord", ultimately from dominus, "lord".

Manorial system

Under the manorial system of medieval Europe, a manor's lands were typically divided between the demesne, farmed directly for the lord with the labour services owed by tenants, and the tenants' own holdings, by which they supported themselves. Demesne land included the arable home farm around the manor house, and often the park, gardens, and woods reserved to the lord.

Royal demesne

The royal demesne was the land kept by the Crown itself rather than granted out to barons and other tenants-in-chief. In medieval England, the revenues of the royal demesne were a principal source of the king's ordinary income, and its extent was recorded in detail in the Domesday Book of 1086 as terra regis. Over the centuries much of it was alienated by grants and sales, though the Crown Estate in the United Kingdom descends in part from this institution.

Later usage

In Ireland, the term survived to describe the grounds and parkland immediately surrounding a great house, set apart for the owner's use and often enclosed by a demesne wall. In modern legal usage "demesne" survives chiefly in historical contexts and in the term "ancient demesne", denoting manors that were in the hands of the Crown at the time of the Norman Conquest.
