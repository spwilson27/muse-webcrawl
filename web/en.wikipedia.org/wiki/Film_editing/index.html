Film editing

Film editing is the art, technique and practice of assembling shots into a coherent sequence — the process by which an editor works with raw footage, selecting and combining shots into sequences and ultimately creating a finished motion picture. It is sometimes described as an "invisible art" because a successful edit leaves the audience unaware of the cuts.

History

Early films were single shots; pioneers such as Georges Méliès and Edwin S. Porter discovered editing for narrative and effects, D. W. Griffith developed cross-cutting, and Soviet filmmakers Lev Kuleshov and Sergei Eisenstein theorised montage. Physical cutting on Moviolas and flatbeds gave way from the 1990s to non-linear digital editing.

Methods and terms

Editors build an assembly, rough cut, fine cut and picture lock, working with the director. Techniques include continuity editing, match cuts, jump cuts, J- and L-cuts, eyeline matches and the 180-degree rule. The Kuleshov effect shows how context changes a shot's meaning.

Post-production workflow

Editing interacts with sound design, visual effects, colour grading and music. Major recognition includes the Academy Award for Best Film Editing, often correlated with Best Picture winners.
