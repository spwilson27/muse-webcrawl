Damp proofing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Damp proofing in construction is a type of moisture control applied to building walls and floors to prevent moisture from passing into the interior spaces of a building. Damp problems are among the most common defects in buildings and can arise from several distinct sources: rising damp (groundwater drawn up by capillarity), penetrating damp (rain driven through the envelope), and condensation. Effective treatment requires correct diagnosis of the source.

Damp-proof course

A damp-proof course (DPC) is a horizontal barrier, usually built into the wall near ground level, designed to resist moisture rising through the structure by capillary action. Originally made of slate or lead, DPCs in modern construction are typically flexible membranes of bitumen-polymer or polyethylene; floors are protected with a continuous damp-proof membrane (DPM) linked to the wall DPCs.

In the United Kingdom, damp-proof courses have been required by building regulations since the nineteenth century (the Public Health Act 1875 is commonly cited), and damp proofing has been mandatory in modern Building Regulations for decades.

Remedial treatment

For existing buildings without an effective physical DPC, a common retrofit is the injection of a chemical damp-proof course—silane/siloxane creams or fluids inserted into a line of drilled holes along a mortar joint to create a water-repellent band. Other approaches include electro-osmotic systems and physical insertion of a new membrane. The efficacy of chemical injection and the true prevalence of "rising damp" have been debated among building surveyors, with some arguing that misdiagnosed condensation or bridging of existing DPCs accounts for many reported cases.
