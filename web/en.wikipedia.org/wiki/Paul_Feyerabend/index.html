Paul Karl Feyerabend (13 January 1924 – 11 February 1994) was an Austrian philosopher of science best known for his work on the philosophy of science, in which he argued against the existence of universal methodological rules. A professor of philosophy at the University of California, Berkeley, for many years, he became famous for his self-described "anarchistic" view of scientific progress and for his rejection of the claim that science proceeds according to a single, fixed method.

Life

Feyerabend was born in Vienna in 1924. He served in the German army during the Second World War and was seriously wounded on the Eastern Front. After the war he studied history, sociology and physics at the University of Vienna, where he completed a doctorate in 1951. He studied under Karl Popper at the London School of Economics and later held posts at the University of Bristol before moving to Berkeley in 1958. He spent much of his later career dividing his time between Berkeley and the ETH Zurich. He died in Switzerland in 1994.

Against Method

His most influential work, Against Method (1975), argued that there are no methodological rules that are always followed by scientists. Feyerabend examined episodes from the history of science — most famously Galileo's defence of Copernican astronomy — and concluded that scientific progress often depended on breaking accepted rules, using propaganda, rhetoric and counter-inductive reasoning. His slogan "anything goes" summarised the claim that the only principle that does not inhibit progress is that no single principle is universally binding. See also scientific method and epistemology.

Other works

Feyerabend's other books include Science in a Free Society (1978), Farewell to Reason (1987) and the posthumously published autobiography Killing Time (1995). He also wrote influential papers on the theory of measurement, the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and incommensurability, a concept he developed in parallel with Thomas Kuhn.

Criticism and legacy

Critics accused Feyerabend of promoting irrationalism and relativism; he replied that he intended his arguments as a corrective to dogmatism rather than as a positive doctrine. His work remains a standard reference point in debates about scientific method, pluralism and the demarcation problem.
