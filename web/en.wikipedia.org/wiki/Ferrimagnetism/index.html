Ferrimagnetism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ferrimagnetism is a type of permanent magnetism in which the magnetic moments of atoms on different sublattices are opposed, as in antiferromagnetism, but the opposing moments are unequal, so a spontaneous net magnetisation remains, as in ferromagnetism. The phenomenon was described theoretically by Louis Néel in 1948.

Explanation

In a ferrimagnetic material, neighbouring ions' magnetic moments align antiparallel through superexchange interactions, but the two sublattices contain different ions (or different numbers of the same ion), so their magnetisations do not cancel. Ferrimagnets, like ferromagnets, exhibit hysteresis, magnetic domains and a critical temperature (the Néel/Curie point) above which thermal agitation destroys the ordering and the material becomes paramagnetic. Their temperature-dependent magnetisation can pass through a "compensation point" where the sublattice magnetisations exactly cancel.

Materials and applications

The classic ferrimagnets are the ferrites — ceramic oxides such as magnetite (Fe3O4, the original lodestone), and spinel and garnet ferrites such as yttrium iron garnet (YIG). Because ferrites are electrical insulators, they avoid eddy-current losses and are widely used in high-frequency applications: transformer and inductor cores, microwave devices, antennas, magnetic recording and permanent magnets.
