Public interest law refers to legal practice undertaken on behalf of individuals, groups or causes that are underrepresented in the legal system, rather than for commercial profit. The term, popularised in the United States from the 1960s onward, covers work in civil rights, environmental protection, consumer advocacy, housing, immigration and poverty law, carried out by legal aid organisations, nonprofit law firms and private firms working pro bono.

History

Its antecedents include the legal aid movement of the late 19th century and the NAACP Legal Defense Fund's litigation campaign that culminated in Brown v. Board of Education (1954). The federal Legal Services Program (1965) and the Legal Services Corporation (1974) institutionalised publicly funded civil legal aid in the U.S., while foundation-funded public interest firms — such as the Natural Resources Defense Council (1970) and the ACLU (founded 1920) — professionalised impact litigation aimed at changing law and policy, not only winning individual cases.

Methods

Public interest lawyers use test cases and class actions, administrative advocacy, community organising support, and legislative lobbying. Because clients often cannot pay, funding comes from charitable grants, membership dues, cy pres awards and statutory attorney's-fee provisions for prevailing plaintiffs.

Around the world

Public interest litigation is especially prominent in India, where courts relaxed standing rules in the late 1970s to allow petitions on behalf of the disadvantaged, and in South Africa, where organisations such as the Legal Resources Centre litigate under the post-apartheid constitution.
