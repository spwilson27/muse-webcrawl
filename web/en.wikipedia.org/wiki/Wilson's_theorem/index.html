Wilson's theorem


Field Number theory

Wilson's theorem states that a natural number p > 1 is a prime number if and only if

(p − 1)! ≡ −1 (mod p)

That is, the factorial of p − 1 plus one is divisible by p exactly when p is prime.

History

The result was first stated by the English mathematician John Wilson, a student of Edward Waring, who published it in 1770 while acknowledging he could not prove it. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the first published proof in 1771, together with the converse. The theorem had in fact been known much earlier to the Persian mathematician Ibn al-Haytham (c. 1000 CE).

Proof idea

For a prime p, every non-zero residue modulo p pairs with its distinct inverse, except 1 and p − 1, which are their own inverses. The product of all residues 1, 2, …, p − 1 therefore reduces to 1 × (p − 1) ≡ −1 (mod p). For composite n > 4, (n − 1)! ≡ 0 (mod n), so the congruence fails.

Applications

Although it gives a necessary and sufficient condition for primality, Wilson's theorem is impractical as a primality test because factorials grow far too quickly to compute for large numbers. It is mainly of theoretical interest, and it generalises to results about factorials in finite fields and Gauss factorials.
