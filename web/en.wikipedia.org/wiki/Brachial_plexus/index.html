The brachial plexus: roots, trunks, divisions, cords and branches.

The brachial plexus is a network of nerves formed by the ventral rami of the lower four cervical nerves and the first thoracic nerve (C5–T1). This plexus extends from the spinal cord, through the cervicoaxillary canal in the neck and over the first rib, into the axilla (armpit). It supplies almost the entire upper limb with motor and sensory innervation, the exception being a small area of skin near the axilla supplied by the intercostobrachial nerve.

Structure

The plexus is conventionally described in five parts, from medial to lateral: roots, trunks, divisions, cords and branches. The five roots unite into three trunks (upper C5–C6, middle C7, lower C8–T1) behind the scalene muscles. Each trunk splits into anterior and posterior divisions; the divisions recombine into three cords — lateral, posterior and medial — named for their relationship to the axillary artery. The cords give off the five terminal branches: the musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median and ulnar nerves, along with numerous smaller branches to the shoulder girdle muscles.

Function

Through its branches the plexus controls all movements of the shoulder, arm, forearm and hand and conveys sensation from most of the limb. The median nerve, for instance, supplies most forearm flexors and the thenar muscles, while the radial nerve supplies the extensors and the ulnar nerve most of the small muscles of the hand.

Clinical significance

The plexus is vulnerable to injury in motorcycle accidents, falls and difficult childbirth. Damage to the upper trunk (Erb's point) causes Erb–Duchenne palsy, with the arm hanging in the "waiter's tip" position; injury to the lower trunk produces Klumpke's palsy, affecting the hand. Traction injuries at birth (obstetric brachial plexus palsy) usually affect the upper roots. The plexus can also be compressed in thoracic outlet syndrome, infiltrated by tumours of the lung apex (Pancoast tumour), or deliberately anaesthetised by a brachial plexus block for surgery on the arm.
