Traditional Korean musical instruments

Traditional Korean musical instruments comprise a wide range of string, wind and percussion instruments. Many were developed over centuries of court (jeongak) and folk (minsogak) music, and a number were adapted from Chinese models under Confucian ritual influence.

Classification

Instruments are traditionally classified by the material from which they are made, following the Chinese system of eight sounds: metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, leather and wood.

String instruments

The best-known are the zithers: the twelve-string gayageum, central to folk and court music, and the six-string geomungo, played with a bamboo stick and associated with scholar-officials. The ajaeng is a bowed zither used in court ensembles. The bipa, a pear-shaped lute, fell out of use but has been reconstructed. The haegeum is a two-stringed fiddle held vertically.

Wind instruments

The daegeum is a large transverse bamboo flute with a buzzing membrane, accompanied by the medium junggeum and small so. The pili and cylindrical tungso are oboe-like and flute-like instruments respectively. The saenghwang is a free-reed mouth organ of seventeen bamboo pipes set in a gourd, and the double-reed taepyeongso provides the piercing melodic lead in farmers' dance music.

Percussion instruments

Percussion dominates folk ensembles: the hourglass janggu drum leads most genres, complemented by the barrel drum buk, the large gong jing and the small gong kkwaenggwari, whose player typically directs a pungmul troupe. Court ritual music uses the stone chimes pyeonjong bells and pyeongyeong stones, the wooden clapper bak, and the tiger-shaped scraper eo.

Preservation

Many instruments and genres are preserved under South Korea's Important Intangible Cultural Property system, and taught at the National Gugak Center in Seoul, founded in 1951.
