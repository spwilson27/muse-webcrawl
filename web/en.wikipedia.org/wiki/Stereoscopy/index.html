Stereoscopy

Stereoscopy is a technique for creating or enhancing the illusion of depth in an image by means of binocular vision. Two slightly offset images, corresponding to the viewpoints of the left and right eyes, are presented separately to each eye; the brain fuses them into a single scene with a convincing sense of depth. The horizontal difference between corresponding points in the two images is called binocular disparity or parallax.

History

The principles of binocular vision were understood in antiquity, but practical stereoscopy began with Charles Wheatstone's reflecting stereoscope of 1838, demonstrated before photography existed, using hand-drawn images. David Brewster's lenticular viewer and the advent of photography made stereo cards a mass entertainment of the Victorian era; companies such as Underwood & Underwood and Keystone View sold millions of views of landscapes, cities and current events.

Methods

Free viewing (parallel or cross-eyed) requires no equipment. Anaglyph images encode the two views in complementary colours — traditionally red and cyan — separated by filtered glasses. Cinema and television have used polarised light, active shutter glasses and autostereoscopic displays that direct different views to each eye without eyewear. Toy systems such as the View-Master (1939) put stereo pairs on rotating reels, while modern virtual-reality headsets are direct descendants of the stereoscope.

Applications

Beyond entertainment, stereoscopy is used in photogrammetry and aerial survey mapping, medical imaging, microscopy, crystallography, rangefinding and remote sensing, where the depth cues help interpret structure that a single view would leave ambiguous.
