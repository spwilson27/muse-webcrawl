Advanced Video Coding (AVC), also referred to as H.264 or MPEG-4 Part 10, is a video compression standard based on block-oriented, motion-compensated coding. It is jointly published as ITU-T Recommendation H.264 and ISO/IEC 14496-10, having been developed by the Joint Video Team of the ITU-T Video Coding Experts Group (VCEG) and the ISO/IEC Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG). Finalised in 2003, it became for two decades the most widely used video coding format in the world.

Overview

AVC was designed to provide good video quality at substantially lower bit rates than previous standards such as MPEG-2 and H.263, without increasing design complexity excessively. Key innovations include variable block-size motion compensation down to 4×4 pixels, multiple reference frames, quarter-pixel motion accuracy, an integer discrete cosine-like transform, in-loop deblocking filtering, and advanced entropy coding methods (CAVLC and CABAC).

Profiles and levels

The standard defines profiles that select feature sets for different applications: the Baseline and Constrained Baseline profiles for low-delay applications such as videoconferencing, the Main profile, and the High profile (with later additions such as High 10 and High 4:2:2) used for broadcast and Blu-ray Disc. Levels bound parameters such as resolution, frame rate and bit rate.

Applications

H.264/AVC is used in Blu-ray Discs, broadcast television (DVB), video streaming services such as YouTube and Netflix (especially in its earlier years), web video through HTML5, video conferencing, and surveillance systems. It has been succeeded in many uses by HEVC (H.265) and AV1, but remains near-universal in hardware support.

Licensing

Most AVC patents were pooled and licensed by MPEG LA (now part of Via Licensing Alliance). The reference software is the Joint Model (JM); widely used open implementations include the x264 encoder and the decoders in FFmpeg.
