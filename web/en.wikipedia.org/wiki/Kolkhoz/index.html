A kolkhoz (Russian: колхоз, IPA: [kɐlˈxos]) was a form of collective farm in the Soviet Union. The word is a contraction of kollektivnoye khozyaystvo (коллективное хозяйство), "collective farm" or "collective enterprise". Together with the state farms known as sovkhozes, kolkhozes formed the backbone of Soviet agriculture after the collectivisation campaigns of the late 1920s and 1930s.

Organisation

In a kolkhoz, land, livestock, and machinery were held and worked collectively by the member households, at least in theory. Members were remunerated through a share of the farm's income distributed according to the number of "labour-days" (trudodni) each person had worked, a system that persisted until guaranteed monthly wages were introduced in 1966. Kolkhoz members were permitted small private plots — typically a fraction of a hectare — along with a few head of livestock; these plots, though tiny in area, produced a disproportionate share of the Soviet Union's vegetables, fruit, milk, and meat.

The kolkhoz was nominally a cooperative governed by an elected chairman and a general meeting of members, but in practice it was tightly integrated into the state planning system: production quotas were set from above, procurement prices were fixed by the state, and chairmen were answerable to local party and government authorities. Machine Tractor Stations (MTS) supplied heavy machinery to kolkhozes until their abolition in 1958.

History

Collectivisation was launched in earnest under Joseph Stalin's first Five-Year Plan in 1928. The campaign of 1929–33, accompanied by the "dekulakisation" drive against wealthier peasants, forced the great majority of the Soviet peasantry into kolkhozes within a few years. The upheaval contributed to catastrophic famines, most notoriously the Holodomor in Ukraine and famines in Kazakhstan and Russia in 1932–33, in which millions died.

A Model Statute of the Agricultural Artel, adopted in 1935, codified the kolkhoz system for the following decades. Membership carried significant legal disabilities: kolkhozniks were not issued internal passports until the 1970s, effectively binding them to their farms, and they were excluded from the state pension system until 1964.

After 1991

With the dissolution of the Soviet Union, most successor states undertook land reform, and kolkhozes were formally reorganised into joint-stock companies, cooperatives, or private farms. In Russia and several other states, however, many successor enterprises retained the collective structure in altered form, while in Belarus the kolkhoz system survived largely intact.
