Ink wash painting

Ink wash painting (simplified Chinese: 水墨画; traditional Chinese: 水墨畫; pinyin: shuǐmò huà), also known as literati painting in some traditions, is a type of East Asian brush painting that uses black ink — the same as used in East Asian calligraphy — in various concentrations. It emerged during China's Tang dynasty (618–907) and overturned earlier conventions of polychrome portraiture and landscape painting, valuing spontaneity, suggestion, and the personality of the brushstroke over literal likeness.

History and philosophy

The poet-painter Wang Wei (699–759) is traditionally credited as the founder of ink wash landscape painting. By the Song dynasty (960–1279), scholar-officials ("literati") had made monochrome ink the supreme medium of painting, closely tied to calligraphy, Chan (Zen) Buddhism, and Daoist ideas of simplicity and emptiness: large areas of blank paper suggest mist, water, or sky. The revered Yuan-dynasty masters — among them Ni Zan and Huang Gongwang — codified the literati ideal of painting as self-expression rather than professional craft.

Spread across East Asia

The technique spread to Korea (as sumukhwa) and to Japan, where it became suibokuga or sumi-e, practised by Zen monk-painters such as Sesshū Tōyō in the 15th century. In each country it developed local variants while retaining the core discipline of the loaded brush and irreversible stroke.

Technique

The artist grinds an ink stick with water on an inkstone to the desired density and applies it with a soft animal-hair brush to absorbent paper or silk. Tonal range comes from dilution alone — from deep black to the palest grey — combined with dry-brush "flying white", pooling washes, and broken-ink effects. Errors cannot be corrected, so mastery demands long training; the finished work is typically mounted as a hanging scroll or handscroll and inscribed with poems and seals.
