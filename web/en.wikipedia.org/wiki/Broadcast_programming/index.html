Broadcast programming

FieldTelevision and radio broadcasting
Key conceptsDayparts, scheduling, counterprogramming, hammocking

Broadcast programming is the practice of organizing or ordering (scheduling) of broadcast media shows, typically radio and television, in a daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or season-long schedule. Modern broadcasters use the term to describe both the internal scheduling of content and the development of new content.

Scheduling strategies

Programmers divide the broadcast day into dayparts such as morning drive time, daytime, prime time and late night, each with distinct audiences. Common strategies include block programming (grouping similar shows), counterprogramming (offering an alternative to a rival's hit), hammocking (placing a new show between two hits), tent-poling (anchoring a night around one strong show), and stripping (running a show at the same time five days a week).

History

Scheduling became a central competitive art with the rise of commercial radio networks in the 1930s and television networks after the Second World War. The arrival of cable, satellite, video recorders, and finally streaming on-demand services has weakened the power of fixed schedules, though live events and linear channels still rely on programming strategy.
