James–Stein estimator

The James–Stein estimator is a biased estimator of the mean of a multivariate normal distribution. Introduced by Charles Stein in 1956 and reformulated with Willard James in 1961, it famously demonstrates that the ordinary sample mean is inadmissible — that is, it can be beaten in total mean squared error — whenever three or more parameters are estimated simultaneously.

Statement

Given observations from a multivariate normal distribution with unknown mean and known covariance, the James–Stein estimator shrinks the sample mean towards a chosen central point (often the origin or the grand mean) by a data-dependent factor. Despite shrinking each coordinate using information from all the others, the total risk is strictly less than that of the maximum likelihood estimator for dimensions three and above.

Significance

The result, often called Stein's paradox, was startling because it showed that combining unrelated estimation problems could improve overall accuracy. It laid the foundations for shrinkage estimation, empirical Bayes methods, and ideas connected to modern regularisation techniques such as ridge regression.
