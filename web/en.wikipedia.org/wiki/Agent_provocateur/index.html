Agent provocateur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An agent provocateur (French for "inciting agent") is a person who infiltrates a group — typically a political movement, protest organisation or criminal milieu — on behalf of a police force, intelligence service, employer or other interested party, and attempts to provoke its members into committing illegal or discrediting acts, so as to justify arrests, repression or public condemnation. The term and practice are most closely associated with political policing in 19th-century Europe and with later state infiltration of dissident movements.

History and examples

French and Tsarist police made extensive use of provocateurs: the Okhrana's penetration of the Russian revolutionary movement produced notorious double agents such as Yevno Azef, head of the Socialist Revolutionary Combat Organization, who organised assassinations while reporting to the police, and Roman Malinovsky, a Bolshevik deputy exposed as a police spy. In Britain, controversy has repeatedly surrounded undercover officers embedded in protest movements — from 19th-century cases to the modern "spycops" scandal, subject of a public inquiry from 2020; in North America, debates over FBI informants in protest and extremist groups, and over provocateurs at demonstrations such as the 2001 Genoa G8 protests, recur whenever infiltration is revealed. The existence of real provocateurs also fuels conspiracy theories in which movements blame internal advocates of violence on infiltration, sometimes wrongly.

Legal status

Provocation shades into the legal doctrine of entrapment: in many jurisdictions (notably the United States) a defendant may be acquitted if the crime was induced by state agents in a person not predisposed to commit it, whereas merely providing the opportunity for a crime is lawful policing. English law treats entrapment as grounds for a stay of proceedings rather than a substantive defence. Rules for undercover officers usually formally prohibit provoking offences, and cases in which informants instigated crimes — such as plots manufactured around vulnerable suspects — have led to acquittals, overturned convictions and official inquiries.
