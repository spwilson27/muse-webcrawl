Consonant

The International Phonetic Alphabet pulmonic consonant chart.

In articulatory phonetics, a consonant is a speech sound produced with a complete or partial closure of the vocal tract, so that airflow is obstructed to some degree; examples include [p], [t], [s], and [m]. Consonants contrast with vowels, which are produced with an open vocal tract and typically form the nucleus of a syllable. Consonants may be voiced or voiceless and are classified by their place and manner of articulation.

Classification

Consonants are described by three main parameters: voicing (whether the vocal folds vibrate), place of articulation (bilabial, alveolar, velar, glottal, and so on), and manner of articulation—including plosives (stops), fricatives, affricates, nasals, liquids (laterals and rhotics), and approximants. Additional distinctions cover aspiration, ejectives and implosives, clicks, and secondary articulations such as labialization and palatalization. The International Phonetic Alphabet organizes pulmonic consonants in a chart by place (columns) and manner (rows).

Consonants in writing

The term is also used for letters of alphabets that represent consonant sounds. In English the consonant letters are B, C, D, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, X, Y (sometimes), and Z. Some writing systems, such as Arabic and Hebrew in their unvocalized forms, primarily record consonants (abjads), while syllabaries and alphabets record consonants and vowels more evenly.

Distribution

All spoken languages have consonants; inventories range from about six (Rotokas) to over a hundred (such as the click-rich Taa language). The most common consonants across languages include /m/, /k/, and the voiceless stops /p t k/.
