Gastroparesis (from Greek gastro-, "stomach", and -paresis, "partial paralysis") is a chronic disorder in which the stomach empties its contents into the small intestine much more slowly than normal, in the absence of any mechanical obstruction. It is sometimes described as a "paralysed stomach" and can cause disabling nausea and vomiting.

Signs and symptoms

The cardinal symptoms are nausea, vomiting (often of undigested food eaten hours earlier, typically worse after meals and in the evening), postprandial fullness, early satiety (feeling full after a few bites), bloating and upper abdominal pain. Severity varies greatly; complications include weight loss, malnutrition, dehydration, unpredictable blood-sugar control in diabetes, and the formation of bezoars (masses of retained food).

Causes

The single largest group of cases is idiopathic (no identified cause). Among known causes, diabetes — long-standing type 1 or type 2 — is the most common, through damage to the vagus nerve and the stomach's pacemaker cells. Other causes include gastric or oesophageal surgery (especially with vagotomy), viral infections, certain medications (such as opioids and GLP-1 receptor agonists), Parkinson's disease, scleroderma and amyloidosis. Damage to the interstitial cells of Cajal and enteric nerves underlies many cases. Women are affected about four times as often as men.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires demonstrating objectively delayed gastric emptying after excluding obstruction by endoscopy or imaging. The reference standard is gastric emptying scintigraphy, in which a radiolabelled solid meal is followed for at least four hours; retention of more than 10% of the meal at four hours is abnormal. Alternatives include the wireless motility capsule and stable-isotope breath tests.

Treatment

Management begins with dietary measures — small, frequent, low-fat, low-fibre meals, liquid or soft calories, and good glycaemic control in diabetes. Antiemetic drugs control nausea. Prokinetic agents that stimulate stomach contractions include metoclopramide (the only approved drug for gastroparesis in the United States, limited by side effects such as tardive dyskinesia), erythromycin for short-term use, and domperidone. Refractory cases may be treated with gastric per-oral endoscopic myotomy (G-POEM), botulinum toxin injection of the pylorus, gastric electrical stimulation, or, rarely, surgery.
