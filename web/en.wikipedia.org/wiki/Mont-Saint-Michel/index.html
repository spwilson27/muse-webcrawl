Mont-Saint-Michel is a tidal island and mainland commune in Normandy, France. The island lies approximately one kilometre off the country's north-western coast, at the mouth of the Couesnon River near Avranches. Crowned by its medieval abbey, it is one of France's most recognisable landmarks and has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979.

History

According to tradition, the Archangel Michael appeared in 708 to Aubert, bishop of Avranches, and instructed him to build a church on the rocky islet. A Benedictine abbey was founded in 966, and the island became a major centre of pilgrimage and learning, famed for its illuminated manuscripts. Its fortifications withstood repeated English sieges during the Hundred Years' War, making it a symbol of French resistance; during the French Revolution it served as a prison, a use that lasted until 1863.

Tides and access

The bay of Mont-Saint-Michel experiences some of the largest tidal ranges in Europe — up to about 14 metres — and at low tide the mount is surrounded by vast sandflats, historically dangerous to pilgrims caught by the rapidly returning sea. A causeway built in 1879 silted up the bay and ended the island's insularity for a century; a restoration project completed in 2015 replaced the causeway with a bridge on stilts and a flushing dam, allowing the tides once more to surround the mount.

The abbey

The abbey church, begun in 1023, combines Romanesque and Gothic architecture, and the monastic buildings known as La Merveille ("The Marvel"), raised on the north face in the thirteenth century, include cloisters, refectory and chapter houses stacked in tiers. Mont-Saint-Michel attracts around three million visitors a year.
