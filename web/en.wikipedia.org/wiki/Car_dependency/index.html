Car dependency

Car dependency is a concept in urban planning and transport policy describing a pattern of development in which the automobile is the only practical means of reaching most destinations, so that residents must own and drive cars regardless of preference or means. It is associated with low-density sprawl, single-use zoning, extensive road and parking provision, and the absence or inadequacy of public transport, walking and cycling infrastructure.

A car-dependent suburban arterial: destinations are reachable in practice only by car.

Car dependency
FieldUrban planning, transport studies
IndicatorsVehicle miles travelled per capita; cars per household; mode share
Contributing factorsLow density, separated land uses, free parking, road capacity expansion
CountermeasuresTransit-oriented development, mixed-use zoning, road pricing, complete streets

Causes

Car dependency is generally understood as a systemic, self-reinforcing condition rather than a simple consumer preference. Dispersed, single-use development makes distances too great to walk; infrequent transit cannot compete, so ridership and political support for transit stay low; road widening induces additional traffic (induced demand), prompting further dispersal. Minimum parking requirements, highway funding formulas and mortgage rules that favour detached suburban housing all reinforce the cycle.

Measurement

Researchers Peter Newman and Jeffrey Kenworthy famously plotted transport energy use against urban density for cities worldwide, finding sharply higher per-capita fuel consumption in sprawling North American and Australian cities than in compact European and Asian ones. Later work has qualified the relationship, emphasising income, fuel prices and transit supply alongside density.

Consequences

  • Equity: households without cars — the young, elderly, poor and many disabled people — face restricted access to jobs and services; transport costs consume a large share of low incomes.
  • Environment: high emissions, air pollution and land take for roads and parking.
  • Health: sedentary travel patterns, road injuries and pollution-related disease.
  • Economics: household spending on vehicles, congestion, and the public cost of dispersed infrastructure.

Responses

Policies aimed at reducing car dependency include transit-oriented development, upzoning near stations, abolishing parking minimums, congestion and road pricing, bus priority, protected cycle networks, and concepts such as the 15-minute city, in which daily needs are within a short walk or ride. Critics of the term argue that driving reflects genuine preferences for space and flexibility and that technology (electric and automated vehicles) can mitigate its harms.
