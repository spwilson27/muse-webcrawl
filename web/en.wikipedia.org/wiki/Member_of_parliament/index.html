Member of parliament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A member of parliament (MP) is a person elected or appointed to a parliament. The term is used in many Commonwealth and European countries for members of the lower house in particular: in the United Kingdom, MPs are the 650 members of the House of Commons; Canada, India, Australia and New Zealand use the same title for their lower-house members.

MPs represent a constituency (or, in list systems, a region or party list), make and scrutinise laws, approve taxation and spending, and hold the government to account through debates, questions and committees. Many also handle constituents' individual problems with public authorities — “casework”. Whether an MP should act as a delegate bound by constituents' wishes or as a trustee exercising independent judgement is a classic question of representation, posed famously by Edmund Burke in his 1774 address to the electors of Bristol.

Elections and tenure

MPs are normally elected for fixed or maximum terms and may be subject to recall, disqualification (for example on bankruptcy or certain criminal convictions), or expulsion by the house. In most systems they enjoy parliamentary privilege, including freedom of speech in debate, so that they cannot be sued for what they say in the chamber.

Roles

Government MPs support the ministry; opposition MPs form alternative governments-in-waiting, with a recognised Leader of the Opposition. Party discipline, enforced by whips, is strong in Westminster systems and weaker in some others. Independent MPs sit without party affiliation.
