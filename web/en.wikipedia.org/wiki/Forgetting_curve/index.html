Forgetting curve

The forgetting curve hypothesises the decline of memory retention over time. The curve shows how information is lost when there is no attempt to retain it: retention drops steeply at first and then levels off, so a large share of newly learned material is forgotten within the first hours or days, after which the rate of forgetting slows.

Ebbinghaus's experiments

The curve was first described by German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in his 1885 work Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology. Using himself as his only subject, Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables (such as "WID" and "ZAT") and measured how many repetitions were needed to relearn them after various intervals — the "savings method". He found forgetting to be roughly exponential: about half the material was lost within an hour, and around two-thirds within a day, with retention then stabilising. His results also yielded the learning curve and pioneering findings on the spacing effect.

Applications

The forgetting curve underlies spaced repetition, a learning technique that schedules reviews at expanding intervals to counteract forgetting, implemented in flashcard software such as SuperMemo and Anki. The related spacing effect — that distributed study beats massed study — was also observed by Ebbinghaus. Modern replications (e.g. Murre and Dros, 2015) have reproduced his curve closely. Critics note that nonsense syllables differ from meaningful material, which is forgotten more slowly, and that sleep, interference and retrieval practice all modify the curve.
