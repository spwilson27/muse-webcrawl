Darter

The darters are a family of fish, Percidae subfamily Etheostomatinae, native to North America. Darters are small, colorful, bottom-dwelling freshwater fish found primarily in streams and rivers across the eastern and central United States and Canada. The group is remarkably diverse, with more than 200 described species, making it one of the most species-rich groups of freshwater fish in North America.

Description

Darters are generally small fish, typically 2.5 to 23 cm (1 to 9 in) in length, though most species are under 10 cm. They have two dorsal fins, the first spiny and the second soft-rayed, and most lack a functional swim bladder, which helps them remain on the bottom of streams. Many males develop brilliant breeding coloration — vivid oranges, blues, greens, and reds — during the spawning season, making them popular study subjects and aquarium fish.

Habitat and behavior

Darters inhabit a wide range of habitats, from cold, fast-flowing mountain streams to warm, slow-moving lowland rivers and lakes. Many species are habitat specialists associated with particular substrates such as riffles, gravel runs, or sand. They feed primarily on small aquatic invertebrates, especially insect larvae. Darters get their name from their habit of making short, darting movements along the streambed.

Reproduction

Most darters spawn in spring. Depending on the species, eggs may be buried in gravel, attached to rocks or vegetation, or guarded by the male. Breeding males are often intensely colored to attract females and defend territories.

Diversity and conservation

Well-known genera include Etheostoma, Percina, Nothonotus, and Ammocrypta. Because many darter species have very small ranges and specific habitat requirements, a considerable number are considered threatened or endangered, and they are frequently used as indicator species of stream health.
