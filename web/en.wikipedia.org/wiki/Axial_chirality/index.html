Axial Chirality

Axial chirality is a special case of chirality in which a molecule contains no stereogenic centre but has an axis of chirality — an axis about which substituents are arranged in a non-planar, propeller-like fashion, so that the molecule is not superposable on its mirror image. Axial chirality arises most commonly when rotation about a bond is restricted, as in sterically hindered biaryl compounds (atropisomers) and in certain allenes and spiranes.

Examples

The classic example is 1,1′-bi-2-naphthol (BINOL) and its derivative BINAP, a diphosphine ligand of great importance in asymmetric catalysis: the two naphthalene rings cannot rotate freely past each other, so the molecule exists as a pair of stable enantiomers. Even-carbon cumulenes and allenes with different substituents at each end are also axially chiral, because the substituent planes at the two ends of the double-bond system are perpendicular. Spiro compounds with suitably substituted rings show analogous chirality.

Nomenclature

Axially chiral molecules are assigned configurations using the descriptors Ra and Sa (or the helicity descriptors P and M for plus and minus twist) under the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules, by viewing the molecule along the chiral axis and ranking the substituents.

Significance

Because axial chirality can be transferred to new stereogenic centres during a reaction, axially chiral ligands and auxiliaries are central tools in enantioselective synthesis. The barrier to rotation about the chiral axis determines whether enantiomers can be isolated at room temperature; barriers above roughly 90–100 kJ/mol generally allow stable atropisomers.
