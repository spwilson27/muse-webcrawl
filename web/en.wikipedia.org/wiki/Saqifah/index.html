Saqifah

This article is about the event of the Saqifah. For the succession dispute more broadly, see Succession to Muhammad.


Medina, where the meeting at the Saqifah took place

The Saqifah (Arabic: السقيفة, romanized: al-Saqīfah), specifically the Saqifah of the Banū Sāʿidah, was the place and, by extension, the meeting held there in Medina in 632 CE (11 AH), immediately after the death of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, at which a group of his companions selected Abu Bakr as his successor — the first caliph. The event became the starting point of the succession to Muhammad and one of the roots of the later division between Sunni and Shia Islam.

Background and the meeting

The saqīfah was a roofed portico or courtyard belonging to the Banu Sa'idah clan of the Khazraj tribe, used as a communal meeting place. As Muhammad lay dying, or immediately after his death on 8 June 632, the Ansar (the Medinan helpers) gathered at the Saqifah and reportedly discussed choosing one of their own number, Sa'd ibn Ubadah, to lead the community. Word reached Abu Bakr, Umar and Abu Ubaydah ibn al-Jarrah of the Muhajirun (the Meccan emigrants), who hurried to the meeting.

After debate over whether leadership should go to the Ansar, the Muhajirun, or be shared, Abu Bakr argued that the Arabs would accept leadership only from Muhammad's tribe, the Quraysh, and proposed Umar or Abu Ubaydah. Umar instead pledged allegiance (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr, and the assembly followed, giving Abu Bakr a public pledge at the Prophet's Mosque the next day.

Absence of Ali and its significance

Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law Ali ibn Abi Talib, his wife Fatima, and members of the Banu Hashim were absent, occupied with the prophet's burial preparations. In Shia tradition the Saqifah meeting is regarded as having disregarded Muhammad's designation of Ali — most notably at Ghadir Khumm — and as a usurpation of Ali's right to lead. Sunni tradition regards the selection of Abu Bakr as a legitimate act of consultation (shura) by the community. Ali himself withheld his pledge to Abu Bakr for a period — in Shia accounts until after Fatima's death some months later — before giving it to preserve unity.

The Saqifah thus frames the first fitna-era disputes and remains central to Sunni–Shia accounts of early Islamic history, with historians continuing to debate the details of the early Arabic sources that report it.
