Civil liberties in the United States

Civil liberties in the United States are certain unalienable rights retained by (as opposed to privileges granted to) citizens of the United States under the Constitution of the United States, as interpreted and clarified by the Supreme Court. Civil liberties are individual rights protected by law from unjust governmental or other interference. The Bill of Rights, ratified in 1791, and the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) form their principal constitutional basis.

The Bill of Rights on display at the National Archives.

Scope

The First Amendment protects freedoms of speech, religion, the press, assembly, and petition. Other amendments protect the right to keep and bear arms, freedom from unreasonable searches and seizures, due process, speedy and public trial, freedom from cruel and unusual punishment, and (through the Fourteenth Amendment) equal protection of the laws. Through the doctrine of incorporation, most of these guarantees have been applied against state governments as well as the federal government.

History

The scope of civil liberties has been contested throughout American history: the Alien and Sedition Acts (1798), suspension of habeas corpus during the Civil War, suppression of dissent in the First World War, Japanese American internment upheld in Korematsu v. United States (1944), and McCarthy-era loyalty programmes all restricted them. The Warren Court of the 1950s–60s dramatically expanded protections in cases such as Brown v. Board of Education, Miranda v. Arizona, and Griswold v. Connecticut. After the September 11 attacks, debates centred on surveillance, detention, and the USA PATRIOT Act. Advocacy organisations such as the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU, founded 1920) litigate to defend these rights.
