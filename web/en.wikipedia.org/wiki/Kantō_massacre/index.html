Kantō Massacre

The Kantō Massacre (Korean: 간토 대학살; Japanese: 関東大虐殺) was a mass killing that took place in the aftermath of the Great Kantō earthquake of 1 September 1923, in which Japanese military personnel, police, and vigilante groups murdered an estimated 6,000 or more people — overwhelmingly ethnic Koreans living in Japan — amid false rumours that Koreans were poisoning wells, setting fires, and preparing rebellion.

Background

The magnitude 7.9 earthquake destroyed Tokyo and Yokohama and killed well over 100,000 people. In the chaos, rumours spread that Koreans — resented in a climate of colonial domination of Korea since 1910 and fear after the 1919 March First Movement — were exploiting the disaster. Newspapers repeated the claims, and officials in some cases endorsed them; the Home Ministry circulated notices warning against Korean "plots", lending the rumours authority.

Killings

Martial law was declared, and army and police units, together with civilian vigilante corps (jikeidan), stopped people at checkpoints, testing their pronunciation of Japanese phrases such as "jūgo-en gojiss-sen" (fifteen yen, fifty sen) — a shibboleth that Koreans, and also Chinese and Japanese from certain regions, were said to fail. Those who failed were beaten, bayoneted, or shot. Chinese residents and Japanese socialists and labour activists were also targeted; in the Kameido incident police murdered union organisers, and in the Amakasu incident military police murdered the anarchist Ōsugi Sakae, his partner Itō Noe, and his six-year-old nephew. Estimates of the Korean death toll, long disputed, most often centre around 6,000.

Aftermath and memory

The government prosecuted some vigilantes with light sentences but suppressed reporting, and no comprehensive official acknowledgment followed. Memorial services by Korean residents' groups have been held in Tokyo annually since the 1970s; the customary message of condolence from the Governor of Tokyo was discontinued by Governor Yuriko Koike after 2016, a decision that remains controversial. The massacre remains a sensitive topic in Japan–South Korea relations and in Japanese debates over historical memory.
