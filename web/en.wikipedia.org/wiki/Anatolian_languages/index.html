The Anatolian languages are an extinct branch of the Indo-European language family spoken in Anatolia in the second and first millennia BC. They include Hittite, the earliest attested Indo-European language, known from cuneiform archives of the Hittite Empire from around 1650 BC. Because Anatolian preserves features lost elsewhere — most famously consonantal reflexes of the laryngeal consonants reconstructed for Proto-Indo-European — it is central to Indo-European studies, and many scholars regard it as the first branch to split from the proto-language (the "Indo-Hittite" hypothesis).

Languages

The best-attested Anatolian languages are Hittite (official language of the Hittite Empire, written in cuneiform), Luwian (written in both cuneiform and a native hieroglyphic script), and Palaic (a cult language of northern Anatolia). First-millennium descendants and relatives include Lycian, Lydian, Carian, Sidetic, and Pisidian, written in alphabets adapted from Greek. Hittite was deciphered by Bedřich Hrozný in 1915–1917, a breakthrough that confirmed the laryngeal theory proposed decades earlier by Ferdinand de Saussure.

Features

Anatolian languages generally have simpler inflection than other early Indo-European languages: Hittite has two genders (common and neuter), no subjunctive or optative moods of the Greek type, and an aorist–present verb system based on two conjugations (mi- and hi-verbs). Word order tends to be subject–object–verb, with clause-initial particles and clitic chains characteristic of Hittite syntax.

Extinction

The collapse of the Hittite Empire around 1180 BC ended cuneiform Hittite; Luwian survived in the Neo-Hittite states of south-eastern Anatolia and northern Syria into the eighth century BC. The alphabetic Anatolian languages of the west faded during the Hellenistic period as Greek spread, and Anatolian was extinct by late antiquity.
