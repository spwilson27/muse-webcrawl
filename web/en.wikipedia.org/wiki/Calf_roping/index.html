Calf roping

Calf roping, officially known in the Professional Rodeo Cowboys Association as tie-down roping, is a rodeo event derived from the working practices of cattle ranching. A rider on horseback pursues a calf released from a chute, lassoes it around the neck with a lariat, dismounts, throws the calf to the ground by hand (called “flanking”), and ties three of its legs together with a short rope known as a piggin' string. The run is timed, and elite competitors complete the sequence in roughly seven to nine seconds.

The run

The calf is given a head start — a “score” — before the barrier is released for the horse and rider; breaking the barrier early incurs a ten-second penalty. The roping horse is trained to stop abruptly when the rope is thrown and to keep the rope taut while backing slightly as the roper works on the ground, then to ease the tension so the tie can be judged. After tying, the roper remounts and rides forward; the tie must hold for six seconds after the slack is put in the rope for the time to count.

History and welfare debate

Tie-down roping descends directly from ranch work, in which calves were roped and restrained for branding and medical treatment. It is one of the standard events of professional rodeo, contested at events culminating in the National Finals Rodeo in Las Vegas. The event has also attracted criticism from animal-welfare organisations, and it has been banned or restricted in some jurisdictions and venues; in response, the PRCA and other bodies have adopted rules intended to reduce rough handling, such as penalties for jerking a calf down with the rope (“jerk-downs”). A related but distinct event, breakaway roping, in which the rope is not tied to the saddle and the calf is not thrown, has grown rapidly, particularly in women's rodeo.
