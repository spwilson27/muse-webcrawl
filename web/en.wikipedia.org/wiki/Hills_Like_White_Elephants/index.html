"Hills Like White Elephants" is a short story by American writer Ernest Hemingway. It was first published in 1927 in the literary magazine transition and later included in Hemingway's second short-story collection, Men Without Women (1927). The story is one of the most famous examples of Hemingway's "iceberg theory" of writing, in which the true subject of the story is never named directly.

Plot summary

An American man and a girl named Jig sit at a railway station in the Ebro valley in Spain, waiting for the express train to Madrid. It is a hot day, and they drink beer and anise liqueur while looking out at the hills across the valley. Jig remarks that the hills look like white elephants; the man replies that he has never seen one, and the exchange becomes tense.

Through oblique, repetitive dialogue, it gradually becomes clear that the man is urging Jig to undergo an operation — implied to be an abortion — which he describes as "awfully simple" and "not really an operation at all." Jig is ambivalent, questioning whether things will ever be the same afterward and whether he will still love her. The story ends unresolved as the train approaches: Jig says she feels fine, and the reader is left uncertain about her decision.

Themes and style

The story consists almost entirely of dialogue, with minimal narration. Critics have discussed its themes of communication failure, gender and power, choice, and ambiguity. The "white elephant" of the title — an idiom for a burdensome possession that is difficult to dispose of — has been read as symbolizing the unborn child, the couple's relationship, or Jig's own perceptions. Hemingway never states the nature of the operation, making the story a landmark of literary implication and understatement.

Reception

"Hills Like White Elephants" is among Hemingway's most anthologized stories and is widely taught in literature courses as an example of minimalist fiction and subtext.
