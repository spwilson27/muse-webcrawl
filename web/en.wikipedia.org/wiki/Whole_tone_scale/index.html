Whole tone scale

In music, a whole tone scale is a scale in which each note is separated from its neighbours by the interval of a whole step (whole tone). There are only two distinct whole tone scales, each containing six notes, and they are complementary: together they contain all twelve pitch classes of the chromatic scale.

The whole tone scale on C: C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A♯.

Structure

Because every interval in the scale is a whole step, the whole tone scale has no semitones, and therefore no leading note and no tonic in the traditional sense — a property that gives music written in it a floating, directionless quality. The two whole tone scales are:

  • C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A♯
  • D♭, E♭, F, G, A, B

The scale is symmetrical: it can be transposed by a whole step without changing its pitch content, and it contains only augmented triads as its triads.

History and use

Although whole tone passages appear in earlier music, the scale is most associated with French impressionist composers, above all Claude Debussy, who used it extensively — for example in Voiles from the first book of Préludes — to evoke dreamlike, exotic, or aquatic atmospheres. It also appears in the music of Maurice Ravel, and in jazz, where it is used over augmented and dominant-seventh chords with sharpened fifths. In popular music, whole tone runs are a common device for dream sequences and "underwater" effects.
