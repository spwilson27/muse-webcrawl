Lelu ruins

The Lelu ruins (also spelled Leluh) are the remains of a prehistoric stone city on the island of Kosrae in the Federated States of Micronesia. Built on an artificial islet in the lagoon off Lelu Island, the city is constructed of basalt and coral stone and covers roughly 27 hectares, including canals, causeways, and around a hundred walled compounds. It is often compared with Nan Madol on neighbouring Pohnpei, the other great megalithic centre of Micronesia.

History

Kosraean oral tradition and archaeology indicate that Lelu was founded around 1400 AD and flourished as the capital of the island's paramount chiefs until the mid-1800s. At its height it may have housed around 1,500 people. The city's compounds served as residences for royalty and nobility, temples, and royal tombs. Kosrae's strongly stratified society concentrated power in a single ruler, and Lelu's monumental construction reflects that centralisation.

Contact with European whalers and traders in the 19th century brought disease and social disruption; by the 1870s the city was largely abandoned, and the last paramount chief's authority waned as the population collapsed.

Architecture

The ruins consist of high-walled compounds built of stacked columnar basalt logs and coral boulders, enclosed by walls up to about six metres high. Narrow canals penetrated the city so that canoes could deliver goods directly to the compounds. Distinct quarters existed for the king, nobles, priests, and commoners, as well as sacred areas and royal burial compounds where chiefs were interred with grave goods.

Preservation

Lelu was designated a United States National Historic Landmark in 1983, when Kosrae was administered as part of the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands. The site remains significant to Kosraeans and is a focus of cultural heritage and tourism on the island.
