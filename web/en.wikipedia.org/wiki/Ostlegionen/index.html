The Ostlegionen ("Eastern Legions") were military units of the German Army during World War II composed of personnel recruited from the peoples of the Soviet Union and the occupied eastern territories. Raised from late 1941, the legions were organised by nationality—among them Armenian, Azerbaijani, Georgian, North Caucasian, Turkestani and Volga-Tatar legions—and trained chiefly in Poland and Ukraine before being deployed, mostly in battalion strength, on occupation, security and front-line duties.

Background

The invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 placed millions of Soviet prisoners of war and civilians under German control. Despite Nazi racial ideology, manpower needs and the hope of exploiting anti-Soviet and nationalist sentiment led the army and the Reich Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories to authorise national legions from non-Russian peoples. Recruitment mixed genuine volunteers, men seeking escape from starvation in POW camps, and varying degrees of coercion.

Organisation and employment

Each legion was intended to produce infantry field battalions (Feld-Bataillone) under German cadre officers. From 1943 many eastern battalions were transferred to France, the Netherlands, Italy and the Balkans for coastal defence and anti-partisan duties. Morale and reliability varied; some units deserted or mutinied, while others fought on to the end of the war. The legions were distinct from, though related to, the broader mass of eastern battalions (Ostbataillone) and from the later Russian Liberation Army of Andrey Vlasov.

Legacy

After the war, many legion members were forcibly repatriated to the Soviet Union under the Yalta agreements and faced imprisonment or execution as collaborators, while others remained in exile and formed veterans' and émigré organisations in the West.
