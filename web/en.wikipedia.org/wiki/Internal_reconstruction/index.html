Internal reconstruction


Alternations within a single language can reveal its earlier stages

Internal reconstruction is a method of recovering information about the earlier history of a language by analysing features present in that language alone — in particular, patterned alternations within its morphology and phonology. It contrasts with the comparative method, which reconstructs an ancestral language by comparing cognate forms in several related languages.

Principle

The method rests on the observation that irregularities and alternations in a language's paradigms are often the fossilised results of earlier regular sound changes that applied before a conditioned variant became unpredictable. By "undoing" these changes within the language itself, the linguist can project back to an earlier stage in which the system was more regular. For example, alternations such as English foot ~ feet or mouse ~ mice preserve evidence of a prehistoric umlaut process in which a vowel in the following syllable fronted the root vowel.

Applications

Internal reconstruction is especially valuable for languages with no known relatives (isolates) and for probing periods of a language's history earlier than those reachable by comparison — sometimes called pre-proto-languages. Famous applications include Ferdinand de Saussure's 1879 analysis of the Indo-European vowel system, in which he posited abstract "sonantic coefficients" (later identified with the laryngeals) to explain otherwise irregular ablaut patterns — a hypothesis strikingly confirmed decades later when Hittite preserved one of these sounds as ḫ. The method has also been used to reconstruct earlier stages of Chinese, Japanese and many other languages, and to investigate the prehistory of consonant gradation and tone.

Limitations

Because it relies on alternations that survive within paradigms, internal reconstruction can only recover changes that left such traces, and it becomes less reliable the further back it is pushed, as later changes obscure earlier ones. It also cannot determine the phonetic quality of reconstructed sounds with the precision the comparative method allows, nor can it establish subgrouping. The two methods are normally used together, internal reconstruction complementing and refining comparative results.
