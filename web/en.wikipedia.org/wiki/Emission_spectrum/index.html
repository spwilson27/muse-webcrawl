Emission spectrum

The emission spectrum of a chemical element or chemical compound is the spectrum of frequencies of electromagnetic radiation emitted due to an electron making a transition from a high energy state to a lower energy state. The photon energy of the emitted photon is equal to the energy difference between the two states.

Origin

When atoms are excited — by heat, an electric discharge, or absorbed light — their electrons occupy higher energy levels. As the electrons fall back to lower levels they release the excess energy as photons of specific wavelengths. Because each element has a unique set of energy levels, each produces a characteristic pattern of bright spectral lines, like a fingerprint; this makes emission spectroscopy a powerful tool for identifying substances.

Applications

Emission spectra are used in analytical chemistry (for example in flame tests and inductively coupled plasma spectroscopy) and in astronomy, where the spectra of stars and nebulae reveal their composition, temperature and motion. The hydrogen Balmer series — including the red H-alpha line at 656 nm — is one of the best-known emission patterns. The study of spectral lines by Kirchhoff, Bunsen, Balmer and others laid the groundwork for quantum mechanics and Bohr's model of the atom.
