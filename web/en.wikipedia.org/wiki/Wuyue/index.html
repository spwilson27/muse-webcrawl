Wuyue (吳越) was a dynastic state in coastal eastern China during the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period (907–979). Founded by Qian Liu in 907, it ruled from its capital at Qiantang — modern Hangzhou — over territory corresponding roughly to modern Zhejiang province together with parts of southern Jiangsu and northern Fujian. Wuyue is remembered as one of the most stable and prosperous of the Ten Kingdoms, lasting over seventy years before its last king peacefully surrendered the state to the Song dynasty in 978.

Founding

Qian Liu (852–932), a former salt smuggler turned Tang military commander, consolidated control of the region in the chaos of the late Tang. When the Tang fell in 907, the succeeding Later Liang dynasty enfeoffed him as King of Wuyue, a title combining the names of the ancient states of Wu and Yue that had once ruled the region. Qian Liu pursued a pragmatic foreign policy, formally submitting to whichever dynasty controlled the north while governing his realm independently.

Prosperity and public works

Wuyue's kings cultivated trade, agriculture and Buddhism. Qian Liu's construction of seawalls along the Qiantang River estuary protected Hangzhou from the river's famous tidal bore and became the stuff of legend — the king is said to have shot arrows at the tide. The kingdom's capital grew into one of China's great commercial cities, trading by sea with Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia. Wuyue was also a centre of Buddhist culture: the Qian kings sponsored printing, including some of the world's earliest printed texts, and temple building, most famously the Leifeng Pagoda overlooking Hangzhou's West Lake.

Surrender to Song

The last king, Qian Chu (Qian Hongchu), maintained Wuyue's tradition of loyalty to the northern sovereign. After the Song dynasty destroyed the neighbouring Southern Tang in 975, Qian Chu judged resistance hopeless and in 978 formally surrendered his kingdom to Emperor Taizong of Song — one of the few peaceful reunifications of the period. The Qian family was honoured at the Song court, and Hangzhou continued to flourish, later becoming the Southern Song capital.
