Child protection

Child protection is the safeguarding of children from violence, exploitation, abuse and neglect, and the set of services, laws and institutions — usually involving government social services — that respond when a child is at risk of harm. Article 19 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) requires states to protect children from all forms of physical or mental violence, injury, abuse, neglect and exploitation.

How systems work

Modern systems typically include mandatory or encouraged reporting of suspected abuse by professionals, investigation by child protective services, safety planning and family support services, and — where a child cannot remain safely at home — court-ordered removal into kinship care, foster care or residential care, with adoption possible where reunification fails. Balancing child safety against family integrity and parental rights is a persistent tension, and systems are criticised both for under-intervention (after high-profile child deaths) and for over-intervention, including disproportionate removal of children from poor and minority families.

Scope

Child protection encompasses physical, sexual and emotional abuse and neglect within families, as well as child labour, trafficking, child marriage, female genital mutilation, online exploitation, and protection of children in armed conflict and emergencies. Prevention programmes — home visiting, parenting support, poverty reduction — are increasingly emphasised alongside investigation.

History

Organised child protection began with 19th-century humane societies, such as the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children (1875), and was transformed in 1962 by C. Henry Kempe's description of the "battered-child syndrome", which led to mandatory reporting laws. Landmark legislation includes the U.S. Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act (1974) and the UK Children Act 1989.
