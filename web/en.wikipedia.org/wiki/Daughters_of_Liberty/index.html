Daughters of Liberty

The Daughters of Liberty was the female association that worked to support the patriot cause during the American Revolution. Formed in 1765 in response to the Stamp Act, the Daughters of Liberty were the counterpart to the Sons of Liberty, and they played a crucial role in making colonial boycotts of British goods work in practice.

Activities

Because women managed household consumption, boycotts of British imports depended on them. The Daughters organised spinning bees — public, festive gatherings at which women spun thread and wove homespun cloth — so families could clothe themselves without British textiles; wearing homespun became a political statement. They promoted substitutes for imported tea, brewing "liberty teas" from local herbs such as raspberry leaves and goldenrod, and they enforced the boycotts socially, shunning merchants and neighbours who broke them. Some groups also collected funds, made cartridges and uniforms during the war, and in a famous 1776 episode, women of Greenfield Hill, Connecticut, melted down a statue-like lead supply to cast musket balls.

Significance

The Daughters of Liberty marked one of the first organised entries of American women into political action. Their work politicised the household economy and gave women a public, collective role in the revolutionary movement, even though formal political rights remained closed to them after independence.
