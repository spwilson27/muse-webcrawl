TX-0

Developer: MIT Lincoln Laboratory

Released: 1956

Successor: TX-2

Memory: 64 K words of 18 bits (core memory)

The TX-0, for Transistorized Experimental computer zero, but affectionately referred to as tixo (pronounced "tix-oh"), was an early fully transistorized computer and contained what was then a huge 64 K of 18-bit words of magnetic-core memory. Construction of the TX-0 began in 1955 and ended in 1956. It was used continually until the early 1970s at MIT.

History

Built at MIT Lincoln Laboratory largely as a testbed for transistor circuit design and for its large (for the time) core-memory system, the TX-0 was essentially a transistorized version of the earlier Whirlwind computer's design ideas, reduced to a relatively small 18-bit machine. Its design team was led under the supervision of Kenneth Olsen, who later co-founded Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC).

The TX-0 proved that transistors could be used to build a large, fast, reliable computer, and it led directly to the TX-2, which in turn influenced the design of DEC's PDP series of minicomputers. When the TX-2 project began, the TX-0 was loaned on a long-term basis to MIT's Research Laboratory of Electronics in 1958, where it became a focus of the early hacker culture.

Legacy

At MIT, the TX-0 was used by early hackers including members of the Tech Model Railroad Club. Programs written for it included a mouse-in-a-maze experiment and early interactive programs using its oscilloscope display and light pen input. The machine provided around-the-clock hands-on interactive access, a novelty at the time, and helped shape the culture of interactive computing.
