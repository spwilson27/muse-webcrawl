Edward Jenner (17 May 1749 – 26 January 1823) was an English physician and scientist who pioneered the concept of vaccines and created the smallpox vaccine, the world's first vaccine. He is often called 'the father of immunology', and his work is said to have saved more lives than the work of any other human.

In 1796 Jenner inoculated eight-year-old James Phipps with material from a cowpox sore on milkmaid Sarah Nelmes's hand, then showed the boy was immune to smallpox. Jenner published his findings in 1798; vaccination (from Latin vacca, cow) spread worldwide within years, displacing the riskier practice of variolation. Jenner, based in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, also studied cuckoos and hibernation, and was elected to the Royal Society.

Context

Variolation — deliberate infection with smallpox — was already practised in Asia and introduced to Britain by Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, but carried real risk of death and contagion. Jenner's safer cowpox method, once governments promoted free vaccination (compulsory in England from 1853), began smallpox's long retreat toward the 1980 eradication.
