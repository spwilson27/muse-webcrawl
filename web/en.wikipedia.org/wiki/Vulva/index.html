Vulva

The vulva (pl.: vulvas or vulvae) consists of all of the external organs of the female reproductive system. The vulva includes the mons pubis, labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, vestibular bulbs, vulval vestibule, urinary meatus, vaginal opening, hymen, and Bartholin's and Skene's glands. The urinary meatus is also included as it opens into the vulval vestibule.

Structure

The main structures are: the mons pubis, a pad of fatty tissue over the pubic bone; the labia majora, two outer folds of skin that enclose and protect the other structures; the labia minora, two inner folds that meet above the clitoris; the clitoris, an erectile organ at the front junction of the labia minora, whose glans is covered by the clitoral hood; and the vestibule, the area between the labia minora containing the openings of the urethra and vagina. Appearance varies considerably among individuals in size, shape and colour — a normal variation that has become better documented in medical literature in recent decades.

Development

The vulva develops from the genital tubercle, urogenital folds and labioscrotal swellings of the embryo, the same primordia that produce the penis and scrotum in males. During puberty, rising estrogen causes the labia, clitoris and mons to enlarge and pubic hair to grow.

Clinical significance

Conditions affecting the vulva include infections (such as candidiasis and herpes), skin disorders such as lichen sclerosus, vulvodynia (chronic pain), and vulvar cancer, which accounts for a small percentage of gynaecological cancers and is associated with HPV infection and lichen sclerosus. The vulva is also affected by female genital mutilation practices, which health organisations worldwide campaign to eliminate.

Society and culture

Representations of the vulva are among the oldest in art, from Palaeolithic "Venus" figurines and the Irish sheela na gig carvings to modern works. Terminology is often confused in everyday language, where "vagina" is sometimes used (inaccurately) for the vulva as a whole.
