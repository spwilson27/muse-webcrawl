Egyptian revolution of 1919

The Egyptian revolution of 1919 (Arabic: ثورة 1919, Thawrat 1919) was a countrywide revolution in Egypt against the British occupation of Egypt and Sudan. It was carried out by Egyptians from all walks of life in the wake of the British-ordered exile of the revolutionary Egyptian Nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul, and other members of the Wafd Party in 1919. The event led to Egyptian independence in 1922, and the implementation of a new constitution in 1923.

Background

During the First World War, Egypt, formally a British protectorate since 1914, supplied labour and resources to the British war effort, generating widespread resentment. In November 1918, Saad Zaghlul and colleagues formed a delegation — the Wafd — and asked the British High Commissioner for permission to present Egypt's case for independence at the Paris Peace Conference. The request was refused.

Events

On 8 March 1919 Zaghlul and three colleagues were arrested and exiled to Malta. The following day mass protests erupted in Cairo and spread across the country, involving students, workers, peasants, and, notably, women — including prominent demonstrations led by Huda Sha'arawi. Striking workers paralysed transport and communications; in the countryside, protesters cut railway and telegraph lines. British forces responded with violence, and roughly 800 Egyptians were killed, with many more wounded. Zaghlul was released in April 1919, and unrest continued intermittently.

Aftermath

The revolution prompted the Milner Mission and negotiations that ultimately led to Britain's Unilateral Declaration of Egyptian Independence on 28 February 1922, which recognised Egypt as an independent sovereign state while reserving certain matters — including the security of imperial communications and the defence of Egypt — to British discretion. A constitution followed in 1923, with Zaghlul's Wafd winning elections and Zaghlul becoming prime minister in 1924. The 1919 revolution became a foundational event of modern Egyptian nationalism, commemorated in literature, art, and the design of the Egyptian flag.
