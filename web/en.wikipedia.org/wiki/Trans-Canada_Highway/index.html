Trans-Canada Highway

The Trans-Canada Highway (French: Route Transcanadienne) is a transcontinental federal–provincial highway system that travels through all ten provinces of Canada, from the Pacific Ocean on the west coast to the Atlantic Ocean on the east coast. The main route spans 7,476 km (4,645 mi) — one of the longest national highways in the world — with a marker at Mile Zero in Victoria, British Columbia, and its eastern terminus in St. John's, Newfoundland and Labrador.

History

The highway was approved by the Trans-Canada Highway Act of 1949, with construction commencing the following year. The route officially opened in 1962, with the completion of the Rogers Pass section between Golden and Revelstoke in British Columbia — then the largest Canadian highway project — and the highway was fully completed in 1971. Prime Minister John Diefenbaker officially opened the highway on 3 September 1962 at Rogers Pass.

Route description

From Victoria, the highway crosses Vancouver Island, reaches the mainland by ferry, and passes through the Fraser Canyon and the Rocky Mountains via Rogers Pass in Glacier National Park, Banff National Park, Calgary, the Prairies through Regina and Winnipeg, northern Ontario — including the long shore route around Lake Superior — then through Ottawa, Montreal, Quebec City, New Brunswick, and Prince Edward Island (via the Confederation Bridge), Nova Scotia, and finally, by ferry, Newfoundland, ending in St. John's.

Signage

The Trans-Canada Highway is marked by a distinctive green-and-white shield depicting a maple leaf. Unlike American Interstate highways, it is not a single numbered route nationally; each province assigns its own route numbers to its sections.

Control and standards

Contrary to popular belief, the Trans-Canada Highway is not maintained by the federal government but by the provinces, and its standard varies widely — from freeway in populated areas to two-lane highway through much of northern Ontario and the mountains.
