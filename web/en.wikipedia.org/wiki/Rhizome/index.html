Rhizome

A rhizome is a modified plant stem that grows horizontally at or below the soil surface and can produce roots and shoots from its nodes. Rhizomes allow a plant to store nutrients, survive unfavorable seasons and spread vegetatively into new territory.

Type Modified stem
Growth Horizontal, underground or occasionally above ground
Examples Ginger, turmeric, iris, bamboo, lily of the valley and many grasses and ferns

Structure

Unlike a root, a rhizome has nodes, internodes, buds and often scale-like leaves. Roots emerge from the lower side or nodes, while shoots grow upward from buds. A cut section containing a viable bud can often grow into a genetically identical plant.

Function

Stored starch and other nutrients support rapid spring growth or regrowth after fire, grazing or mowing. Horizontal spread helps plants colonize open ground and stabilize soil. The same persistence can make rhizomatous weeds—including some forms of bindweed, couch grass and Japanese knotweed—difficult to eradicate because fragments may resprout.

Distinctions

A stolon is a horizontal stem that usually grows above or just at the surface and roots at nodes. A tuber is a swollen storage portion of a stem or root; a bulb consists largely of fleshy leaf bases around a short stem. Ginger “root” in cooking is actually a rhizome.

Human uses

Rhizomes provide foods, spices and medicines, notably ginger, turmeric and galangal. Gardeners divide rhizomes to propagate irises and other ornamentals. Running bamboos spread by rhizomes and may require barriers in gardens.

Figurative use

Philosophers Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari adopted “rhizome” as a model of non-hierarchical, multiply connected organization, contrasting it with tree-like structures. The metaphor is widely used in cultural theory and network studies.
