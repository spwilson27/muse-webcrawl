Heart of Darkness is a novella by Polish-English novelist Joseph Conrad, first published as a three-part serial in Blackwood's Magazine in 1899 and in book form in 1902. It tells, within a frame narrative, the story of Charles Marlow, a seaman who travels up the Congo River to find the ivory trader Kurtz, an agent who has become a demigod among the local people and has descended into brutality and madness. The novella is widely regarded as a landmark of modernist literature and a critique of European colonialism, in particular the regime of Leopold II's Congo Free State — though its depiction of Africa and Africans has itself been the subject of intense debate.

Plot summary

Aboard the yacht Nellie on the Thames, Marlow recounts how, keen to command a river steamer in Africa, he obtained a post with a Belgian trading company. In the Congo he witnesses the cruelty and futility of the colonial enterprise at the Outer and Central Stations, hears admiring talk of the exceptional agent Kurtz, and journeys upriver with the manager to relieve him. Approaching Kurtz's Inner Station, the steamer is attacked; Marlow finds Kurtz ruling through intimidation, his station adorned with severed heads, and his manuscript on civilising Africa ending in the scrawl "Exterminate all the brutes!" The dying Kurtz is taken aboard and expires with the famous last words "The horror! The horror!" Back in Europe, Marlow visits Kurtz's fiancée ("the Intended") and preserves her illusions by reporting that Kurtz's last word was her name.

Sources and themes

Conrad drew on his own 1890 service as captain of the steamer Roi des Belges on the Congo. Themes include the hypocrisy and violence of imperialism, the fragility of civilisation and identity, darkness as both place and moral condition, and the unreliability of language and narrative. Chinua Achebe's 1975 lecture "An Image of Africa" famously condemned the novella as racist, arguing Africa and Africans serve merely as backdrop for a European psychological drama; others defend the work as an anti-colonial critique while acknowledging its period limitations, and the debate remains central to its teaching.

Influence and adaptations

The novella influenced works from T. S. Eliot's poetry (an epigraph tradition via The Hollow Men's "Mistah Kurtz — he dead") to Francis Ford Coppola's film Apocalypse Now (1979), which transposes the story to the Vietnam War, and the video game Spec Ops: The Line.
