Hypertension

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hypertension, also known as high blood pressure, is a long-term medical condition in which the blood pressure in the arteries is persistently elevated. It is usually diagnosed when repeated readings reach about 140/90 mmHg or above (some guidelines use 130/80). Because it rarely causes symptoms, it is called the 'silent killer': it quietly damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes, and is the leading global risk factor for early death, driving strokes, heart attacks and heart failure.

Around 1.3 billion adults have hypertension, most unaware or undertreated. In about 90–95% of cases it is 'primary' — no single cause, but a mix of genetics, age, salt intake, body weight, alcohol, inactivity and stress; the rest is secondary to kidney or endocrine disease and other causes.

Management

Lifestyle measures — less salt, weight loss, exercise, limited alcohol, a diet rich in vegetables (DASH) — can lower pressure substantially. Drugs (thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers) are usually combined at low doses. Blood-pressure control is among the most cost-effective interventions in all of Medicine.

Measurement

Pressure varies through the day and with stress ('white-coat' hypertension), so guidelines require repeated or ambulatory/home measurements. The sphygmomanometer cuff remains the standard instrument, a design little changed for a century.
