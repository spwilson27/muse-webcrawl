Zooplankton

Zooplankton are the heterotrophic (sometimes detritivorous) component of plankton — the diverse collection of organisms living in water bodies that are unable to propel themselves against a current. The name derives from the Greek zōion ("animal") and planktos ("wanderer" or "drifter"). Zooplankton range from microscopic protozoans and tiny crustaceans such as copepods and krill to large jellyfish, and they form a crucial link in aquatic food webs by consuming phytoplankton and in turn being eaten by fish, whales, and other animals.

Classification

Zooplankton are divided by life history into holoplankton, which spend their entire lives in the plankton (copepods, krill, arrow worms, many jellyfish), and meroplankton, which are planktonic only for part of their lives — typically the larval stages of crabs, sea urchins, molluscs, and fish. They are also grouped by size, from microzooplankton (single-celled protists, 20–200 µm) through mesozooplankton (copepods and larvae visible to the naked eye) to macro- and megazooplankton such as krill and large jellyfish.

Ecology

Many zooplankton undertake diel vertical migration, rising to surface waters at night to feed and descending to darker depths by day to avoid predators — collectively one of the largest daily migrations on Earth. Grazing by zooplankton controls phytoplankton blooms, and their faecal pellets and carcasses export carbon to the deep ocean as part of the biological pump. Zooplankton abundance and composition are sensitive indicators of climate change and ocean health.
