Thrace (Bulgarian: Тракия, Trakiya; Greek: Θράκη, Thráki; Turkish: Trakya) is a geographical and historical region in Southeast Europe, bounded by the Balkan Mountains to the north, the Aegean Sea to the south, and the Black Sea to the east. It comprises southeastern Bulgaria (Northern Thrace), northeastern Greece (Western Thrace), and the European part of Turkey (Eastern Thrace). The ancient Thracians — an Indo-European people made up of many tribes — gave the region its name.

History

Ancient Thrace was famed in Greek myth as the home of Orpheus, Ares and the Boreads, and its people were renowned as warriors and horsemen; Greek colonies lined its coasts from the 7th century BC. The Odrysian kingdom united many Thracian tribes in the 5th–4th centuries BC, leaving celebrated gold treasures such as those of Panagyurishte and Rogozen and rock tombs like Kazanlak and Sveshtari, now UNESCO sites. Thrace passed under Macedonian, then Roman rule — the Romans organised it as the province of Thracia — and the great road Via Egnatia crossed it. In Byzantine times it was the empire's European heartland, guarding Constantinople.

Ottoman Turks conquered Thrace in the 14th century, making Edirne (Adrianople) their capital before the fall of Constantinople in 1453. The region was contested in the Balkan Wars of 1912–13 and the First World War; the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne fixed most of the present frontiers and the Greco-Turkish population exchange reshaped its population.

Geography

The Maritsa (Evros/Meriç) River forms much of the Greek–Turkish and Bulgarian borders. Eastern Thrace is largely rolling agricultural land, Turkey's chief source of sunflower and wheat; Western Thrace in Greece is home to a recognised Muslim minority and the cities of Xanthi, Komotini and Alexandroupoli; Bulgarian (Northern) Thrace includes Plovdiv, one of Europe's oldest continuously inhabited cities, and the fertile Upper Thracian Plain beneath the Balkan and Rhodope mountains.

Culture

Thracian folk music, with its distinctive odd (aksak) rhythms, Thracian gold craftsmanship, wine production since antiquity, and the shared culinary heritage of its three modern states, remain hallmarks of the region's identity.
