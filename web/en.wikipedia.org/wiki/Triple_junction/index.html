Triple junction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A triple junction is the point where the boundaries of three tectonic plates meet. At the triple junction each of the three boundaries is one of three types: a ridge (R), trench (T) or transform fault (F), and triple junctions can be described according to the types of plate margin that meet at them (for example, RRR, TTT, TFF). Of the many possible types, only some are stable over geological time — that is, the geometry of the junction is preserved as the plates move.

History and theory

The theory of triple junctions was developed in the late 1960s and early 1970s, notably by W. Jason Morgan, Dan McKenzie and Tanya Atwater, building on the Wilson cycle and the kinematics of rigid plates on a sphere. Stability analysis uses velocity-space diagrams: a junction is stable if the three boundary velocity lines can meet at a single point. For example, a ridge–ridge–ridge (RRR) junction is always stable, whereas a trench–trench–trench (TTT) junction is generally unstable.

Examples

Afar triple junction

The Afar triple junction in East Africa is an RRR junction where the Red Sea Rift, the Gulf of Aden Rift and the East African Rift meet, progressively splitting the African Plate into the Nubian and Somali plates.

Mendocino triple junction

Off the coast of northern California, the Mendocino triple junction (a TFF/FFT-type junction) is where the San Andreas Fault, the Cascadia subduction zone and the Mendocino fracture zone meet the Pacific, North American and Juan de Fuca (Gorda) plates. It is one of the most seismically active regions of the United States.

Other examples

The Galápagos triple junction (Pacific–Cocos–Nazca), the Azores triple junction in the mid-Atlantic, the Boso triple junction off Japan (a rare TTT example), and the Rodrigues triple junction in the Indian Ocean are classic study areas. The Chile triple junction, where an oceanic spreading ridge is being subducted beneath South America, is an important R-T-T example.
