Collard (plant)

Collard
SpeciesBrassica oleracea
Cultivar groupAcephala Group
OriginEastern Mediterranean
RelatedKale, spring greens, cabbage

Collard is a group of loose-leaf cultivars of Brassica oleracea, the same species as many common vegetables, including cabbage and broccoli. They are part of the Acephala Group of the species, which includes kale and spring greens. Collards are grown for their large, dark-green, edible leaves, which are cooked and eaten as a vegetable. The name "collard" comes from the word "colewort" (the wild cabbage plant).

Description and cultivation

Collards are biennial where winter frost occurs and perennial in more tropical areas; they tolerate heat and poor soil better than most other forms of B. oleracea. The plant has an upright stalk, often growing up to 60 cm (2 ft) tall, bearing smooth, broad leaves that do not form a head. Flavour improves after exposure to frost. Well-known cultivars include 'Georgia Southern', 'Vates', and 'Morris Heading'.

History

Greeks and Romans cultivated loose-leaf brassicas resembling collards more than two thousand years ago. Collards reached the American South by the 18th century and became a staple of Southern and African-American cooking, where the leaves are traditionally slow-cooked with smoked or salted meats and the nutrient-rich cooking liquid ("pot liquor") is prized. In 2011, South Carolina designated collard greens its official state vegetable.

Culinary use

Collard greens are a staple in the cuisine of the Southern United States, often served with cornbread and as part of a New Year's Day meal with black-eyed peas and pork for luck and prosperity. They are also eaten in East Africa (as sukuma wiki, "stretch the week"), in Brazil (as couve, served with feijoada), and in Portuguese soups such as caldo verde.

Nutrition

Collards are an excellent source of vitamin K, vitamin A, vitamin C, folate, calcium, and dietary fibre, and are low in calories.
