Behavioural ecology

FieldEvolutionary biology, ecology, ethology
Key figuresNiko Tinbergen, John Maynard Smith, W. D. Hamilton

Behavioural ecology (American: behavioral ecology) is the study of the evolutionary basis for animal behavior due to ecological pressures. It examines how behaviour contributes to survival and reproduction — that is, to fitness — and how natural selection shapes behaviour in response to the environment, including other organisms.

History

The field emerged from ethology, comparative psychology, and ecology in the mid-twentieth century. Niko Tinbergen's "four questions" — mechanism, ontogeny, function, and phylogeny — provided a framework distinguishing proximate from ultimate explanations of behaviour. The publication of key works in the 1970s and the founding of the journal Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology (1976) and the textbook An Introduction to Behavioural Ecology by Krebs and Davies (1981) consolidated the discipline.

Key topics

Major topics include optimal foraging theory — which models how animals maximise energy intake per unit time; mating systems and sexual selection; parental care and parent–offspring conflict; social behaviour and cooperation, explained in part by kin selection and Hamilton's rule; communication and signalling, including honest signalling and handicap theory; and predator–prey arms races and coevolution.

Methods

Behavioural ecologists combine field observation and experiments with mathematical modelling, such as game theory — notably the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) concept introduced by John Maynard Smith and George Price — and state-dependent dynamic models. Comparative studies across species test whether behavioural traits evolve as adaptations to ecological conditions.
