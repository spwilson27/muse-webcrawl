Poison

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, poisons are chemical substances that cause death, injury or harm to organs when an organism absorbs a sufficient quantity. In medicine (particularly toxicology) and in industry the term is used more specifically: a poison is a substance that causes death or harm through chemical action, as distinguished from a venom, which an animal actively delivers by bite or sting. As Paracelsus observed in the 16th century, "the dose makes the poison" — virtually any substance, including water, is harmful in sufficient quantity.

Classification

Poisons are classified by their effects (neurotoxins, hepatotoxins, nephrotoxins), their chemical nature (heavy metals such as arsenic, lead and mercury; gases such as carbon monoxide and cyanide; organic compounds such as strychnine), their origin (plant alkaloids, bacterial toxins such as botulinum toxin — the most acutely lethal substance known), and their intended use in regulation. Hazardous materials are labelled with pictograms such as the skull and crossbones under the Globally Harmonized System.

Poisoning

Poisoning may be acute or chronic, accidental, occupational, or deliberate (homicidal poisoning features throughout history, from hemlock — used to execute Socrates — to arsenic, "the inheritance powder"). Treatment involves removing exposure, supportive care, and in some cases specific antidotes: for example naloxone for opioid overdose, atropine for organophosphate poisoning, and chelation therapy for heavy-metal poisoning. Poison control centres provide expert advice worldwide.

In nature

Many organisms produce poisons defensively or to subdue prey: poisonous plants (deadly nightshade, poison hemlock, castor bean — source of ricin), fungi such as the death cap mushroom (amatoxins), and animals including poison dart frogs, whose skin secretions are passively toxic rather than injected, and pufferfish (tetrodotoxin). Predators and prey engage in evolutionary arms races of toxicity and resistance.
