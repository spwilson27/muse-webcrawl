Inositol, or more precisely in its commonest form myo-inositol, is a carbocyclic sugar that is abundant in the brain and other mammalian tissues. It is a structural component of cell membranes as phosphatidylinositol and of the inositol phosphate secondary messenger system. It is made naturally in the human body from glucose and was once (incorrectly) regarded as a B vitamin, sometimes called vitamin B8; it is no longer classified as an essential nutrient because the body synthesises sufficient quantities.

Isomers

Inositol exists in nine possible stereoisomers, which differ in the orientation of their six hydroxyl groups around the cyclohexane ring. The most important are myo-, scyllo-, muco-, chiro- (D- and L-), neo-, allo-, epi- and cis-inositol. Of these, myo-inositol is by far the most abundant in nature.

Biological role

myo-Inositol plays a key role in intracellular signal transduction. Hydrolysis of the membrane lipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate produces the second messengers inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol, which regulate calcium release and protein kinase C activity. Inositol phosphates and phosphatidylinositols also serve as structural lipids and participate in osmoregulation, nerve conduction and fat metabolism.

Dietary sources and uses

Inositol is present in many foods, particularly fruits such as cantaloupe and citrus fruits, beans, grains and nuts, largely in the form of phytic acid (inositol hexaphosphate). It is sold as a dietary supplement and has been studied for possible roles in polycystic ovary syndrome, gestational diabetes, and some psychiatric conditions, although clinical evidence varies. Industrial applications include use as an inert cutting agent and in infant formula.

Synthesis

In the body, myo-inositol is synthesised mainly in the kidneys from glucose-6-phosphate, which is first isomerised to inositol-1-phosphate and then dephosphorylated. An average adult produces several grams per day.
