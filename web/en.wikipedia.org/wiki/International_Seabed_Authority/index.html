International Seabed Authority

AbbreviationISA
Formation16 November 1994
HeadquartersKingston, Jamaica
Membership169 member states plus the European Union
BasisUNCLOS Part XI

The International Seabed Authority (ISA) is an autonomous international organisation established under the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Based in Kingston, Jamaica, it organises and controls activities in "the Area" — the seabed and ocean floor beyond the limits of national jurisdiction, which the Convention declares the common heritage of mankind. Through the ISA, states parties administer the mineral resources of the deep seabed, particularly polymetallic nodules, seafloor massive sulphides and cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts.

Structure

The ISA's principal organs are the Assembly, comprising all member states; the 36-member Council, elected by the Assembly from groups representing consumers, investors, exporters and developing countries as well as ensuring equitable geographic distribution; the Secretariat, headed by a Secretary-General; and advisory bodies including the Legal and Technical Commission and the Finance Committee. The Convention also envisaged an operational arm, the Enterprise, to mine on behalf of developing countries, though it has never become operational in its intended form.

Exploration contracts

States and companies sponsored by a state party may obtain 15-year exploration contracts for defined areas, most notably in the Clarion–Clipperton Zone of the Pacific Ocean. By the early 2020s the ISA had issued around 30 exploration contracts. Contractors must collect environmental baseline data and, for each block claimed, reserve an equivalent area for the Enterprise or developing countries.

Towards exploitation

For many years the ISA drafted regulations for commercial exploitation. In June 2021 the island state of Nauru triggered the so-called "two-year rule", requiring the Council to finalise the exploitation regulations within two years; the deadline passed in July 2023 without completed rules, leaving the legal position of any commercial mining application uncertain. The debate has sharpened divisions between states and companies seeking to begin deep-sea mining — citing demand for battery metals such as nickel, cobalt and manganese — and a growing number of states, scientists and environmental groups calling for a moratorium or precautionary pause because of the risks to poorly understood deep-sea ecosystems.
