Sound recording and reproduction

Sound recording and reproduction is the electrical, mechanical, electronic, or digital inscription and re-creation of sound waves, such as spoken voice, singing, instrumental music, or sound effects. The two main classes of sound recording technology are analog recording and digital recording.

History

The earliest practical device was Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville's phonautograph (1857), which inscribed sound waves as tracings on paper but could not play them back. Thomas Edison's phonograph (1877) both recorded and reproduced sound using a tinfoil-wrapped cylinder, and Emile Berliner's gramophone (1887) introduced flat discs, which became the dominant consumer format for most of the twentieth century.

Electrical recording, introduced in the 1920s, replaced purely mechanical capture with microphones and electronic amplification, greatly improving fidelity. Magnetic tape, developed in Germany in the 1930s and widely adopted after the Second World War, made editing and multitrack recording practical. The long-playing record (1948) and the compact cassette (1963) extended playing time and portability.

Digital recording

Digital recording converts the analog waveform into a stream of numbers by sampling and quantization, as standardized in pulse-code modulation. The compact disc (1982) brought digital audio to consumers; later formats and distribution methods include MiniDisc, MP3 and other compressed codecs, digital audio workstations, and internet streaming services, which by the 2010s had become the dominant means of music distribution.

Process

A recording chain typically involves transduction by a microphone, amplification, processing, and storage on a medium such as tape, disc, or computer memory. Reproduction reverses the chain through a digital-to-analog converter or pickup, amplification, and loudspeakers or headphones. See also sound engineering.
