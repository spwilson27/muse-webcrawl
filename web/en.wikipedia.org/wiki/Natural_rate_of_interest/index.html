Natural rate of interest

The natural rate of interest, often denoted r* ("r-star"), is the real interest rate that supports the economy at full employment and maximum output while keeping inflation constant. It is the rate at which monetary policy is neither expansionary nor contractionary, and it is a central concept in modern macroeconomic theory and central banking.

History

The concept originates with the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell, who developed it in his 1898 work Interest and Prices. Wicksell defined the natural rate as the rate of interest on loans that is neutral with respect to commodity prices — neither raising nor lowering them — and argued that it corresponds to the expected yield on newly created real capital. When the market rate set by banks fell below the natural rate, Wicksell argued, a cumulative process of inflation would follow; when it rose above, deflationary pressure resulted.

Modern usage

Contemporary economists, including Michael Woodford, revived the Wicksellian framework within New Keynesian models. In this setting the natural rate is the equilibrium real rate that would prevail under flexible prices. Central banks such as the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank attempt to estimate r* to gauge the stance of policy: a policy rate below the natural rate is stimulative, and above it is restrictive.

Estimation

The natural rate is not directly observable and must be estimated from models. A widely used approach is the Laubach–Williams model, which uses a Kalman filter to jointly estimate the natural rate, potential output, and trend growth from data on output, inflation, and interest rates. Estimates are subject to considerable uncertainty and are frequently revised. Many researchers have documented a long-run decline in r* across advanced economies since the 1980s, attributed to slower trend growth, demographic ageing, a global savings glut, and increased demand for safe assets.

Criticism

Critics note that the concept depends on model assumptions and that its imprecision makes it a fragile guide for policy. Post-Keynesian and Austrian economists have offered alternative interpretations of Wicksell's original idea.
