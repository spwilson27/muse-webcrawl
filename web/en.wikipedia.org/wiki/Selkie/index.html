Selkie

Selkies (also spelled silkies or selchies) are mythological beings in Scottish, Irish, and Faroese folklore capable of changing between seal and human form by shedding their sealskin. The word derives from Scots and Orcadian selch, meaning a seal. Tales are especially associated with the Northern Isles of Scotland — Orkney and Shetland — and with coastal communities elsewhere in the North Atlantic.

Folklore

The typical story tells of a female selkie who comes ashore and removes her sealskin; a human man steals and hides the skin, preventing her return to the sea, and she becomes his wife and bears his children, all the while longing for the ocean. Years later she (or one of her children) finds the hidden skin, and she immediately returns to the sea, sometimes visiting her children or being seen watching over them as a seal. Male selkies appear in other tales as handsome, melancholy figures who seek out dissatisfied women on land. Common motifs include the midsummer or every-ninth-night return of selkies to shore, selkie children born with webbed fingers or seal-like features, and the taboo against harming seals, which might be selkies in disguise.

Interpretations and modern culture

Folklorists have read selkie tales as reflections on marriage, captivity, homesickness, and the dangers of the sea; some older writers speculated that the stories preserve memories of encounters with Sámi or Inuit people arriving in skin boats wearing sealskin clothing. Selkies remain popular in modern fantasy and film, notably the Irish animated films The Secret of Kells creators' Song of the Sea (2014), the film The Secret of Roan Inish (1994), and numerous novels and songs retelling the selkie-wife story.
