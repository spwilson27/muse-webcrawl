Scheimpflug principle

The Scheimpflug principle is a geometric rule describing the orientation of the plane of focus of an optical system (such as a camera) when the lens plane is not parallel to the image plane. It states that the plane of the lens, the plane of the image (film or sensor), and the plane of sharp focus all intersect along a common line. The principle is named after Austrian army captain Theodor Scheimpflug (1865–1911), who described it in connection with aerial photography and photogrammetry, though related rules had been noted earlier by others.

Application

Tilting the lens relative to the sensor — possible with view cameras and tilt-shift lenses — rotates the plane of focus, allowing a sloped subject such as a landscape receding into the distance, a product on a table, or a tall building façade to be rendered sharp from near to far without a very small aperture. The reverse tilt produces a shallow wedge of focus exploited in the miniature-like "tilt-shift" effect.

Related concepts

The hinge rule describes how the focus plane rotates about an axis as focus is adjusted when the lens is tilted. Scheimpflug imaging is also used in ophthalmology, where rotating Scheimpflug cameras image the anterior segment of the eye, and in particle image velocimetry, where the principle keeps a laser light sheet in focus.
