False imprisonment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

False imprisonment is the unlawful restraint of a person against their will, without legal justification. In common-law systems it is both a tort (a civil wrong) and, in aggravated or statutory forms, a criminal offence. The restraint need not involve physical force or locks: threats, assertions of legal authority, or other means that cause a reasonable person to believe they cannot leave can suffice.

Elements

A claim of false imprisonment generally requires (1) an act intended to confine another person within fixed boundaries, (2) actual confinement of which the person is conscious or by which they are harmed, and (3) the absence of lawful privilege or consent. Even a brief detention can be actionable, and the victim need not attempt to escape or prove additional damage. Damages may include compensation for humiliation, distress, and lost time.

Defences and privileges

Police officers have privileges to arrest pursuant to warrants, probable cause, or statutory authority; detention beyond those limits can become false imprisonment. A widely recognised but narrow shopkeeper's privilege in the United States permits merchants to detain suspected shoplifters in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time for investigation. Consent, and lawful imprisonment following conviction, also defeat a claim.

Relation to other doctrines

False imprisonment differs from malicious prosecution, which concerns wrongful legal proceedings, and from kidnapping, which typically requires unlawful confinement coupled with movement (asportation) or aggravating intent. The writ of habeas corpus is the classic judicial remedy for testing the legality of a detention.
