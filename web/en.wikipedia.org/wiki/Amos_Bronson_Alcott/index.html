Amos Bronson Alcott (29 November 1799 – 4 March 1888) was an American teacher, writer, philosopher and reformer. As an educator, Alcott pioneered new ways of interacting with young students, focusing on a conversational style and avoiding traditional punishment. A leading figure of Transcendentalism, he was the father of novelist Louisa May Alcott, author of Little Women.

Education experiments

Largely self-educated, Alcott worked as a travelling salesman before turning to teaching. In 1834 he opened the Temple School in Boston, where, assisted by Elizabeth Peabody and Margaret Fuller, he taught through Socratic "conversations" intended to draw out children's inner spiritual knowledge. His book Conversations with Children on the Gospels (1836–37) provoked controversy and the school closed amid financial and public pressure — intensified when he admitted an African-American pupil and refused to dismiss her.

Fruitlands and Concord

In 1843 Alcott co-founded Fruitlands, a utopian agrarian community in Harvard, Massachusetts, whose members practised veganism, abstained from animal labour and wore linen garments. The commune collapsed within seven months, an experience fictionalised in his daughter Louisa's sketch "Transcendental Wild Oats". The family settled in Concord, Massachusetts, near Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau and Nathaniel Hawthorne. Alcott later founded the Concord School of Philosophy (1879), one of the first adult education institutions in the United States.

Beliefs and legacy

Alcott was an abolitionist, a supporter of women's rights and an advocate of educational reform. Though impractical in financial matters and supported for much of his life by Emerson's generosity and later by his daughter's literary earnings, he was regarded by contemporaries as a profound conversationalist and idealist, sometimes called the "American Pestalozzi".
