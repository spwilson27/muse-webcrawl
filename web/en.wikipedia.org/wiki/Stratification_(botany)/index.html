Stratification (botany)

In botany and horticulture, stratification is the process of treating seeds to break dormancy by simulating the natural conditions — typically a period of cold, moist conditions — that the seeds must experience before germination. Many temperate-climate species produce seeds that will not sprout until they have passed through a winter in damp soil.

Method

In artificial cold stratification, seeds are mixed with a moist medium such as sand, peat or vermiculite, or placed between layers of damp paper, and refrigerated at roughly 1–5 °C (34–41 °F) for a period ranging from a few weeks to several months, depending on the species. The seeds must remain moist but not waterlogged, and some species additionally require a warm period before or after the cold treatment.

Purpose

Cold stratification reduces physiological dormancy maintained by germination inhibitors such as abscisic acid in the seed coat and embryo, allowing gibberellins to promote germination when spring conditions arrive. Related techniques include scarification, in which the seed coat is physically or chemically weakened.
