Air-to-air missile

An air-to-air missile (AAM) is a missile fired from an aircraft for the purpose of destroying another aircraft. AAMs are typically powered by one or more rocket motors, sometimes preceded by a ramjet sustainer, and are guided to their targets by radar, infrared homing, or laser guidance systems. They form a principal armament of modern fighter aircraft and have largely replaced the gun as the primary weapon of air combat since the 1960s.

History

The first air-to-air missiles were developed during the Second World War. Germany's Ruhrstahl X-4, a wire-guided rocket-powered weapon, was tested late in the war but never entered operational service. In the postwar period, the United States produced the AIM-4 Falcon and the AIM-9 Sidewinder, while Britain fielded the Firestreak. The Sidewinder, which used a comparatively simple infrared seeker homing on the heat of an enemy aircraft's engines, proved exceptionally successful and remains in service in continually updated forms.

Early combat use in the 1958 Taiwan Strait Crisis and the Vietnam War exposed the limitations of first-generation missiles: they could only be fired from directly behind the target, had narrow engagement envelopes, and were easily defeated by flares, manoeuvre, or poor weather. Successive generations added all-aspect seekers, improved counter-countermeasures, and greater off-boresight capability.

Classification

Air-to-air missiles are commonly grouped by range:

  • Short-range or "dogfight" missiles (such as the AIM-9X Sidewinder, IRIS-T, and ASRAAM) are optimised for close combat within visual range, typically out to about 30 km, and use imaging infrared seekers with thrust-vectoring control for extreme agility.
  • Medium-range missiles (such as the AIM-120 AMRAAM and the European Meteor) engage targets beyond visual range (BVR), using inertial navigation with datalink updates and an active radar seeker for the terminal phase.
  • Long-range missiles (such as the AIM-54 Phoenix, retired, and Russia's R-37) are designed to attack large, high-value aircraft such as bombers and airborne early warning platforms at ranges exceeding 150 km.

Guidance

Infrared homing ("heat-seeking") missiles track the infrared signature of the target, chiefly the hot exhaust of its engines. Modern imaging-seeker missiles form a thermal picture of the aircraft and are far more resistant to flare decoys. Semi-active radar homing requires the launching aircraft to illuminate the target with its own radar throughout the engagement. Active radar homing missiles carry their own small radar transmitter and receiver, permitting "fire-and-forget" operation and allowing the shooter to manoeuvre or engage other targets after launch.

Propulsion and airframe

Most AAMs use solid-propellant rocket motors, often with reduced-smoke formulations. Some designs, notably the Meteor, employ a throttleable ducted rocket (ramjet) to sustain energy through the endgame, enlarging the "no-escape zone" within which a target cannot outrun the missile. Control is by aerodynamic fins, thrust vectoring, or both; typical missiles manoeuvre at 30–60 g.

Warheads and fuzing

Warheads are usually high-explosive fragmentation types, in some designs forming an expanding ring of metal fragments (the "continuous rod" pattern). Proximity fuzes — radar, laser, or infrared — detonate the warhead at the point of closest approach, since a direct hit is difficult to achieve.
