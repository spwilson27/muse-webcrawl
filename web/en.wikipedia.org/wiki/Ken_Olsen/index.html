Ken Olsen

Kenneth Harry Olsen (20 February 1926 – 6 February 2011) was an American engineer and businessman who co-founded Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) in 1957 with Harlan Anderson. Under Olsen's leadership, DEC became the second-largest computer company in the world and pioneered the minicomputer industry.

Early life and education

Olsen was born in Bridgeport, Connecticut, and grew up in Stratford. He served in the United States Navy near the end of World War II, then studied electrical engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), earning bachelor's and master's degrees. At MIT's Lincoln Laboratory he worked on early computers, including the Whirlwind project and the Memory Test Computer, and on the TX-2.

Digital Equipment Corporation

In 1957, Olsen and Anderson founded DEC in an old wool mill in Maynard, Massachusetts, with $70,000 in venture capital. The company initially made circuit modules before launching the PDP series of computers. The PDP-8 (1965) is generally regarded as the first commercially successful minicomputer, and the PDP-11 and VAX lines made DEC a dominant force in scientific, engineering, and eventually business computing. At its peak in the late 1980s, DEC employed over 120,000 people and had revenues exceeding $14 billion.

Olsen was famous for his management style and for engineering aphorisms. He is also often quoted (sometimes out of context) as saying in 1977 that "there is no reason for any individual to have a computer in his home" — a remark he later explained referred to computers controlling every aspect of a home, not personal computers.

Later years

Olsen stepped down as DEC's president in 1992 as the company struggled to adapt to the personal computer and workstation era. DEC was acquired by Compaq in 1998. Olsen remained an influential figure in the technology industry and in Christian philanthropy; he helped found Gordon-Conwell Theological Seminary. He died in Indianapolis in 2011.
