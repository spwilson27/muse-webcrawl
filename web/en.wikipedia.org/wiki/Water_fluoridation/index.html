Water fluoridation

Fluoride is added at controlled doses during municipal water treatment.

Water fluoridation is the controlled adjustment of fluoride to a public water supply solely to reduce tooth decay. Fluoridated water maintains fluoride levels effective for cavity prevention, whether naturally occurring or adjusted by adding a fluoride compound. Fluoridated water operates on tooth surfaces: in the mouth, it creates low levels of fluoride in saliva, which reduces the rate at which tooth enamel demineralizes and increases the rate of remineralization of early cavities.

History

The practice grew out of early 20th-century investigations by dentist Frederick McKay into "Colorado brown stain" — mottled enamel in communities whose water was naturally rich in fluoride — and the observation that those teeth were unusually resistant to decay. Researcher H. Trendley Dean of the U.S. Public Health Service quantified the relationship between fluoride, fluorosis and cavities in the 1930s and 1940s. The first deliberate fluoridation trial began in Grand Rapids, Michigan, in 1945, and reported large reductions in childhood tooth decay.

Practice and evidence

Fluoridation is practiced in varying degrees in countries including the United States, Canada, Australia, Ireland, Brazil and Singapore, while many European countries rely instead on fluoridated salt, milk or topical fluoride. The U.S. Public Health Service recommends a concentration of 0.7 milligrams per liter; the World Health Organization's guideline value is 1.5 mg/L. Reviews by bodies such as the CDC — which named water fluoridation one of the ten great public health achievements of the 20th century — find it reduces cavities in children and adults, though the size of the effect has narrowed in the era of fluoride toothpaste. Excess fluoride causes dental fluorosis and, at much higher natural levels, skeletal fluorosis.

Controversy

Fluoridation has been opposed since its introduction, on grounds ranging from health concerns to objections to mass medication and government compulsion; debate has continued with new studies and litigation. See water fluoridation in the United States.
