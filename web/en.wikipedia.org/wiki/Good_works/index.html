Good works

In Christian theology, good works are actions done in accordance with God's will — acts of charity, mercy, obedience, and devotion — whose place in salvation has been one of the central disputes between Christian traditions. The scriptural anchor texts include Ephesians 2:8–10 ("created in Christ Jesus for good works"), the Epistle of James ("faith without works is dead", James 2:26), and Matthew 25's parable of the sheep and the goats.

Catholic and Orthodox teaching

The Catholic Church teaches that justification is by grace through faith working in love, and that good works performed in a state of grace are truly meritorious for eternal life, a position defined at the Council of Trent (1547). Corporal and spiritual works of mercy — feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless, visiting the sick and imprisoned — are classic expressions. Eastern Orthodox teaching similarly stresses synergy between divine grace and human cooperation.

Protestant teaching

The Reformers, following Martin Luther, taught justification by faith alone (sola fide): good works cannot earn salvation but are the necessary fruit and evidence of a living faith — in Luther's phrase, faith is "a living, busy, active, mighty thing." Reformed and Lutheran confessions both reject the idea that works justify before God while insisting that they necessarily follow faith. Methodism added an emphasis on works of piety and mercy as means of grace.

Modern discussion

The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation recorded a broad consensus that salvation is by grace alone, received in faith, while affirming that good works follow justification — substantially narrowing the historic controversy.
