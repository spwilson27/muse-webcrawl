Oskar Wilhelm Fischinger (22 June 1900 – 31 January 1967) was a German-American abstract animator, filmmaker, and painter, notable for creating abstract musical animation many decades before the appearance of computer graphics and music videos. He created special effects for Fritz Lang's 1929 Woman in the Moon, one of the first sci-fi rocket movies, and he also worked on the influential abstract films of the "absolute film" movement in 1920s Germany.

Career

Born in Gelnhausen, Fischinger trained in music and worked as an engineer and organ builder before turning to film. In Munich and Berlin he produced the celebrated Studie films (1929–1934), abstract animations synchronised to popular and classical music, and advertising films in Gasparcolor such as the cigarette film Muratti Greift Ein. His Composition in Blue (1935) won prizes at Venice. With the Nazi regime hostile to "degenerate" abstract art, Fischinger emigrated to Hollywood in 1936, working briefly at Paramount and MGM (where he made An Optical Poem, 1938, set to Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2) and contributing — without credit — to the Toccata and Fugue sequence of Disney's Fantasia, from which he withdrew over the commercialisation of his design.

Later work and legacy

Supported by grants from the Guggenheim Foundation (through Hilla Rebay) and others, Fischinger made Motion Painting No. 1 (1947) to Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 3, painting on Plexiglas in oils and photographing each brushstroke — the film won the Grand Prix at the Brussels experimental film festival and is on the US National Film Registry. He also invented the Lumigraph, a device for performing colour-light displays by hand, and produced oil paintings related to his films. Fischinger's meticulous "visual music" profoundly influenced experimental animation, including the work of John and James Whitney, Jordan Belson and later motion-graphics design.
