Touch typing

Touch typing (also called touch type or touch keyboarding) is a style of typing in which the typist does not look at the keyboard, and each finger is responsible for a specific area of the keyboard. Most modern desktop keyboards have a raised dot or bar on the F and J keys so that touch typists can feel the correct position of the left and right index fingers on the home row.

Touch typing improves typing speed and accuracy significantly. A typist who uses the "hunt and peck" method must find each key visually, while a touch typist relies on muscle memory. The average professional typist reaches speeds of 50 to 80 words per minute (WPM), while some advanced typists work at 100 WPM or more.

History

Touch typing is associated with Frank Edward McGurrin, a court stenographer from Salt Lake City, Utah, who taught typing classes. In 1888, McGurrin, who was reportedly the only person using touch typing at the time, won a decisive victory over Louis Traub in a typing contest held in Cincinnati. The results were publicized in the press, and the interest generated by McGurrin's win helped popularize the new technique.

Home row

The home row is the middle row of a keyboard, where the fingers rest when not typing. On a QWERTY keyboard the home row keys are A S D F for the left hand and J K L ; for the right. In touch typing, each finger is assigned to a column of keys, and the typist returns the fingers to the home row after each keystroke.

Alternative layouts

Although QWERTY is the dominant layout, alternatives such as the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard and Colemak were designed with touch-typing ergonomics in mind, reducing finger travel and, their proponents argue, increasing speed and comfort.
