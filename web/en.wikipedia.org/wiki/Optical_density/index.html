Optical density

Optical density may refer to several related quantities. In spectroscopy and radiometry it usually means absorbance: the logarithmic measure of the attenuation of light through a material. In photographic and printing contexts it describes the opacity of a developed film or print. The term is also informally used for refractive index.

Absorbance

Absorbance A is defined as the base-10 logarithm of the ratio of incident to transmitted radiant power: A = −log₁₀(T), where T is transmittance. Under the Beer–Lambert law, absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing species and to the path length, making it a standard measurement in chemistry and in microbiology, where the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) is used to estimate bacterial cell density in liquid culture.

Photographic density

In photography, density is the logarithm of the opacity of a negative or print: regions of a negative that block more light have higher density. Densitometers measure it for process control, and characteristic (Hurter–Driffield) curves plot density against the logarithm of exposure.

Usage note

Because "optical density" has been used inconsistently, standards bodies recommend the unambiguous terms absorbance (for the logarithmic attenuation) and refractive index. Numerical values also depend on measurement geometry, since scattering as well as absorption can attenuate transmitted light.
