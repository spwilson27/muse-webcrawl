Hill figure

A hill figure is a large visual representation created by cutting into a steep hillside and revealing the underlying geology, usually chalk. Hill figures are most common in southern England, where they are cut into the chalk downlands, but the form also exists elsewhere in the world, including the geoglyphs of South America such as the Nazca Lines (which are made in desert pavement rather than hillsides).

The Uffington White Horse, Oxfordshire

History in Britain

The oldest and most famous British hill figure is the Uffington White Horse in Oxfordshire, dated by optically stimulated luminescence to the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age. The Cerne Abbas Giant in Dorset and the Long Man of Wilmington in Sussex were long thought to be ancient, although dating evidence and documentary records suggest origins ranging from the prehistoric period to the seventeenth century; the Cerne Giant's first unambiguous records are early modern. Many other figures — regimental badges at Fovant, crosses, and white horses across Wiltshire and neighbouring counties — were cut from the eighteenth century onward.

Creation and maintenance

Figures are made by removing turf and topsoil to expose the pale chalk beneath, and they survive only while volunteers periodically clean and re-chalk them, a process traditionally called "scouring". Figures that are neglected quickly grass over and vanish; several recorded figures have been lost in this way, while others have been restored by local communities. During the Second World War a number of figures were camouflaged or allowed to grass over to deny landmarks to enemy aircraft.
