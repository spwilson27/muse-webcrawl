Thomas Hart Benton (painter)

Thomas Hart Benton (April 15, 1889 – January 19, 1975) was an American painter and muralist. Along with Grant Wood and John Steuart Curry, he was at the forefront of the Regionalist art movement. His fluid, sculpted figures and scenes of everyday life in the American Midwest made him one of the most popular — and controversial — American artists of the 1930s.

Early life and training

Benton was born in Neosho, Missouri, into a family of politicians; his great-uncle, for whom he was named, was the Missouri senator Thomas Hart Benton. He studied at the Art Institute of Chicago and in Paris at the Académie Julian, experimenting with modernist styles before rejecting European abstraction in favour of representational American subjects.

Regionalism

During the 1930s Benton became the most visible spokesman of Regionalism, arguing that art should depict American life rather than imitate European avant-gardes. His murals include the America Today cycle (1930–31, now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art), murals for the 1933 Century of Progress exposition in Chicago (the Indiana Murals), and the Missouri State Capitol mural A Social History of the State of Missouri (1936).

Benton taught at the Art Students League of New York and later at the Kansas City Art Institute; his most famous pupil was Jackson Pollock, whose later abstract style Benton publicly disliked. Late commissions included the Truman Library mural Independence and the Opening of the West (1961) and a mural of Abraham Lincoln for Lincoln University.

Death and legacy

Benton died in his Kansas City studio in 1975, shortly after completing a mural for Nashville's Country Music Hall of Fame. His home and studio are preserved as the Thomas Hart Benton Home and Studio State Historic Site.
