Haldane effect

The Haldane effect is a property of haemoglobin first described by the Scottish physiologist John Scott Haldane: oxygenation of the blood in the lungs promotes the unloading of carbon dioxide, while deoxygenation of blood in the tissues increases its ability to carry carbon dioxide. It is the reciprocal counterpart of the Bohr effect.

Mechanism

When haemoglobin releases oxygen, its affinity for hydrogen ions increases and it more readily binds carbon dioxide directly as carbamino compounds. Deoxyhaemoglobin thus acts as a better buffer for the hydrogen ions released when carbon dioxide is converted to bicarbonate, facilitating CO2 uptake in the tissues. In the lungs, oxygen binding reverses these changes, driving carbon dioxide out of bicarbonate and carbamino stores for exhalation. The Haldane effect accounts for roughly half of carbon dioxide exchange under typical conditions and is clinically important in patients with lung disease receiving supplemental oxygen.
