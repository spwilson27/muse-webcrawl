Synecdoche, New York

Directed by: Charlie Kaufman
Written by: Charlie Kaufman
Starring: Philip Seymour Hoffman, Samantha Morton, Michelle Williams, Catherine Keener
Release date: 2008
Running time: 124 minutes

Synecdoche, New York is a 2008 American postmodern psychological drama film written and directed by Charlie Kaufman in his directorial debut. It stars Philip Seymour Hoffman as Caden Cotard, a theater director who, after receiving a MacArthur "Genius" grant, sets out to create a sprawling, hyper-realistic theatrical production of his own life inside a giant warehouse in New York City — hiring actors to play himself and everyone around him in a work that expands for decades and gradually swallows his entire existence.

Plot

Caden Cotard, a theater director in Schenectady, New York, suffers from a mounting catalogue of ailments and a disintegrating marriage: his artist wife Adele leaves for Berlin with their daughter Olive. Awarded a MacArthur Fellowship, Caden rents an enormous warehouse and begins building a life-size replica of New York, casting a doppelgänger (Sammy) to play himself and directing his cast to live out improvised lives drawn from his own. As years pass, the production grows recursively — warehouses within warehouses — while Caden's health, relationships, and sense of self erode. The play is never performed; Caden dies inside it, still taking direction through an earpiece.

Title

The title is a play on "Schenectady", the city where the story begins, and synecdoche, the literary trope in which a part of something represents the whole — a figure that describes Caden's production, in which a constructed replica stands in for a life.

Reception

The film premiered at the 2008 Cannes Film Festival. Initial reviews were divided — some critics found it impenetrable and self-indulgent, others a masterpiece — but its reputation rose steadily: Roger Ebert named it the best film of the 2000s decade, and it has appeared on numerous lists of the greatest films of the 21st century. It was a commercial failure on release, grossing only a fraction of its roughly $20 million budget.
