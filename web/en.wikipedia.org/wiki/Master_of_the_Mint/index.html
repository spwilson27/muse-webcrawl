Master of the Mint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Master of the Mint was an important office in the governments of Scotland and England, and later of Great Britain and the United Kingdom, responsible for the coinage and for the operation of the royal mints. The Master was usually a senior politician or courtier who contracted with the Crown to produce coin to the required standard at a set price, profiting from the difference; under him the Warden and other officers conducted the detailed work.

History

Distinct masters operated the mints in the Tower of London and in Edinburgh in the later middle ages. After the Union of the Crowns, the Wardenship and Mastership of the English Mint were frequently combined. The office became especially significant during recoinages, notably the Great Recoinage of 1696–98, when the Master, in succession to the warden Isaac Newton, oversaw the replacement of the clipped hammered silver coinage with milled coin. Newton himself served as Warden from 1696 and as Master from 1699 until his death in 1727 — unusually, taking the work intensely seriously: he pursued counterfeiters vigorously and supervised output with scientific precision.

The office was reorganised by the Coinage Act 1870, which made the Chancellor of the Exchequer Master of the Mint ex officio, a provision preserved in the Coinage Act 1971. The working head of the Royal Mint is accordingly styled Deputy Master (and Comptroller) of the Mint, who serves as its chief executive. The Chancellor remains titular Master to this day.

Scottish office

Scotland had its own Master of the Mint (later styled Master of the Royal Mint at Edinburgh), distinct until the Union of 1707, after which Scottish coinage was recoined to English standards and the Edinburgh mint gradually ceased production.
