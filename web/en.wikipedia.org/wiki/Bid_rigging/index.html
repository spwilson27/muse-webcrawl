Bid rigging

Bid rigging is a form of collusive fraud in which businesses or individuals conspire to determine in advance the winner of a bidding process, defeating the purpose of competitive tendering. It is a form of price fixing and market allocation, and is illegal in most legal systems as a violation of competition (antitrust) law.

[Image: Sealed bid envelopes being opened at a procurement tender]

Forms

Common schemes include bid rotation, in which conspirators take turns submitting the winning bid; complementary (cover) bidding, in which non-winning participants submit deliberately high or defective bids; bid suppression, in which some conspirators refrain from bidding; and market allocation, in which competitors divide territories or customers among themselves.

Effects and detection

Bid rigging raises the prices paid by purchasers—often governments procuring construction, supplies, or services—and reduces quality and innovation. Warning signs include identical errors in bid documents, regular rotation of winners, large gaps between winning and losing bids, and losing bidders later appearing as subcontractors to the winner.

Legal treatment

In the United States, bid rigging is a per se violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Act and a felony punishable by fines and imprisonment; the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice prosecutes such conspiracies, especially in public construction and auto parts. The OECD has published guidelines for fighting bid rigging in public procurement, and leniency programs encourage cartel members to self-report in exchange for reduced penalties.
