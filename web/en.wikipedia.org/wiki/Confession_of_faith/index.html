A confession of faith, or simply a confession, is a statement of the beliefs shared by a religious community, often a Christian denomination. In the Protestant tradition, confessions are formal, systematic documents that summarise doctrine — on Scripture, God, salvation, the church, and the sacraments — and serve as standards for teaching, ordination, and membership. The term is sometimes used interchangeably with creed, though creeds (such as the Nicene Creed) are typically shorter, older, and liturgical in use.

History

The Reformation produced a proliferation of confessions as churches defined themselves against Rome and each other. The Lutheran Augsburg Confession (1530), presented to the Emperor Charles V, became the foundational document of Lutheranism. Reformed churches produced the Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism (1563), and the Canons of Dort (1619). The Church of England adopted the Thirty-nine Articles. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), drawn up during the English Civil War, remains the doctrinal standard of Presbyterian churches worldwide, and Baptists adapted it in the Second London Baptist Confession (1677/1689).

Function

  • To state a community's interpretation of Scripture in summary form
  • To provide a test of orthodoxy for ministers and teachers
  • To teach converts and members the essentials of the faith
  • To distinguish one denomination's position from others

Most confessional churches describe their confessions as subordinate standards, authoritative only insofar as they agree with Scripture, which remains the supreme standard.
