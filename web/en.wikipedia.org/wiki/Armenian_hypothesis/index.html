Armenian hypothesis

[Image: Armenian hypothesis — Map of the Armenian Highlands, the proposed Proto-Indo-European homeland]

The Armenian hypothesis is a theory of the Proto-Indo-European homeland that locates the Urheimat in the Armenian Highlands, in eastern Anatolia and the southern Caucasus. It was developed in the early 1980s by the Georgian and Russian scholars Tamaz V. Gamkrelidze and Vyacheslav V. Ivanov, and is closely associated with the glottalic theory of Proto-Indo-European consonants.

Argument

Gamkrelidze and Ivanov argued on linguistic grounds — including loanwords between early Indo-European and the Kartvelian and Semitic languages, and vocabulary for the mountain flora, fauna and technologies of the region — that Proto-Indo-European was spoken in the Armenian Highlands around the 4th millennium BC. From there, Indo-European languages would have spread westward into Anatolia and Greece, and eastward and northward onto the steppe, making the migration to Europe a secondary, steppe-mediated movement.

Reception

The hypothesis has received support from some archaeologists who associate the homeland with Neolithic and Bronze Age cultures south of the Caucasus, and recent ancient-DNA studies locating a key ancestral population in the Armenian Highlands region for the later steppe pastoralists have revived interest in a version of the proposal. However, the mainstream remains the Kurgan (steppe) hypothesis for the late Proto-Indo-European homeland, with Greek, Armenian and Indo-Iranian possibly diverging from an earlier stage south of the Caucasus — a compromise sometimes called the "steppe" vs "Armenian" or Indo-Anatolian model.
