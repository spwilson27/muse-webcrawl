Cross-examination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, cross-examination is the interrogation of a witness called by one's opponent — the counterpart to direct examination by the calling party. It tests the witness's perception, memory, narration and sincerity, elicits facts favourable to the cross-examiner, and impeaches credibility (by prior inconsistent statements, bias, convictions or incapacity). Wigmore's Evidence treatise called it "beyond any doubt the greatest legal engine ever invented for the discovery of truth" — praise quoted ever since, alongside research on confident error and the risks of aggressive cross of truthful witnesses.

Rules and rights

Cross-examination is constitutionally anchored in the United States by the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause (Crawford v. Washington, 2004, re-founded hearsay doctrine on it). Procedural rules typically allow leading questions on cross (unlike direct), limit scope differently across systems (the US federal rule restricts it principally to the subject matter of direct and credibility), and empower judges to protect vulnerable witnesses — with special measures (screens, video links, pre-recorded cross, restrictions on personal cross by defendants in sexual cases) now common in many jurisdictions. In inquisitorial systems, judge-led questioning reduces the adversarial cross's centrality, one of comparative procedure's clearest divides.
