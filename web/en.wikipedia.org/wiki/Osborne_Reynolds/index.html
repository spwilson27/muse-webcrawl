Osborne Reynolds FRS (23 August 1842 – 21 February 1912) was an Irish-born British innovator in the understanding of fluid dynamics. Separately, his studies of heat transfer between solids and fluids brought improvements in boiler and condenser design. He spent his entire career at what is now the University of Manchester.

Life

Born in Belfast, Reynolds moved with his family to Dedham, Essex, where his father was a school headmaster and curate, and he inherited a strong interest in mechanics: several generations of his family were connected with the sea and with patents for agricultural machinery. After apprenticeship with a shipbuilder and studies at Queens' College, Cambridge, where he graduated as seventh wrangler in 1867, he was appointed in 1868 to the new chair of engineering at Owens College, Manchester — one of the first professors of engineering in Britain — a post he held until retirement in 1905.

Fluid mechanics

In his celebrated 1883 paper, Reynolds described experiments in which a filament of dye injected into water flowing through a glass tube remained straight at low speeds (laminar flow) but broke into turbulent eddies above a critical speed. He showed that the transition depends on a single dimensionless quantity — now the Reynolds number, Re = ρvL/μ — comparing inertial and viscous forces. In 1895 he published the time-averaging treatment of the Navier–Stokes equations for turbulent flow that yields the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations and the concept of Reynolds stresses; he also contributed to lubrication theory (1886), explaining the pressure generation in journal bearings, and developed the Reynolds analogy between heat transfer and skin friction. His name is also attached to work on the rain, the behaviour of granular materials (dilatancy), and tidal estuary modelling.
