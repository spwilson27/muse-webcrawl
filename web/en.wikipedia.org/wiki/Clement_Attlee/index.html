Clement Attlee

Clement Richard Attlee, 1st Earl Attlee (3 January 1883 – 8 October 1967), was a British statesman of the Labour Party who served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1945 to 1951, succeeding Winston Churchill in the final weeks of the Second World War. A modest, uncharismatic-seeming man — Churchill is supposed to have called him "a modest man with much to be modest about", an attribution Attlee himself cheerfully repeated — he led Labour to its landslide victory of 1945 and presided over the most reforming British government of the twentieth century: the creation of the National Health Service and the modern welfare state, a great programme of nationalisation, the granting of independence to India, Pakistan, Burma and Ceylon and the end of the British Mandate in Palestine, the founding of NATO, and Britain's secret decision to build its own atomic bomb. He had served through the war as deputy prime minister in Churchill's coalition, and replaced him at the Potsdam Conference midway through its sessions, when the election results arrived on 26 July 1945.

Early life

Attlee was born into a prosperous upper-middle-class family in Putney, educated at Haileybury and University College, Oxford, and called to the bar in 1906. His politics were made in the East End of London: managing a boys' club in Stepney and working for Toynbee Hall converted the patriotic conservative into a socialist; he joined the Independent Labour Party in 1908, lectured at the London School of Economics, and became mayor of Stepney in 1919. In the First World War he served as an infantry officer at Gallipoli — the last man of his battalion evacuated from Suvla Bay — and in Mesopotamia and France, ending as a major; unlike many of his party he never doubted the war's necessity. In 1922 he entered Parliament for Limehouse, in the heart of the East End he would represent for twenty-eight years.

Leader of the opposition and wartime deputy

After Labour's collapse in 1931 Attlee was one of the few senior figures left standing and became leader in 1935, beating Herbert Morrison in a contest few thought mattered much. Through the 1930s he moved Labour away from pacifism toward rearmament and resistance to appeasement. In May 1940 he and Arthur Greenwood refused to serve under Neville Chamberlain, precipitating Churchill's succession; Attlee then ran the home front as the coalition's indispensable second figure — Lord Privy Seal, then secretary for the dominions, and from 1942 deputy prime minister — chairing the cabinet committees that kept civil government turning while Churchill waged war. He attended Potsdam in July 1945 as an observer alongside Churchill, under a pact that whichever of them won the election would take over — and returned on 28 July as prime minister.

Premiership (1945–1951)

The Attlee government's domestic record was built on the Beveridge Report and wartime promises: the National Health Service (launched 5 July 1948 under Aneurin Bevan) providing free care at the point of use; the National Insurance Act completing universal social security; the nationalisation of the Bank of England, coal, railways, electricity, gas, and (contentiously) steel; a million new homes, mostly council houses; and the Town and Country Planning and New Towns Acts. Abroad, Ernest Bevin as foreign secretary bound Britain to the Western alliance — the Brussels Treaty, the Marshall Plan's administration, the Berlin airlift, NATO (1949) — while the empire was liquidated with historic speed: independence and partition for India and Pakistan (August 1947, under Mountbatten), Burma and Ceylon (1948), and withdrawal from Palestine (1948). In 1950–51 Britain rearmed for the Korean War, imposing NHS charges that split the cabinet (Bevan and Harold Wilson resigned); exhausted and reduced to a wafer-thin majority in 1950, Labour narrowly lost the election of October 1951 despite winning the popular vote. Attlee led the party until 1955, then took an earldom — Earl Attlee — and a seat in the Lords.

Legacy

Attlee's reputation has risen steadily: historians and even political opponents frequently rank him among the greatest British prime ministers, for the durability of his government's creations (the NHS above all), the orderliness of decolonisation compared with France's colonial wars, and a style of leadership — committee-minded, self-effacing, ruthless when required — that got more done than Churchill's oratory did in peacetime. He died in 1967 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
