Replication (statistics)

In engineering, science, and statistics, replication is the repetition of an experimental condition so that the variability associated with the phenomenon can be estimated. Replication is one of the three core principles of the design of experiments identified by Ronald Fisher, alongside randomisation and local control (blocking).

Purpose

Repeating a treatment on multiple independent experimental units allows the experimenter to estimate experimental error. Without an estimate of error, there is no basis for judging whether an observed difference between treatments is larger than would be expected by chance. Replication also increases the precision of treatment comparisons: the standard error of a mean falls with the square root of the number of replicates.

Replication versus repeated measurement

Replication must be distinguished from repeated measurement. Taking several readings on the same unit — for example, measuring one leaf three times — does not provide independent replicates of a treatment applied to whole plants; treating such readings as replicates is known as pseudoreplication, a common design flaw identified by Stuart Hurlbert in 1984, particularly in ecological field experiments.

Reproducibility

In the broader scientific literature, "replication" also describes attempts by independent researchers to repeat a published study to check whether its findings hold. Failures of high-profile results to replicate in psychology, medicine and economics prompted discussion of a "replication crisis" in the 2010s, driven by publication bias, low statistical power and questionable research practices.
