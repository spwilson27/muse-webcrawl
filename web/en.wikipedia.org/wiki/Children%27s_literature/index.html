Children's literature or juvenile literature includes stories, books, magazines and poems written for or enjoyed by children. Modern children's literature is classified by age group — picture books for the youngest readers, early readers, chapter books, middle-grade and young adult fiction — and ranges from folk and fairy tales to novels, poetry and non-fiction.

History

Early books for children were largely didactic, mixing instruction with religion and manners. John Newbery's A Little Pretty Pocket-Book (1744) is often regarded as the first book published specifically for children's entertainment. The nineteenth century saw a golden age with Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), and works by Robert Louis Stevenson, Rudyard Kipling and Beatrix Potter followed. The twentieth century added classics from A. A. Milne, C. S. Lewis, Roald Dahl and Dr. Seuss, and the century ended with the global success of J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter series.

Themes and scholarship

Children's books often centre on growth, friendship, imagination and moral development. The field is studied academically, with its own journals, and honoured by prizes such as the Newbery and Caldecott Medals in the United States, the Carnegie Medal in Britain, and the international Hans Christian Andersen Award.
