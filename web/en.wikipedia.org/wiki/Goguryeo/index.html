Goguryeo (Korean: 고구려) was a Korean kingdom located in the northern and central parts of the Korean Peninsula and the southern and central parts of Manchuria. Along with Baekje and Silla it formed the Three Kingdoms of Korea. At its height in the fifth and sixth centuries it was one of the great powers of East Asia, controlling a vast territory stretching from the Liao River to the Han River.

History

Tradition dates Goguryeo's founding to 37 BCE by Jumong, a prince from Buyeo. The kingdom expanded steadily, reaching its greatest extent under King Gwanggaeto the Great (r. 391–413) and his son Jangsu, who moved the capital to Pyongyang in 427. Goguryeo famously repelled invasions by the Chinese Sui dynasty — contributing to Sui's collapse in 618 — and by Tang China, including the victory of the general Eulji Mundeok at the Battle of Salsu (612). Weakened by internal succession struggles, it finally fell in 668 to a combined Tang–Silla army; much of its territory was later claimed by Balhae, and the Korean name "Goryeo" (from which "Korea" derives) preserves its name.

Culture and legacy

Goguryeo adopted Buddhism in 372 and developed a distinctive culture whose most celebrated remains are its fortress cities and mural-painted tombs, now UNESCO World Heritage Sites in both North Korea and China. The kingdom's history has become a point of modern dispute between Korean and Chinese historiography (the "Northeast Project" controversy), reflecting its importance to national identity in both countries.
