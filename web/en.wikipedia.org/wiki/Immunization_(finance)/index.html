Immunization (finance)

In finance, immunization or duration immunization is a strategy that ensures that a change in interest rates will not affect the value of a portfolio. Similarly to the vocabulary of biology, a portfolio is said to be "immunized" against interest rate risk. Immunization can be accomplished by cash flow matching, duration matching, or convexity matching, and is widely used by insurance companies, pension funds and banks to protect the value of assets earmarked to meet future liabilities.

History and theory

The strategy was introduced by the British actuary Frank Redington in 1952. His immunization conditions require that the present value of assets equals the present value of liabilities, that the duration (first derivative of value with respect to the interest rate) of assets and liabilities is matched, and that the convexity of the assets exceeds that of the liabilities. Under these conditions, small parallel shifts in the term structure of interest rates leave the surplus of assets over liabilities approximately unchanged, because gains from reinvesting cash flows at higher rates offset the fall in market values, and vice versa.

Techniques

Cash flow matching buys bonds whose coupons and maturities exactly cover each liability payment — a perfectly immunizing but restrictive approach. Duration matching is more flexible, using portfolios whose Macaulay or modified duration equals that of the liabilities, rebalanced over time as durations drift. Contingent immunization combines active management with an immunization fallback if the portfolio value falls to a trigger level.

Limitations

Classical immunization protects only against small, parallel shifts in a flat yield curve; non-parallel shifts, credit risk and embedded options require more sophisticated modelling or dedicated portfolios.
