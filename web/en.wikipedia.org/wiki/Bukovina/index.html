Bukovina

Bukovina (Romanian: Bucovina; Ukrainian: Буковина, Bukovyna; German: Buchenland) is a historical region in Eastern Europe, located on the northern slopes of the central Eastern Carpathians and the adjoining plains. Its name derives from the Slavic word buk, meaning beech tree. The region is today divided between Romania (the south, forming part of Suceava County) and Ukraine (the north, Chernivtsi Oblast).

History

In the Middle Ages the territory formed part of the Principality of Moldavia. In 1774–75 the Habsburg Monarchy annexed the region from Moldavia, administering it first as part of Galicia and, from 1849, as a separate crown land, the Duchy of Bukovina, within Cisleithanian Austria. Its capital was Czernowitz (now Chernivtsi), which grew into a famously multicultural city with Romanian, Ukrainian (Ruthenian), German, Jewish, Polish and Armenian communities, and a German-language university founded in 1875.

After the First World War, Bukovina was united with the Kingdom of Romania in 1918. In June 1940, under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the Soviet Union occupied Northern Bukovina (along with Bessarabia); Romania regained it in 1941, and the USSR retook it in 1944. The northern part was incorporated into the Ukrainian SSR, a division confirmed by the 1947 Paris Peace Treaties and retained after Ukrainian independence in 1991.

Culture

Bukovina is noted for the painted monasteries of southern Bukovina, including Voroneț, Sucevița and Moldovița, built in the 15th and 16th centuries under Stephen the Great and Petru Rareș and listed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The region's folk traditions include painted Easter eggs (pysanky) and distinctive embroidered costume.
