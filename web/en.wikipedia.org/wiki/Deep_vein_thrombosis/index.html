Deep vein thrombosis

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the formation of a blood clot (thrombus) in a deep vein, most commonly in the legs or pelvis. Together with pulmonary embolism, into which a clot may break off and travel to the lungs, it forms venous thromboembolism (VTE).

Deep vein thrombosis
Specialty: Haematology, vascular medicine
Symptoms: Leg pain, swelling, redness; may be asymptomatic
Risk factors: Surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, thrombophilia
Diagnosis: Ultrasound, D-dimer, Wells score
Treatment: Anticoagulation

Causes

Mechanisms are summarised by Virchow's triad: venous stasis (long travel, bed rest, paralysis), endothelial injury (surgery, trauma, catheters) and hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, oestrogen therapy, inherited disorders such as factor V Leiden). Provoked DVT follows a transient factor; unprovoked DVT has none identified.

Diagnosis and treatment

Clinical probability is assessed with the Wells score; D-dimer testing can exclude DVT in low-probability patients, and compression ultrasonography is the standard imaging test. Treatment is anticoagulation—historically heparin then warfarin, now often direct oral anticoagulants such as apixaban or rivaroxaban—for at least three months, with thrombolysis or thrombectomy in selected severe cases (e.g. phlegmasia cerulea dolens). Complications include pulmonary embolism and post-thrombotic syndrome. Prevention uses early mobilisation, compression stockings and prophylactic anticoagulants in hospital.
