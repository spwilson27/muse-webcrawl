Forward converter

TypeSwitched-mode power supply topology
IsolationGalvanic, via transformer
Derived fromBuck converter
Typical powerTens to hundreds of watts

The forward converter is a DC/DC converter that uses a transformer to increase or decrease the output voltage (relative to the input) and provide galvanic isolation. Although it superficially resembles the flyback converter, it operates on a different principle: energy is transferred through the transformer while the switch is on, rather than being stored in the core during the on-period.

Operation

When the transistor switch conducts, the input voltage is applied to the primary winding and energy flows directly to the secondary, where it is rectified and filtered by an inductor-capacitor output stage much like that of a buck converter. A demagnetizing (reset) winding or other reset mechanism returns the core flux to its starting point during the off-period to prevent saturation.

Comparison with flyback

Because energy is not stored in the transformer, the forward converter's transformer is smaller than a flyback's for the same power, and its output ripple is lower. The additional output inductor and reset circuitry, however, make it somewhat more complex. Forward converters are common in offline power supplies and telecommunications equipment in roughly the 100–200 W range.

Variants

Common variants include the two-switch forward converter, which simplifies core reset and clamps switch voltage, and active-clamp forward designs that improve efficiency at higher switching frequencies.
