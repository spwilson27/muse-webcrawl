Somatic cell nuclear transfer

In genetics and developmental biology, somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) is a laboratory strategy for creating a viable embryo from a body cell and an egg cell. The technique consists of taking a denucleated oocyte and implanting a donor nucleus from a somatic cell.

SCNT
TypeCloning technique
Famous resultDolly the sheep (1996)
UsesReproductive and therapeutic cloning, stem cells

Procedure

The nucleus is removed from an unfertilised egg; a somatic cell nucleus — or the whole cell, fused by electric pulse — is introduced. The reconstructed cell is stimulated to divide, forming a blastocyst genetically nearly identical to the donor (mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg).

History

Nuclear transfer was pioneered in amphibians by Robert Briggs, Thomas King and John Gurdon in the 1950s–60s. In 1996 Ian Wilmut's team at the Roslin Institute produced Dolly, the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell. In 2013, human embryonic stem cells were derived via SCNT.

Applications and ethics

Applications include producing transgenic livestock, conserving endangered species, and patient-matched stem cells. Efficiency is low and cloned animals show health problems, raising ethical and welfare concerns; human reproductive cloning is widely prohibited.
