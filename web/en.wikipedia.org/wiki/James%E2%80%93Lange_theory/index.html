James–Lange theory

The James–Lange theory is a hypothesis on the origin and nature of emotions, independently developed by the American psychologist William James and the Danish physiologist Carl Lange in the 1880s. It proposes that emotions are the result of physiological changes in the body: an external stimulus triggers a bodily response (such as trembling, sweating or a racing heart), and the perception of that response is the emotion.

Formulation

James famously reversed the common-sense sequence: instead of "we feel sorry because we cry," he argued "we feel sorry because we cry" — that is, we meet a bear, run away, and then interpret our bodily arousal as fear. Lange focused specifically on the vasomotor system, linking emotion to changes in blood circulation.

Criticism

The theory was challenged by the Cannon–Bard theory, which held that physiological arousal and emotional experience occur simultaneously and independently. Walter Cannon argued that visceral changes are too slow and too uniform to account for the speed and variety of emotions. Later appraisal and two-factor theories, such as the Schachter–Singer theory, incorporated elements of both views, emphasising cognitive interpretation of arousal.
