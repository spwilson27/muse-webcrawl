Logistic map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The logistic map is a polynomial mapping (equivalently, a recurrence relation) of degree 2, often cited as an archetypal example of how complex, chaotic behaviour can arise from very simple nonlinear dynamical equations. It is written

xn+1 = r xn(1 − xn)

where xn is a number between 0 and 1 representing a population as a fraction of its maximum possible size, and r is a positive parameter representing the combined rate of reproduction and starvation. The map was introduced as a discrete-time demographic model analogous to the logistic equation of Pierre François Verhulst, and was popularised by the biologist Robert May in a celebrated 1976 paper in Nature.

Behaviour

As r increases, the long-term behaviour of the map changes dramatically. For 0 < r < 1 the population dies out; for 1 < r < 3 it settles to a single stable value. At r = 3 the fixed point loses stability and the population oscillates between two values (a period-2 cycle); further increases split the cycle into periods 4, 8, 16, and so on—a cascade of period-doubling bifurcations that accumulates at r ≈ 3.56995, beyond which the map is chaotic for most values, with islands of periodic behaviour such as a period-3 window near r ≈ 3.83. Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered that the ratios of successive bifurcation intervals approach a universal constant, δ ≈ 4.6692, which appears in many unrelated systems—the first hint of universality in chaos theory. In the chaotic regime, iterated points fill intervals in intricate patterns displayed in the famous bifurcation diagram, whose self-similar structure made the logistic map one of the emblematic objects of nonlinear dynamics.
