Scythians

RegionPontic–Caspian steppe
Periodc. 9th century BC – 3rd century AD
LanguagesScythian (Iranian)
Known forHorse archery, kurgan burials, gold art

The Scythians (also called Scyth, Saka, or Sacae) were an ancient group of Iranian peoples famous as equestrian nomads who dominated the Pontic steppe — the vast grasslands from modern Ukraine and southern Russia to Central Asia — from about the 9th century BC until the early centuries AD. The Greeks used "Scythian" broadly for the peoples of this region; the related Saka occupied its eastern parts.

Society and warfare

Scythian society was organised in tribes under kings, with a warrior aristocracy. Masters of mounted archery with the composite bow, they fought with short swords (akinakes), spears and distinctive arrowheads, and used tactics of feigned retreat. Our richest literary source is Herodotus, whose Histories describe their customs, including elaborate royal funerals, hemp vapour baths, and scalping of enemies — accounts partly confirmed by archaeology.

Art and archaeology

The Scythians left no writings of their own; they are known from grave mounds (kurgans) such as those at Arzhan, Pazyryk and Solokha, which preserved frozen tombs, tattooed bodies, horse sacrifices and spectacular gold work in the "animal style" — stags, panthers and griffins rendered with extraordinary dynamism. Some of the frozen Pazyryk burials preserve the world's oldest surviving pile carpet.

Decline

From the 3rd century BC the western Scythians were progressively displaced by the related Sarmatians; a reduced Scythian kingdom survived in Crimea until the Gothic invasions of the 3rd century AD.
