Indictment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An indictment is a formal accusation that a person has committed a crime. In jurisdictions retaining them (notably the United States federal system and some states), a grand jury issues the indictment ("true bill") if it finds probable cause; the Fifth Amendment makes grand-jury indictment a federal constitutional right for capital and "infamous" crimes (a right famously not incorporated against the states, Hurtado, 1884). The indictment names the accused, states the offences and their essential facts, and begins formal prosecution, followed by arraignment where the plea is entered.

Function and criticism

The grand jury's screening role is defended as a citizen buffer between state and accused and criticised as a prosecutorial instrument — proceedings are secret, one-sided (no judge, defence or cross-examination in most jurisdictions), and indictments almost always follow the prosecutor's request (prompting Judge Sol Wachtler's 1985 quip that a prosecutor could persuade a grand jury to "indict a ham sandwich"). Alternatives — prosecution by information after a preliminary hearing before a judge — prevail in many states and most other countries, which abolished grand juries (England in 1933) while retaining committal proceedings or judicial investigation. Sealed indictments, superseding indictments and high-profile no-bills (as in police-shooting cases) periodically return the institution to political controversy.
