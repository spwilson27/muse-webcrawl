Wandering Jew

The Wandering Jew (occasionally referred to as the Eternal Jew, German: der Ewige Jude) is a mythical immortal man whose legend began to spread in Europe in the 13th century. In the original legend, a Jew who taunted Jesus on the way to the Crucifixion was cursed to walk the earth until the Second Coming. The figure became a recurrent subject of Christian art, folklore, literature, and — in the 19th and 20th centuries — antisemitic propaganda.

Origins

The earliest written account appears in Roger of Wendover's chronicle Flores historiarum (c. 1228), which tells of an Armenian archbishop visiting England who described a man called Cartaphilus: once Pontius Pilate's doorkeeper, he struck Jesus and was told "I go, but thou shalt wait until I return." Versions proliferated in chapbooks across Europe, especially after a popular German pamphlet of 1602, in which the figure, named Ahasuerus, was seen in Hamburg in 1542.

In literature and art

The Wandering Jew appears in works by writers including Percy Bysshe Shelley, Eugène Sue (whose 1844 novel Le Juif errant was an international sensation), Alexandre Dumas, and Mark Twain, and in Gustave Doré's celebrated engravings (1856). Philosophers and poets used the figure as a symbol of exile, guilt, and mankind's restless quest.

Antisemitic use

In the 19th century the legend was increasingly turned to antisemitic ends, portraying the Jew as rootless and accursed. Nazi propaganda revived the motif in the 1937 Munich exhibition "Der ewige Jude" ("The Eternal Jew") and the notorious 1940 propaganda film of the same name.

Other uses

The name "wandering Jew" is also given to several plants of the genus Tradescantia, named for the figure's restless wandering because they spread easily and root wherever they touch the ground.
