Stock exchange

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange beneath the great board
The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange beneath the great board

A stock exchange is an exchange (or bourse) where stockbrokers and traders buy and sell securities — shares of stock, bonds and derivatives. Exchanges provide the continuous, regulated marketplace that gives securities Liquidity and public prices: listing standards, disclosure rules, central order books (today almost wholly electronic) and clearing. The largest are the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq (together hosting roughly half the world's equity market value), followed by Shanghai, Euronext, Tokyo and Hong Kong.

History

Joint-stock trading began in Amsterdam (the VOC's shares, 1602; the first purpose-built bourse and the first short sales), spread to London's coffee houses (formalised 1773) and Wall Street (Buttonwood Agreement, 1792). Open-outcry floors defined the image for two centuries before electronic matching (Nasdaq from 1971, then everywhere) emptied them; high-frequency trading now dominates volumes in microseconds.

Functions and issues

Exchanges serve capital-raising (IPOs), price discovery, liquidity and corporate governance signals; around them cluster indices (S&P 500, FTSE 100, Nikkei 225), regulators' market-abuse regimes, and debates about exchanges as for-profit companies themselves, dark pools, meme-stock volatility and the declining number of listed firms in Western markets.
