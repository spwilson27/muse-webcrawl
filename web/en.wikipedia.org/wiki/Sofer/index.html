Sofer

A sofer (Hebrew: סופר סת״ם, plural soferim) is a Jewish scribe who can transcribe Torah scrolls (Sifrei Torah), tefillin (phylacteries), and mezuzot (the texts affixed to doorposts), collectively known by the acronym STaM (סת״ם). A female scribe is sometimes called a soferet. The work is governed by an extensive body of halakha (Jewish law) specifying everything from the preparation of parchment to the exact form of each letter.

Qualifications and training

A sofer must be a religiously observant adult Jew thoroughly trained in the hundreds of laws of safrut (scribal practice). Traditionally, soferim were ordained or certified by a recognized authority; today many study in specialized institutes, particularly in Israel. A sofer must write with proper intention (kavanah): the names of God must be written with special sanctity, and before writing one the scribe traditionally declares his intent and may immerse in a mikveh (ritual bath).

Materials and rules

Sacred texts are written on klaf (parchment) prepared from the skin of a kosher animal, with ink (d'yo) made to a traditional recipe, and a quill or reed pen. The letters must follow the Ashkenazi, Sephardi, or other communal script traditions (such as the script of the Ari) precisely, including the tagin (crownlets) on certain letters. A Torah scroll must contain exactly 304,805 letters. Errors in the Divine Name generally cannot be corrected; other errors may be scraped away, but certain defects render a scroll pasul (unfit) until repaired.

Because a single missing or malformed letter can invalidate the whole scroll, completed scrolls are checked carefully, nowadays often with computer scanning in addition to proofreading by expert scribes (magihim).

Other documents

Beyond STaM, soferim also write documents such as gittin (divorce documents), ketubot (marriage contracts, often illuminated), and the Five Megillot, especially the Book of Esther read on Purim.
