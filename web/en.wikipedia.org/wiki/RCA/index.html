The Radio Corporation of America (RCA) was an American electronics company founded in 1919 at the instigation of the US Navy, which wanted American control of radio technology after the First World War. Under David Sarnoff it became a dominant force in radio, television, recorded music and broadcasting.

History

RCA was formed by General Electric around the patents of the American Marconi company, and quickly built a patent pool with GE, Westinghouse and AT&T. It created the National Broadcasting Company (NBC) in 1926 and manufactured radios under the Radiola brand. RCA's laboratories, led by Vladimir Zworykin, were central to the development of electronic television, and RCA introduced the compatible colour television standard adopted as NTSC in 1953. Its Victor record division (RCA Victor) was one of the great record labels, home to artists from Enrico Caruso to Elvis Presley, and its "His Master's Voice" dog Nipper became an iconic trademark.

Decline and breakup

From the 1960s RCA diversified into car rental (Hertz), publishing and frozen foods, while its core electronics business lost ground to Japanese competitors; an expensive foray into mainframe computers and the failed SelectaVision videodisc drained resources. General Electric reacquired RCA in 1986 and broke it up: NBC stayed with GE, the consumer electronics business went to Thomson, and the record label to Bertelsmann. The RCA trademark survives under licence on consumer products.
