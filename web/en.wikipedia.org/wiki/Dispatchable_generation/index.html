Dispatchable generation

Dispatchable generation refers to sources of electricity that can be dispatched on demand at the request of power grid operators, according to market or system needs. Dispatchable generators can be turned on or off, or can adjust their power output. Conventional sources such as coal, natural gas, nuclear, geothermal, biomass, and reservoir hydropower are generally dispatchable, as are energy storage systems; in contrast, variable renewable sources such as wind and solar photovoltaic power are usually classed as non-dispatchable because their output depends on the weather, though they can be curtailed.

Characteristics of dispatchability

Key attributes include how quickly a plant can start (ramp-up time), its ramp rate, minimum run and down times, minimum stable output, and start-up costs. Open-cycle gas turbines and reciprocating engines can start in minutes and serve as peaking power plants; combined-cycle gas plants start more slowly but run more efficiently as mid-merit capacity; nuclear and coal plants are comparatively inflexible and are typically operated as baseload, though modern nuclear units can load-follow. Hydroelectric plants with reservoirs can often respond within minutes and also provide black start capability.

Role in the grid

Grid operators use dispatchable generation to balance supply with fluctuating demand, follow the daily load curve, provide frequency regulation and operating reserves, and compensate for forecast errors in variable generation. As wind and solar shares rise, system planners increasingly value flexibility itself: fast-ramping gas, batteries, pumped storage, demand response, and interconnection are used to firm variable output. Markets in some jurisdictions pay separately for capacity and flexibility services to ensure enough dispatchable resource remains available.
