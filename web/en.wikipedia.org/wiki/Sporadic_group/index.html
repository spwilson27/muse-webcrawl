Sporadic group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In group theory, a discipline within mathematics, a sporadic group is one of the 26 exceptional finite simple groups that do not fit into any of the infinite families in the classification of finite simple groups. The remaining simple groups comprise the cyclic groups of prime order, the alternating groups, and the groups of Lie type; together with the 26 sporadics these complete the classification, proved over the course of the twentieth century.

The 26 sporadic groups

The sporadic groups are usually organised into generations. The first five discovered were the Mathieu groups M11, M12, M22, M23, and M24, found by Émile Mathieu in the 1860s and 1870s. The second generation comprises the seven Leech lattice groups arising from the automorphism group of the Leech lattice — the three Conway groups Co1, Co2, Co3, the Suzuki sporadic group Suz, the McLaughlin group McL, the Higman–Sims group HS, and the Janko group J2. The third generation, closely related to the Monster group, includes the three Fischer groups Fi22, Fi23, Fi24′, the Held group He, the Harada–Norton group HN, the Thompson group Th, and the Baby Monster group B. The remaining six, termed the pariahs because they are not involved in the Monster, are the Janko groups J1, J3, J4, the O'Nan group O'N, the Rudvalis group Ru, and the Lyons group Ly.

The Monster

The largest sporadic group is the Monster group, also called the Friendly Giant, predicted by Bernd Fischer and Robert Griess and constructed by Griess in 1982. Its order is approximately 8 × 1053 — exactly 808,017,424,794,512,875,886,459,904,961,710,757,005,754,368,000,000,000. The Monster is connected to the theory of modular functions through the "monstrous moonshine" conjectures of John Conway and Simon Norton, proved by Richard Borcherds, who received the Fields Medal in part for this work. Twenty of the sporadic groups occur as subquotients of the Monster and are known as the "Happy Family"; the six pariahs do not.

History

Mathieu's groups went almost unnoticed for a century. The subject revived in the 1960s when Zvonimir Janko discovered J1 in 1965, the first new sporadic group in 100 years, sparking an intense period of discovery that produced most of the 26 within a decade. The last, J4, was found in 1975. No further sporadic groups exist: the classification of finite simple groups, completed around 2004, confirmed the list is exhaustive.
