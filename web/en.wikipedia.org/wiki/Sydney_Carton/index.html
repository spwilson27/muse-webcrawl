Sydney Carton

Sydney Carton
Created by: Charles Dickens
Appears in: A Tale of Two Cities (1859)
Occupation: Barrister (junior to Stryver)
Nationality: English

Sydney Carton is a central character in Charles Dickens's 1859 novel A Tale of Two Cities. A talented but dissolute and self-loathing English barrister, he works as the unacknowledged brains behind the flamboyant lawyer Stryver. Carton is remembered chiefly for his unrequited love for Lucie Manette and his redemptive self-sacrifice at the novel's climax.

Role in the novel

Carton first appears at the Old Bailey trial of Charles Darnay, where his sharp observation — pointing out his own striking physical resemblance to the prisoner — helps secure Darnay's acquittal. Despite his cynicism and alcoholism, he falls in love with Lucie, confessing that she has awakened in him a longing for a better life, though he expects nothing in return.

When Darnay is imprisoned in Paris during the Reign of Terror and condemned to the guillotine, Carton fulfils a promise made to Lucie: exploiting his resemblance to Darnay, he changes places with him in the Bastille, aided by the turnkey-spy Barsad. He comforts a young seamstress condemned alongside him and goes to his death in Darnay's place.

Famous lines

Carton's closing reflection is among the most famous passages in English literature: "It is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done; it is a far, far better rest that I go to than I have ever known."

Reception

Carton has been portrayed in numerous stage and screen adaptations and is widely regarded as one of Dickens's most memorable creations, an archetype of the redeemed wastrel whose wasted life is given meaning through a single act of love and sacrifice.
