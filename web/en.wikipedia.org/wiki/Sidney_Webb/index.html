Sidney James Webb, 1st Baron Passfield (13 July 1859 – 13 October 1947) was a British socialist, economist, and reformer who co-founded the London School of Economics. He was an early member and leading theorist of the Fabian Society, and together with his wife Beatrice Webb wrote influential studies of trade unionism, local government, and the poor laws.

Life and work

Webb worked as a civil service clerk while studying law and economics, and in 1884 helped found the Fabian Society, writing its first tract. His essay in Fabian Essays in Socialism (1889) set out the doctrine of gradual, democratic collectivism. With Beatrice Potter, whom he married in 1892, he produced The History of Trade Unionism (1894) and Industrial Democracy (1897). The Webbs founded the London School of Economics in 1895 with a Fabian bequest, and the magazine New Statesman in 1913. Their Minority Report of the Poor Law Commission (1909) laid intellectual groundwork for the welfare state.

Political career

Webb drafted the Labour Party's 1918 constitution, including the original Clause IV committing the party to common ownership. Elected MP for Seaham in 1922, he served as President of the Board of Trade in Ramsay MacDonald's first Labour government (1924), and as Secretary of State for the Colonies and Dominion Affairs (1929–31) after being raised to the peerage as Baron Passfield. The Passfield White Paper of 1930, restricting Jewish immigration and land purchase in Palestine, provoked controversy. In the 1930s the aging Webbs controversially praised the Soviet Union in Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? (1935).
