Carbon-13

Carbon-13 (13C) is a natural, stable isotope of carbon with a nucleus containing six protons and seven neutrons. It makes up about 1.07% of all carbon on Earth. Because its nucleus has a spin of ½, carbon-13 is the isotope that makes carbon NMR spectroscopy possible, and its ratio to carbon-12 is widely used as a tracer in geology, ecology, medicine and archaeology.

Properties

Carbon-13 is one of two stable carbon isotopes, the other being carbon-12 (≈98.93%). Its slight excess mass and nuclear spin distinguish it physically from carbon-12: the spin-½ nucleus possesses a magnetic moment, so it resonates in a magnetic field at a characteristic frequency (about one quarter that of the proton), enabling nuclear magnetic resonance detection. The isotope is slightly heavier per atom, which produces small but measurable kinetic and equilibrium isotope effects in chemical and biological reactions.

Isotope ratio and δ13C

Natural processes fractionate carbon isotopes: photosynthesis, for example, discriminates against the heavier isotope, so plant tissues are depleted in 13C relative to atmospheric CO2. Scientists report the ratio R = 13C/12C as δ13C in parts per thousand relative to the VPDB standard. C3 plants average about −27‰, C4 plants about −13‰, and marine carbonates near 0‰. These signatures let researchers reconstruct diets of ancient animals, trace food webs, identify sources of methane, and detect adulteration of honey and other foods.

Applications

  • NMR spectroscopy: 13C NMR is a routine tool for determining organic molecular structures, despite low natural abundance and sensitivity.
  • Metabolic tracers: compounds enriched in 13C (such as 13C-glucose) trace metabolic pathways safely in humans because the isotope is stable and non-radioactive; the 13C-urea breath test diagnoses Helicobacter pylori infection.
  • Earth sciences: δ13C excursions in sediments mark events such as the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum and help correlate strata.
  • Hyperpolarised MRI: hyperpolarised 13C compounds are an emerging imaging technique for real-time metabolism.

Detection

Abundance is measured precisely by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry, which combusts samples to CO2 and compares the ion currents at mass-to-charge ratios 44, 45 and 46.
