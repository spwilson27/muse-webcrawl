IS–LM model

The IS–LM model, or Hicks–Hansen model, is a two-dimensional macroeconomic tool that represents the relationship between interest rates and real output in the markets for goods and money services. The intersection of the IS and LM curves shows a short-run equilibrium in which both the goods market and the money market are in balance.

The downward-sloping IS curve and upward-sloping LM curve intersect to determine equilibrium income and the interest rate.

The IS curve

The IS curve (Investment–Savings) describes combinations of the interest rate and output at which the goods market clears: planned investment equals savings, or equivalently aggregate demand equals output. Because investment falls as the interest rate rises, the IS curve slopes downward. Increases in government spending or autonomous consumption shift the IS curve to the right.

The LM curve

The LM curve (Liquidity preference–Money supply) describes combinations at which money demand equals the (central-bank-set) real money supply. Money demand rises with income and falls with the interest rate, so the LM curve slopes upward. An increase in the money supply shifts it right (or down); in modern formulations with interest-rate-targeting central banks, the LM curve is often drawn as horizontal at the policy rate.

History and use

The framework was developed by John Hicks in 1937 as a formalisation of Keynes's General Theory and popularised by Alvin Hansen, becoming the centrepiece of the "neoclassical synthesis" and of undergraduate macroeconomics teaching. It is used to analyse fiscal and monetary policy: in a liquidity trap (flat LM), monetary expansion is ineffective while fiscal policy is powerful; in the "classical" case (vertical LM), the reverse holds. The model has been criticised for lacking microfoundations and dynamic expectations, and in policy institutions it has largely been supplemented by IS–LM–AS/AD and New Keynesian DSGE models.
