Image dissector

The image dissector is a television camera tube in which an electron image produced by a photoemissive cathode is focused and scanned by deflecting coils onto a small aperture; the current passing through the aperture at any instant is proportional to the brightness of the corresponding part of the image. It was invented by American television pioneer Philo Farnsworth in the 1920s and was central to the first fully electronic television system.

History

Farnsworth conceived the idea as a teenager in 1921 and filed a patent in 1927; on 7 September 1927 he transmitted the first image — a straight line — using his dissector camera. The device figured prominently in the patent interference battle between Farnsworth and RCA's Vladimir Zworykin, whose iconoscope worked on a different, charge-storage principle.

Operation

Light from the scene is focused onto a photocathode, which emits electrons in proportion to local brightness, forming an "electron image". Magnetic deflection coils sweep this electron image across a small scanning aperture behind which sits an electron multiplier; only the electrons from one picture element pass through at a time, so the output current reproduces the image as a time-varying signal.

Limitations and legacy

Because the dissector discards nearly all of the emitted electrons except those at the scanned point, it required extremely bright illumination and was far less sensitive than charge-storage tubes such as the iconoscope and later the image orthicon. Nevertheless, dissector-type tubes (photodissector and image dissector variants) found later use in star trackers, optical character recognition and early satellite and astronomical applications, where their instantaneous readout and lack of lag were advantageous.
