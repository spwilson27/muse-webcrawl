Cnidocyte

A cnidocyte and its explosive nematocyst organelle.

A cnidocyte (also known as a cnidoblast or nematocyte) is an explosive cell found in animals of the phylum Cnidaria — including jellyfish, sea anemones, corals, and hydras. Each cnidocyte contains one large secretory organelle called a cnidocyst, the best-known type of which is the nematocyst. When triggered, the organelle everts a hollow, often barbed and venom-bearing thread that can pierce the skin of prey or predators, delivering a sting.

Structure and discharge

The cnidocyte's capsule holds a coiled, hollow tubule under extremely high internal osmotic pressure — in some species the pressure inside the undischarged capsule has been measured around 150 atmospheres. A hair-like trigger, the cnidocil, protrudes from the cell surface. Mechanical and chemical stimulation causes calcium ions to flood the cell, the capsule's lid (operculum) opens, and the tubule everts within microseconds. The acceleration of the discharged stylets has been measured at over five million times the acceleration of gravity, making nematocyst discharge one of the fastest known cellular processes.

Function

Cnidocytes are used for prey capture and defense against predators. The injected venom can paralyze small prey such as crustaceans and fish, which the animal then moves to its mouth with its tentacles. Cnidocytes are single-use cells: after discharge, they are replaced by new cells differentiating from interstitial stem cells. Some animals, such as certain nudibranchs, steal functional cnidocytes from the cnidarians they eat and store them in their own tissues, a phenomenon called kleptocnidae.

Types

More than thirty types of cnidocysts have been described, differing in tubule structure and armature. Nematocysts pierce targets and inject venom; spirocysts are adhesive tubules used mainly by sea anemones to grip prey; and ptychocysts are used by tube anemones to build the linings of their burrows.
