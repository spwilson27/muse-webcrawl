Brussels effect

EU regulations made in Brussels often become de facto global standards.

The Brussels effect is the process of regulatory globalization whereby the European Union's rules become global standards, either because foreign governments adopt EU-style laws (the de jure effect) or because multinational companies find it more efficient to comply with EU regulation across all their operations (the de facto effect). The term was coined by Finnish-American legal scholar Anu Bradford in a 2012 paper and developed in her 2020 book The Brussels Effect: How the European Union Rules the World.

Mechanism

Bradford identifies five conditions: a large internal market, strong regulatory capacity, a preference for stringent rules, inelastic targets (firms cannot credibly threaten to leave the market), and non-divisibility of production — it is cheaper to standardize worldwide than to maintain separate products. Classic examples include EU chemicals regulation (REACH), food and product safety rules, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and EU competition rulings against large technology firms.

Comparison

The Brussels effect is analogous to the "California effect", in which the standards of a large, wealthy U.S. state spread nationwide. Critics argue the effect can impose compliance costs globally and may weaken as other powers, such as China, set competing standards.
