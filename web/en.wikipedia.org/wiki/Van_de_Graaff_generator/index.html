A Van de Graaff generator is an electrostatic generator that produces very high direct-current voltages by mechanically transporting electric charge on a moving insulating belt to a hollow metal terminal. It was invented by the American physicist Robert J. Van de Graaff beginning in 1929 at Princeton University and developed at MIT, initially as a power source for early particle accelerators.

Principle of operation

A motor drives a continuous belt of rubber or similar insulating material over two rollers inside an insulating column. At the base, a high-voltage supply sprays charge onto the moving belt through a row of sharp metal points (a corona comb). The belt carries the charge up into a hollow conducting sphere, where a second comb transfers the charge to the sphere. Because charge resides on the outside of a conductor, the sphere can accumulate charge regardless of how high its potential rises, limited mainly by leakage and electrical breakdown of the surrounding air. Reversing the spray polarity charges the terminal negatively instead.

Applications

Large Van de Graaff machines powered a generation of nuclear-physics accelerators; the tandem configuration, in which negative ions are accelerated toward a positive terminal, stripped of electrons, and accelerated away again, long remained a workhorse of nuclear structure research. Smaller generators are a staple of science museums and classrooms, famously making a volunteer's hair stand on end. They are also used in X-ray generation, food sterilisation research and engineering tests.
