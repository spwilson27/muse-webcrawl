Kino-Eye

Kino-Eye (Russian: Кино-глаз, Kino-glaz) is a film technique and theory developed by the Soviet filmmaker Dziga Vertov, and the name of the group and film series associated with it. It holds that the movie camera — the "kino-eye" — perceives the world more perfectly than the human eye and can reveal truths hidden from ordinary vision.

Theory

Vertov set out his ideas in manifestos such as "WE: Variant of a Manifesto" (1922) and "Kinoks: A Revolution" (1923). He rejected staged fiction film as a bourgeois opiate and advocated "life caught unawares": candid documentary footage organised through montage. Techniques associated with Kino-Eye include candid filming, hidden cameras, time-lapse, slow motion, split screens, and associative editing.

The group and films

Vertov's "Council of Three" included his wife, editor Elizaveta Svilova, and his brother, cameraman Mikhail Kaufman. The Kino-Eye group produced the Kino-Pravda newsreel series (1922–25) and features including Kino-Eye (1924), Stride, Soviet! (1926), and the celebrated Man with a Movie Camera (1929), the fullest expression of the theory. Vertov's ideas strongly influenced later documentary movements, notably cinéma vérité, whose French name translates his term kino-pravda ("film truth").
