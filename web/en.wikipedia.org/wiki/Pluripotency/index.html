Pluripotency

In cell biology, pluripotency is the ability of a stem cell to differentiate into any of the three germ layers—endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm—and hence into almost any cell type of the adult body. Pluripotent cells include embryonic stem cells derived from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst and induced pluripotent stem cells created by reprogramming somatic cells.

Potency hierarchy

Developmental potency decreases as cells specialize. A totipotent cell (like the zygote and earliest blastomeres) can form a complete organism including extra-embryonic tissues such as the placenta. A pluripotent cell can form all body cell types but not, on its own, an entire organism with supporting tissues. Multipotent cells (for example, hematopoietic stem cells) are restricted to a family of related cell types, oligopotent cells to a few types, and unipotent cells to one.

Pluripotent cell types

  • Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) – derived from the inner cell mass of pre-implantation embryos; mouse ESCs were isolated in 1981 and human ESCs in 1998 by James Thomson's group.
  • Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) – generated in 2006 by Shinya Yamanaka's team by expressing four transcription factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc—the "Yamanaka factors") in adult cells; work recognized by the 2012 Nobel Prize.
  • Embryonic germ cells and embryonal carcinoma cells – other naturally occurring pluripotent lines.

Signalling and states

Pluripotency is maintained by a core transcriptional network centred on Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog, and by signalling pathways that vary by species and state. Mouse ESCs are supported by LIF/STAT3 and BMP signals or by "2i" inhibitors, representing a naïve (ground) state resembling the inner cell mass, whereas conventional human ESCs are closer to a "primed" state resembling the post-implantation epiblast, akin to mouse epiblast stem cells.

Testing pluripotency

Standard assays include differentiation into embryoid bodies in vitro, teratoma formation containing tissues of all three germ layers in immunodeficient mice, and—most stringently in mice—chimera formation and germline transmission. Molecular markers include surface antigens (such as SSEA-3/4 and TRA-1-60 in human cells) and expression of the core pluripotency factors.

Applications

Pluripotent cells are used for disease modelling, drug screening, developmental biology, and the development of cell-replacement therapies, including retinal, cardiac, pancreatic, and dopaminergic neuron grafts now in clinical trials.
