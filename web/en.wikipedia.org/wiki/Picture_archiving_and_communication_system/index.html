Picture archiving and communication system

A radiologist reading studies on a PACS workstation.

A picture archiving and communication system (PACS) is a medical imaging technology which provides economical storage and convenient access to images from multiple modalities (imaging machine types). Electronic images and reports are transmitted digitally via PACS; this eliminates the need to manually file, retrieve or transport film jackets, the folders used to store and protect X-ray film.

Components and standards

The universal format for PACS image storage and transfer is DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine). Non-image data, such as scanned documents, may be stored using common formats such as PDF and integrated into the record. A PACS consists of four major components:

  • Imaging modalities such as CT, MRI, ultrasound and X-ray equipment.
  • A secured network for the transmission of patient information.
  • Workstations for interpreting and reviewing images.
  • Archives for the storage and retrieval of images and reports.

PACS usually works together with a radiology information system (RIS) and the hospital information system, coordinated through standards such as HL7 and the IHE integration profiles.

History and benefits

The principles of PACS were first discussed at meetings of radiologists in 1982, and the term is credited to Dr Andre Duerinckx. Large-scale installations spread through hospitals in the 1990s as digital modalities replaced film. Benefits include filmless operation, simultaneous remote viewing, teleradiology, easier comparison with prior studies and reduced loss of examinations. Modern developments include vendor-neutral archives (VNA), cloud storage and integration with artificial-intelligence tools for image analysis.
