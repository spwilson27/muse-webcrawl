Microarchitecture

A simplified microarchitecture block diagram

In computer engineering, microarchitecture, also called computer organization, is the way a given instruction set architecture (ISA) is implemented in a particular processor. A given ISA may be implemented with different microarchitectures; implementations may vary due to different goals of a given design or due to shifts in technology. Microarchitecture is distinct from the ISA, which is the set of instructions, registers, and data structures visible to a programmer.

Elements

A microarchitecture typically includes a datapath (functional units such as ALUs and floating-point units, registers, and buses), control logic, and a memory hierarchy of caches. Techniques commonly found in modern designs include instruction pipelining, superscalar and out-of-order execution, branch prediction, register renaming, speculative execution, simultaneous multithreading, and multiple cache levels with prefetching. The clock cycle time and the number of instructions executed per cycle (IPC) together determine single-thread performance.

Examples

Well-known microarchitectures include Intel's P6, NetBurst, Core, Nehalem, Sandy Bridge, Skylake and Golden Cove designs, and AMD's K7, K8, Zen and its successors. ARM processor cores such as the Cortex-A series and Apple's Firestorm and Avalanche cores are also described by their microarchitectures, even though many of them implement the same ARM instruction set. Two chips can share an ISA yet differ greatly in performance and power because of microarchitectural choices.

Relation to Moore's law

As transistor scaling has slowed, microarchitectural innovation — larger caches, smarter prediction, domain-specific accelerators — has become an increasingly important source of performance gains, alongside process technology improvements.
