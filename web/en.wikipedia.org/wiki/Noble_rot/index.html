Noble rot

Noble rot (French: pourriture noble; German: Edelfäule; Italian: muffa nobile; Hungarian: aszúsodás) is the beneficial form of the grey mould fungus Botrytis cinerea affecting wine grapes. Under the right conditions the fungus perforates grape skins, causing water to evaporate and concentrating sugars, acids and flavour compounds in the remaining juice. Grapes so shrivelled and mouldy — individually picked in successive passes through the vineyard — produce some of the world's most celebrated sweet wines.

Conditions

Noble rot develops when mature, thin-skinned grapes are exposed to morning mist (often from a nearby river or lake) that lets the fungus establish, followed by dry, sunny afternoons that halt its spread and dry the berries. Too much humidity and the infection becomes destructive grey rot, ruining the crop; too little and the fungus never develops. Suitable sites include Sauternes (where the Ciron meets the Garonne), the Tokaj region of Hungary (the Bodrog and Tisza rivers), and the great German vineyards of the Rhine and Mosel.

Effect on the wine

Botrytised grapes may lose more than half their water, while the fungus contributes glycerol, gluconic acid and distinctive aroma compounds associated with honey, apricot, marmalade and saffron notes. The resulting wines combine intense sweetness with high acidity, giving them extraordinary longevity — great Sauternes and Tokaji can age for many decades. Because yields are tiny (a vine may produce only a glass of wine) and picking requires multiple selective passes (tries), production is labour-intensive and the wines expensive.

History

Hungarian tradition credits the deliberate production of botrytised wine (Tokaji Aszú) to the 17th century, when harvest was delayed by war. Famous legends also attach to Schloss Johannisberg in the Rheingau, where a delayed harvest courier in 1775 supposedly allowed the rot to develop, founding the German late-harvest (Spätlese) tradition. In Sauternes, estates such as Château d'Yquem built their reputations on rigorously selected botrytised harvests in the 19th century.
