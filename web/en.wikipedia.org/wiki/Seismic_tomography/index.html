Seismic tomography

Placeholder image: velocity anomalies in the mantle imaged by seismic tomography.

Seismic tomography is a technique for imaging the subsurface of the Earth using seismic waves generated by earthquakes and explosions. Analogous to medical CT scanning, it combines arrival times (and increasingly full waveforms) recorded at many seismometers to construct three-dimensional models of seismic wave speed in the crust and mantle. Fast anomalies are generally interpreted as cold, dense material such as subducted slabs; slow anomalies indicate hot, buoyant material such as rising mantle plumes.

Method

The fundamental data are the travel times of P-waves and S-waves between many source–receiver pairs. Because earthquakes are unevenly distributed, resolution varies with ray coverage. Modern methods include finite-frequency tomography, which accounts for the wave nature of the signal, ambient-noise tomography, which uses correlations of background noise to image the crust, and full-waveform inversion.

Findings

Tomography has imaged subducted slabs descending into the deep mantle, low-velocity provinces above the core–mantle boundary, and narrow plume conduits beneath hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland. It is also applied at smaller scales in resource exploration, engineering, and volcano monitoring.
