The Samanid Empire (Persian: سامانیان, Sāmāniyān), also known as the Samanid dynasty or Samanid emirate, was a Persian Sunni Muslim empire in Central Asia and eastern Iran from 819 to 999. Ruled from Bukhara, it was the first native Persian dynasty to exercise effective power in the region after the Arab conquest of Persia, and it is remembered as a golden age of Persian culture — sometimes called the "Persian Renaissance."

History

The dynasty descended from Saman Khuda, a Persian landowner (dehqan) from the village of Saman near Balkh who converted to Islam. His grandsons served the Abbasid caliphs as governors in Transoxiana, and in 875 Nasr ibn Ahmad received the governorship of the region. His brother Ismail Samani (r. 892–907) united Transoxiana and Khorasan, made Bukhara his capital, and established de facto independence while maintaining nominal allegiance to Baghdad. Under his successors the empire stretched from the Caspian approaches to the borders of India, controlling the Silk Road trade in slaves, furs, and silver dirhams that reached as far as Scandinavia.

Culture

The Samanid court patronised the revival of New Persian as a literary language written in Arabic script. The poet Rudaki, often called the father of Persian poetry, flourished at Bukhara; Ferdowsi began the Shahnameh under Samanid patronage; and the polymath Ibn Sina (Avicenna) was born near Bukhara and gained access to the royal library. Bukhara became a major centre of Islamic learning, ceramics and architecture — the Ismail Samani Mausoleum in Bukhara remains a masterpiece of early Islamic brickwork.

Decline and fall

In the later tenth century the empire weakened through succession disputes, the growing power of Turkish slave-soldier (ghulam) commanders, and pressure from the Turkic Qarakhanids to the east and the rising Ghaznavids to the south. In 999 the Qarakhanids took Bukhara, ending Samanid rule; the last emir, Ismail Muntasir, was assassinated in 1005 while attempting a restoration.
