A motion picture content rating system classifies films according to their suitability for different audiences, based on content such as violence, sex, language, drug use, and themes. Rating systems vary widely between countries: some are administered by government agencies with legal force, while others are voluntary industry schemes. Nearly all countries restrict some material for children, and many also rate home video, streaming, and television content by related systems.

United States

In the United States, films were governed from the 1930s until 1968 by the Motion Picture Production Code (Hays Code), a censorship regime. In 1968 the Motion Picture Association, under Jack Valenti, replaced it with a voluntary rating system administered by the Classification and Rating Administration (CARA). The current categories are:

  • G — General audiences; all ages admitted.
  • PG — Parental guidance suggested; some material may not be suitable for children.
  • PG-13 — Parents strongly cautioned; some material may be inappropriate for children under 13. Introduced in 1984 after controversy over intense scenes in Gremlins and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom; the first film released with the rating was Red Dawn.
  • R — Restricted; under 17 requires an accompanying parent or adult guardian.
  • NC-17 — Adults only; no one 17 and under admitted. Introduced in 1990 to replace the X rating, which had been appropriated by the pornography industry.

Films may also be released unrated. Ratings are advisory for G, PG, and PG-13 but are enforced as conditions of admission by most theatre chains for R and NC-17; NC-17 films face limited distribution and advertising restrictions.

Other countries

The British Board of Film Classification (BBFC), founded in 1912, assigns U, PG, 12A (cinema) and 12 (video), 15, 18, and R18 ratings; its classifications have statutory force for video works and are adopted by local authorities for cinemas. Australia's Classification Board issues G, PG, M (recommended for mature audiences), MA15+ (legally restricted), R18+, and X18+ classifications with legal effect. Germany's FSK uses age levels 0, 6, 12, 16, and 18; France's CNC uses graded authorizations including "tous publics" and bans for under-12s, 16s, and 18s; Canada's ratings are issued provincially; Japan's Eirin uses G, PG12, R15+, and R18+. Many systems also rate video games separately (for example ESRB in North America and PEGI in Europe).

Criticism and comparison

Scholars comparing systems note systematic differences: American ratings are generally stricter about sexual content and profanity, European ratings stricter about violence. Critics fault the US system for anonymous raters, inconsistent decisions, and the commercial penalty of NC-17; supporters argue ratings give parents information without government censorship. No international standard exists, and a film may receive very different classifications in different countries.
