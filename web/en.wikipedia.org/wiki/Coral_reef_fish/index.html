Coral reef fish

Coral reef fish are fish which live amongst or in close relation to coral reefs. Coral reefs form complex ecosystems with tremendous biodiversity. Among the myriad of inhabitants, the fish stand out as particularly colourful and interesting to watch. Hundreds of species can exist in a small area of a healthy reef, many of them hidden or well camouflaged. Reef fish have developed many ingenious specialisations adapted to survival on the reefs.

Diversity

Coral reefs occupy less than 1% of the world's ocean surface, yet they are home to about 25% of all marine fish species — an estimated 6,000–8,000 species. Major families include the damselfishes (Pomacentridae), wrasses (Labridae), gobies (Gobiidae), butterflyfishes (Chaetodontidae), angelfishes (Pomacanthidae), surgeonfishes (Acanthuridae), parrotfishes (Scaridae), groupers (Serranidae) and cardinalfishes (Apogonidae).

Adaptations and ecology

Reef fish display specialised feeding strategies: herbivorous parrotfish graze algae and excavate coral, producing sand; butterflyfish pick at coral polyps and invertebrates; cleaner wrasses remove parasites from larger fish at cleaning stations. Many species are brightly coloured for species recognition and camouflage within the dappled reef light, while others, like stonefish and scorpionfish, are cryptic ambush predators. Numerous reef fish change sex during their lives (sequential hermaphroditism), and most have a pelagic larval stage that disperses them between reefs.

Threats

Coral reef fish are threatened by coral bleaching and habitat loss, overfishing (including for the aquarium trade), and invasive species such as the Indo-Pacific lionfish in the Atlantic.
