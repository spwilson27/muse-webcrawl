Car classification

Cars arranged by market segment, from city car to executive saloon

Car classification is the grouping of vehicles according to their size, purpose, body style, price or regulatory characteristics. Governments and regulatory agencies classify cars for purposes such as taxation, safety and emissions regulation, while manufacturers and the automotive press use market segments to position and compare models. No single system is used worldwide, and categories overlap considerably.

Market segments

In Europe, passenger cars are commonly divided into lettered segments: A-segment (city cars), B-segment (superminis), C-segment (small family cars), D-segment (large family cars), E-segment (executive cars) and F-segment (luxury saloons), with further categories for SUVs and crossovers (J-segment), sports cars (S-segment) and multi-purpose vehicles. In the United States, the EPA classifies cars by interior volume into classes such as subcompact, compact, mid-size and large, while market terms include economy car, muscle car, pony car and full-size car.

Classification by body style

Cars are also classified by body configuration: sedan (saloon), hatchback, estate (station wagon), coupé, convertible, roadster, minivan, SUV and pickup truck. Microcars, kei cars in Japan, and low-speed neighbourhood electric vehicles form special categories defined by strict size and performance limits.

Regulatory classification

Regulators use classifications for fuel-economy standards, emissions testing, driver licensing and insurance grouping. Japan's kei car rules and China's vehicle categories, for example, directly influence the dimensions and engine sizes manufacturers build to, while racing sanctioning bodies define classes such as touring cars, GT cars and prototypes by elaborate technical rules.
