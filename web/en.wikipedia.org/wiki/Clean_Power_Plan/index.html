The Clean Power Plan was a United States environmental regulation announced by President Barack Obama in August 2015 to combat climate change by reducing carbon dioxide emissions from existing electricity-generating plants. Administered by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) under Section 111(d) of the Clean Air Act, it set state-specific emission-rate and mass-based goals that would have cut power-sector carbon emissions by 32 percent below 2005 levels by 2030.

Design

The EPA calculated state goals using three "building blocks": improving heat rates at coal plants, shifting generation from coal to natural gas, and increasing zero-emission generation such as wind and solar. States could meet their targets through plant improvements, trading programs, or regional cooperation, and were required to submit implementation plans to the EPA. A related rule covered new power plants under Section 111(b).

Legal challenges

Twenty-seven states and industry groups sued to block the plan. On February 9, 2016, the Supreme Court took the unprecedented step of staying the rule before lower-court review was complete; the plan never took effect. After the death of Justice Antonin Scalia days later, the D.C. Circuit litigation was held in abeyance.

Repeal and aftermath

In 2017 President Donald Trump signed an executive order directing review of the plan, and in 2019 the EPA repealed it and replaced it with the Affordable Clean Energy (ACE) rule, which focused narrowly on heat-rate improvements at individual plants. The D.C. Circuit vacated the ACE rule in January 2021. In West Virginia v. EPA (2022), the Supreme Court held that the Clean Air Act did not authorize the EPA to require generation shifting as the "best system of emission reduction," invoking the major questions doctrine and effectively foreclosing a revived Clean Power Plan of the same design.
