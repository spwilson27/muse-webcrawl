Church polity

Church polity
DefinitionThe operational and governance structure of a Christian church or denomination
Main formsEpiscopal, presbyterian, congregational, connexional
Related termEcclesiology

Church polity is the structure of authority and government in a Christian church: who ordains ministers, who owns property, how decisions are made and how far local congregations answer to wider bodies. Christian denominations differ as sharply over polity as over doctrine, and most historical schisms involved disputes over it.

Episcopal polity

In episcopal polity (from Greek episkopos, overseer), authority flows through bishops, who govern dioceses and alone ordain clergy. The Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches, and most Anglican and some Lutheran churches, are episcopal; Catholic polity is monarchical-episcopal, with the Pope as supreme head. Methodism's connexional polity is a variant, with bishops and itinerant ministers deployed by conferences.

Presbyterian polity

Presbyterian polity (from presbyteros, elder) governs through graded assemblies of elders: the local session, the presbytery, and synods or a general assembly. Developed by John Calvin in Geneva and John Knox in Scotland, it is characteristic of Reformed and Presbyterian churches, and combines representative government with connectional oversight.

Congregational polity

In congregational polity each local congregation is autonomous — calling its own minister, governing its own affairs and owning its own property — with associations of churches purely advisory. Baptists, Congregationalists, Disciples of Christ and most independent and Pentecostal churches follow this pattern, grounding it in the priesthood of all believers.
