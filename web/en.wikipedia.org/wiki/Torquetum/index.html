Torquetum

The torquetum is a medieval astronomical instrument designed to measure and convert between the three sets of celestial coordinates: horizon (altitude and azimuth), equatorial, and ecliptic. By means of a set of hinged, graduated plates it performed the coordinate transformations mechanically, sparing the astronomer laborious trigonometric calculation.

Description

The instrument consists of a horizontal base plate aligned to the horizon, above which an equatorial plate is inclined at the angle of the celestial pole for the observer's latitude. A further plate representing the ecliptic can be set upon this, and a vertical semicircle with sighting rules allows stars to be observed. Scales engraved on the plates read off the coordinates directly in the desired system.

History

The torquetum is generally attributed to the twelfth-century astronomer Jabir ibn Aflah (Geber) of Seville, and was described by later authors including Franco of Poland and Nicholas of Cusa; Regiomontanus built celebrated examples in the fifteenth century. It remained in use into the sixteenth century, appearing in Peter Apian's Astronomicum Caesareum (1540), before being superseded by simpler instruments and printed tables.
