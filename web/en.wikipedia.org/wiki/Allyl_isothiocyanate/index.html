Allyl isothiocyanate (AITC) is an organosulfur compound with the formula CH2CHCH2NCS. This colourless, pungent oil is responsible for the sharp, biting taste and nasal heat of mustard, horseradish and wasabi. It is a lachrymator, irritating the eyes and mucous membranes.

Occurrence and biosynthesis

Allyl isothiocyanate is not present as such in intact plant tissue. The seeds of black mustard and brown mustard, and the roots of horseradish and wasabi, instead contain the glucosinolate sinigrin. When the tissue is crushed or chewed, the enzyme myrosinase comes into contact with sinigrin and hydrolyses it, releasing allyl isothiocyanate. This "mustard oil bomb" is a defence system against herbivores.

The compound produces its burning sensation by activating the TRPA1 ion channel — the same receptor targeted by wasabi and mustard — and, more weakly, the heat-sensitive TRPV1 channel also activated by capsaicin.

Production

Allyl isothiocyanate is obtained commercially by steam distillation of mustard seeds, yielding mustard oil, of which AITC is the principal component. It can also be synthesised by the reaction of allyl chloride with alkali metal thiocyanates.

Uses

AITC is used as a food flavouring and preservative, as a fungicide and soil fumigant in agriculture, and historically as a counterirritant in liniments (in "mustard plasters"). Because of its antimicrobial activity, it is employed in food packaging materials to inhibit spoilage organisms.

Safety

Allyl isothiocyanate is irritating to skin, eyes and the respiratory tract and is toxic in concentrated form. In culinary quantities, as in mustard and wasabi, it is consumed safely, though occupational exposure to the concentrated oil is regulated.
