In Praise of Folly

In Praise of Folly, also translated as The Praise of Folly (Latin: Stultitiae Laus or Moriae Encomium; Greek title: Morias enkomion), is an essay by Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam written in 1509 and first printed in Paris in 1511. One of the most famous works of Renaissance humanism, it is a satirical mock-encomium in which Folly herself, personified as a goddess, delivers a speech praising self-deception and folly while satirising the corruption of the Catholic Church, scholastic theologians, monks, and the superstitions of Erasmus's age. The book is often credited with helping to lay the groundwork for the Protestant Reformation.

Background

Erasmus wrote the work in 1509 while staying at the London home of his friend Thomas More, during a visit to England. The Latin title Moriae Encomium puns on More's name — Moria is the Greek word for folly, and the work is dedicated to More. Erasmus said he drafted it in about a week. It was first printed in Paris in 1511, then in an expanded edition from Strasbourg (1514) and in the famous Basel editions of Johann Froben (from 1515), one copy of which was illustrated in the margins by Hans Holbein the Younger. Erasmus revised and enlarged the text repeatedly until 1532.

Summary

The narrator is Folly (Stultitia), daughter of Plutus, god of wealth, and the nymph Youth. In the first part of her oration she praises herself cheerfully: she claims credit for all human happiness, arguing that self-love, flattery, forgetfulness, and pleasant delusion make life bearable; marriage, friendship, and social life all depend on folly, and the wise man, incapable of illusion, is miserable. The tone darkens in the middle section, where Folly attacks the follies of particular professions: grammarians, philosophers and scholastic theologians lost in syllogisms, monks and mendicant friars ignorant and superstitious yet proud, theologians debating absurd questions, kings and courtiers, and — most sharply — popes, cardinals, and bishops who live in luxury while neglecting the teaching of Christ. In the final part the satire gives way to a surprising sincerity: Folly praises the "folly of the Cross", arguing that true Christian piety, contemptuous of worldly wisdom, is itself a kind of madness, and citing St Paul's teaching that "the foolishness of God is wiser than men".

Reception and influence

The work was an immediate and lasting bestseller, translated across Europe, but its attacks on monks and church abuses drew hostility, and it was later placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books (Paul IV's Index of 1559 banned Erasmus's works entirely). Martin Luther's circle read Erasmus's satire as preparation for the Reformation — prompting the aphorism that "Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched", though Erasmus rejected the label and broke publicly with Luther in the debate over free will (1524–1525). In Praise of Folly remains a classic of satire and a landmark of Renaissance humanism.
