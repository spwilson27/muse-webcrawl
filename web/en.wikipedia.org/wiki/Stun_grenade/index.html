Stun grenade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Less-lethal grenade
Also known as: Flashbang, flash grenade
Used by: Military and law-enforcement special units
Effects: Intense light and loud report to disorient

A stun grenade, also known as a flash grenade, flashbang, thunderflash, or sound bomb, is a less-lethal explosive device used to temporarily disorient an enemy's or suspect's senses. Upon detonation, a stun grenade produces a blinding flash of light and an extremely loud "bang". It is commonly used by police, military, and special forces units in close-quarters combat and hostage rescue situations to momentarily incapacitate occupants of a room before entry, reducing the risk of injury to both the entrants and the occupants.

Effects

The grenade is designed to produce a blinding flash of light of approximately 7 million candela and an intensely loud bang of about 170 decibels at close range. The flash activates the photoreceptor cells in the eye, causing temporary blindness lasting several seconds, while the blast disturbs the fluid in the inner ear and disrupts balance. The combined effect renders the subject temporarily disoriented and unable to react effectively, giving entry teams a window of a few seconds in which to act.

Design

Unlike a fragmentation grenade, a stun grenade is constructed with a casing intended to remain largely intact during deflagration of its pyrotechnic mixture, commonly a metal powder oxidizer such as magnesium or aluminium combined with an oxidiser such as ammonium perchlorate or potassium perchlorate. Vents in the casing allow the flash and sound to escape while limiting dangerous fragmentation.

History

Stun grenades were developed in the 1970s, with the British Special Air Service (SAS) among the first units to develop and employ them, notably during the Iranian Embassy siege in London in 1980. They subsequently became standard equipment for counter-terrorist and tactical police units worldwide.

Risks

Although classified as less-lethal, stun grenades can cause serious injury or death, particularly if they detonate in contact with a person, ignite fires, or trigger medical conditions such as seizures. Their use has therefore attracted scrutiny in a number of jurisdictions.
