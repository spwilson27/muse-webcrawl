Kaleidoscope

TypeOptical toy / instrument
InventorDavid Brewster
Patented1817
PrincipleMultiple reflection between angled mirrors

A kaleidoscope is an optical instrument with two or more reflecting surfaces tilted toward each other at an angle, so that one or more objects at one end are repeatedly reflected when viewed from the other end. The reflectors are usually enclosed in a tube, often with loose, coloured pieces of glass or other materials in a chamber at the far end; rotating the tube causes the pieces to shift, producing an endless variety of symmetrical patterns. The name derives from the ancient Greek kalos (beautiful), eidos (form), and skopeo (to look at).

History

Multiple reflection was known to the ancient Greeks, and in 1816 the Scottish scientist David Brewster developed the kaleidoscope during experiments on light polarisation. He patented it in 1817; it quickly became a popular parlour toy across Europe and America. Brewster estimated that hundreds of thousands were sold in London and Paris within months, many of them imitations, since the patent was widely infringed.

Design

Two-mirror systems produce a single repeating sector whose symmetry depends on the angle between the mirrors; three-mirror systems fill the whole field of view. Chambers may be dry, filled with liquid, or replaced by interchangeable wheels and, in modern instruments, by projected or digital images.

In culture

The kaleidoscope has become a common metaphor for constant change, shifting patterns and fragmented perception, and its geometry influenced decorative arts, textile design and psychedelic art of the 1960s.
