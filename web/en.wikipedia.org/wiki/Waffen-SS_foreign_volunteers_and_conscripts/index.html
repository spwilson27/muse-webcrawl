During the Second World War, the Waffen-SS recruited large numbers of foreign volunteers and conscripts from German-occupied Europe and beyond. What began in 1940 with small units of volunteers from Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, and Flanders — justified under the SS's "Germanic" racial ideology — expanded dramatically after the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, as the war against the USSR was framed in Nazi propaganda as a European crusade against Bolshevism.

Recruitment and composition

Early formations included the Wiking division, with Dutch, Flemish, Danish, and Norwegian volunteers. As manpower needs grew, recruitment broadened far beyond the "Germanic" peoples: French, Walloon, Spanish, Italian, Croatian, Bosnian Muslim (the Handschar division), Albanian, Hungarian, Romanian, Serbian, Bulgarian, and Cossack units were raised, as were two Latvian and one Estonian division. In the Baltic states and Ukraine, service was frequently by conscription rather than volunteering; by 1944–45 non-Germans may have made up more than half of the Waffen-SS's strength, which approached a million men on paper.

Motivations varied: anti-communism, nationalism and hopes of independence, opportunism, coercion, and material inducement. Foreign-manned units fought principally on the Eastern Front and in the Balkans, where many took part in anti-partisan warfare; Waffen-SS formations, including foreign ones, were implicated in numerous war crimes and atrocities.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials the SS was declared a criminal organisation, though conscripts who had committed no crimes were exempted from the judgment in principle. Baltic conscripts were treated by the Western Allies as distinct from ideological volunteers in many cases, while men from Western Europe were commonly tried in their home countries for treason and collaboration. The legacy of foreign Waffen-SS service remains contested, especially in the Baltic states and Ukraine, where veterans have sometimes been commemorated as anti-Soviet national fighters — commemorations widely criticised internationally.
