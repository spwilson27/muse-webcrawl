A pickled cucumber — commonly known as a pickle in the United States and Canada and traditionally as a gherkin in Britain, Ireland, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand — is a usually small or miniature cucumber that has been pickled in a brine, vinegar, or other solution and left to ferment for a period of time, either by immersion in an acidic solution or through souring by lacto-fermentation.

Methods

In brine fermentation, cucumbers are submerged in salted water and colonised by Lactobacillus bacteria, producing lactic acid and the cloudy brine, complex flavour, and soft tang of "kosher dill" and half-sour pickles. Vinegar pickling is faster and gives a sharper, more uniform result; most commercial pickles — including the enormous output once shipped by firms such as Heinz — are vinegar-pickled and pasteurised for shelf stability.

Varieties

  • Dill pickles — flavoured with dill and garlic; spears, chips, and wholes.
  • Gherkins and cornichons — small, bumpy cucumbers; cornichons are the tart French style served with charcuterie.
  • Bread-and-butter pickles — sweet, sliced, with onion and mustard seed.
  • Half-sours — lightly brined, bright green, and crisp, sold refrigerated.

In culture

Pickles are a global preservation tradition, kin to the mixed-vegetable piccalilli of Britain and the oil-and-spice pickles of South Asia. The pickle appears in sandwiches, salads, and as the classic deli accompaniment; pickle juice has even found modern fame as a briny cocktail ingredient and sports drink.
