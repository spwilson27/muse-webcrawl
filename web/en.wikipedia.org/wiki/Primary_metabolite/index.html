Primary metabolite

A primary metabolite is a chemical compound produced by an organism's normal growth, development and reproduction — part of the core metabolic network found, in broadly similar form, across many forms of life. Primary metabolites are directly involved in essential processes: they include amino acids, nucleotides, simple sugars, organic acids, vitamins, and the building blocks of proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and carbohydrates. They contrast with secondary metabolites, which are not required for growth and often serve ecological roles such as defence or signalling.

Characteristics

Primary metabolites are typically produced during the growth phase of an organism or culture (in microbes, the trophophase), accumulate in significant quantities, and are widely distributed across taxa because the pathways making them — glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, amino-acid biosynthesis — are ancient and conserved. Failure to produce a primary metabolite is usually lethal or severely disabling, whereas loss of a secondary metabolite typically is not. The boundary is not absolute: some compounds, such as certain pigments and hormones, blur the distinction.

Industrial importance

Because microbes make them in bulk, primary metabolites anchor much of the fermentation industry. Ethanol, citric acid, glutamic acid (for monosodium glutamate), lysine and other amino acids, lactic acid, and several B vitamins are produced at scales of millions of tonnes per year by engineered strains of bacteria and fungi, using strain improvement and metabolic engineering to divert carbon flux toward the desired product. Primary metabolite overproduction is also the starting point for making many secondary products, including antibiotics.

Examples

Common examples include glucose and other monosaccharides; the twenty standard amino acids; nucleotides such as ATP; fatty acids; organic acids of the citric acid cycle (citrate, succinate, malate); and vitamins such as riboflavin. In plants, primary metabolism encompasses photosynthesis, respiration and the synthesis of structural polymers such as cellulose and lignin, while alkaloids, terpenoids and most pigments of flowers count as secondary.
