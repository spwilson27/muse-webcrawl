Transplant rejection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transplant rejection occurs when the immune system of a transplant recipient attacks the transplanted organ or tissue, recognising it as foreign. The recipient's immune system identifies antigens on the donor organ — chiefly the highly variable human leukocyte antigens (HLA) — as non-self and mounts a response that can damage or destroy the graft.

Types

Hyperacute rejection occurs within minutes to hours and is caused by pre-formed antibodies against the graft, now largely prevented by cross-matching. Acute rejection develops over days to months and is driven mainly by T lymphocytes reacting against donor HLA; it is graded histologically from biopsy samples, e.g. by the Banff classification for kidney grafts. Chronic rejection is a slow, progressive loss of graft function over months to years involving fibrosis and vascular changes, and is a leading cause of late graft failure.

Prevention and treatment

Rejection risk is reduced by matching donor and recipient HLA types and blood groups, and by lifelong immunosuppressive therapy. Standard regimens combine a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or ciclosporin), an antiproliferative agent (mycophenolate), and corticosteroids; antibody therapies such as basiliximab or anti-thymocyte globulin may be used for induction. Episodes of acute rejection are treated with high-dose corticosteroids or antibody therapy. Immunosuppression trades rejection risk against increased susceptibility to infection and malignancy.

Graft-versus-host disease

The inverse phenomenon, graft-versus-host disease, occurs mainly after haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, when immune cells in the graft attack the recipient's tissues.
