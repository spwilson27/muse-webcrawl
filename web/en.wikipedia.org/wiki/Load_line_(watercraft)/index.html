The load line, also known as the Plimsoll line or Plimsoll mark, is a marking painted on the hull of a merchant ship that indicates the maximum depth to which the vessel may be safely loaded — that is, the maximum permitted draft — for given water conditions. It ensures that a ship retains sufficient freeboard and reserve buoyancy so that it does not become dangerously overloaded.

History

Rules on loading existed as early as medieval maritime codes, but systematic enforcement came in the 19th century. Overloading of unseaworthy "coffin ships" for insurance profit caused heavy loss of life in British shipping. The politician Samuel Plimsoll campaigned for reform, and the Merchant Shipping Act 1876 made a load line mark compulsory on British ships, though its positioning was initially left to shipowners. Subsequent legislation in 1890 and later acts placed the assignment of the line under the Board of Trade and classification societies. The International Convention on Load Lines (1930, and the 1966 convention under the IMO) standardised the rules worldwide.

The marks

The load line mark consists of a circle bisected by a horizontal line — the Plimsoll mark — together with a grid of secondary lines ("the comb") whose levels correspond to different densities and seasonal conditions:

  • TF – tropical fresh water
  • F – fresh water
  • T – tropical sea water
  • S – summer sea water
  • W – winter sea water
  • WNA – winter North Atlantic

The summer line (S) is at the same level as the line through the Plimsoll circle. Fresh water lines are higher because a ship floats deeper in less dense fresh water; the allowance is S + Δ/4, where Δ is the ship's displacement-related fresh water allowance. The winter North Atlantic line is the lowest, providing extra freeboard for severe weather.

Purpose and enforcement

By limiting loading, the load line guarantees adequate stability and seaworthiness. Port officials and surveyors can verify compliance at a glance: if the applicable line is submerged, the ship is overloaded and may be detained. Load lines are assigned by classification societies such as Lloyd's Register, DNV or the American Bureau of Shipping, acting on behalf of flag states.
