Sewall Wright, a founder of population genetics

Sewall Green Wright (December 21, 1889 – March 3, 1988) was an American geneticist known for his influential work on evolutionary theory and for inventing path analysis, a statistical method for analysing causal systems. Together with Ronald Fisher and J. B. S. Haldane, he was a founder of population genetics, the mathematical discipline that underpinned the modern evolutionary synthesis.

Life and work

Wright studied zoology at Harvard, completing his doctorate in 1915 on the inheritance of coat colour in guinea pigs. From 1915 to 1925 he worked at the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Bureau of Animal Industry, studying inbreeding in livestock (his brother Quincy Wright became a noted political scientist; another brother, Theodore Paul Wright, an aeronautical engineer). He then spent three decades at the University of Chicago before moving to the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1955, remaining scientifically active into his late nineties; his four-volume treatise Evolution and the Genetics of Populations appeared between 1968 and 1978.

Contributions

Wright introduced the coefficient of inbreeding (F) and the F-statistics (FST and relatives) still used to measure population structure. His shifting balance theory proposed that evolution proceeds most effectively in populations subdivided into small, semi-isolated demes: genetic drift lets a deme cross a "valley" in the adaptive landscape to reach a higher fitness peak, selection improves the combination within the deme, and successful demes then export migrants to convert the species as a whole — a long-running contrast with Fisher's emphasis on selection in large panmictic populations. Wright's metaphor of the fitness (adaptive) landscape became one of evolutionary biology's enduring images. He also developed the method of path coefficients (1918–1921), the first rigorous approach to causal modelling from correlations, later foundational in statistics, econometrics, and structural equation modelling. His later debate with Fisher over the evolution of dominance and the causes of evolutionary change was one of the great scientific disputes of the century.

Honours

Wright received the National Medal of Science (1966) and the Darwin Medal of the Royal Society (1980), among many honours, and was a member of the National Academy of Sciences.
