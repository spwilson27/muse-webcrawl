A volcanic bomb or lava bomb is a mass of partially molten rock (tephra) larger than 64 mm (2.5 inches) in diameter, formed when a volcano ejects viscous fragments of lava during an eruption. Because volcanic bombs cool after they leave the vent, they are extrusive igneous rocks. They can be thrown many kilometres from an erupting vent and often acquire distinctive shapes through aerodynamic forces while molten and in flight.

Types

Volcanic bombs are classified by shape, which reflects the fluidity and gas content of the lava and its motion through the air:

Spherical bombs form from low-viscosity lava with little deformation. Spindle or fusiform bombs are elongated by rotation in flight. Cow-dung bombs form when still-fluid bombs flatten on impact. Breadcrumb bombs have cracked, crust-like surfaces produced when the outer skin solidifies while the interior continues to expand as gases escape. Ribbon bombs are drawn out into irregular strips, and cored bombs enclose a fragment of older rock within a rim of lava.

Hazards

Bombs pose a serious ballistic hazard near erupting vents: large bombs may retain enough heat and mass to start fires, destroy structures and kill. Observers have been injured or killed by bombs at volcanoes such as Stromboli and Kīlauea even during relatively mild eruptions.
