Surfactant

Surfactants are chemical compounds that decrease the surface tension or interfacial tension between two liquids, a liquid and a gas, or a liquid and a solid. The word is a contraction of surface-active agent. Surfactants are usually amphiphilic organic compounds, with a hydrophilic ("water-loving") head and a hydrophobic ("water-hating") tail, so they adsorb at interfaces and can form structures such as micelles.

Mechanism

Because of their dual affinity, surfactant molecules accumulate at interfaces, with tails pointing away from water and heads in it. Above a characteristic concentration, the critical micelle concentration (CMC), they aggregate into micelles whose hydrophobic cores can solubilise oils and greases — the basis of detergency. Surfactants also act as wetting agents, emulsifiers, foaming agents and dispersants.

Classification

  • Anionic — negative head group; e.g. soaps (fatty-acid salts), sodium dodecyl sulfate, linear alkylbenzene sulfonates in laundry detergents.
  • Cationic — positive head group; e.g. quaternary ammonium compounds such as benzalkonium chloride, used as disinfectants and fabric softeners.
  • Non-ionic — uncharged; e.g. alcohol ethoxylates and polysorbates, common in cosmetics and low-foam detergents.
  • Zwitterionic (amphoteric) — both charges; e.g. cocamidopropyl betaine in shampoos.

Applications

Surfactants are indispensable in detergents and soaps, paints, inks, pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, food processing (as emulsifiers), oil recovery, flotation in mining, and firefighting foams. In the human body, pulmonary surfactant — a lipoprotein mixture of phospholipids (chiefly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) and surfactant proteins — coats the alveoli, reducing surface tension so the lungs can inflate; its deficiency in premature infants causes respiratory distress syndrome, treated since the 1980s with replacement surfactant preparations.

Environmental issues

Some surfactants are toxic to aquatic life, and poorly biodegradable branched alkylbenzene sulfonates caused persistent foam in rivers in the 1950s–60s until replaced by linear, biodegradable forms. Fluorinated surfactants (PFAS), prized for repelling both water and oil, are environmentally persistent and increasingly restricted.
