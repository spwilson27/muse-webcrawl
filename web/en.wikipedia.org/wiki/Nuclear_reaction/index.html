Nuclear reaction

In nuclear physics and nuclear chemistry, a nuclear reaction is a process in which two nuclei, or a nucleus and an external subatomic particle, collide to produce one or more new nuclides. Thus, a nuclear reaction must cause a transformation of at least one nuclide to another. This contrasts with radioactive decay, which is spontaneous, and with chemical reactions, which involve only electrons.

Notation and conservation laws

Reactions are written in the form a + X → b + Y, often abbreviated X(a,b)Y. For example, the first artificially produced nuclear reaction, performed by Ernest Rutherford in 1919, was 14N(α,p)17O. In all reactions, charge, baryon number, momentum and total energy are conserved; the difference in rest mass appears as kinetic energy or gamma radiation (the Q value).

Types of reaction

  • Nuclear fission — a heavy nucleus, struck by a neutron, splits into two lighter nuclei with neutrons and energy; the basis of nuclear power and atomic bombs.
  • Nuclear fusion — light nuclei combine into a heavier nucleus, as in the sun and in hydrogen bombs.
  • Neutron capture — a nucleus absorbs a neutron, key to nucleosynthesis (s-process and r-process) in stars.
  • Scattering — elastic or inelastic collisions in which the identities of the nuclei are unchanged but energy and momentum are exchanged.
  • Spallation — a high-energy particle shatters a nucleus into many fragments.
  • Alpha, proton and gamma-induced reactions — used in laboratories, particle accelerators and astrophysical modelling.

Cross sections

The probability of a reaction is expressed as a cross section, measured in barns (10−28 m2), which generally varies strongly with the energy of the incoming particle and may show sharp resonances. Chain reactions occur when neutrons released by one fission go on to induce further fissions at a self-sustaining rate.
