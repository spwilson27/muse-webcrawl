The Sack of Rome, then part of the Papal States, on 6 May 1527 was carried out by mutinous troops of Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor, during the War of the League of Cognac. The largely unpaid Imperial army — Spanish soldiers, German Landsknechte, and Italian mercenaries — marched on Rome, stormed the city, and subjected it to months of plunder, violence, and occupation. Pope Clement VII escaped to the Castel Sant'Angelo and later capitulated.

Background

The Italian Wars pitted Habsburg Spain and the Holy Roman Empire against France for control of Italy. After the Imperial victory at Pavia in 1525, Pope Clement VII joined the League of Cognac with France, Venice, Florence, and Milan against Charles V. The Imperial army in Italy, chronically unpaid, mutinied and marched south under Charles III, Duke of Bourbon, effectively beyond its commanders' control.

The assault

On 6 May 1527 the Imperial troops attacked the walls of the Borgo in thick fog. The Duke of Bourbon was killed early in the assault — the goldsmith Benvenuto Cellini later claimed to have fired the shot. The papal defenders, including the Swiss Guard, were overwhelmed; in the famous last stand of the Swiss Guards, 147 guardsmen died defending St. Peter's steps and the Pope's escape along the Passetto to Castel Sant'Angelo, while 42 survivors escorted Clement to safety.

The sack

For days the city was looted systematically. Churches, palaces, and tombs were plundered; clergy and civilians were killed, ransomed, or tortured for hidden wealth. Lutheran Landsknechte added mockery of Catholic ritual to the violence. The population of Rome fell sharply through massacre, famine, and plague, and the occupying troops remained for months, extracting a huge ransom under the capitulation agreed in June 1527.

Consequences

The sack shocked Europe and is often taken as a symbolic end of the Italian Renaissance in Rome; artists and scholars fled, and papal authority was humbled before the Emperor. Clement VII, in no position to resist, later crowned Charles V at Bologna in 1530 and refused Henry VIII of England's request for an annulment — a refusal entangled with the Pope's dependence on Charles, whose aunt Catherine of Aragon was Henry's wife — helping set the stage for the English Reformation.
