Carpet bombing
Also calledArea bombing, saturation bombing
First major useSpanish Civil War; Second World War

Carpet bombing, also known as saturation bombing or area bombing, is a large-area aerial bombing tactic in which a heavy concentration of unguided bombs is dropped so as to devastate an entire target area, like a carpet laid across the ground. Rather than aiming at a single precise point, aircraft release their loads in dense patterns, accepting the destruction of everything within the bombed zone.

History

The tactic emerged in the 1930s, notably with the bombing of Guernica (1937) during the Spanish Civil War, and reached its greatest scale in the Second World War. The British Royal Air Force adopted night area bombing of German cities—including Hamburg (1943) and Dresden (1945)—under Bomber Command, while the United States Army Air Forces practiced mass formation bombing by day. Carpet bombing was also used tactically to break front lines, as in Operation Cobra (1944) during the breakout from Normandy, where heavy bombers pounded a narrow strip ahead of advancing troops, and in the destruction of Saint-Lô and Caen.

After 1945, the United States used carpet bombing extensively in Korea and in Southeast Asia, where B-52 Stratofortresses dropped enormous tonnages during Operations Arc Light, Linebacker, and the secret bombing of Cambodia—by some estimates more explosives than were used in all of the Second World War.

Law and criticism

Area bombing of cities has been widely condemned for its indiscriminate killing of civilians. The 1977 Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions prohibits bombardment that treats separated military objectives within a concentration of civilians as a single target, effectively outlawing carpet bombing of populated areas for states party to the protocol. The spread of precision-guided munitions since the 1990s has made saturation bombing militarily less necessary, though the term remains in common use.
