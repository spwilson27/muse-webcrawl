Battle for Caen

The Battle for Caen was a series of operations fought between 6 June and 6 August 1944, during the Battle of Normandy, for control of the city of Caen and its surroundings. Caen, a road and communications hub about 14 km inland from the D-Day beaches, was an objective for the British 3rd Infantry Division on 6 June itself, but strong German resistance — above all from the 21st Panzer Division and later the 12th SS Panzer Division Hitlerjugend — turned the sector into a two-month battle of attrition. Operations Perch, Epsom, Windsor, Charnwood, Jupiter, Goodwood, Atlantic and Spring progressively ground down the German forces; the northern half of the ruined city fell on 9 July after Operation Charnwood and a heavy RAF bombing, and the whole city was cleared by 19 July. The fighting around Caen drew in and consumed the bulk of Germany's armour in Normandy, enabling the American breakout (Operation Cobra) in the west.

Course of operations

The initial rush for Caen stalled on D-Day at the strongpoints of the 21st Panzer Division, the only armoured division to reach the coast that day. Operation Perch (7–14 June), including the repulse of Michael Wittmann's Tigers at Villers-Bocage, failed to envelop the city. Operation Epsom (26 June–1 July) crossed the Odon and seized the vital Hill 112 but was contained by II SS Panzer Corps. Operation Charnwood (8–9 July), preceded by a controversial heavy bombing of the northern suburbs, finally took Caen north of the Orne. In the east, Operation Goodwood (18–20 July) — the largest tank battle yet fought by the British Army — and the Canadian Operation Atlantic cleared the southern suburbs, including the Bourguébus Ridge fighting in which the 12th SS and other units bled themselves resisting. The Canadian Operation Spring (25 July) toward Falaise was costly and unsuccessful, but by then German reserves were exhausted; Caen's fall and the attrition it imposed had set the conditions for the Normandy breakout.

Debate and legacy

The battle has generated enduring historiographical debate: whether Caen was meant to be taken on D-Day, whether Montgomery's strategy of drawing German armour onto the British-Canadian sector was deliberate from the start, and the cost-effectiveness and civilian toll of heavy bombing. The city, rebuilt in the 1950s, hosts the Mémorial de Caen peace museum, one of France's leading Second World War memorials.
