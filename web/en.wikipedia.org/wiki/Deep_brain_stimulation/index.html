Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a neurosurgical procedure involving the placement of a medical device called a neurostimulator, which sends electrical impulses through implanted electrodes to specific targets in the brain (brain nuclei) for the treatment of movement disorders, including Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, dystonia, and other brain conditions. While its underlying principles and mechanisms are not fully understood, DBS has proven to have significant benefits for some patients.

The deep brain stimulation system consists of three components: the implanted pulse generator (IPG), the lead, and an extension. The IPG is a battery-powered neurostimulator encased in a titanium housing, which sends electrical pulses to the brain that interfere with neural activity at the target site. The lead is a coiled wire insulated in polyurethane with four platinum–iridium electrodes, placed in one or both target nuclei. The lead is connected to the IPG by the extension, an insulated wire that runs below the skin, from the head, down the side of the neck, behind the ear, to the IPG, which is placed subcutaneously below the clavicle.

Medical uses

Parkinson's disease

DBS is used to manage some of the symptoms of Parkinson's disease that cannot be adequately controlled with medications. It is recommended for people who have Parkinson's disease with motor fluctuations and tremor inadequately controlled by medication, or for those who are intolerant to medication, as long as they do not have severe neuropsychiatric problems. Four areas of the brain have been used, though the subthalamic nucleus and globus pallidus internus are the most common targets.

Essential tremor and dystonia

DBS targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus is an established treatment for essential tremor. It is also approved under a humanitarian device exemption for dystonia, a movement disorder in which sustained muscle contractions cause twisting and repetitive movements.

Psychiatric disorders

DBS has been studied in small clinical trials for treatment-resistant major depression and obsessive–compulsive disorder. In 2009 the United States Food and Drug Administration approved DBS for obsessive–compulsive disorder under a humanitarian device exemption.

History

Electrical stimulation of deep brain structures was explored in the mid-20th century, and modern chronic DBS emerged in the 1980s. In 1987, Alim-Louis Benabid and colleagues in Grenoble, France, reported that high-frequency stimulation of the thalamus suppressed tremor, an observation made during thalamotomy surgery. Subsequent work extended the technique to the subthalamic nucleus for Parkinson's disease, and the FDA approved DBS for essential tremor in 1997 and for Parkinson's disease in 2002.

Mechanism of action

The exact mechanism of DBS is not fully understood. It is thought to modulate pathological network activity rather than simply inhibiting or exciting local neurons: high-frequency stimulation may produce an informational lesion, disrupt abnormal oscillations, and affect neurotransmitter release in connected structures.

Adverse effects

Potential complications include surgical risks such as haemorrhage and infection, hardware problems (lead migration or fracture, battery failure), and stimulation-related effects such as paraesthesia, muscle contractions, speech disturbance and, in some patients, mood changes including apathy or hypomania.
