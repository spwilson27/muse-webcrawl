Paranoia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

SpecialtyPsychiatry, psychology
SymptomsDistrust, hypervigilance, fear, misinterpretation of others' actions as threatening, belief in conspiracy theories, suspicion of others' motives

Paranoia is an instinct or thought process that is believed to be heavily influenced by anxiety or fear, often to the point of delusion and irrationality. Paranoid thinking typically includes persecutory beliefs, or beliefs of conspiracy concerning a perceived threat towards oneself (e.g., the American expression "Everyone is out to get me"). Paranoia is distinct from phobias, which also involve irrational fear, but usually no blame.

Making false accusations and the general distrust of other people also frequently accompany paranoia. For example, a person who is paranoid might believe that an incident was intentional when most people would view it as an accident or coincidence. Paranoia is a central symptom of psychosis.

History

The word paranoia comes from the Greek παράνοια (paranoia), "madness", and that from παρά (para), "beside, by", and νόος (noos), "mind". The term was originally used to describe a mental illness in which a delusional belief was the sole or most prominent feature. In this definition, the belief does not have to be persecutory to be classified as paranoid, so any number of delusional beliefs can be classified as paranoia.

Throughout the 19th century the concept shifted. Emil Kraepelin used the term in his classification of mental disorders, distinguishing paranoia from dementia praecox and manic-depressive illness. Later writers, including those associated with Sigmund Freud, used it largely for delusional states organized around persecution and grandiosity.

Signs and symptoms

The most common symptoms include:

  • Fear, anxiety, and emotional distress
  • Hypervigilance toward the environment in a search for threats
  • Mistrust of others and beliefs that others mean harm
  • Difficulty forgiving and feelings of resentment
  • Subtle beliefs of conspiracy that are not fully delusional

Paranoid symptoms may range from mild personality traits to frank psychotic disorders such as delusional disorder, persecutory type, and paranoid schizophrenia. Paranoia may also occur in the context of substance use, particularly with stimulants, and in some neurological conditions.

Causes

The causes of paranoia are not fully understood. Research implicates genetic vulnerability, neurochemical abnormalities (particularly involving dopamine), childhood trauma, social isolation, stress, and sleep disturbance. Cannabis, amphetamines, and other drugs can precipitate or worsen paranoid states. Social and psychological theories emphasize the role of low self-esteem, threat anticipation, and reasoning biases such as jumping to conclusions.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on clinical interview and history. Paranoid ideation is assessed in the context of disorders including schizophrenia, delusional disorder, paranoid personality disorder, and major mood disorders with psychotic features.

Treatment typically involves antipsychotic medication for psychotic forms and psychotherapy, particularly cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for psychosis, for addressing distressing beliefs and safety behaviours. Establishing trust with the patient is considered important because suspicion often extends to clinicians.
