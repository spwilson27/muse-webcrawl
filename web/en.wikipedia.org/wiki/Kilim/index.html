Kilim

An Anatolian kilim with geometric tribal motifs
TypeFlat-woven textile (rug, hanging, saddlebag)
TechniqueSlit tapestry weave (weft-faced plain weave)
RegionsAnatolia, the Balkans, Persia, the Caucasus, Central Asia
MaterialsWool (traditionally), cotton warp

A kilim (Turkish: kilim; Persian: گلیم, gelim) is a flat tapestry-woven textile, traditionally made as a rug or floor covering across a broad region from the Balkans through Anatolia and Persia to Central Asia. Unlike pile carpets, kilims have no raised nap: their patterns are formed entirely by the coloured weft threads of a slit-weave technique, giving them a flat, reversible surface.

Technique

Kilims are woven on a loom with a (usually cotton or wool) warp that is completely covered by the weft. Where two colour areas meet, the weaver returns the weft around the last warp of its own area, producing characteristic narrow slits between blocks of colour — the "slit tapestry" structure that constrains designs to bold, stepped and diagonal geometry. Weavers worked from memory rather than cartoons, so motifs vary freely and asymmetries are prized as marks of the individual maker.

Motifs and symbolism

Traditional kilim designs use stylised geometric motifs to which tribal and folk meanings are attached: the hooked elibelinde ("hands on hips") figure for motherhood and fertility, the koçboynuzu (ram's horn) for strength and heroism, the star, the comb, the evil-eye (göz) amulet for protection, and the tree of life. Regional schools — Anatolian, Balkan (Pirot, Chiprovtsi), Persian (Senneh, Bakhtiari), Caucasian and Central Asian — each developed distinctive palettes based on local natural dyes such as madder red, indigo blue and walnut brown.

Uses and history

Beyond floor coverings, flatweaves served as tent hangings, prayer rugs, grain sacks, saddlebags and dowry pieces across nomadic and village life. Antique kilims became collectible in the West from the late 19th century and strongly influenced modernist tastes for abstraction. Today production continues both as a living craft — often by women's cooperatives — and as an industrial product, while fine antique pieces command high prices at auction.
