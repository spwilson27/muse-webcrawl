The I Ching (Book of Changes) is an ancient Chinese divination manual and one of the oldest Chinese classics. Its core consists of 64 hexagrams — figures of six broken and unbroken lines — each with cryptic judgements and line texts, consulted traditionally by casting yarrow stalks or coins. Appended commentaries, the 'Ten Wings' traditionally ascribed to Confucius, transformed it into a philosophy of change, yin and yang, and timely action, studied by scholars and rulers for over two millennia.

Influence

The I Ching structured Chinese cosmology alongside Confucianism and Taoism; Leibniz, receiving its hexagram order from China, saw in it a confirmation of binary arithmetic, and C. G. Jung's foreword to the Wilhelm translation made it famous in the West as a vehicle of 'synchronicity'. It remains in daily use as a book of wisdom and oracle.
