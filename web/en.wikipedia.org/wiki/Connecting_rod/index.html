Connecting rod

A connecting rod, often called a con rod, is the engine component that connects a piston to the crankshaft. Together with the crank, the connecting rod forms a simple mechanism that converts the reciprocating motion of the piston into rotary motion of the crankshaft — or, in compressors and pumps, the reverse. Connecting rods are used in internal combustion engines, steam engines, and reciprocating compressors.

Design

A typical connecting rod consists of a small end that pivots on the piston's gudgeon (wrist) pin, a shank (usually of I-beam or H-beam cross-section for stiffness at low weight), and a big end that encircles the crankpin, split into a rod and a cap joined by bolts or studs so it can be assembled onto the crankshaft. Plain bearings (bushes in the small end; split shell bearings in the big end) carry the oscillating and rotating loads and are supplied with pressurized oil, frequently delivered through drillings in the crankshaft and sometimes up the shank itself. Rods must withstand enormous tensile and compressive forces: at high engine speeds the inertia of the piston tries to stretch the rod at top dead centre, while firing pressure compresses it.

Materials and manufacture

Automobile rods are commonly forged from steel or powdered metal (sinter-forged, with the cap separated by fracture-splitting for a perfectly matched joint), or cast. High-performance and racing engines use forged aluminium or titanium rods for lightness. In very large engines — such as marine diesels and steam locomotives — connecting rods and related motion components (crossheads, piston rods, and coupling rods) are correspondingly massive and often of distinctive forms.

Failure

Connecting rod failure is usually catastrophic: a broken rod may be thrown through the crankcase ("throwing a rod"), destroying the engine. Causes include bearing seizure from oil starvation, bolt failure, fatigue, and overspeeding.
