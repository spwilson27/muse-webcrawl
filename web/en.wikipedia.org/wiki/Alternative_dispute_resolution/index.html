Alternative dispute resolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alternative dispute resolution (ADR) refers to processes for resolving disputes outside the courts — principally negotiation, mediation and arbitration — and, by extension, hybrids such as med-arb, early neutral evaluation and ombuds schemes. Mediation, a facilitated negotiation by a neutral who does not decide, dominates family, community and much commercial practice (settlement rates are commonly reported in the 60–80 per cent range where mediation is attempted); arbitration is adjudicative — a private tribunal renders a binding award, enforceable internationally under the New York Convention of 1958, which has more than 170 parties.

The ADR movement expanded from the 1970s (the 1976 Pound Conference is the conventional landmark) under pressure of court delay and cost, and is now institutionalised: courts annex mediation programmes, contracts embed ADR clauses, and legislation promotes pre-action settlement. Critiques persist — privatised justice lacks precedent and publicity, repeat-player advantages (Galanter) may favour corporations, and mandatory arbitration clauses in consumer and employment contracts (upheld in the US by cases such as AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion, 2011, curtailing class actions) are argued to suppress claims rather than resolve them.
