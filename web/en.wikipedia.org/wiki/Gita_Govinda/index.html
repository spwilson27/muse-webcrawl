Gita Govinda

The Gita Govinda (Sanskrit: गीतगोविन्दम्, "Song of Govinda") is a 12th-century Sanskrit lyric poem by the poet Jayadeva, composed at the court of the Ganga dynasty in the region of modern Odisha. It describes the love of the god Krishna (Govinda) and his consort Radha — their separation, longing and reunion — and is one of the most influential works in Indian literature, devotion and the arts.

Structure and content

The poem is arranged in twelve cantos (sargas) containing twenty-four songs (prabandhas) set to specific ragas and talas, framed by narrative verses. Dramatised through the voices of Krishna, Radha and her confidante (the sakhi), the work begins with a celebrated invocation of the ten avatars of Vishnu (the Dashavatara). Its refrain songs — most famously Dhira-samire Yamuna-tire — remain staples of Indian classical music and Odissi dance.

Influence

The Gita Govinda shaped the development of bhakti devotion centred on Radha-Krishna across Bengal, Odisha, Assam and Braj, and it inspired centuries of painting (notably in the Pahari and Rajput schools), temple sculpture, and the Odissi and Manipuri dance traditions; it is still sung nightly at the Jagannath Temple in Puri. The poem's erotic mysticism — earthly love as an allegory of the soul's longing for God — made it a touchstone of Vaishnava theology. The first English translation was by Sir William Jones in 1792; Friederike Rückert's German version later inspired scholarly and literary enthusiasm in Europe.
