Assumption of Mary

Assumption of Mary
Observed byRoman Catholic Church; related Dormition feast in Eastern Christianity
Date15 August
Dogma defined1950, by Pope Pius XII

The Assumption of Mary is one of the four Marian dogmas of the Catholic Church. It teaches that the Virgin Mary, "having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory". Pope Pius XII defined the dogma in the apostolic constitution Munificentissimus Deus on 1 November 1950, exercising papal infallibility — the only such exercise since the First Vatican Council defined the doctrine in 1870. The feast of the Assumption is celebrated on 15 August and is a holy day of obligation in many countries, as well as a public holiday in several.

History and tradition

Scripture does not record Mary's death; the tradition developed in apocryphal accounts of her "falling asleep" (Dormition) and translation to heaven, which circulated from about the fourth or fifth century. The feast of the Dormition was celebrated in the East by the sixth century, fixed on 15 August under Emperor Maurice, and spread to the West. Medieval theologians debated whether Mary died before her assumption; the dogmatic definition deliberately leaves that question open, and the Eastern Orthodox Church celebrates the Dormition, teaching that Mary died and was then resurrected and taken up.

Theology

Catholics understand the Assumption as the fitting conclusion of Mary's role as Mother of God and her freedom from original sin (the Immaculate Conception): the first fruits of the resurrection promised to all believers, anticipated in her case. Most Protestant churches reject the doctrine as lacking scriptural warrant, though some Anglicans and Lutherans keep the 15 August feast as a commemoration of Mary.

In art

The Assumption is a major subject of Western art — famous treatments include Titian's huge altarpiece in the Frari in Venice, works by Rubens and Murillo, and Caravaggio's Death of the Virgin, which approaches the related moment.
