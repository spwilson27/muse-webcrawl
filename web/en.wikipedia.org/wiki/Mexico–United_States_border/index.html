The Mexico–United States border is the international boundary between Mexico and the United States, extending 3,145 kilometres (1,954 miles) from the Pacific Ocean near San Diego and Tijuana in the west to the Gulf of Mexico near Brownsville and Matamoros in the east. It is the most frequently crossed international border in the world, with hundreds of millions of legal crossings each year.

Geography

From El Paso, Texas, eastward, the border follows the Rio Grande for roughly 2,000 kilometres to the Gulf of Mexico. West of El Paso it runs overland across the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts and mountain ranges to the Pacific coast. Four US states and six Mexican states adjoin the border, and the twin-city pairs along it — such as San Diego–Tijuana and El Paso–Ciudad Juárez — form one of the largest binational metropolitan regions in the world.

History

The modern boundary was largely fixed by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), which ended the Mexican–American War and transferred Mexico's northern territories to the United States, and adjusted by the Gadsden Purchase of 1853. The International Boundary and Water Commission, founded in 1889, manages boundary and water issues, including shifts in the course of the Rio Grande.

Crossings, trade and security

Dozens of official ports of entry carry enormous flows of trade — Mexico is among the United States' largest trading partners — as well as commuters, shoppers and tourists. The border is also a focus of US immigration enforcement, drug-trafficking interdiction and debate over border barriers; substantial stretches are fenced or walled, while other stretches, particularly along the Rio Grande and in remote desert, have only vehicle barriers or natural obstacles. Migration, asylum policy and border security remain prominent issues in the politics of both countries.
