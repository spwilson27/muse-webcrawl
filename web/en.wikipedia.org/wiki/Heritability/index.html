Heritability

FieldQuantitative genetics, statistics
Symbolh2 (narrow-sense), H2 (broad-sense)
Range0 to 1

Heritability is a statistic used in the fields of breeding and genetics that estimates the degree of variation in a phenotypic trait in a population that is due to genetic variation between individuals in that population. In other words, the concept of heritability can be expressed in the form of the following question: "What is the proportion of phenotypic variation in a population that is attributable to genetic variation?"

Heritability is estimated by comparing individual phenotypic variation among related individuals in a population, by examining the associations between individual phenotype and genotype data, or even by modeling summary-level data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Heritability is an important concept in quantitative genetics, particularly in selective breeding and behavior genetics.

Definition

Phenotypic variance in a population is the sum of genetic variance and environmental variance, together with their interaction and covariance. Broad-sense heritability, H2, is defined as the proportion of phenotypic variance that is attributable to all genetic variance: H2 = VG/VP. Narrow-sense heritability, h2, is the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to additive genetic variance alone, and it is this quantity that predicts the response of a trait to artificial or natural selection.

Estimation methods

Classic designs include parent–offspring regression, in which the slope of the regression of offspring phenotype on the mid-parent value estimates narrow-sense heritability; full-sib and half-sib analyses of variance; and twin studies, which compare monozygotic and dizygotic twins reared together or apart. Modern molecular approaches, such as GREML as implemented in GCTA, estimate the variance explained by genotyped single-nucleotide polymorphisms directly from unrelated individuals.

Interpretation

Heritability is frequently misunderstood. A heritability of 0.7 for a trait does not mean that 70 percent of an individual's trait value is caused by genes; it describes variation in a particular population under particular environmental conditions. Heritability can change if the environment changes or if allele frequencies change. Highly heritable traits can still be strongly affected by environmental interventions: human height, for example, is highly heritable yet average height has risen substantially with improved nutrition.

Examples

In humans, twin studies typically estimate the heritability of height at around 0.8. In livestock breeding, heritabilities of production traits such as milk yield in dairy cattle are often in the range 0.25–0.35, while some carcass traits exceed 0.4. Traits closely tied to fitness tend to show lower narrow-sense heritabilities because selection erodes additive genetic variance.
