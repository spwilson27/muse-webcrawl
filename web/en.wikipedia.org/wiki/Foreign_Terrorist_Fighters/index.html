Foreign Terrorist Fighters

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foreign terrorist fighters (FTFs) is a term used by the United Nations and security agencies for individuals who travel abroad to join or support terrorist groups and participate in armed conflict or terrorist acts. The modern usage centres on the tens of thousands who travelled to Syria and Iraq after 2011 to join the Islamic State (IS) and other jihadist factions, though foreign volunteers in conflicts have a long history, from the Afghan Arabs of the 1980s to earlier ideological brigades.

Scale

Estimates by the UN and research centres such as the Soufan Group put the flow to Syria and Iraq at more than 40,000 fighters from over 100 countries at its peak, including several thousand from Europe and large contingents from Tunisia, Saudi Arabia, Russia and Central Asia. Women and children travelled in significant numbers as family members, creating acute legal and humanitarian questions when the IS "caliphate" collapsed in 2017–2019.

International response

In September 2014 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2178, requiring member states to criminalise travel for terrorist purposes, financing and recruitment, and to strengthen border controls and information sharing. States have responded variously with passport confiscation, prosecution on return, citizenship deprivation, deradicalisation programmes and — controversially — leaving nationals, including children, in detention camps in north-east Syria such as al-Hol.

Debate

Policymakers debate the actual threat posed by returnees: many are disillusioned or traumatised rather than committed attackers, yet a small number have carried out attacks in their home countries. Researchers also note the definitional problem that the "terrorist" label depends on which groups a state designates, complicating data and law.
