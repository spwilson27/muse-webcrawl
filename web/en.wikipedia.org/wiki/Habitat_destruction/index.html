Habitat destruction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Habitat destruction is the process by which a natural habitat becomes unable to support the species that live there. It is the foremost cause of species extinction and biodiversity decline worldwide, operating through outright habitat loss, fragmentation of what remains into isolated patches, and degradation that lowers habitat quality.

Forms and effects

Conversion of land for agriculture is the largest driver, followed by urbanisation, mining, infrastructure and, in the oceans, trawling and coastal development. Fragmentation creates 'edge' effects and isolates populations, which then suffer inbreeding and local extinction; corridors and stepping-stone habitats can reconnect them. Degradation includes pollution, altered fire and water regimes, invasive species and climate change — which shifts suitable climate zones, sometimes off the tops of mountains or beyond the poles.

Conservation responses

Protected areas now cover roughly a sixth of land and a smaller share of the oceans, with a global target ('30 by 30') of 30% by 2030 agreed in 2022. Habitat restoration, rewilding, conservation easements and biodiversity offsets aim to slow or reverse losses, and environmental impact assessment is meant to prevent the worst damage in advance.
