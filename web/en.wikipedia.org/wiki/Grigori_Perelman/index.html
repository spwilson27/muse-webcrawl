Grigori Yakovlevich Perelman (born 13 June 1966) is a Russian mathematician who proved the Poincaré conjecture, one of the most famous problems in mathematics, posed in 1904 and listed among the Millennium Prize Problems. His proof, posted to the arXiv in 2002–2003 in three papers on Ricci flow with surgery, also established Thurston's geometrisation conjecture. Perelman famously declined both the Fields Medal in 2006 and the US$1,000,000 Millennium Prize in 2010, and withdrew from professional mathematics.

Early life and career

Perelman was born in Leningrad and trained at Leningrad Secondary School No. 239, a school with advanced mathematics and physics programmes; he won a gold medal with a perfect score at the 1982 International Mathematical Olympiad. He earned his PhD at Leningrad State University and worked at the Steklov Institute. In the early 1990s he held positions in the United States, where he proved the soul conjecture, before returning to St. Petersburg in 1995, declining offers from leading universities.

The Poincaré proof

Building on Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow programme, Perelman introduced new ideas — including an entropy formula for the flow and a non-collapsing theorem — and a technique of surgery to remove singularities, taming the flow long enough to read off the topology of the underlying 3-manifold. Between November 2002 and July 2003 he posted three preprints. Teams led by Cao and Zhu, Kleiner and Lott, and Morgan and Tian verified the proof over the following years; by 2006 the mathematical community accepted it, and Science named the proof its "Breakthrough of the Year" — the first time the honour went to mathematics.

Refusal of prizes and later life

Perelman declined the Fields Medal saying he was not interested in fame and that the medal was "completely irrelevant" to him; he later declined the Millennium Prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute, objecting that Richard Hamilton's contribution was no less than his and criticising the organised mathematical community's ethics. He left his post at the Steklov Institute around 2005–2006 and has lived reclusively in St. Petersburg with his mother, avoiding journalists. His story was told in Masha Gessen's biography Perfect Rigor (2009).
