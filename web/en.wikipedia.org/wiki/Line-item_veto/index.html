Line-item veto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The line-item veto is a power, granted to an executive in some systems, to reject individual provisions (line items) of a bill — typically appropriations items — without vetoing the whole. Most US state governors possess it; the President of the United States does not, a distinction settled after a brief experiment: the Line Item Veto Act of 1996 authorised the President to cancel specific spending and tax-benefit provisions, President Clinton exercised it 82 times, and the Supreme Court struck the Act down in Clinton v. City of New York (1998) as violating the Presentment Clause — lawmaking must follow bicameral passage and presentment of the whole bill.

Supporters argue the item veto curbs pork-barrel spending and logrolling; opponents reply that it shifts the power of the purse to the executive, that governors use it to punish legislators, and that evidence for large savings is thin. Congress has periodically proposed constitutional or statutory substitutes (expedited rescission procedures), none enacted as of the mid-2020s. In other presidential systems (much of Latin America), partial vetoes are a routine and accepted feature.
