George Caspar Homans (August 11, 1910 – May 29, 1989) was an American sociologist, a founder of behavioural sociology and social exchange theory, and the 49th president of the American Sociological Association. A member of a prominent Boston family — he was a descendant of presidents John Adams and John Quincy Adams — he spent his entire academic career at Harvard University.

Career

Homans graduated from Harvard College in 1932 in English literature and drifted into sociology through the university's "Pareto circle" around physiologist L. J. Henderson, and through the Harvard Business School research milieu of Elton Mayo. His first book, English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century (1941), was a work of medieval social history based on manorial court rolls. After naval service in the Second World War, he returned to Harvard, joining the new Department of Social Relations.

Major works

  • The Human Group (1950): a systematic re-analysis of five classic small-group studies, introducing the distinction between the "external system" (behaviour adapting to the environment) and the "internal system" (emergent sentiments and norms).
  • Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms (1961): the classic statement of Homans's exchange theory, reducing social interaction to the exchange of rewards and costs governed by propositions drawn from behavioural psychology — the success, stimulus, value, deprivation–satiation, and aggression–approval propositions, plus the rationality proposition.
  • "Social behavior as exchange" (1958), his influential presidential-address-era paper, and his 1964 ASA presidential address "Bringing men back in", a famous critique of structural-functionalism and Talcott Parsons.

Approach and legacy

Homans insisted that sociological explanations must ultimately be grounded in propositions about individual behaviour, a position sometimes called psychological reductionism, which he defended cheerfully as the correct logic of explanation. His theory of distributive justice — that people expect rewards proportional to their investments — fed directly into equity theory. Though his deductive programme was overtaken by other movements in sociology, social exchange theory remains foundational in sociology, anthropology, and organisational studies. His memoir Coming to My Senses (1984) is a candid account of his life and of Harvard sociology.
