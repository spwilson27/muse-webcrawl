Commodification

Commodification describes the transformation of goods, services, ideas and people into commodities.

Commodification is the transformation of goods, services, ideas, nature, personal information or people into commodities or objects of trade. A commodity at its most basic, according to Arjun Appadurai, is "anything intended for exchange", or any object of economic value.

Commodification is often criticised on the grounds that some things ought not to be treated as commodities — for example water, education, data, information, knowledge, human life, and animal life. Karl Marx extensively analysed the topic in Capital.

Terminology

The earliest use of the word "commodification" in English attested in the Oxford English Dictionary dates from 1975. The related term "commoditization" originated in business contexts to describe the process by which goods that have economic value and are distinguishable in terms of attributes end up becoming simple commodities in the eyes of the market or consumers.

Marxist theory

In Marxist theory, commodification is the process of assigning economic value to a good, service or entity that previously did not have such value. The transformation of labour itself into a commodity — labour power sold on the market — is central to Marx's account of capitalism. Marx's concept of commodity fetishism describes how social relationships between people come to be masked by relationships between things.

Forms and examples

Scholars and critics discuss many domains in which commodification is said to occur:

  • Human and animal bodies — the sale of organs, surrogate pregnancy, and the trade in blood and other body products.
  • Culture — the conversion of cultural heritage, festivals and identity into marketable products, sometimes called the commodification of culture or cultural appropriation for profit.
  • Nature — the assignment of market prices to water, land, carbon emissions and ecosystem services.
  • Public goods — the privatisation of education, healthcare and other services previously provided outside the market.
  • Data and attention — the treatment of personal information and user attention as commodities in digital platform economies.

Criticism

Critics argue that commodification can erode social values, deepen inequality, and subject essential needs to market logic. Defenders of market expansion counter that commodification can improve allocation, signal scarcity, and extend access. The debate continues in fields such as bioethics, environmental economics, and the sociology of consumption.
