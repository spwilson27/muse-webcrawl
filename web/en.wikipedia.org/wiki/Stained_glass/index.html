Stained glass

Stained glass is coloured glass used to make decorative windows and other objects through which light passes. Traditionally, pieces of glass coloured with metallic salts during manufacture are arranged in pictorial designs and held together by strips of lead (cames) within a rigid frame.

A medieval rose window; stained glass is seen by transmitted light

Technique

Pot-metal glass is coloured throughout by metal oxides — cobalt for blue, copper for green and ruby red, manganese for purple. Details are painted on with grisaille (vitreous paint) and silver stain, which produces yellows, then fired. The pieces are assembled with lead cames, soldered, and cemented to make the panel weatherproof.

History

The art reached its height in the Gothic cathedrals of the 12th–16th centuries — Chartres, Sainte-Chapelle, York Minster — where vast windows narrated Bible stories to largely illiterate congregations. It declined after the Renaissance, was revived by the Gothic Revival and Arts and Crafts movements in the 19th century, and flourished anew in the work of Louis Comfort Tiffany and, later, Marc Chagall and contemporary architectural glass artists.
