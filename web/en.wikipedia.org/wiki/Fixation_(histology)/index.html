Fixation (histology)

PurposePreservation of biological tissue
Common fixativesFormalin, glutaraldehyde, ethanol, Bouin's solution

In histology, pathology and cell biology, fixation is the preservation of biological tissues from decay by terminating ongoing biochemical reactions and increasing the tissue's mechanical strength and stability. It is the first step in preparing samples for examination under a microscope or for long-term storage.

Methods

Chemical fixation immerses tissue in a fixative. Cross-linking fixatives such as formaldehyde (used as 10% neutral buffered formalin) form methylene bridges between proteins; precipitating fixatives such as ethanol and methanol denature proteins. Glutaraldehyde is preferred for electron microscopy because it preserves ultrastructure, while Bouin's solution is used for delicate tissues. Physical methods include heat fixation of bacterial smears and rapid freezing (cryofixation), which preserves enzyme activity and antigens better than chemical treatment.

Considerations

Fixation must balance preservation against distortion: underfixation permits autolysis, while overfixation can mask antigens and degrade nucleic acids. Fixatives penetrate tissue slowly — roughly a millimetre per hour for formalin — so specimens are sliced thin and given ample time, commonly 24 hours or more, before processing, embedding and sectioning.
