The Iran crisis of 1946, also known as the Azerbaijan crisis, was one of the first confrontations of the Cold War. It arose when the Soviet Union refused to withdraw its troops from northern Iran after the end of the Second World War, in breach of the 1942 Tripartite Treaty, and sponsored two separatist states: the Azerbaijan People's Government and the Kurdish Republic of Mahabad.

Background

British and Soviet forces had occupied Iran in 1941 to secure supply lines and oilfields and to depose Reza Shah, whose sympathies were seen as pro-German. Under the Tehran Declaration of 1943 the occupiers pledged to leave within six months of the war's end. When the March 1946 deadline passed, Soviet forces remained and backed autonomous governments in Tabriz and Mahabad.

Resolution

Iran brought the first complaint ever heard by the newly formed United Nations Security Council. Under combined UN and American diplomatic pressure — historians debate whether President Truman issued an explicit ultimatum — Stalin agreed in April 1946 to evacuate, in exchange for a promised (but later rejected by the Iranian Majlis) Soviet oil concession in the north. Soviet troops withdrew by May 1946, and Iranian forces reoccupied Azerbaijan and Kurdistan in December, ending both republics. The crisis helped prompt the Truman Doctrine and the Western policy of containment of Soviet expansion.
