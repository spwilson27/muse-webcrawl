Six Principles of Chinese painting

Six Principles
Chinese六法 (liùfǎ)
AuthorXie He (謝赫), 6th century CE
SourceRecord of the Classification of Old Painters (古畫品錄)

The Six Principles of Chinese painting (Chinese: 六法; pinyin: liùfǎ) were formulated by the Chinese art historian and critic Xie He (active 5th–6th centuries CE) in the preface to his Record of the Classification of Old Painters. They became the most influential framework for evaluating painting in China and, later, in Japan and Korea.

The six principles

  1. Spirit resonance (qìyùn shēngdòng 氣韻生動) — "Spirit Resonance (or Vibration of Vitality) and Life Movement": the painting should capture the living spirit and energy of its subject. This was considered the most important principle.
  2. Bone method (gǔfǎ yòngbǐ 骨法用筆) — "Bone Method in Using the Brush": confident, structural brushwork, closely tied to calligraphy.
  3. Correspondence to the object (yìngwù xiàngxíng 應物象形) — fidelity to the appearance of the thing depicted.
  4. Suitability to type (suílèi fùcǎi 隨類賦彩) — correct application of colour according to the nature of the subject.
  5. Division and planning (jīngyíng wèizhì 經營位置) — composition and placement of elements.
  6. Transmission by copying (chuányí móxiě 傳移模寫) — learning and preserving tradition through copying old masters.

Influence

Xie He's principles framed connoisseurship for over a millennium; later critics such as Zhang Yanyuan discussed and ranked them. The first principle, spirit resonance, elevated inner vitality over mere likeness and underpins the literati ideal of painting as an expression of the artist's character.
