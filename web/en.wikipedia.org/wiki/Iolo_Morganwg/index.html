Iolo Morganwg (10 March 1747 – 18 December 1826), born Edward Williams, was a Welsh antiquarian, poet and collector, famous — and notorious — for his invention and forgery of Welsh literary tradition. Taking his bardic name from Morganwg, the Welsh name for his native Glamorgan, he founded the Gorsedd of the Bards of the Isle of Britain, whose rituals still form part of the modern National Eisteddfod. Many of the "ancient" manuscripts and poems he produced are now known to be his own creations.

Life

Williams was born at Pen-onn in the Vale of Glamorgan and worked for much of his life as a stonemason, travelling widely in Wales and England collecting manuscripts and oral traditions. Largely self-educated, he acquired a formidable knowledge of Welsh literature and a command of the classical metres. In London in the 1770s he attached himself to the Welsh literary societies, and in later years he was associated with radical and Unitarian circles. He suffered from asthma, for which he took laudanum, becoming addicted — a fact sometimes invoked to explain his fabrications. He spent his later years at Flemingston (Trefflemin) in Glamorgan, running a small school and shop, and continued to write and compile until his death.

Gorsedd and Druidic revival

In 1792 Iolo held the first meeting of the Gorsedd on Primrose Hill in London, presenting it as a revival of the assemblies of the ancient British bards and Druids, complete with a circle of stones, bardic grades (Ovate, Bard and Druid) and a peace ritual with a sheathed sword. He elaborated an elaborate "Barddas" theology and philosophy which he attributed to ancient Welsh sources. In 1819 the Gorsedd was attached to the eisteddfod at Carmarthen, beginning the association that continues today: the Gorsedd ceremony, with its colourful robes and the Chairing and Crowning of the winning poets, is Iolo's invention, though it is now cherished as a Welsh national institution.

Forgeries

Iolo forged on a grand scale. He produced poems which he attributed to the 14th-century master Dafydd ap Gwilym (several were included in the 1789 edition of Dafydd's works), fabricated genealogies and chronicles, and compiled the so-called third series of the Welsh Triads. His manuscripts were used in good faith by the editors of The Myvyrian Archaiology of Wales (1801–1807), which became a foundational source for Welsh studies. Suspicion grew during the 19th century, and the scholar G. J. Williams demonstrated conclusively in the 20th century that much of Iolo's material was invented. His son Taliesin Williams published some of his papers posthumously, including the Iolo Manuscripts (1848) and the unfinished Cyfrinach Beirdd Ynys Prydain.

Legacy

Although exposed as a forger, Iolo Morganwg remains a central figure in Welsh cultural history. His fabrications were motivated in part by regional pride — he sought to prove that Glamorgan had preserved the true bardic tradition and a strain of primitive Unitarian Christianity untouched by Rome — and in part by genuine creative genius. Modern scholarship regards his invented traditions as among the most influential in Europe, comparable to Macpherson's Ossian, while the Gorsedd he created continues as the ceremonial heart of the National Eisteddfod.
