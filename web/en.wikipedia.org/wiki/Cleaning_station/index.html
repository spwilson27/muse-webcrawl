Cleaning station

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cleaning station is a location where wild animals — most famously marine fish, sea turtles, and sharks — congregate to be cleaned by smaller animals, such as cleaner shrimp and cleaner wrasse, which remove parasites, dead tissue, and debris from the client's body, gills, and mouth.

Marine stations

On coral reefs, cleaner fish occupy small territories that clients visit repeatedly, adopting signaling postures (hovering head-up or head-down) to indicate they are not hunting. Studies show that reefs deprived of cleaner wrasse lose fish diversity and that clients' parasite loads rise, making cleaning a keystone mutualism. Even predators such as groupers and moray eels allow cleaners to enter their mouths unharmed.

Terrestrial analogues

Oxpeckers perform a similar role on African megafauna, and giant tortoises present themselves to finches in the Galápagos. Konrad Lorenz documented comparable behavior in terrestrial birds and mammals.

Significance

Cleaning symbioses illustrate mutualism maintained by partner choice and punishment of cheating (cleaners that bite mucus instead of parasites). Stations are focal points for divers and for reef ecology research.
