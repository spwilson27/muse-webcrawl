Piltdown Man

The Piltdown Man was a paleoanthropological hoax in which bone fragments were presented as the fossilised remains of a previously unknown early human. The fragments, a skull and jawbone, were supposedly collected in 1912 from a gravel pit at Piltdown, East Sussex, England. The Latin name given to the specimen, Eoanthropus dawsoni ("Dawson's dawn-man"), honoured the collector, Charles Dawson.

The discovery

In December 1912, Dawson and Arthur Smith Woodward of the British Museum (Natural History) announced that a workman at the Piltdown gravel pit had found a human-like skull and an ape-like jaw. The discovery was greeted as the long-sought "missing link" between ape and human, because it appeared to combine a large, modern braincase with a primitive jaw — exactly what then-current theories of human evolution predicted.

Exposure

Doubts grew over the decades, especially as genuine fossils from Africa showed the opposite pattern: small brains with human-like jaws. In 1953, fluorine testing and close physical examination exposed the Piltdown remains as a forgery: the skull was that of a modern human, the jaw that of an orangutan, the teeth had been filed, and the bones had been artificially stained to appear ancient.

The perpetrator

The identity of the forger remains debated. Charles Dawson is the principal suspect, though others — including Martin Hinton, Arthur Keith, and even the writer Arthur Conan Doyle — have been accused. A 2016 study concluded that the forgery was most likely the work of Dawson alone, using consistent methods across the Piltdown I and Piltdown II finds.

Significance

The Piltdown hoax delayed the acceptance of genuine African fossils such as the Taung Child by decades and remains one of the most famous scientific frauds in history.
