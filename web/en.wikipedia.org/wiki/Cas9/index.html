Cas9 (CRISPR associated protein 9, formerly called Cas5, Csn1, or Csx12) is a 160 kilodalton protein which plays a vital role in the immunological defense of certain bacteria against DNA viruses and plasmids, and is heavily utilized in genetic engineering applications. Its main function is to cut DNA and thereby alter a cell's genome. The CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing technique was a significant contributor to Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna winning the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.

Function in CRISPR immunity

In bacteria and archaea, CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) sequences, together with Cas proteins, form an adaptive immune system that stores fragments of invading viral DNA as "spacers". When the invader returns, spacer-derived RNA guides the Cas9 enzyme to the matching foreign DNA. Cas9 unwinds the foreign DNA and, if it is complementary to the guide RNA and adjacent to a short DNA motif called a PAM (protospacer adjacent motif), cleaves both strands of the target DNA using its HNH and RuvC nuclease domains, disabling the virus.

Genome editing

More technically, Cas9 is a dual RNA-guided DNA endonuclease enzyme. In 2012, teams led by Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, and separately work by Virginijus Šikšnys, showed that Cas9 could be reprogrammed with a synthetic single guide RNA (sgRNA) to cut any chosen DNA sequence in vitro. Feng Zhang's and George Church's laboratories then demonstrated its use in human and mouse cells in 2013. Since then, CRISPR-Cas9 has become the most widely used genome-editing tool in biology, applied in basic research, agriculture, and medicine — including the first approved CRISPR therapy, for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia.

Structure

Cas9 has a bilobed architecture: a recognition (REC) lobe that binds the guide RNA and target DNA, and a nuclease (NUC) lobe containing the RuvC and HNH nuclease domains and the PAM-interacting domain. Binding of the guide RNA triggers a large conformational rearrangement that activates the enzyme for target cleavage.

Variants and related enzymes

Engineered variants include catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9), used for gene regulation (CRISPR interference and activation), nickase Cas9, and high-fidelity versions with reduced off-target activity. Other Cas effectors, such as Cas12a (Cpf1) and Cas13 (which targets RNA), have broadened the editing toolbox.
