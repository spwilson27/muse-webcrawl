The Inland Revenue was, until April 2005, a department of the British Government responsible for the collection of direct taxation, including income tax, national insurance contributions, capital gains tax, inheritance tax, corporation tax, and stamp duty, as well as the payment of child benefit and tax credits. The name distinguished its domestic ("inland") revenue functions from those of HM Customs and Excise, which collected duties at the frontier.

History

The Board of Inland Revenue was created by the Inland Revenue Board Act 1849, merging the boards of stamps and taxes with the excise administration (excise was later transferred to Customs in 1909). Income tax itself had been introduced by Pitt the Younger in 1799 to fund the Napoleonic Wars, abolished, and reintroduced permanently by Robert Peel in 1842. The department administered the introduction of PAYE (Pay As You Earn) in 1944 and of self-assessment in the 1990s.

Merger into HMRC

Following the O'Donnell review, the Inland Revenue merged with HM Customs and Excise on 18 April 2005 to form HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC), a single department for nearly all UK tax collection. The term "the Revenue" remains common British shorthand for the tax authorities.

In popular culture

The Inland Revenue featured widely in British humour and song as the archetypal taxman — the Beatles' "Taxman" (1966), written by George Harrison, famously protested the 95% top marginal rate of the era administered under the supertax (surtax) regime.
