Castile and León

Castile and León (Spanish: Castilla y León [kasˈtiʎa i leˈon]) is an autonomous community in north-western Spain. It is the largest autonomous community in Spain by area, covering 94,222 km² — about a fifth of the national territory — but is sparsely populated, with roughly 2.4 million inhabitants. Its de facto capital and largest city is Valladolid, seat of the regional government (the Junta) and parliament (the Cortes).

History

The community was created in 1983 by merging the historic regions of Old Castile and León, whose kingdoms — the Kingdom of León (910) and the County, later Kingdom, of Castile — were central to the Christian reconquest of Iberia and were definitively united under Ferdinand III in 1230. The region contains an extraordinary concentration of Romanesque architecture and eight UNESCO World Heritage Sites, including the old towns of Ávila, Salamanca and Segovia, Burgos Cathedral, the Atapuerca archaeological site and Las Médulas gold mines.

Geography and economy

The region occupies the northern part of the Meseta Central plateau, drained by the Duero (Douro) river and ringed by mountain ranges including the Cantabrian Mountains and the Sierra de Gredos. Its economy rests on agriculture (cereals, sugar beet, vineyards — the Ribera del Duero wine region is famous worldwide), livestock, food processing, and automobile manufacturing around Valladolid and Palencia. Population decline and rural depopulation — the so-called España vaciada ("emptied Spain") — are major political issues.
