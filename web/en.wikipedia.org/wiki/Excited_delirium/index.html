Excited delirium

Excited delirium (also called agitated delirium) is a controversial proposed condition characterised by agitation, aggression, acute distress, and hyperthermia, often in the setting of drug intoxication or psychiatric illness. The term has been used especially in connection with deaths in police custody.

History

Descriptions of fatal agitated states date to Luther Bell's 1849 account of "Bell's mania." The modern term was popularised in the 1980s by Charles Wetli and colleagues in Miami, describing deaths associated with cocaine use. It gained prominence in forensic and emergency-medicine literature in North America.

Controversy

Excited delirium is not recognised as a diagnosis in the DSM-5, and the American Medical Association and American Psychiatric Association oppose its use as a diagnosis. Critics argue the term has been applied disproportionately to rationalise deaths during restraint, and several jurisdictions have restricted or banned its use as a cause of death. The American College of Emergency Physicians endorsed the concept in 2009 but withdrew that endorsement in 2023.

Signs and proposed mechanisms

Reported features include extreme agitation, imperviousness to pain, unusual strength, sweating, and elevated body temperature. Proposed mechanisms involve catecholamine excess and dopamine dysregulation, compounded by stimulant use, metabolic acidosis, and restraint. Treatment of severe agitation focuses on rapid sedation and management of hyperthermia and acidosis.
