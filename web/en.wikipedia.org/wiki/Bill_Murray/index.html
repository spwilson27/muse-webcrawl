William James Murray (born September 21, 1950) is an American actor and comedian. Known for his deadpan delivery, he first rose to fame on Saturday Night Live (1977–1980), then headlined a string of hit comedies including Meatballs (1979), Caddyshack (1980), Stripes (1981), Ghostbusters (1984), and Groundhog Day (1993). He later won critical acclaim for dramatic and melancholic roles, notably in Lost in Translation (2003), for which he received an Academy Award nomination.

Early life

Murray was born in Evanston, Illinois, and raised in Wilmette, one of nine children in an Irish Catholic family. He worked as a golf caddy — experience that informed his role in Caddyshack — and studied briefly at Regis University in Denver before joining Chicago's Second City improvisational troupe at the invitation of his brother Brian Doyle-Murray. He then worked with John Belushi on The National Lampoon Radio Hour.

Career

After a season on a short-lived ABC variety show, Murray joined NBC's Saturday Night Live in its second season, eventually succeeding Chevy Chase's Weekend Update slot and creating characters such as Nick the lounge singer. His film career took off with Meatballs and continued through the 1980s with Ghostbusters, one of the highest-grossing comedies of its time. A dramatic detour, The Razor's Edge (1984), which he co-wrote, failed commercially, and he withdrew from acting for several years.

Murray returned with Scrooged (1988) and achieved enduring success with Groundhog Day, directed by frequent collaborator Harold Ramis. Beginning with Rushmore (1998), director Wes Anderson cast him in nearly all of his films, and Sofia Coppola's Lost in Translation brought him the BAFTA and Golden Globe for Best Actor and an Oscar nomination. Later work includes Broken Flowers, Zombieland (playing himself), St. Vincent, and voice work in animated films.

Public image

Murray is a famously elusive public figure: he has no agent or manager and famously considers scripts submitted through a 1-800 number. Stories of his spontaneous appearances at parties, kickball games, and strangers' events have given him a folk-hero status sometimes called "the Murray legend." He is an avid golfer, a fixture at the Pebble Beach Pro-Am, and a co-owner of minor-league baseball teams.
