Arche

Archē (Greek: ἀρχή) is a Greek word with primary senses "beginning", "origin", "source of action", and later "first principle" or "element". It is a central concept in the philosophy of the Presocratics and was adopted by Aristotle as a technical term for a first principle or starting point of knowledge and of being.

In Presocratic philosophy

The earliest Greek philosophers sought a single underlying principle from which all things arise and to which they return. Thales of Miletus proposed water as the archē; Anaximander argued instead for the apeiron, the indefinite or boundless; Anaximenes chose air. Heraclitus pointed to fire and, more fundamentally, to the logos governing change, while the Pythagoreans held number to be the principle of all things. Empedocles multiplied the archai into four roots — earth, air, fire, and water — moved by Love and Strife, and Democritus proposed atoms and the void.

Aristotle

In the Physics and Metaphysics, Aristotle defines an archē as "the first point from which a thing either exists or comes into being or is known". He catalogues several senses: the starting point of motion, the best point from which to begin, the immanent source of generation (such as the keel of a ship), an external source (such as parents of a child), that by whose choice things are moved (hence rulers and governments, archai in the political sense), and the first principle of knowledge, such as the premises of a demonstration.

Later usage

The term passed into theology, where the Gospel of John opens with the words "In the beginning (en archē) was the Word". In modern philosophy and the sciences its derivatives survive in words such as "archaic", "archetype", and "anarchy" (the absence of an archē, or ruler).
