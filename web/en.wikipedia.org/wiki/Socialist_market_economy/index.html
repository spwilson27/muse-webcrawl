The socialist market economy is the economic system of the People's Republic of China. It combines a predominantly state-owned economy with market mechanisms, private enterprise and openness to foreign investment, under the political leadership of the Chinese Communist Party.

Background

The concept emerged from the reform and opening-up programme launched after the Third Plenum of the 11th Central Committee in 1978. Deng Xiaoping's formulation "socialism with Chinese characteristics" provided the ideological cover for introducing market forces into a centrally planned economy. The term "socialist market economy" was formally adopted at the 14th National Congress of the CCP in 1992.

Characteristics

Key features include:

  • Public ownership as the mainstay: state-owned enterprises dominate strategic sectors such as energy, banking, telecommunications and defence.
  • Market allocation: prices for most goods are set by supply and demand rather than the state.
  • Macroeconomic control: five-year plans set broad development goals while the market guides day-to-day activity.
  • Mixed ownership: private firms, joint ventures and wholly foreign-owned enterprises operate alongside SOEs.

Development

Successive reforms dismantled the commune system, created special economic zones beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, permitted township and village enterprises, and culminated in WTO accession in 2001. By the 2010s China had become the world's largest economy by purchasing power parity.

Assessment

Supporters credit the model with the fastest sustained poverty reduction in history. Critics argue that political control over capital allocation breeds corruption, overcapacity and opacity, and question whether the system is meaningfully "socialist" at all. The European Union and the United States have at various times refused to grant China market-economy status in trade disputes.
