Acousmatic music

Acousmatic music is a form of electroacoustic music composed for presentation through loudspeakers, in which the sound sources are typically not visible or identifiable. The word "acousmatic" derives from the Greek akousmatikoi, the disciples of Pythagoras who listened to their teacher from behind a curtain so that his appearance would not distract from his words; the term was revived by the writer Jérôme Peignot and adopted by composer Pierre Schaeffer in the 1950s.

Concepts

Schaeffer proposed reduced listening (écoute réduite) — attending to sound as an object in itself (the objet sonore), abstracted from its cause or meaning. Acousmatic works are fixed on a playback medium (tape, and later digital files), composed in the studio from recorded and synthesized sounds, and "performed" in concert by diffusion: a performer balances and spatializes the sound over an orchestra of loudspeakers, such as François Bayle's Acousmonium (1974), an array of some 80 speakers of varying sizes and characters.

History and practitioners

Acousmatic practice grew out of Schaeffer's musique concrète in Paris (from 1948) and the work of the Groupe de Recherches Musicales (GRM). Major figures include François Bayle, Bernard Parmegiani, Luc Ferrari, Michel Chion (a leading theorist of the acousmatic), Beatriz Ferreyra, and, in the UK and Canada, the communities around the University of Birmingham's BEAST system, Denis Smalley's writings on spectromorphology, and Francis Dhomont. Festivals such as Futura (France) and organisations like the Canadian Electroacoustic Community promote the repertoire.

Aesthetics

Because sources may be unidentifiable, acousmatic music plays with ambiguity between abstract sound-shapes and recognizable real-world references ("anecdotal" sound). Smalley's concept of source bonding describes listeners' natural tendency to relate sounds to imagined sources, a tendency composers may exploit or frustrate.
