Safari

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A safari is an overland journey to observe or, historically, to hunt wild animals, especially in eastern and southern Africa. The word comes from the Swahili safari, meaning a journey or expedition, itself borrowed from Arabic safar (travel). The term became established in English in the late nineteenth century as European and American travellers undertook big-game expeditions with professional hunters, porters and caravans.

History

Early safaris were hunting expeditions lasting weeks or months, of which Theodore Roosevelt's 1909–1910 Smithsonian expedition is among the most famous. The hunting safari gave rise to a distinctive culture and dress — khaki clothing, pith helmets, and the safari jacket — and to literary works by writers such as Ernest Hemingway, whose stories drew on his East African trips.

Modern safaris

Today most safaris are photographic and conservation-oriented. Game drives in national parks and private reserves — such as the Serengeti, Maasai Mara, Kruger and Okavango Delta — focus on viewing the "Big Five" (lion, leopard, elephant, rhinoceros and buffalo) and other wildlife from open vehicles or on foot. Ecotourism revenue is a major funding source for protected areas, though its distribution and impact remain debated. Regulated trophy hunting continues in some countries and is defended by proponents as a conservation incentive and condemned by critics on ethical grounds.
