Strabismus

Strabismus is a vision disorder in which the two eyes do not properly align with each other when looking at an object. The eye that is focused on an object can alternate, and the misalignment may be present occasionally or constantly. Other terms for the condition include squint, crossed eyes and wall-eye. Strabismus may cause loss of depth perception and, in children, amblyopia ("lazy eye") as the brain suppresses the image from the deviating eye.

Classification

Strabismus is named by the direction of the turn of the misaligned eye: esotropia (inward, "cross-eyed"), exotropia (outward, "wall-eyed"), hypertropia (upward) and hypotropia (downward). It may be comitant (the angle is the same in all directions of gaze) or incomitant (varying with gaze, often due to paralysis of an extraocular muscle or its cranial nerve).

Causes

Most childhood strabismus results from a combination of hereditary factors, refractive errors such as uncorrected hyperopia, and the brain's control of eye alignment. In adults, new-onset strabismus with double vision may indicate cranial nerve palsy from stroke, diabetes, trauma or tumour, and warrants prompt investigation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis uses the cover test and cover–uncover test, corneal light reflex tests (Hirschberg and Krimsky), and measurement of the deviation with prisms. Vision in each eye is assessed, and children are examined for amblyopia.

Treatment

Treatment aims to realign the eyes and preserve or restore binocular vision. Options include corrective glasses, patching the stronger eye to treat amblyopia, prism lenses, injections of botulinum toxin into an extraocular muscle, and eye muscle surgery, in which muscles are recessed, resected or repositioned to change their effective pull.
