Orisha

An altar with offerings to the orishas.

Orishas (singular: orisha; Yoruba: òrìṣà) are spirits that play a key role in the Yoruba religion of West Africa and several religions of the African diaspora that derive from it, such as Cuban, Dominican and Puerto Rican Santería and Brazilian Candomblé. The preferred spelling varies depending on the language in question: òrìṣà is the spelling in the Yoruba language, orisha, orisá, and orixá in Spanish, Portuguese and some English contexts. According to the teachings of these religions, the orishas are spirits sent by the supreme creator, Olodumare, to assist humanity and to teach them to be successful on Earth (Ayé). They are venerated through offerings, festivals, divination, and spirit possession, in which an orisha is said to "mount" a devotee.

Principal orishas

Tradition speaks of 401 orishas, though in practice a smaller number receive regular worship. Among the best known are:

  • Èṣù (Eshu/Eleguá) — messenger of the gods, guardian of crossroads, trickster and opener of ways; no ritual begins without honouring him.
  • Ògún (Ogun) — orisha of iron, war, labour, and technology.
  • Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀sì (Ochosi) — hunter and patron of justice.
  • Ṣàngó (Shango) — deified king of Oyo, lord of thunder and lightning.
  • Ọya (Yansá) — warrior orisha of winds, storms, and the cemetery gates.
  • Òṣun (Oshun) — orisha of fresh waters, love, beauty, and fertility.
  • Yemọja (Yemaya) — motherly orisha of the sea and motherhood.
  • Obàtálá (Oxalá) — elder orisha of purity and creation, who fashioned human bodies.
  • Ọ̀rúnmìlà — orisha of wisdom and divination, presiding over the Ifá oracle.
  • Ọsanyìn (Osain) — orisha of herbs and healing.

Worship and diaspora

Worship centres on altars housing the consecrated stones and implements of each orisha, offerings of food, drumming and song that invite possession, and divination with cowrie shells (erindilogun) or the Ifá system supervised by priests called babalawos. Carried to the Americas by enslaved Yoruba people, orisha worship survived through syncretism with Roman Catholic saints — Shango with Saint Barbara, Oshun with Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre — and now counts tens of millions of adherents worldwide.
