Quantum Hall effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph of Hall resistance showing flat quantised plateaus versus magnetic field]
Quantised Hall plateaus at exact fractions of h/e².

The quantum Hall effect (QHE) is a quantum-mechanical version of the Hall effect, observed in two-dimensional electron systems subjected to low temperatures and strong magnetic fields, in which the Hall resistance takes on quantised values R_xy = h/(νe²) with extraordinary precision. The integer effect was discovered by Klaus von Klitzing in 1980 (Nobel Prize 1985) — the resistance quantum h/e² ≈ 25,812.8 Ω is now called the von Klitzing constant and (since the 2019 SI revision) defines the ohm in terms of fixed h and e.

The fractional quantum Hall effect (Tsui, Störmer, 1982; explained by Laughlin, 1983; Nobel 1998) occurs at fractional fillings ν = 1/3, 2/5… and revealed a new state of matter: an incompressible quantum fluid whose excitations carry fractional charge (e/3) and anyonic statistics. The QHE founded the modern field of topological phases of matter — its quantisation is protected by topology (a Chern number), an idea extended to topological insulators and sought in Majorana systems for fault-tolerant Quantum computing. Its robustness makes it both a metrological standard and a paradigm: quantisation from topology, not microscopic detail.

Mechanism in brief

In a strong field, electrons occupy discrete Landau levels; when a level is exactly filled, disorder localises the excess states and the Hall conductivity locks to e²/h per level with a plateau. The bulk is insulating while dissipationless current flows in chiral edge channels — the picture from which the topological interpretation (TKNN invariant, 1982) follows.
