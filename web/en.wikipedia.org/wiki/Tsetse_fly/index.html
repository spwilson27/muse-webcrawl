Tsetse flies (genus Glossina) are large biting flies of mid-continental Africa, roughly 8–17 mm long, recognisable by their forward-pointing proboscis, hatchet-shaped wing cell, and by folding their wings flat over the abdomen at rest. They feed exclusively on the blood of vertebrates and are the biological vectors of trypanosomes, the parasites that cause African sleeping sickness in humans and nagana in livestock.

Biology

Unlike most insects, tsetse have an unusual reproduction: the female produces a single larva at a time, nourishing it inside her uterus with secretions from a "milk gland" — a form of adenotrophic viviparity. The mature larva burrows into the soil and pupates, emerging as an adult after about a month. This low reproductive rate makes tsetse populations vulnerable to control measures such as insecticide-treated targets, traps, bush clearing, and the sterile insect technique.

Disease impact

Human African trypanosomiasis, caused by Trypanosoma brucei gambiense and T. b. rhodesiense, was historically devastating, with epidemics killing hundreds of thousands; treatment and vector control have reduced reported cases to under a thousand per year in recent times. The animal disease nagana still excludes cattle-keeping from some 10 million square kilometres of fertile land, with large economic consequences.

Ecologically, tsetse have been called "the best game wardens in Africa": by making land dangerous for cattle and people, they preserved vast wildlife habitats that later became national parks.
