The Vandals under King Genseric sacked Rome for fourteen days in June 455.

The sack of Rome in 455 AD was carried out by the Vandals, a Germanic people under King Genseric, who had established a kingdom in North Africa centred on Carthage. Arriving by sea at the mouth of the Tiber, the Vandals occupied and systematically plundered the city of Rome for two weeks, from 2 to 16 June 455. It was the third sack of Rome in the 5th century, following the Visigothic sack of 410 under Alaric and preceding the sack of 472.

Background

The sack followed a period of political turmoil in the Western Roman Empire. The emperor Valentinian III had murdered the powerful general Flavius Aetius in September 454, and Valentinian himself was assassinated on 16 March 455 by soldiers loyal to Aetius. The wealthy senator Petronius Maximus seized the throne and forced Valentinian's widow, Licinia Eudoxia, to marry him. According to later accounts, Licinia Eudoxia appealed to Genseric in Carthage for help, although the historicity of this appeal has been questioned. Genseric also had a dynastic grievance: his son Huneric had been betrothed to Eudocia, the daughter of Valentinian III, but Petronius Maximus married her instead to his own son Palladius.

The sack

Genseric sailed with his fleet to Italy and landed at Ostia. Petronius Maximus attempted to flee Rome but was stoned to death by an angry Roman mob on 31 May 455, after a reign of only about two and a half months. Pope Leo I met Genseric outside the city walls and, according to tradition, persuaded him to refrain from murder, arson, and torture, in exchange for the Vandals being allowed to enter the city unopposed. Unlike the Visigothic sack of 410, the Vandal sack was a thorough and orderly plundering rather than an orgy of destruction. For fourteen days the Vandals stripped Rome of its remaining wealth, including the treasures of the Capitol, among them the gilded bronze tiles of the Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, and the sacred vessels from the Second Temple in Jerusalem that Titus had brought from Jerusalem in 71 AD — including the great menorah, which the Vandals carried back to Carthage.

Thousands of Roman captives were taken to Carthage, including the empress Licinia Eudoxia and her daughters Eudocia and Placidia. Eudocia was married to Huneric, Genseric's son. Many captives were ransomed through the efforts of the church, notably Bishop Deogratias of Carthage, who sold church plate to free them.

Legacy

The thoroughness of the Vandal plundering gave rise, much later, to the modern word "vandalism" — coined in 1794 by Henri Grégoire, a French revolutionary bishop, to describe the destruction of art and property during the French Revolution. Ironically, the 455 sack was relatively disciplined compared with other sacks of the city, but the Vandals' systematic removal of portable wealth over two weeks left Rome significantly impoverished. After 455 Rome was sacked once more, in 472, and the Western Empire itself came to an end in 476 with the deposition of Romulus Augustulus.
