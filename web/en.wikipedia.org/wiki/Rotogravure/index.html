Rotogravure

Rotogravure (or gravure) is a type of intaglio printing process in which an image is engraved into the surface of a metal cylinder. The cylinder is inked and the excess wiped away, leaving ink in the recessed cells; the substrate — paper, film or foil — is pressed against the cylinder by a rubber-covered roller and draws the ink out of the cells by capillary action. In modern rotary gravure, the cylinders rotate on high-speed web presses, and the process is used for very long runs of magazines, catalogues, packaging and decorative laminates, prized for its rich, continuous-tone image reproduction.

Process

The image is etched or engraved into a copper-plated steel cylinder as a pattern of tiny cells. In conventional gravure the cells vary in depth (and sometimes area), so that darker tones hold more ink; in modern electromechanical engraving, a diamond stylus cuts cells of varying area and depth. After engraving, the cylinder is chrome-plated for durability and mounted on the press. Each colour requires its own cylinder and printing unit. A doctor blade wipes the cylinder surface clean as it rotates through the ink fountain, and the impression roller presses the web into contact. Gravure inks are low-viscosity and solvent-based (increasingly water-based), drying rapidly by evaporation, which permits press speeds of several metres per second and long uninterrupted runs.

History

Intaglio printing from engraved plates dates to the 15th century, but the rotary photogravure process was developed in the late 19th century, notably through the work of Karel Klíč in the 1890s, whose carbon-tissue method enabled photographic images to be etched into cylinders. Newspaper picture sections printed by rotogravure became common in the early 20th century — the "roto" sections of Sunday papers — and after the Second World War gravure became the dominant process for high-volume magazines and mail-order catalogues.

Applications and characteristics

Because cylinder preparation is expensive, gravure is economical only for long runs, typically hundreds of thousands of copies or more. It is the benchmark process for flexible packaging (food wrappers, labels), gift wrap, wall coverings, floor laminates and publication printing. Gravure reproduces photographs and fine gradients exceptionally smoothly, but text and line art may show slightly serrated edges because all elements are screened into cells. Environmental pressure on solvent emissions has driven recovery systems and a partial shift to water-based inks and competing processes such as flexography for shorter packaging runs.
