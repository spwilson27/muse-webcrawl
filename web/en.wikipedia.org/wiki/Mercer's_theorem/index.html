Mercer's theorem

Mercer's theorem is a representation theorem in functional analysis and integral equations, first stated by James Mercer in 1909. It gives conditions under which a symmetric, positive-definite kernel can be expressed as a uniformly convergent series of products of eigenfunctions, generalizing the spectral decomposition of a positive-definite matrix to integral operators on function spaces.

Statement

Let K be a continuous symmetric kernel defined on a compact interval [a, b] × [a, b] that is positive-definite, meaning that for every square-integrable function the associated quadratic form is nonnegative. Then there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions φi of the integral operator defined by K, with corresponding nonnegative eigenvalues λi, such that

K(x, y) = Σi λi φi(x) φi(y)

where the series converges absolutely and uniformly.

Significance

Mercer's theorem is a cornerstone of the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and underlies the kernel trick in machine learning: it guarantees that a positive-definite kernel corresponds to an inner product in some feature space, so algorithms such as support vector machines can operate implicitly in high- or infinite-dimensional spaces. It also provides a theoretical foundation for Karhunen–Loève expansions of stochastic processes.
