Julius Wellhausen (17 May 1844 – 7 January 1918) was a German biblical scholar and orientalist, best known for formulating the documentary hypothesis, the influential theory that the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Hebrew Bible) was compiled from four originally independent documents.

Career

Wellhausen studied theology at Göttingen under Georg Heinrich August Ewald. He became professor of Old Testament at Greifswald in 1872, but resigned in 1882, troubled that his critical conclusions undermined the practical training of clergy; he wrote that he had become a theologian because the scientific treatment of the Bible interested him, and that he felt unfit to prepare students for the ministry. He moved to Halle and Marburg as professor of oriental languages, and returned to Göttingen in 1892, where he taught until his death.

The documentary hypothesis

In works including Die Composition des Hexateuchs (1876/77) and especially the Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels (1878; reissued 1883), Wellhausen built on earlier scholarship by Karl Heinrich Graf and others to argue that the Pentateuch combined four sources, conventionally labelled J (Yahwist), E (Elohist), D (Deuteronomist) and P (Priestly), redacted over centuries. He arranged them in a historical scheme in which the Priestly source — with its elaborate cultic law — was late, dating from the Babylonian exile and after, reversing the traditional view that the law came first and the prophets later.

The hypothesis dominated Pentateuchal scholarship for over a century, and although it has been substantially modified and challenged since the 1970s, Wellhausen's historical synthesis remains a landmark of biblical criticism.

Other work

Wellhausen also made major contributions to the study of early Islam and pre-Islamic Arabia, notably The Arab Kingdom and Its Fall (1902), and to New Testament scholarship, writing commentaries and an Introduction to the First Three Gospels.
