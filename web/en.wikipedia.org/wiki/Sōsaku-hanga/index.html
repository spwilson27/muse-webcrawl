Sōsaku-hanga

Sōsaku-hanga (創作版画, "creative prints") is an art movement in Japanese printmaking that emerged in the early twentieth century. In contrast to the traditional ukiyo-e workshop system, in which artist, carver, printer, and publisher were separate people, sōsaku-hanga artists followed the principle of jiga, jikoku, jizuri — self-drawn, self-carved, self-printed — producing prints as personal creative expression.

History

The movement is conventionally dated from Kanae Yamamoto's print Fisherman (1904), made while he was a student. It grew through artist-run magazines, exhibitions, and print societies in the Taishō and early Shōwa periods. Artists such as Onchi Kōshirō, Hiratsuka Un'ichi, and Munakata Shikō became central figures, and the movement gained international recognition in the 1950s, when Japanese prints won prizes at the São Paulo and Lugano biennials.

Contrast with shin-hanga

Sōsaku-hanga is usually contrasted with shin-hanga ("new prints"), which retained the traditional collaborative production system under a publisher while using modern subjects and marketing. Both movements revitalised Japanese woodblock printing in the twentieth century.
