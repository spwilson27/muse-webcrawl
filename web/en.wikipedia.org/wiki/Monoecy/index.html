Monoecy

[Image placeholder: monoecious maize]

Monoecy (from Greek móno, "single", and oikía, "house") is a sexual system in which an individual organism bears both male and female reproductive organs or flowers, but in separate structures. A species exhibiting monoecy is described as monoecious: a single individual produces both pollen and ovules (in plants) or both sperm and eggs (in animals), though not in the same flower or organ.

In plants

Monoecy is widespread in flowering plants: an estimated 10 percent of angiosperm species bear separate male (staminate) and female (pistillate) flowers on the same plant. Familiar examples include maize (corn), in which the tassel is the male inflorescence and the ear is female; oaks, birches and alders, which bear male catkins and female flowers on one tree; and cucurbits such as cucumber and squash. Most conifers are also monoecious, carrying male and female cones on the same tree.

Ecology and evolution

Monoecy can promote outcrossing when male and female functions are separated in time (dichogamy) or space, reducing self-pollination and geitonogamy, while still allowing a solitary plant to reproduce. The system is thought to have evolved repeatedly from hermaphroditic ancestors and may be favoured in wind-pollinated plants and in colonising species.

Contrast with related systems

Monoecy is distinguished from dioecy, in which male and female individuals are separate, and from hermaphroditism (bisexual flowers or combined organs) in which a single flower or gonad contains both sexes. Intermediate conditions include andromonoecy (male and bisexual flowers on one plant) and gynomonoecy (female and bisexual flowers on one plant).
