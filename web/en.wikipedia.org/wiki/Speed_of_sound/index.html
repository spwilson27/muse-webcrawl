The speed of sound is the distance travelled per unit of time by a sound wave as it propagates through an elastic medium. In dry air at 20 °C (68 °F), the speed of sound is 343 metres per second (about 1,235 km/h, or 767 mph). More generally, the speed depends on the medium's elasticity and density: sound travels much faster in liquids and solids than in gases — about 1,480 m/s in fresh water and roughly 5,100 m/s in steel. The ratio of an object's speed to the local speed of sound is its Mach number.

Formula

For an ideal gas, the speed of sound is:

c = √(γRT/M)

where γ is the adiabatic index (1.4 for diatomic gases such as air), R the universal gas constant, T the absolute temperature, and M the molar mass. In air this yields the useful approximation c ≈ 331.3 + 0.606·θ m/s, where θ is the temperature in degrees Celsius. For solids, c = √(E/ρ), where E is Young's modulus and ρ the density, for longitudinal waves in a thin rod.

History

Isaac Newton computed a value for the speed of sound in the Principia (1687) assuming isothermal compression, obtaining a figure some 15% too low; Laplace corrected the theory in 1816 by treating the compressions as adiabatic, which accounts for the discrepancy. Early measurements timed the interval between seeing a cannon's flash and hearing its report; the French Academy's 1738 trials produced a value within a few metres per second of the modern one. The "sound barrier" was famously broken in level flight by Chuck Yeager in the Bell X-1 on 14 October 1947.

Altitude and medium dependence

Because temperature falls with altitude in the troposphere, the speed of sound at cruising altitude (11 km) is about 295 m/s. In the ocean, speed varies with temperature, pressure, and salinity, producing a deep sound channel (the SOFAR channel) near 1,000 m depth in which low-frequency sound can travel thousands of kilometres. Humidity slightly increases the speed in air.
