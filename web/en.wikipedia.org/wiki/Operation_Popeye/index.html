Operation Popeye

Date: 20 March 1967 – 5 July 1972

Location: Southeast Asia (Ho Chi Minh Trail)

Part of: Vietnam War

Type: Military cloud-seeding / weather modification program

Unit: 54th Weather Reconnaissance Squadron

Operation Popeye (also known as Project Popeye, Motorpool, and Intermediary-Compatriot) was a highly classified United States military cloud-seeding project during the Vietnam War. From 1967 to 1972, the 54th Weather Reconnaissance Squadron flew missions to seed clouds over the Ho Chi Minh Trail, with the aim of extending the monsoon season and softening road surfaces to impede North Vietnamese troop and supply movements.

The program

Operating from Udorn Royal Thai Air Force Base in Thailand, WC-130 and RF-4C aircraft dispensed silver iodide and lead iodide flares into suitable clouds over target areas in Laos, Cambodia, and North and South Vietnam. The squadron's motto became "make mud, not war." Secretary of Defense Melvin Laird was among senior officials who publicly denied, including to Congress, that weather modification was being used as a weapon, despite the program's existence.

Exposure and aftermath

Columnist Jack Anderson revealed the program in March 1971, and further details emerged in the Pentagon Papers and Senate hearings in 1972; the operation was terminated in July 1972. Public and congressional reaction was strongly critical: Senator Claiborne Pell led efforts for an international ban, and the affair contributed directly to the negotiation of the Environmental Modification Convention (ENMOD), signed in 1977, which prohibits the hostile use of environmental modification techniques. The United States also renounced the use of weather modification in warfare by Senate resolution in 1973.

The effectiveness of the program remains uncertain: participants claimed rainfall was increased in some areas and traffic was hampered, but assessing the contribution of seeding against natural monsoon variability was inherently difficult.
