Socialist Unity Party of Germany

The Socialist Unity Party of Germany (German: Sozialistische Einheitspartei Deutschlands, SED) was the founding and ruling party of the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) from the state's formation in 1949 until 1989. It was created in April 1946 in the Soviet occupation zone by the forced merger of the Communist Party of Germany (KPD) and the eastern branch of the Social Democratic Party (SPD), and it governed through a system in which other parties existed only as members of the SED-led National Front. Its leaders included Walter Ulbricht (1950–1971) and Erich Honecker (1971–1989).

History

The merger congress of April 1946 united the KPD of Wilhelm Pieck and the SPD of Otto Grotewohl; both served as co-chairmen. In the Western zones the SPD under Kurt Schumacher rejected the merger, and the SED remained an eastern party. After the founding of the GDR in October 1949, the SED declared itself a "party of the new type" on the Soviet model, purged former Social Democrats, and consolidated a one-party-dominated state secured by the Ministry for State Security (the Stasi). The party ordered the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961 under Ulbricht.

Honecker succeeded Ulbricht in 1971, combining consumer-oriented "unity of economic and social policy" with continued political repression. Membership reached about 2.3 million by 1989 — roughly one in six East German adults. In autumn 1989, mass protests and the opening of the Berlin Wall on 9 November destroyed the party's authority within weeks: Honecker was replaced by Egon Krenz, the leadership resigned, and in December an extraordinary congress re-founded the party as the SED-PDS, soon simply the Party of Democratic Socialism (PDS), a democratic socialist party that later merged into The Left (Die Linke).

Organisation

The SED was organised by democratic centralism: local, district and county organisations elected delegates to party congresses, but real power lay with the Politburo and Secretariat of the Central Committee and with the nomenklatura system of party-approved appointments. The party controlled the state, the armed forces, mass organisations and the media; its constitutionally guaranteed "leading role" was deleted from the GDR constitution only in December 1989.
