Pentose phosphate pathway

LocationCytosol
Key productsNADPH, ribose 5-phosphate
Rate-limiting enzymeGlucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase
Also known asPhosphogluconate pathway; hexose monophosphate shunt

The pentose phosphate pathway (also called the phosphogluconate pathway or hexose monophosphate shunt) is a metabolic pathway parallel to glycolysis that operates in the cytosol of most organisms. It does not directly consume or produce ATP; instead it generates NADPH — the reducing power used in fatty-acid and steroid synthesis and in maintaining reduced glutathione — and ribose 5-phosphate, the sugar precursor for nucleotide and nucleic acid synthesis.

Phases

The pathway has two distinct phases. In the irreversible oxidative phase, glucose 6-phosphate is oxidised by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), the pathway's rate-limiting and main regulatory step, through 6-phosphogluconolactone and 6-phosphogluconate to ribulose 5-phosphate, generating two molecules of NADPH and releasing one CO2. In the reversible non-oxidative phase, the enzymes transketolase and transaldolase interconvert ribulose 5-phosphate, ribose 5-phosphate, and xylulose 5-phosphate with the glycolytic intermediates fructose 6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, allowing the cell to balance its needs for NADPH, ribose, and ATP according to conditions.

Regulation and clinical relevance

Flux through the pathway is highest in tissues active in lipid synthesis — liver, adipose tissue, lactating mammary gland, and adrenal cortex — and in red blood cells, where NADPH protects against oxidative damage. Deficiency of G6PD, one of the most common human enzyme defects (an X-linked trait affecting hundreds of millions worldwide), leaves red cells vulnerable to haemolysis triggered by infections, certain drugs, and fava beans (favism), while also conferring partial protection against malaria. Transketolase depends on thiamine (vitamin B1), linking the pathway to Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome in thiamine deficiency.
