Terracotta Army

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Ranks of terracotta soldiers in Pit 1 of the mausoleum complex]

The Terracotta Army is a collection of terracotta sculptures depicting the armies of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of China, buried with him in 210–209 BC to guard his mausoleum and serve him in the afterlife. Discovered in 1974 by farmers digging a well near Lintong, Shaanxi, the site contains an estimated 8,000 soldiers — each with individualised features — together with 130 chariots, 520 horses and 150 cavalry horses, arranged in battle formation in vast pits a mile from the emperor's still-unexcavated tomb mound. It is among the greatest archaeological discoveries of the 20th century and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Discovery and contents

Pit 1, the largest, holds the main army in corridors: infantry ranks, archers and crossbowmen in kneeling and standing poses, chariots and officers distinguished by headdress and armour. The figures were originally vividly painted and held real bronze weapons, many still sharp when excavated; mass-produced from moulds in modular parts, they were finished and painted by hand, so no two faces are alike. Pits 2 and 3 contain cavalry, chariots and a command post; a fourth pit was left empty, apparently unfinished at the dynasty's fall.

The mausoleum

Sima Qian's history describes the tomb itself as a subterranean palace with rivers of mercury, crossbow traps and a ceiling of stars — accounts partly corroborated by high mercury readings in the mound's soil. Chinese archaeologists have not excavated the tomb chamber, citing preservation concerns (the warriors' paint flaked within minutes of exposure before modern techniques). The site museum, opened in 1979, receives millions of visitors yearly; figures have toured the world as China's most famous cultural ambassadors.
