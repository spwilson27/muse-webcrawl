Sonorism

Sonorism (Polish: sonoryzm) is an approach to musical composition associated with a number of notable Polish composers of the 20th century. The term was coined by the Polish musicologist Józef Michał Chomiński in 1956 to describe a style in which the emphasis is put on specific characteristics and qualities of sound, such as timbre, texture and movement, in place of conventional melody, harmony, rhythm and meter.

Characteristics

Sonoristic music treats pure sonority as a primary structural element. Composers employed dense clusters, glissandi, quarter tones, unusual string techniques (playing behind the bridge, striking the soundboard), and masses of sound that evolve over time. The musical form is typically generated by the transformation of timbre itself, rather than by thematic or motivic development.

History

The style emerged after 1956, when the political thaw in Poland allowed composers access to Western avant-garde music. The Warsaw Autumn festival, founded in 1956, became a key venue. Krzysztof Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960), written for 52 string instruments, became the most famous sonoristic work, using cluster bands, extended techniques and graphic notation. Other landmarks include Górecki's Scontri (1960) and Genesis cycle, and works by Kazimierz Serocki and Witold Szalonek.

Legacy

Sonorism was influential on European avant-garde music of the 1960s and contributed distinctive sound-mass techniques later absorbed into film music and contemporary orchestral writing. Many Polish composers later moved toward neo-romanticism or minimalism, but sonoristic devices remained a lasting part of their vocabulary.
