Valentinus

Valentinus
Born: c. AD 100, Phrebonis, Egypt
Died: c. AD 160–180, possibly Cyprus
Occupation: Theologian, poet
Known for: Founder of Valentinianism, an influential Gnostic school
Surviving work: Fragments; the Gospel of Truth is often attributed to him

Valentinus (Greek: Οὐαλεντῖνος; c. AD 100 – c. 160/180) was an Egyptian Christian theologian, the founder of Valentinianism, the most sophisticated and influential of the Gnostic schools of the 2nd century. According to Epiphanius he was born at Phrebonis in the Nile Delta and educated in Alexandria, where he may have studied under the Gnostic teacher Basilides.

Life

Valentinus came to Rome around AD 136–140, during the episcopate of Hyginus, and became a prominent teacher there; Tertullian reports that he was at one point considered for the Roman episcopate and broke with the church only after being passed over. He left Rome around 155 and may have spent his final years in Cyprus.

Teaching

Only fragments of Valentinus's own writings survive, quoted by church fathers such as Clement of Alexandria: elegant letters, sermons and a poem on the harvest. The elaborate system of his followers, described by Irenaeus in Against Heresies, taught that the material world arose from a rupture within the divine fullness, the Pleroma: thirty aeons emanate in pairs from the primal Bythos (Depth) and Sige (Silence); the youngest aeon, Sophia (Wisdom), fell through her desire to know the Father, and from her passion the Demiurge — the ignorant creator of the material cosmos — and matter itself were produced.

Humanity was divided into three classes: the material (hylics), destined for destruction; the psychic (psychics), ordinary church Christians, saved by faith and works; and the spiritual (pneumatics), the Gnostics, who possess the divine spark and are saved by knowledge (gnosis) of their origin. Christ's mission restores the pneumatics to the Pleroma.

Legacy

Valentinus's disciples — Ptolemy, Heracleon, Theodotus and Marcus among them — carried his teaching across the Mediterranean, and Valentinian communities persisted in Egypt and Syria into the 4th century and beyond. The Gospel of Truth, preserved among the Nag Hammadi codices discovered in 1945, is widely regarded as possibly a work of Valentinus himself, and its gentler, more meditative tone has led modern scholars to see the founder as a more mainstream and poetic figure than the heresiologists' portrait of his school suggests.
