Trousseau sign of latent tetany

The Trousseau sign of latent tetany is a medical sign observed in patients with low calcium (hypocalcaemia). It is elicited by inflating a blood pressure cuff around the upper arm to above systolic pressure for about three minutes, occluding the brachial artery. In a positive response, the resulting ischaemia of the nerves of the arm provokes spasm of the muscles of the hand and forearm — carpopedal spasm — in which the wrist and metacarpophalangeal joints flex, the interphalangeal joints extend, and the thumb adducts, producing the characteristic posture sometimes called the "obstetrician's hand" (main d'accoucheur).

Physiology

Low ionised calcium lowers the threshold for excitation of peripheral nerves, making them hyperexcitable. Ischaemia during cuff occlusion further increases nerve excitability, so that in a hypocalcaemic patient the forearm muscles go into sustained tetanic contraction. Healthy people may develop the sign after prolonged occlusion, but in hypocalcaemia it appears quickly and reliably; the sign is reported to be present in the great majority of patients with confirmed hypocalcaemia and in only a small percentage of people with normal calcium.

History

The sign is named after the French physician Armand Trousseau (1801–1867), who described it in the nineteenth century in connection with tetany. Trousseau is also the namesake of a different, unrelated sign — Trousseau's syndrome (Trousseau sign of malignancy), the migratory thrombophlebitis associated with occult cancer, which Trousseau famously diagnosed in himself.

Related signs

Trousseau's sign is usually assessed together with Chvostek's sign, in which tapping over the facial nerve just in front of the ear provokes twitching of the facial muscles; Chvostek's sign is less specific, occurring in a significant minority of healthy people. Both signs indicate increased neuromuscular excitability, which may accompany hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia and alkalosis. Latent tetany signs are most often encountered after thyroid or parathyroid surgery, in vitamin D deficiency, and in chronic kidney disease.
