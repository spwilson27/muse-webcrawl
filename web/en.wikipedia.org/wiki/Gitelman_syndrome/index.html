Gitelman syndrome

Specialty: Nephrology

Symptoms: Muscle cramps, fatigue, salt craving, low blood pressure

Cause: Mutations in the SLC12A3 gene

Inheritance: Autosomal recessive

Named after: Hillel J. Gitelman

Gitelman syndrome (GS) is an autosomal recessive kidney tubule disorder characterised by low blood levels of potassium and magnesium (hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia), decreased urinary calcium excretion (hypocalciuria), and metabolic alkalosis. It is caused by inactivating mutations in the SLC12A3 gene, which encodes the thiazide-sensitive sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC) of the distal convoluted tubule of the kidney.

Signs and symptoms

Gitelman syndrome usually presents in adolescence or adulthood, later than the related Bartter syndrome. Many people are mildly affected or asymptomatic, and the condition may be discovered incidentally on blood tests. Symptoms include muscle cramps and weakness, fatigue, salt craving, dizziness, tingling, and episodes of tetany, particularly during illness or after exercise. Blood pressure tends to be low or low-normal despite activation of the renin–angiotensin system. Long-standing magnesium deficiency can cause chondrocalcinosis (calcium pyrophosphate deposits in joints), and cardiac arrhythmias, including prolongation of the QT interval, are a recognised risk.

Genetics and pathophysiology

Loss of function of the NCC transporter reduces sodium chloride reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule. The resulting mild volume depletion increases sodium reabsorption elsewhere at the expense of potassium and hydrogen ions, causing hypokalemic alkalosis, while defective distal function also impairs magnesium reabsorption and enhances calcium reabsorption — hence the paradoxically low urinary calcium, which distinguishes Gitelman syndrome from Bartter syndrome. The estimated prevalence is around 1 in 40,000, making it one of the most common inherited tubulopathies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the characteristic electrolyte pattern — hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, and hypocalciuria — after excluding diuretic abuse and chronic vomiting; genetic testing for SLC12A3 mutations confirms the diagnosis. There is no cure, and treatment is lifelong supplementation with potassium and magnesium salts, a liberal salt intake, and potassium-sparing diuretics such as amiloride if needed. The long-term prognosis is generally good, though symptoms can significantly affect quality of life.

History

The syndrome was described by the American nephrologist Hillel J. Gitelman and colleagues in 1966, in a family they likened to Bartter syndrome but with hypocalciuria and hypomagnesemia. The causative gene was identified in 1996.
