The shakuhachi (Japanese: 尺八, pronounced [ɕakɯhatɕi]) is a Japanese longitudinal, end-blown bamboo flute, traditionally made from the root end of madake bamboo. Its name derives from its standard length of one shaku eight sun (about 54.5 cm). The instrument is known for its expressive, breathy timbre and its close association with Zen Buddhism.

History

End-blown flutes reached Japan from China via Korea as part of the gagaku court ensemble by the Nara period. The modern shakuhachi emerged in the Edo period, when it became the instrument of the komusō, wandering Zen monks of the Fuke sect. These monks, recognisable by the woven basket hats (tengai) that concealed their faces, played meditative solo pieces called honkyoku as a form of spiritual practice known as suizen, or "blowing zen". After the Fuke sect was abolished in the Meiji period (1871), the shakuhachi survived as a secular instrument, in the sankyoku chamber ensemble with koto and shamisen, and later in contemporary and popular music worldwide.

Construction and technique

The flute has five finger holes, giving a minor pentatonic scale (D–F–G–A–C on the standard instrument), extended by half-holing and by meri/kari embouchure techniques that lower or raise the pitch by tilting the head. The oblique blowing edge, the utaguchi, is often inlaid with horn or ivory. Longer instruments (chōkan) give lower keys and are used in honkyoku performance. Techniques such as muraiki (forceful breath bursts) and yuri (oscillation) produce the instrument's characteristic palette of noise and colour.

Repertoire and modern use

The honkyoku repertoire, transmitted by schools such as Kinko-ryū and Tozan-ryū (the latter using a more standardised notation), includes pieces such as Shika no tōne ("The Distant Call of the Deer"). In the twentieth century composers including Henry Cowell, Toru Takemitsu (November Steps, 1967) and John Cage wrote for the instrument, and shakuhachi masters such as Yokoyama Katsuya brought it to international audiences. It is now played in jazz, film scores, ambient and world music settings.
