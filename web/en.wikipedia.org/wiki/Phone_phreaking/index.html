Phone phreaking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A blue box, the iconic tool of the phone phreak.

Phone phreaking is a slang term coined to describe the activity of a culture of people who study, experiment with, or explore telecommunication systems, such as equipment and systems connected to public telephone networks. The term phreak is a sensational spelling of the word freak with the ph- from phone, and may also refer to the use of various audio frequencies to manipulate a phone system. Phreak, phreaker, or phone phreak are names used for individuals who participate in phreaking.

The term first referred to groups who reverse engineered the system of tones used to route long-distance calls. By re-creating these tones, phreaks could switch calls from the phone handset and make free long-distance calls, bypassing the normal billing process.

History

Phreaking began in the late 1950s, when telephone switching moved from human operators to automated systems controlled by in-band signaling tones. Blind teenager Joe Engressia discovered that whistling a tone at 2600 Hz into a telephone mouthpiece caused the line to reset, allowing him to place calls that were not billed. The toy whistle packaged in boxes of Cap'n Crunch cereal famously produced the same 2600 Hz tone, and was used by John Draper, who adopted the nickname "Captain Crunch".

In the 1970s, devices known as blue boxes were built to generate the multifrequency (MF) tones used by the network for routing, while red boxes simulated the coin-drop tones of payphones and black boxes manipulated line voltage. A 1971 article in Esquire magazine, "Secrets of the Little Blue Box", brought phreaking to wide public attention. Among the most famous phreaks were Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs, who built and sold blue boxes before founding Apple Computer.

Decline

Phreaking declined as telephone networks replaced in-band signaling with out-of-band digital signaling systems such as Signaling System No. 7 (SS7), in which control signals travel on a separate channel inaccessible from an ordinary subscriber line. Laws such as the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act also increased the legal risks. The phreaking scene nonetheless fed directly into the computer hacker culture of the 1980s, including the magazine 2600: The Hacker Quarterly, named after the 2600 Hz tone.
