Cryobiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cryogenic storage in liquid nitrogen is central to cryobiology research and practice.

Cryobiology is the branch of biology that studies the effects of extremely low temperatures on living organisms, tissues, and cells. Its name derives from the Greek words kryos (cold), bios (life), and logos (study). The field's principal practical goal is cryopreservation: storing biological material at very low temperatures — typically in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C — so that it can later be revived with minimal loss of function.

Principles

At low temperatures, the molecular motion that drives biochemical reactions slows and eventually effectively stops, suspending biological time. The main obstacle to successful preservation is ice: as water freezes, ice crystals can puncture cell membranes, while the concentration of solutes in the remaining unfrozen water can damage cells osmotically. Cryobiologists manage this through controlled cooling rates, in which slow cooling draws water out of cells before intracellular ice can form, and through cryoprotectants such as glycerol and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which reduce ice formation and stabilize membranes.

An alternative approach, vitrification, uses high concentrations of cryoprotectants and extremely rapid cooling to turn the cell contents into an amorphous, glass-like solid without ice crystals at all. Vitrification is now widely used for human eggs and embryos in fertility treatment.

Applications

  • Medicine and reproduction: preservation of sperm, eggs, and embryos for assisted reproduction; storage of blood, bone marrow, and stem cells.
  • Transplantation: research into banking tissues and, eventually, whole organs.
  • Conservation: seed banks and "frozen zoos" that store genetic material of endangered plants and animals.
  • Food science: understanding freezing damage in stored foods.
  • Nature study: how organisms such as wood frogs, some insects, and tardigrades survive natural freezing, including by producing natural antifreeze compounds.

History

Systematic cryobiology began in the mid-20th century. A landmark came in 1949, when Christopher Polge and colleagues discovered the cryoprotective effect of glycerol, allowing bull sperm to survive freezing — a breakthrough for animal breeding. The field's professional body, the Society for Cryobiology, was founded in 1964, and its journal Cryobiology remains a central publication. Work by Peter Mazur formalized the "two-factor hypothesis" of freezing injury, explaining damage from both intracellular ice and solute concentration.

Cryobiology is distinct from cryonics, the speculative practice of freezing human bodies after legal death in the hope of future revival, which is not an established scientific application.
