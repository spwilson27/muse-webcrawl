Gardian

A gardian is a mounted herdsman of the Camargue region of southern France, often described as the French equivalent of the cowboy. Gardians work on manades (ranches) in the marshy delta of the Rhône, where they tend the semi-wild herds of black Camargue bulls and white Camargue horses that are central to the region's identity and to the local traditions of bullfighting and bull-running.

History

The gardian tradition dates back several centuries in the Rhône delta. The herdsmen developed a distinctive culture shaped by the flat, waterlogged landscape of reed beds, lagoons and salt marshes. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the Marquis Folco de Baroncelli-Javon became a champion of Camargue traditions and helped codify the gardian's costume and customs, reviving Provençal festivals and founding the Nacioun Gardiano (Gardian Nation) association in 1909 to preserve the culture.

Work and dress

Gardians ride the small, hardy white Camargue horse and use a long wooden trident (lou ferre) to help direct and separate the bulls. Their traditional dress includes a wide-brimmed black hat, a colourful printed shirt, a black velvet jacket, moleskin trousers and leather boots. The gardian's tools and costume are displayed each year at festivals such as the Fête des Gardians in Arles, held on 1 May, when gardians gather to be blessed and to parade through the town.

Culture

The gardian is an emblem of Provençal regional identity. The herds of bulls they manage supply the course camarguaise, a bloodless form of bull-running in which raseteurs attempt to snatch rosettes and tassels from between a bull's horns, as well as bulls destined for the Spanish-style corrida. The gardian lifestyle — solitary, skilled horsemanship in a wild wetland landscape — has been celebrated in Provençal literature and song.
