Bernard Ferdinand Lyot (February 27, 1897 – April 2, 1952) was a French astronomer, celebrated as the inventor of the coronagraph (1930), an instrument that made it possible to observe the Sun's corona and prominences at any time, rather than only during rare total eclipses.

Life and work

Lyot studied engineering and worked as a demonstrator at the École Polytechnique before joining the Meudon Observatory in 1920, where he spent his career. His early work used a polarimeter of extraordinary sensitivity to study the polarisation of light from the planets; from the polarisation of the Moon's surface he inferred that it is covered with a fine dust resembling volcanic ash — a conclusion confirmed decades later by the Apollo landings.

The coronagraph

The corona is about a million times fainter than the solar disc, and scattered light within ordinary telescopes drowns it. Lyot's coronagraph (1930) combined an occulting disc at the focus with a carefully figured single-element objective (free of bubbles and striae) and a field lens with a diaphragm that removed diffracted light from the objective's edge. Working first at Meudon and then at the high-altitude Pic du Midi Observatory in the Pyrenees, where scattered skylight is far weaker, Lyot produced the first motion pictures of solar prominences and routinely measured coronal emission lines. In 1933 he also devised the birefringent monochromator known as the Lyot filter, still used to observe the Sun in a single spectral line, and the Lyot stop remains a standard element in coronagraphic exoplanet imagers today.

Death and honours

Lyot died suddenly of a heart attack in Cairo in 1952, returning from a solar eclipse expedition to Sudan. The lunar crater Lyot and the asteroid 2452 Lyot bear his name.
