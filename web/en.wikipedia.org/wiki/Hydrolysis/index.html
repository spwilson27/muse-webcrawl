Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis (from Greek hydro- "water" and lysis "to unbind") is any chemical reaction in which a molecule of water breaks one or more chemical bonds. The term is used broadly for substitution, elimination, and solvation reactions in which water is the nucleophile. In the reverse of a condensation reaction, a substrate typically splits into two parts, one gaining a hydrogen ion (H+) and the other a hydroxide group (OH−) from the water molecule.

Types

Common types include the hydrolysis of esters (saponification when base-promoted, yielding soap from fats), of amides, nitriles, and salts. Aqueous solutions of salts of weak acids or bases are hydrolysed, making them basic or acidic respectively. Acid- or base-catalysed hydrolysis is used industrially to hydrolyse starch to glucose syrups, cellulose in biofuel production, and triglycerides to fatty acids.

Biological hydrolysis

Living organisms rely on hydrolysis driven by enzymes called hydrolases: amylases break down starch, proteases cleave proteins into amino acids, lipases digest fats, and nucleases split nucleic acids. The hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate releases energy (~30.5 kJ/mol under standard conditions) that powers most cellular work.
