The Banat is a geographical and historical region in Central and Eastern Europe, today divided among eastern Serbia (Vojvodina), western Romania and a small part of southeastern Hungary. Lying in the Pannonian Basin between the Danube to the south, the Mureș river to the north and the Carpathian foothills to the east, it is noted for its fertile plain and its traditionally multiethnic population of Romanians, Serbs, Hungarians, Germans ("Banat Swabians"), Roma, Slovaks, Czechs and others.

History

The region's name derives from the title ban (see Ban (title)), a frontier governor. In the Middle Ages it belonged largely to the Kingdom of Hungary; from 1552 to 1716 the central Banat was the Ottoman Eyalet of Temeşvar. Habsburg forces under Prince Eugene of Savoy conquered it in 1716, and the region became the Banat of Temeswar, a directly administered crown land colonised with German and other settlers. Returned to Hungary in 1779, it remained Hungarian until 1918, when it was partitioned between Romania (the larger, eastern part), Yugoslavia and Hungary — a division confirmed by the Treaty of Trianon (1920) and unchanged since.

Geography and cities

The Romanian Banat includes Timișoara, Reșița and Lugoj; the Serbian Banat (part of Vojvodina) includes Zrenjanin, Pančevo and Kikinda. Its lowland is intensive farmland; the southern hills hold the iron and coal districts around Reșița and the Danube gorges of the Iron Gates.
