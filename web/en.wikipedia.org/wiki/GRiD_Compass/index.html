The GRiD Compass was a portable computer released by GRiD Systems Corporation in April 1982, widely regarded as the first laptop computer in the modern clamshell form. Designed by British industrial designer Bill Moggridge, its patented clamshell case — the screen folding flat over the keyboard — became the template for virtually every laptop that followed.

Design and specifications

The Compass weighed about 5 kg (11 lb) and was built around a die-cast magnesium alloy case. It used an Intel 8086 processor, 256 KB of RAM and 384 KB of non-volatile bubble memory in place of a disk drive — an expensive but rugged technology. Its flat-panel electroluminescent display, in yellow-orange on black, was a striking novelty at a time when portable computers used small CRTs. The machine ran GRiD's proprietary operating system, GRiD-OS.

Use

At a launch price of US$8,150 the Compass was far too costly for the general market, but it was bought in quantity by the United States government and military, and by executives. It is best known for flying aboard NASA's Space Shuttle missions from the mid-1980s, where it was used for navigation experiments and became one of the first laptops in space. Later models, including the Compass II and the IBM PC-compatible GRiDCase series, broadened its appeal.

Legacy

GRiD Systems was acquired by Tandy Corporation in 1988. The Compass's clamshell patent and its combination of a flat display, rugged metal case and portable software made it a landmark in industrial design; an example is held in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art.
