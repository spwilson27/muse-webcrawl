Information overload

Information overload (also known as infobesity, infoxication, or information anxiety) is the difficulty in understanding an issue and effectively making decisions when one has too much information about that issue, and is generally associated with the excessive quantity of daily information. The term was popularised by Alvin Toffler in his 1970 book Future Shock, though concerns about overload go back centuries — Seneca and later scholars complained of the glut of books long before the digital age.

Causes

Major contributors include the low cost of publishing on the web, email and instant messaging, social media feeds, push notifications, and workplace reporting demands. Information-related factors (quantity, frequency, duplication, irrelevance) combine with person-related factors (limited attention and working memory) and task and organisational factors to produce overload.

Effects

Overload is associated with poorer decisions, delayed responses, stress, reduced satisfaction, and a tendency to ignore or miss important messages amid the noise. In organisations it can consume a significant share of knowledge workers' time in searching for and triaging information. Herbert A. Simon anticipated the economics of the problem: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

Responses

Proposed remedies include better filtering and search tools, information visualisation, email discipline (such as inbox zero), summarisation by colleagues or software, media literacy education, and deliberate disconnection practices such as digital detoxes. Critics note that "overload" can also reflect poor information design rather than sheer quantity.
