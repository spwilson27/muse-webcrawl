Acamprosate, sold under the brand name Campral, is a medication used alongside psychosocial treatment to maintain abstinence in alcohol-dependent patients. It was developed in France, approved in Europe in 1989 and by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2004. It is one of three principal medications for alcohol use disorder, alongside naltrexone and disulfiram.

Mechanism and use

Acamprosate is structurally related to the amino acid neurotransmitters GABA and taurine. Its precise mechanism is uncertain, but it is thought to restore the balance between excitatory (glutamate, acting at NMDA receptors) and inhibitory neurotransmission that is disrupted by chronic alcohol exposure and early abstinence, thereby reducing the protracted withdrawal discomfort and craving that drive relapse. It is started after detoxification in patients committed to abstinence, typically at 666 mg three times daily, and is most effective at maintaining abstinence rather than reducing heavy drinking in non-abstinent patients.

Pharmacology and safety

The drug is poorly absorbed, not metabolised, and excreted unchanged in urine, requiring dose reduction in renal impairment and making it safe in liver disease — an advantage in patients with alcoholic liver disease. The most common adverse effect is diarrhoea; it has no abuse potential and no significant interaction with alcohol if a lapse occurs. European trials consistently showed benefit, while the large U.S. COMBINE study found no significant effect, possibly owing to differences in the severity and abstinence status of enrolled patients.
