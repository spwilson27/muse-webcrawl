Sergei Winogradsky

Sergei Winogradsky

Born: 1 September 1856, Kyiv, Russian Empire
Died: 25 February 1953 (aged 96), Brie-Comte-Robert, France
Known for: Chemosynthesis; nitrogen cycle; Winogradsky column

Sergei Nikolaevich Winogradsky (1856–1953) was a Ukrainian and Russian microbiologist, ecologist, and soil scientist, regarded together with Martinus Beijerinck as a founder of microbial ecology and environmental microbiology. He discovered chemosynthesis — the fixation of carbon by organisms using energy from inorganic chemical reactions rather than sunlight — identified the bacteria responsible for key steps of the nitrogen cycle, and devised the Winogradsky column, still used to demonstrate microbial communities in miniature.

Life and career

Winogradsky was born in Kyiv and initially studied law and then natural science at the University of Kyiv before moving to St Petersburg, where he studied botany under Anton de Bary's former students and pursued music as well. He worked in de Bary's laboratory in Strasbourg in the 1880s, where he studied the sulfur bacterium Beggiatoa, then at the University of Zurich with the botanist and plant physiologist, and in 1891 he took a position at the Institute of Experimental Medicine in St Petersburg, where he remained until retiring in 1912. After the Russian Revolution he emigrated to Yugoslavia and then, in 1922, accepted an invitation from Émile Roux to head a division of agricultural bacteriology at the Pasteur Institute's experimental station at Brie-Comte-Robert near Paris, where he worked for the rest of his long life.

Discoveries

Chemosynthesis and lithotrophy

Studying Beggiatoa, which oxidizes hydrogen sulfide to sulfur and then to sulfate, Winogradsky realized the organism derived its energy from this inorganic oxidation and could fix carbon dioxide without light — a radically new mode of life he called chemosynthesis (the organisms being chemoautotrophs or lithotrophs). He went on to characterize iron bacteria and other organisms living on inorganic energy sources, establishing that metabolism was not bound to organic food or sunlight.

The nitrogen cycle

In 1890–91 Winogradsky isolated the bacteria responsible for nitrification, showing it proceeds in two stages: ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (such as Nitrosomonas) convert ammonia to nitrite, and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (Nitrobacter) convert nitrite to nitrate. He also isolated the anaerobic nitrogen-fixing bacterium Clostridium pasteurianum, demonstrating free-living biological nitrogen fixation.

Methods and ecological outlook

Winogradsky championed studying microbes in environments resembling their natural conditions rather than only in pure culture, using enrichment cultures and the soil perfusion and column techniques that bear his name. The Winogradsky column — a glass column of mud, water, and nutrient sources left in sunlight — develops stratified, self-sustaining communities of photosynthetic and chemosynthetic microbes and remains a staple of microbiology teaching.

Legacy

Winogradsky's concepts — that microorganisms drive the Earth's great chemical cycles and can live on rock, air, and water alone — reshaped microbiology and laid foundations for biogeochemistry and the search for life in extreme environments. The bacterial genus Winogradskyella commemorates his name.
