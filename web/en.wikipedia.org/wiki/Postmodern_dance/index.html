Postmodern dance

Postmodern dance embraced ordinary, pedestrian movement and unconventional performance spaces

Postmodern dance is a performance movement that emerged in the United States in the 1960s and 1970s as a reaction against the theatricality and codified technique of modern dance. Centred on the Judson Dance Theater in New York (active 1962–1964) and later the Grand Union collective, postmodern choreographers treated everyday actions — walking, running, sitting, carrying objects — as dance, questioned the need for virtuosity, music, narrative, and even trained dancers, and explored chance procedures, task-based improvisation, and repetition.

History

The movement grew out of composition classes taught by Robert Dunn, a musician influenced by John Cage, at the studio of Merce Cunningham. Participants including Yvonne Rainer, Steve Paxton, Trisha Brown, Lucinda Childs, Deborah Hay, Simone Forti, and David Gordon presented their experiments at Judson Memorial Church. Rainer's 1965 "No Manifesto" famously rejected spectacle, virtuosity, magic, and make-believe. In the 1970s, Paxton's development of contact improvisation and Brown's explorations of accumulation and equipment pieces carried the inquiry forward, while Childs moved toward minimalist pattern and eventually large-scale proscenium works.

Characteristics

  • Use of pedestrian and task-like movement rather than codified dance vocabulary
  • Rejection of narrative, character, and emotional expression
  • Chance, rules, scores, and improvisation as choreographic methods
  • Performance in lofts, galleries, streets, and other non-theatrical spaces
  • Blurring of boundaries between dancers and non-dancers, art and daily life

Postmodern dance profoundly reshaped contemporary dance worldwide, legitimising conceptual approaches to choreography and feeding into performance art and later physical theatre.
