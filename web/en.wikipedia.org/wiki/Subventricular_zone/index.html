Subventricular zone

The subventricular zone (SVZ) is a region situated on the outside wall of each lateral ventricle of the vertebrate brain. It is one of the two principal sites of adult neurogenesis (the other being the subgranular zone of the hippocampal dentate gyrus) and contains the largest population of proliferating neural stem and progenitor cells in the adult mammalian brain.

Structure

The SVZ is a thin layer of cells lining the lateral ventricles beneath the ependyma. It is conventionally described as having up to four layers, from the ventricle outward: an ependymal monolayer (layer I), a hypocellular gap containing processes and few cell bodies (layer II), a ribbon of astrocytes and progenitor cells (layer III), and a transitional zone into the brain parenchyma (layer IV). Its principal cell types include relatively quiescent neural stem cells (type B cells, which resemble astrocytes), rapidly dividing transit-amplifying cells (type C), and migrating neuroblasts (type A).

Function

In rodents, neuroblasts born in the SVZ migrate long distances along the rostral migratory stream to the olfactory bulb, where they differentiate into interneurons — a pathway tied to olfactory learning. In humans, SVZ neurogenesis is prominent in infancy, when neuroblasts migrate to the olfactory bulb and prefrontal cortex, but declines sharply in adulthood; the extent of olfactory-bulb neurogenesis in adult humans is minimal, though SVZ progenitors may contribute neurons to the striatum. The SVZ also responds to injury such as stroke, producing progenitors that migrate toward damaged tissue, a response of interest for regenerative medicine.

Clinical significance

Because SVZ stem cells can proliferate and migrate, the region is implicated in the origin and recurrence of brain tumors, particularly glioblastoma, whose stem-like cells share features with SVZ progenitors and which frequently contact the SVZ. Research explores both harnessing SVZ neurogenesis for repair and targeting its tumor-propagating cells.
