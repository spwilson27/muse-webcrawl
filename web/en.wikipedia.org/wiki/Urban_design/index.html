Urban design

Urban design shapes streets, squares, and districts

Urban design is the process of designing and shaping the physical features of cities, towns, and villages, and the spaces between their buildings. It works at a scale larger than individual architecture — concerned with streets, squares, parks, and whole districts — but smaller than regional planning, and it draws together architecture, landscape architecture, and urban planning.

Scope and principles

Urban designers arrange the massing and appearance of building groups, the layout and character of public space, transport routes, and the mix of uses, with goals including legibility, walkability, safety, sustainability, and a sense of place. Influential figures include Kevin Lynch, whose The Image of the City (1960) identified paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks as the elements by which people understand cities, and Jane Jacobs, whose The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961) championed mixed use and "eyes on the street".

Contemporary practice

Modern approaches include New Urbanism, transit-oriented development, tactical urbanism, and complete-streets policies, all aiming to create more humane and environmentally sound urban environments.
