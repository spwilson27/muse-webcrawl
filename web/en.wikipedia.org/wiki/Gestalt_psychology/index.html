Gestalt psychology
FoundersMax Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, Kurt Koffka
OriginGermany, c. 1910–1912
Key ideasPerceptual organisation, insight learning, "the whole is other than the sum of its parts"

Gestalt psychology is a school of psychology and theory of perception that emerged in Germany in the early twentieth century, founded by Max Wertheimer together with Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka. Against the structuralist attempt to analyse experience into elementary sensations, the Gestaltists argued that the mind perceives organised wholes whose properties cannot be derived from their parts alone — a doctrine often summarised as "the whole is other than the sum of its parts".

Origins

The school is conventionally dated from Wertheimer's 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon: the apparent motion seen when two lights flash in succession at the right interval, an experience of movement that is present in no single stimulus. Wertheimer, Köhler and Koffka, working in Frankfurt and Berlin, proposed that perception is governed by field-like organisational processes in the brain (Köhler's "psychophysical isomorphism"), and reached an international audience with Koffka's English-language Principles of Gestalt Psychology (1935).

Principles of perceptual organisation

Gestalt research catalogued the rules by which the visual system groups elements into figures and objects, subsumed under the law of Prägnanz ("good form"): perception settles into the simplest, most regular organisation available. The classic grouping principles include:

  • Figure–ground segregation — illustrated by Edgar Rubin's vase/faces illusion
  • Proximity and similarity — elements close together or alike are grouped
  • Closure — incomplete contours are perceived as whole shapes
  • Good continuation and common fate — elements aligned, or moving together, belong together

Learning and thinking

Working with chimpanzees at Tenerife during the First World War, Köhler described insight learning: the animal Sultan, faced with out-of-reach bananas, suddenly combined two sticks to rake them in — a solution arrived at by restructuring the problem as a whole rather than by trial and error. Wertheimer's Productive Thinking (published 1945) extended the same approach to human problem-solving and education. Kurt Lewin, associated with the Berlin circle, carried Gestalt ideas into social psychology through his field theory.

Legacy

Driven from Germany by the Nazi rise to power, the founders emigrated to the United States, where Gestalt psychology influenced perception research, cognitive psychology, human-computer interaction (where its grouping laws remain standard design principles), and, more loosely, Gestalt therapy — which, despite the name, drew only selectively on the school's ideas.
