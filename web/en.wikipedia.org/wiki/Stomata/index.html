Stomata

In botany, a stoma (plural stomata, from Greek στόμα, "mouth") is a pore found in the epidermis of leaves, stems and other organs of plants, which controls the rate of gas exchange between the plant and the atmosphere. The pore is bordered by a pair of specialised parenchyma cells known as guard cells that regulate the size of the opening by changing shape.

Through open stomata, carbon dioxide enters the leaf for photosynthesis and oxygen and water vapour exit; this water loss, transpiration, drives the movement of water and nutrients from the roots. Stomatal opening is regulated by light, carbon dioxide concentration, humidity and the hormone abscisic acid, which triggers closure during drought. Guard cells open the pore by accumulating potassium ions and taking up water, increasing turgor and bowing apart.

Stomata are present in the sporophyte generation of most land plants. Their density and size on fossil leaves are used by palaeobotanists as a proxy for past atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations.
