Stinging nettle

Scientific name: Urtica dioica

Family: Urticaceae

Native range: Europe, temperate Asia, western North Africa; now worldwide

Stinging nettle (Urtica dioica), often known simply as common nettle or nettle leaf, is a herbaceous perennial flowering plant in the family Urticaceae. Originally native to Europe, much of temperate Asia, and western North Africa, it is now found worldwide. The species is divided into six subspecies, five of which have hollow stinging hairs called trichomes on the leaves and stems, which act like hypodermic needles, injecting histamine and other chemicals that produce a stinging sensation upon contact ("contact urticaria").

Description

Urtica dioica is a dioecious (with separate male and female plants) herbaceous perennial, usually 1 to 2 m (3 ft 3 in to 6 ft 7 in) tall in summer and dying down to the ground in winter. It spreads by rhizomes and stolons. The leaves are soft, serrated, and heart-shaped, borne in opposite pairs on a square stem.

Uses

Stinging nettle has a long history of use as a source of food, fibre, and traditional medicine. The young leaves are cooked and eaten like spinach and used in soups; cooking destroys the sting. Nettle fibre was used for textiles into the 20th century. The plant is also an important food source for many butterfly and moth larvae, including species of Nymphalidae.

Sting mechanism

The stinging hairs are hollow structures tipped with silica; on contact the tip breaks off, the sharp edge penetrates the skin, and irritants including histamine, acetylcholine, serotonin, and formic acid are delivered. The resulting rash is usually self-limiting.
