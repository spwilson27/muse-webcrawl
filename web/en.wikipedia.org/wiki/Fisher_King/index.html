Fisher King

First appearancePerceval, the Story of the Grail (c. 1180s)
Created byChrétien de Troyes
OccupationKing; guardian of the Holy Grail

The Fisher King is a figure in Arthurian legend, the last in a long line of British kings tasked with guarding the Holy Grail. He suffers a wound — typically to the thigh or groin — that will not heal, and his kingdom becomes a barren wasteland mirroring his affliction. Unable to walk, he spends his time fishing by a river near his castle, awaiting a knight who will ask the right question and restore him.

In the romances

The figure first appears in Chrétien de Troyes's unfinished Perceval (c. 1180), where the young hero visits the king's castle, witnesses the Grail procession, and fails to ask the crucial question, leaving the king uncured. Later authors, including Wolfram von Eschenbach in Parzival and the authors of the Vulgate Cycle and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, expanded and varied the story, sometimes splitting the role between a wounded king and his aged father.

Interpretations

Scholars have linked the Fisher King to Celtic sovereignty myths, in which the land's fertility depends on the king's health, and to vegetation and fertility rituals of the kind discussed by Jessie Weston in From Ritual to Romance. The motif strongly influenced T. S. Eliot's poem The Waste Land (1922), and the figure gives his name to the 1991 film The Fisher King.
