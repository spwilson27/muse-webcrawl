Baroque painting

Baroque painting is the painting associated with the Baroque cultural movement, which began in Italy around 1600 and spread through most of Europe during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Baroque painters sought drama, movement, and emotional intensity, using strong contrasts of light and shadow (chiaroscuro and tenebrism), dynamic diagonal compositions, rich color, and illusionistic space to overwhelm and persuade the viewer—aims closely allied to the Counter-Reformation Church and to absolutist courts.

Origins in Italy

Two revolutionary directions emerged in Rome around 1600: the naturalism of Caravaggio, whose starkly lit, unidealized religious scenes (such as the Calling of St Matthew) scandalized and captivated Europe, and the classical reform of Annibale Carracci and the Bolognese school, which combined observation with idealized design in works like the Farnese Gallery ceiling. Grand illusionistic ceiling painting (quadratura) culminated in works by Pietro da Cortona and later Andrea Pozzo, dissolving architecture into open heavens.

Regional schools

In Flanders, Peter Paul Rubens fused Italian monumentality with Northern detail and color in vast diplomatic and religious commissions, while Anthony van Dyck defined aristocratic portraiture. The Dutch Republic produced a distinctive market-driven art: Rembrandt's profound portraits and biblical scenes, Vermeer's quiet interiors, and thriving genres of landscape, still life, and scene of everyday life. In Spain, Velázquez, Zurbarán, and Murillo painted for court and church; in France, Nicolas Poussin and Claude Lorrain pursued an intellectual classicism, tempered later by the court splendor of Louis XIV. Baroque painting shaded into the lighter, decorative Rococo in the early eighteenth century.

Characteristics and legacy

Recurring features include dramatic lighting, immediacy of the moment depicted, foreshortening, and the breaking of the picture plane to engage the spectator. Long criticized as excessive after the rise of Neoclassicism, the Baroque was rehabilitated by twentieth-century scholarship and remains central to the canon of Western art.
