Papal infallibility

The First Vatican Council defined the dogma in 1870

Papal infallibility is a dogma of the Roman Catholic Church which states that, by reason of Christ's promises, the pope is preserved from the possibility of error when, acting ex cathedra — that is, in his official capacity as shepherd and teacher of all Christians — he defines a doctrine concerning faith or morals to be held by the whole Church. It was solemnly defined at the First Vatican Council in the constitution Pastor aeternus on 18 July 1870.

Conditions and scope

The charism is narrow: it does not imply impeccability (the pope can sin) nor inspiration, and it applies only to solemn definitions on faith and morals intended to bind the universal Church. Catholic teaching also attributes infallibility to the whole body of bishops when, together with the pope, they teach definitively — for example in ecumenical councils. The dogma's most widely acknowledged exercise is Pius XII's definition of the Assumption of Mary in Munificentissimus Deus (1950); the other universally cited instance is Pius IX's definition of the Immaculate Conception (1854), pre-dating the formal definition.

Controversy

The definition was contested in the run-up to 1870 by "inopportunists" who feared its effects, was opposed historically by Gallican and conciliarist traditions, and after 1870 prompted the Old Catholic schism in parts of Germany, Austria and Switzerland. Eastern Orthodox and Protestant churches reject the dogma, making it a significant ecumenical obstacle. The Second Vatican Council reaffirmed it while situating the pope within the college of bishops.
