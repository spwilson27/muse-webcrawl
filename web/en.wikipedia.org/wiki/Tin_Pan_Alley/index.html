Tin Pan Alley

Tin Pan Alley was the collection of music publishers and songwriters who dominated the popular music of the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The name referred to the specific area of West 28th Street between Fifth and Sixth Avenues in Manhattan, New York City, where many of these publishers were headquartered. The term eventually became synonymous with the American sheet music publishing industry as a whole.

Name origin

The origin of the name is often attributed to a joke that compared the sound of many pianos being pounded by "song pluggers" demonstrating songs to the din of tin pans being beaten. Journalist Monroe Rosenfeld is traditionally credited with coining the phrase in the New York Herald around 1903.

History

The industry developed in the mid-1880s when a number of music publishers set up shop in the same Manhattan district. Previously, sheet music publishing had been dominated by firms in cities such as Boston and Chicago. Harry Von Tilzer called himself "the father of Tin Pan Alley", claiming his firm, established around 1892, helped set the trend.

Key to the business were song pluggers: pianists and singers who promoted songs by performing them for publishers, performers and department-store customers. Copyright law shaped the industry profoundly once the Copyright Act of 1909 established compulsory mechanical royalties, and the performance rights society ASCAP, founded in 1914, began collecting royalties for public performance.

Songwriting and output

Tin Pan Alley songs were typically constructed with a verse-and-chorus structure, featuring a catchy 32-bar AABA chorus. Genres included the sentimental ballad, ragtime, the novelty song, comic songs, and tunes for vaudeville and the revue. Landmark songs of the era include "Take Me Out to the Ball Game" (1908), "Let Me Call You Sweetheart" (1910), and the wartime song "Over There" (1917).

Decline

The importance of the Alley waned from the 1930s as radio, sound film, and the phonograph record replaced sheet music as the primary means of distributing popular song. Many of its songwriters migrated to Hollywood or moved into Broadway work; the great songwriting teams of the Great American Songbook generation—Berlin, the Gershwins, Kern, Porter, Rodgers and Hammerstein—carried its craft into the age of broadcasting.
