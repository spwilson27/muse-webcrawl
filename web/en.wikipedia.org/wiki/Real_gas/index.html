The compressibility factor Z of real gases deviates from the ideal value of 1 at high pressure.

Real gases are non-ideal gases whose molecules occupy a non-negligible volume and interact with one another through intermolecular forces. The behavior of a real gas approaches that of an ideal gas at low pressures and high temperatures, but departs from it significantly at high pressures and near the temperature at which the gas condenses into a liquid. Real-gas effects must be included in accurate work in chemical engineering, high-pressure storage, rocketry, and supercritical-fluid processes.

Compressibility factor

Deviation from ideality is commonly measured by the compressibility factor:

Z = PV / nRT

For an ideal gas Z is exactly 1 at all conditions. For real gases, attractive forces make Z < 1 at moderate pressures (the gas is more compressible than ideal), while at high pressures the finite molecular volume dominates and Z > 1. By the principle of corresponding states, gases at the same reduced temperature and pressure have approximately the same Z, which allows generalized compressibility charts to be used for many substances.

Models

  • Van der Waals equation (1873) — adds a parameter a for intermolecular attraction and b for molecular volume: (P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT.
  • Redlich–Kwong equation (1949) — improved temperature dependence of the attraction term.
  • Peng–Robinson equation (1976) — widely used in the petroleum industry for vapor–liquid equilibrium.
  • Virial equations — expansions of Z in powers of density whose coefficients are related to molecular interactions.
  • Benedict–Webb–Rubin equation — an empirical multi-parameter equation for hydrocarbons.
