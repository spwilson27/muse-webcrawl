Berge's lemma

Berge's lemma
FieldGraph theory
Proved byClaude Berge (1957)
StatementA matching is maximum iff it admits no augmenting path

In graph theory, Berge's lemma is a fundamental result about matchings. It states that a matching M in a graph is a maximum matching — that is, a matching of the largest possible cardinality — if and only if there is no augmenting path with respect to M: a path that starts and ends at free (unmatched) vertices and whose edges alternate between edges outside M and edges inside M. The lemma was proved by the French mathematician Claude Berge in 1957.

Proof idea

If an augmenting path exists, the matching can be enlarged by symmetric difference with the path: edges of the path belonging to M are removed and the remaining edges of the path are added, increasing the size of the matching by one. Hence a maximum matching has no augmenting path.

Conversely, if M is not maximum, let M′ be a larger matching. Consider the symmetric difference of M and M′, which decomposes into alternating paths and even cycles. Because M′ has more edges than M, some component must be a path containing more edges of M′ than of M; such a path begins and ends at vertices free with respect to M, so it is an augmenting path for M.

Applications

Berge's lemma underlies augmenting-path algorithms for maximum matching, including the Hungarian search method for bipartite graphs and Edmonds' blossom algorithm for general graphs. It is also the basis of the standard proof of the König–Egerváry theorem characterisation of bipartite matching.
