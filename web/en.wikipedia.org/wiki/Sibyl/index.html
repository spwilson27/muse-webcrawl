The sibyls (Greek: Σίβυλλα, Sibylla) were prophetesses or oracles in ancient Greece and Rome who were believed to utter divine revelations in a frenzied state, often while possessed by the god Apollo. Unlike the Pythia at Delphi, who served a single sanctuary, sibyls were associated with various places throughout the Mediterranean world, and their prophecies were collected in books of oracular verses.

The Delphic Sibyl, painted by Michelangelo on the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512).

The sibyls of antiquity

The Roman scholar Varro listed ten sibyls: the Persian, Libyan, Delphic, Cimmerian, Erythraean, Samian, Cumaean, Hellespontine, Phrygian and Tiburtine sibyls. The most famous was the Cumaean Sibyl of Cumae near Naples, who in legend guided Aeneas through the underworld in Virgil's Aeneid and who, according to Roman tradition, sold the Sibylline Books to King Tarquin — a collection of prophecies kept in the Temple of Jupiter in Rome and consulted by the Senate in times of crisis until their destruction by fire.

Later influence

In the Hellenistic and Roman periods, Jewish and later Christian writers composed the Sibylline Oracles, verses in the sibylline style that presented pagan prophetesses as witnesses to monotheistic truth. Because of this, sibyls were treated sympathetically in Christian art: Michelangelo painted five sibyls alongside the Hebrew prophets on the Sistine Chapel ceiling, and the sibyls appear in the Dies Irae hymn ("teste David cum Sibylla" — "as David and the Sibyl testify").
