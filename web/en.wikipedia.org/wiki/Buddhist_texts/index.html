Buddhist texts

Buddhist texts are the religious writings that preserve the teachings of Buddhism. They are vast in extent — far larger than most scriptural traditions — and are preserved in many languages, principally Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan. Unlike traditions with a single closed canon, Buddhism recognises overlapping canons that differ between the Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana schools.

Early texts and the Pali Canon

The Buddha's discourses were transmitted orally for centuries before being written down. The most complete surviving early canon is the Pali Canon (Tipiṭaka, "three baskets"), committed to writing in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE. It comprises the Vinaya Pitaka (monastic discipline), the Sutta Pitaka (discourses, including the Dhammapada and the Jataka tales of the Buddha's previous lives), and the Abhidhamma Pitaka (systematic philosophy). Parallel discourses, the Agamas, survive in Chinese translation from other early schools.

Mahayana sutras

From around the 1st century BCE, new Sanskrit scriptures appeared presenting themselves as the Buddha's higher teaching: the Prajnaparamita (Perfection of Wisdom) literature, the Lotus Sutra, the Pure Land sutras and the Avatamsaka Sutra among hundreds of others. These texts, translated into Chinese by monks such as Kumarajiva and Xuanzang, form the Chinese Buddhist canon, and were later translated again into Tibetan in the Kangyur, supplemented by the Tengyur of Indian treatises.

Tantras and commentaries

Vajrayana Buddhism added tantric texts setting out ritual, mantra and deity yoga. Around the canons grew enormous commentarial literatures — by Buddhaghosa in the Theravada world, by Nagarjuna, Asanga and Tsongkhapa in the Mahayana — as well as national works such as the Tibetan Book of the Dead (Bardo Thodol).

Preservation

Texts were preserved on palm leaves, birch bark and woodblock prints; the Korean Tripitaka, carved on over 80,000 blocks in the thirteenth century, remains in use at Haeinsa temple. Modern scholarship, through bodies such as the Pali Text Society (founded 1881), has produced critical editions and English translations, and digital corpora now make the canons widely accessible.
