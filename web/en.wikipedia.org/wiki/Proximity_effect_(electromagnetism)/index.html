Proximity effect (electromagnetism)

In electromagnetics, the proximity effect is the tendency for alternating current (AC) in a conductor to be redistributed by the magnetic fields of nearby conductors, so that the current flows in loops and the effective cross-section of the conductor is reduced. The result is an increase in the alternating-current resistance of the conductor, which grows with frequency.

Cause

A changing magnetic field induces eddy currents in a conductor (by Faraday's law of induction). When two conductors carrying AC lie close together — for example the adjacent turns of a coil, or the go and return conductors of a cable — the magnetic field of each conductor drives eddy currents in the other. These add to the main current on one side of the conductor and subtract on the other, crowding the current into a thin region near the facing (or opposite) surfaces, depending on the relative direction of the currents.

Relation to skin effect

The proximity effect is closely related to the skin effect, in which a conductor's own magnetic field drives its current towards its surface. In wound components such as transformers and inductors, the proximity effect is often the dominant source of winding loss at high frequencies, and it can increase the effective resistance by far more than the skin effect alone.

Mitigation

Engineers reduce proximity losses by using litz wire (many thin, individually insulated and transposed strands), by interleaving primary and secondary windings, and by choosing foil or layered winding arrangements that minimise the field build-up across the winding.
