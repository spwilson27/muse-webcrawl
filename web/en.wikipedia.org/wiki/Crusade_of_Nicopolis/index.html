Crusade of Nicopolis

Battle of Nicopolis
Date25 September 1396
LocationNicopolis, on the Danube (modern Nikopol, Bulgaria)
BelligerentsCrusader alliance (Hungary, France, Burgundy, Wallachia, Hospitallers and allies) vs Ottoman Empire
CommandersSigismund of Hungary, John the Fearless; Bayezid I, Stefan Lazarević
ResultDecisive Ottoman victory

The Crusade of Nicopolis was a joint expedition of Hungary, France, Burgundy, Wallachia, the Knights Hospitaller and other Christian powers against the expanding Ottoman Empire. It culminated in the Battle of Nicopolis on 25 September 1396, one of the last large-scale crusades of the Middle Ages, which ended in a crushing Ottoman victory.

Background

After the Ottoman victory at Kosovo in 1389, Sultan Bayezid I pressed into the Balkans and blockaded Constantinople. King Sigismund of Hungary appealed to the West, and Pope Boniface IX proclaimed a crusade. A large Franco-Burgundian contingent — including John, Count of Nevers (the future John the Fearless), Marshal Boucicaut and the constable Philip of Artois — joined Sigismund's Hungarian army and Mircea the Elder's Wallachians in 1396, marching down the Danube, capturing Vidin and Rahovo, and laying siege to Nicopolis.

The battle

Disregarding Sigismund's plan of battle, the French knights charged prematurely up the hill toward the Ottoman lines, scattering the light infantry and cavalry but exhausting themselves against the sipahi reserves behind a palisade of sharpened stakes. Bayezid's counterattack, reinforced by Serbian heavy cavalry under Stefan Lazarević, routed the crusader army. Many knights were captured; against his commanders' advice Bayezid had thousands of prisoners executed in reprisal for the earlier massacre of Ottoman prisoners at Rahovo. The leading nobles were ransomed for the enormous sum of 200,000 florins, paid in 1397.

Aftermath

The defeat shattered Christian resistance in the Balkans for a generation, though Bayezid's defeat by Timur at Ankara in 1402 gave Byzantium a reprieve. The crusade became a byword for chivalric recklessness and the fatal disunity of crusading armies.
