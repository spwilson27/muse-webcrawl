Interlingual homograph

An interlingual homograph is spelled identically in two languages but differs in meaning.

An interlingual homograph is a word that is spelled the same way in two languages but has a different meaning, and often a different pronunciation, in each. Interlingual homographs are studied in linguistics and psycholinguistics because they reveal how the bilingual mind stores and accesses words from two languages.

Examples

Well-known examples include:

  • Gift — in English, a present; in German, poison.
  • Pie — in English, a baked dish; in Spanish, a foot.
  • Red — in English, a colour; in Spanish, a net or network.
  • Actual — in English, real; in Spanish, current or present-day (a "false friend").

Where the meanings are entirely different, interlingual homographs overlap with the traditional category of false friends. Some interlingual homographs, called identical cognates, have the same or similar meanings in both languages, such as hotel in English and Spanish.

Psycholinguistic research

Experiments on bilingual word recognition show that when a bilingual reader encounters an interlingual homograph, lexical candidates from both languages are initially activated, even when the sentence context is entirely in one language. This finding supports non-selective models of bilingual lexical access, such as the Bilingual Interactive Activation (BIA) model developed by Ton Dijkstra and colleagues. Homograph effects are measured using tasks such as lexical decision and eye-tracking during reading, where identical spelling but conflicting meaning produces processing delays compared with control words.
