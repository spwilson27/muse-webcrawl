Thyra (Old Norse: Þyri or Þyrev; also recorded as Thorvi and Thyre) was a Danish queen consort, wife of King Gorm the Old and mother of King Harald Bluetooth. She is remembered on the Jelling stones, the great rune stones raised by her husband and son that are often described as Denmark's birth certificates. Gorm's smaller stone honours her as, in the conventional translation, "Denmark's adornment" (or "Denmark's salvation").

Sources and legends

Little is known with certainty about Thyra's life. She appears in 12th- and 13th-century histories such as Saxo Grammaticus's Gesta Danorum and the Icelandic sagas, which credit her with wisdom and with initiating the construction of the Danevirke earthwork defence in southern Jutland — though the Danevirke is now known to predate her by centuries. Legends describe her cleverness in outwitting suitors and in advising Gorm, and some traditions make her the daughter of an English king or of a nobleman named Klakk-Harald.

Burial

Thyra is generally believed to have died before Gorm, who raised her memorial stone at Jelling and built the great burial mounds there. Dendrochronological evidence suggests the north mound's burial chamber dates to around 959. Excavations in the 19th and 20th centuries found the chamber largely empty of remains, which has been interpreted as consistent with Harald Bluetooth later moving his parents' remains to the church he built at Jelling.

Legacy

The asteroid 115 Thyra is named after her. Her name remains in Danish usage, and the Jelling stone inscription makes her one of the earliest historically documented Danish queens.
