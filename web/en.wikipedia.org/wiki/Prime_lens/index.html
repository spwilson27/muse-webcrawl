A prime lens is a photographic lens of fixed focal length, as opposed to a zoom lens, which covers a range of focal lengths. Common prime focal lengths include 24 mm, 35 mm, 50 mm and 85 mm; a 50 mm prime on a full-frame camera is often called a "standard" or "nifty fifty" lens.

Advantages

Because a prime is designed for a single focal length, it can be made with fewer elements, giving it a larger maximum aperture (commonly f/1.8 or f/1.4) than a comparably priced zoom. Large apertures admit more light for low-light photography and allow a shallow depth of field, separating subjects from blurred backgrounds (bokeh). Primes are generally smaller, lighter and sharper than zooms at similar prices.

Disadvantages and use

The fixed focal length requires the photographer to move physically to change composition — "zooming with your feet" — and covering many focal lengths means carrying several lenses. Many portrait, street and documentary photographers favour primes for their speed and discretion, while photojournalists and event photographers often prefer the flexibility of zooms.

Terminology

The term "prime" originally referred in cinematography to the main lens used with a converter or adapter, but its meaning shifted to denote fixed focal length lenses generally; purists sometimes note the distinction when discussing vintage cinema equipment.
