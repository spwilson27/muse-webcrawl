Measurable cardinal

In mathematics, a measurable cardinal is a certain kind of large cardinal number. In order for a cardinal κ to be measurable, it is sufficient that there exist a κ-additive, non-trivial, two-valued measure on the set of all subsets of κ — equivalently, a non-principal κ-complete ultrafilter on κ, or an elementary embedding of the universe into an inner model that has κ as its critical point.

Definition

A measurable cardinal is an uncountable cardinal κ such that there exists a κ-additive measure μ on κ taking values in {0, 1}, with μ(κ) = 1 and μ({α}) = 0 for every ordinal α < κ. Such cardinals are strongly inaccessible, and indeed far stronger: a measurable cardinal is Mahlo, weakly compact, and carries a normal measure that concentrates on smaller cardinals with strong properties, leading to a rich hierarchy below κ.

History and consequences

The notion arose from the study of real-valued measurable cardinals in measure theory and was developed by Stanisław Ulam in 1930. Dana Scott proved that if measurable cardinals exist, then Gödel's constructible universe is not the whole universe (V ≠ L), a landmark result showing that measurable cardinals have concrete set-theoretic consequences. Measurable cardinals occupy a central place in the theory of large cardinals and inner model theory, and they are a stepping stone toward stronger notions such as supercompact and huge cardinals.
