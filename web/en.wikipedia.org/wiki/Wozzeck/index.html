Wozzeck, Op. 7, is an opera in three acts by the Austrian composer Alban Berg, composed between 1914 and 1922 and first performed in Berlin in 1925. Berg adapted the libretto himself from the unfinished play Woyzeck by Georg Büchner. It is regarded as one of the defining works of twentieth-century opera and a landmark of musical modernism.

Synopsis

Franz Wozzeck, a poor soldier, is tormented by his superiors — the Captain, who mocks him, and the Doctor, who pays him to submit to absurd nutritional experiments. His common-law wife Marie takes a lover, the Drum-Major. Consumed by jealousy and apocalyptic visions, Wozzeck murders Marie by a pond and, returning to recover the knife, drowns himself. The opera ends with their child playing, oblivious, as other children run to see the body.

Music and structure

Each of the three acts contains five scenes. Act I is built as a suite of character pieces (suite, rhapsody, march and lullaby, passacaglia, rondo); Act II is a five-movement symphony; Act III consists of six "inventions" on themes, a note, a rhythm, a chord and a key. Though largely atonal and using Sprechstimme, the opera also quotes folk song, military marches and a tavern piano, and its strict formal architecture underlies its emotional intensity.

Reception

The premiere, conducted by Erich Kleiber at the Berlin State Opera on 14 December 1925, established Berg's international reputation. Despite its demanding idiom, Wozzeck entered the regular repertory of major opera houses worldwide.
