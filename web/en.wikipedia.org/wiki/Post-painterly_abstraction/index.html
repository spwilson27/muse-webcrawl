Post-painterly abstraction

Post-painterly abstraction is a term coined by the art critic Clement Greenberg for an exhibition he curated at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art in 1964. It describes a tendency in abstract painting of the late 1950s and 1960s that reacted against the gestural, emotionally charged brushwork of Abstract Expressionism in favour of openness of composition, clarity of colour, and smooth, impersonal surfaces.

Characteristics and artists

The exhibition included 31 artists, among them Helen Frankenthaler, Morris Louis, Kenneth Noland, Jules Olitski, Ellsworth Kelly, Frank Stella, Sam Francis, and Al Held. Typical features included stain painting into unprimed canvas, hard-edged geometric forms, stripes and target motifs, and large areas of unmodulated colour. The movement overlapped strongly with Color Field painting and hard-edge painting.

Legacy

Greenberg presented post-painterly abstraction as the progressive continuation of modernist painting, stressing flatness and the purity of the medium. Although Greenberg's formalism was later challenged by minimalism and conceptual art, the exhibition remains a landmark in defining 1960s American abstract painting.
