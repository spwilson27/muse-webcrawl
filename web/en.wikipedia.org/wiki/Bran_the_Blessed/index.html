Bran the Blessed (Welsh: Brân Fendigaid or Bendigeidfran, literally "Blessed Crow/Raven") is a giant and a king of Britain in Welsh mythology. He appears in the Second Branch of the Mabinogion, the tale of Branwen ferch Llŷr, and is also mentioned in the Welsh Triads and the poem Preiddeu Annwfn. He is a son of Llŷr and Penarddun, and the brother of Branwen and Manawydan.

In the Second Branch

When Matholwch, king of Ireland, sues for the hand of Bran's sister Branwen, the marriage feast is spoiled by Bran's half-brother Efnisien, who mutilates the Irish horses. Bran makes compensation — including a magic cauldron that restores slain warriors to life (though without speech) — but Branwen is mistreated in Ireland after the birth of her son Gwern. A starling she has taught to speak carries her complaint across the sea, and Bran raises the host of Britain to rescue her. So large is Bran that no ship can carry him and no house contain him: he wades across the Irish Sea beside his fleet and later lies across a river as a bridge for his army — giving rise to the Welsh saying "he who is a leader, let him be a bridge".

Death and the talking head

In the ensuing battle, fought over the fate of Gwern, the Irish are destroyed but the British host is nearly wiped out; Efnisien redeems himself by destroying the cauldron at the cost of his own life. Bran, wounded in the foot by a poisoned spear, orders his seven surviving companions to cut off his head and carry it to the White Hill in London and bury it facing France as a protection against invasion. The head remains uncorrupted and converses cheerfully with the survivors through a long enchanted sojourn — 80 years in the otherworldly assembly at Gwales — before the burial is completed. According to the Triads, the head was later dug up by Arthur, who declared he would hold Britain by his own strength alone — one of the "Three Unfortunate Disclosures". Bran's son Caradog, left as regent, dies of grief when the usurper Caswallon seizes the throne.
