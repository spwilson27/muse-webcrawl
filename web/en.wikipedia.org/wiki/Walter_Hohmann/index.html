Walter Hohmann (18 March 1880 – 11 March 1945) was a German engineer who made an important contribution to orbital mechanics. In his 1925 book Die Erreichbarkeit der Himmelskörper ("The Attainability of Heavenly Bodies") he described the most fuel-efficient way for a spacecraft to move between two circular orbits — the elliptical path now universally known as the Hohmann transfer orbit.

Life and work

Hohmann was born in Hardheim and studied civil engineering in Munich, working as a structural engineer in Vienna, Berlin and Hanover before becoming chief architect for the city of Essen. Astronomy and spaceflight were his spare-time passion. Influenced in part by the science fiction of Kurd Laßwitz, he applied rigorous mathematics to rocket trajectories, rendezvous and aerobraking decades before flight hardware existed.

The transfer orbit

A Hohmann transfer uses two engine burns: one to leave the initial circular orbit onto an ellipse whose far point touches the target orbit, and a second at that far point to circularise. Though slow — an Earth–Mars transfer takes about 259 days — it requires the least propellant of any two-impulse manoeuvre and remains the standard profile for interplanetary probes and satellite orbit-raising. Hohmann also worked on rocket design theory in the 1930s, but distanced himself from the Nazi regime's military rocket programme and died in an Essen hospital in March 1945, days before the end of the war in Europe, in the aftermath of Allied bombing.

Legacy

The lunar crater Hohmann and asteroid 9661 Hohmann are named for him, and his transfer ellipse remains the first concept taught in astrodynamics.
