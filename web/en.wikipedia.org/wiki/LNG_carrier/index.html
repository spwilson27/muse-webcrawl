LNG carrier

An LNG carrier is a tank ship designed for transporting liquefied natural gas (LNG). LNG is natural gas cooled to about −162 °C (−260 °F), at which it becomes a liquid occupying roughly 1/600th of its gaseous volume, making seaborne transport between markets without pipelines economical.

Cargo containment

Two containment systems dominate. Moss-type carriers carry the gas in spherical aluminium tanks that protrude visibly above the deck. Membrane-type carriers use a thin metal membrane (stainless steel or Invar) backed by insulation within the hull, giving a flush deck and larger capacity for a given hull size. Modern standard carriers hold around 174,000 cubic metres; the largest, Qatar's Q-Max class, exceed 266,000 cubic metres. Insulation cannot fully prevent heat ingress, so a fraction of the cargo continuously evaporates as "boil-off gas", historically burned as fuel in steam turbines and now commonly handled by dual-fuel engines or reliquefaction plants.

History and fleet

The trade began with the converted Methane Pioneer, which carried LNG from Louisiana to Britain in 1959; the first purpose-built carrier, Methane Princess, entered service in 1964. Long dominated by Japan, South Korea and (for membrane systems) French technology, the industry expanded rapidly in the 2000s–2020s with new export projects in the United States, Qatar and Australia, and shipbuilding concentrated in Korean yards. A distinctive safety record and specialised crew training reflect the hazards of the cryogenic cargo.
