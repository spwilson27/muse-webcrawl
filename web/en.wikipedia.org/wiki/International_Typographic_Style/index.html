International Typographic Style

The International Typographic Style, also known as the Swiss Style, is a graphic design style that emerged in Russia, the Netherlands and Germany in the 1920s and was developed by designers in Switzerland after 1945. It emphasizes cleanliness, readability and objectivity, and had an immense influence on graphic design, architecture and art in the mid-20th century.

Characteristics

Key features of the style include:

  • Asymmetric layouts structured by a mathematical grid
  • Use of sans-serif typefaces such as Akzidenz-Grotesk and Helvetica
  • Left-aligned, ragged-right text
  • Objective photography instead of illustration
  • Generous white space and a limited colour palette

History

The style grew out of the modernist movements of the early 20th century, including De Stijl, the Bauhaus and the New Typography of Jan Tschichold. It was systematized in postwar Switzerland by designers such as Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann and Emil Ruder, whose grid-based methods were codified in Müller-Brockmann's Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981). The style spread internationally through Swiss-trained designers and multinational corporate identity programmes.

Influence

The Swiss Style shaped corporate, editorial and public information design worldwide, from transport signage to annual reports, and its principles continue to inform contemporary interface and web design.
