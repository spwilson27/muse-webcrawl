Crude oil, or petroleum, is a naturally occurring, yellowish-black liquid mixture of hydrocarbons found in geological formations. The name petroleum covers both naturally occurring unprocessed crude oil and petroleum products made from refined oil; the term comes from Latin petra (rock) and oleum (oil).

Petroleum is the world's most important primary energy source and feedstock: it is refined into fuels such as gasoline (petrol), diesel, kerosene, and liquefied petroleum gas, and into petrochemical building blocks for plastics, fertilizers, solvents, and pharmaceuticals. Global consumption is around 100 million barrels per day. Burning petroleum products is a major source of carbon dioxide emissions and a driver of climate change.

Formation

Petroleum forms from the remains of ancient marine organisms — mainly zooplankton and algae — buried under sedimentary rock and subjected to intense heat and pressure over millions of years. The organic matter first becomes kerogen, which with further heating cracks into liquid and gaseous hydrocarbons. The oil migrates upward through porous rock until it is trapped by impermeable formations, forming reservoirs, from which it is extracted by drilling. The biogenic origin is supported by characteristic biomarker molecules and isotopic signatures, though a minor abiogenic theory of deep-Earth origin has been debated historically.

Composition and classification

Crude oils vary widely in colour (from nearly clear to tar-black), density, and chemistry. They are classified by density using API gravity — light crude (API > 31.1°), medium, heavy, and extra-heavy — and by sulfur content: "sweet" crudes contain less than 0.5% sulfur, "sour" crudes more. Light, sweet crudes command premium prices because they yield more high-value products with less processing. Major traded grades and benchmarks include Brent (North Sea), West Texas Intermediate, and Dubai crude.

Extraction

Production begins with exploration (seismic surveys, exploratory wells), followed by primary recovery using reservoir pressure and pumps (e.g., nodding donkey pumpjacks), secondary recovery (water or gas injection), and enhanced oil recovery (steam, CO₂ injection, chemicals). Offshore production from platforms in areas such as the Gulf of Mexico and the North Sea, and unconventional production from oil sands (Canada, Venezuela) and tight oil via hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling (US shale), supply large shares of world output.

Refining and products

Refineries separate crude oil by fractional distillation into fractions by boiling range, then upgrade them by cracking, reforming, and treating. Typical outputs include liquefied petroleum gas, naphtha, gasoline, aviation fuel, diesel and heating oil, lubricants, waxes, asphalt (bitumen), and petroleum coke; a substantial share of "lighter" feedstocks feeds the petrochemical industry. Refinery configuration determines which crude grades can be processed profitably.

History and industry

Oil seeps were used for thousands of years (for waterproofing, medicine, and warfare), but the modern industry began in the mid-19th century: the 1859 Drake well in Pennsylvania and contemporary developments in Baku launched commercial production; kerosene lighting, then the automobile, created mass demand. Giant companies and cartels — Standard Oil, its successors, and the "Seven Sisters" — dominated the 20th century, followed by the rise of national oil companies and OPEC (founded 1960), the 1973 and 1979 oil shocks, price volatility ever since, and contemporary debate over "peak oil" demand as energy transitions proceed.

Environmental impact

Petroleum use emits roughly a third of global energy-related CO₂. Spills (e.g., Exxon Valdez 1989, Deepwater Horizon 2010), routine leakage, air pollution from combustion, and the plastic lifecycle impose major ecological costs. Oil-producing regions also face issues of land disturbance, produced-water disposal, and methane emissions across the supply chain.
