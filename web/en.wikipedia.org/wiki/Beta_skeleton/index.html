Beta skeleton

A β-skeleton of a set of points, for a value of β between 1 and 2

In computational geometry, the beta skeleton or β-skeleton is a directed graph defined from a set of points in Euclidean space. Two points p and q are connected by an edge if all the angles pqx, for all points x in a certain region determined by p, q and a parameter β, are at most 90 degrees. The concept was introduced by Kirkpatrick and Radke in 1985 as a generalisation of several earlier shape descriptors, including the alpha shape, the Gabriel graph, and the relative neighborhood graph.

Definition

For β ≥ 1, the neighbourhood region of a pair of points p and q is the intersection of two disks of radius β·d(p, q)/2 passing through both points; p and q are joined when this lune-shaped region contains no other points of the set. For 0 < β < 1 a different, union-of-disks region is used. The parameter β controls how "strict" the emptiness condition is, and hence how sparse the resulting graph is.

Special cases and properties

For β = 1 the β-skeleton is the Gabriel graph; for β = 2 it is the relative neighborhood graph. As β increases beyond 1, the graphs become progressively sparser, and in the limit they approach the Euclidean minimum spanning tree. β-skeletons are subgraphs of the Delaunay triangulation for β in the appropriate range, which allows them to be computed efficiently. They have been applied to shape reconstruction from unorganised point samples, wireless network topology control, and the analysis of geographic and astronomical point patterns — including reconstructing the filamentary structure of galaxy distributions.
