Pahlavi dynasty

The Pahlavi dynasty was the last dynasty of Iranian monarchs, ruling Iran (Persia) from 1925 to 1979. Founded by Reza Shah Pahlavi, a Cossack Brigade officer who seized power in the 1920s, the dynasty comprised only two shahs — Reza Shah (r. 1925–1941) and his son Mohammad Reza Shah (r. 1941–1979) — before it was swept away by the Iranian Revolution, which established the Islamic Republic under Ayatollah Khomeini.

Reza Shah

Reza Khan rose through the Persian Cossack Brigade, led the 1921 coup, became war minister and prime minister, and in 1925 deposed the Qajar dynasty, taking the throne as Reza Shah. His reign drove rapid modernisation: a national railway, roads, secular courts and schools, a modern army, the University of Tehran, and state-led industry — alongside authoritarian rule, the forced unveiling of women (1936) and violent campaigns against tribal autonomy. When Britain and the USSR invaded Iran in 1941 to secure supply routes, Reza Shah was forced to abdicate in favour of his son and died in exile in Johannesburg in 1944.

Mohammad Reza Shah

Mohammad Reza Shah's long reign was shaped by oil and the Cold War. The nationalisation of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company under Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh led to the 1953 coup, backed by the CIA and British intelligence, which restored the Shah's authority. From 1963 his "White Revolution" pursued land reform, literacy and women's suffrage, while oil revenues funded industrialisation and a vast military. Political repression by the SAVAK secret police, corruption and the social dislocations of rapid change fed growing opposition from clerics, leftists and bazaar merchants.

Fall

In 1978 mass protests and strikes paralysed the country. The Shah left Iran on 16 January 1979; Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini returned from exile on 1 February, and the monarchy collapsed on 11 February 1979. Mohammad Reza Shah died in Cairo in 1980. His son, Reza Pahlavi, remains head of the house in exile and a figurehead for Iranian monarchist opposition groups.
