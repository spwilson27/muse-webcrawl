Iron Curtain

The Iron Curtain was the political, military and ideological boundary that divided Europe between the Soviet bloc in the east and the Western democracies during the Cold War, from the end of the Second World War in 1945 until the Revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union. The phrase was popularised by Winston Churchill in his "Sinews of Peace" speech of 5 March 1946 in Fulton, Missouri: "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." The metaphorical curtain was made physical by border fortifications, minefields and, in Berlin from 1961, the Berlin Wall.

Origins of the term

The expression "iron curtain" had earlier theatrical and figurative uses — an iron curtain was a fireproof safety curtain in theatres — and was applied to Soviet Russia by several writers from 1920 onward; Joseph Goebbels used it in a 1945 article, and Churchill himself had used it in a 1945 telegram to President Truman before the Fulton speech made it famous. On the Soviet side it was denounced as warmongering, but the phrase came to define the mental map of Cold War Europe: the Eastern Bloc states of the Warsaw Pact on one side, the NATO and Western European states on the other, with neutrals such as Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, Finland and Yugoslavia in between or apart.

The physical barrier

Along much of its roughly 6,800-kilometre length, the divide was a heavily fortified frontier: fences (some electrified or alarmed), watchtowers, ploughed control strips, minefields, anti-vehicle ditches and patrol roads, guarded by border troops with orders, in the East German case, to shoot escapees. The most fortified sections were the inner German border between West and East Germany and the Czechoslovak borders with West Germany and Austria. Crossing legally was restricted and heavily controlled; hundreds of people died attempting to escape across it, including at least 140 at the Berlin Wall alone.

Life along the divide

The Iron Curtain cut through communities, farmland and ecosystems, restricted trade, travel, information and family contact, and symbolised the broader suppression of movement and expression within the Eastern Bloc. Western broadcasts such as Radio Free Europe sought to penetrate it, while Eastern regimes jammed foreign radio. Areas depopulated along the western side of the border strip later became, unintentionally, a wildlife corridor — the European Green Belt.

Fall of the Iron Curtain

The barrier began to open in 1989: Hungary dismantled its border fence with Austria from May 1989 and in September allowed East German holidaymakers to cross to the West, triggering the exodus that helped bring down the East German regime. The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, Germany reunified in 1990, and the Warsaw Pact and the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991. Portions of the fortifications are preserved as memorials and museums, and the term survives in political metaphor — later echoed in phrases describing other closed frontiers, from the "bamboo curtain" once applied to China to digital divides.
