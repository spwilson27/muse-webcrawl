Active listening

Active listening originated in Carl Rogers' client-centred therapy.

Active listening is a communication technique in which the listener fully concentrates on, understands, responds to and remembers what is being said, and gives feedback demonstrating that understanding. It was developed from the work of psychologists Carl Rogers and Richard Farson, who coined the term in 1957 within the framework of client-centred therapy.

Techniques

Core techniques include paying full attention and avoiding interruption; using body language such as eye contact and nodding; paraphrasing and reflecting the speaker's words and feelings back to them; summarising; and asking open, clarifying questions. The listener withholds judgement and avoids immediately offering advice or redirecting the conversation to their own experience.

Applications

Active listening is used in counselling and psychotherapy, conflict resolution and mediation, medicine, education, management and customer service. Research in therapeutic contexts associates it with greater client satisfaction and a stronger working alliance, though critics note it can feel artificial when applied mechanically, and that cultural norms for eye contact and self-disclosure vary.
