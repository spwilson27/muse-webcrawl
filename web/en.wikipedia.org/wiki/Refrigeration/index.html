Refrigeration is the process of removing heat from an enclosed space or substance to lower its temperature, transferring the heat elsewhere. Its principal application is preserving food by slowing bacterial growth, but refrigeration also underpins air conditioning, industrial chemistry, cryogenics, and the global "cold chain" of refrigerated transport.

History

For millennia people stored harvested ice and snow in ice houses. In the 19th century a natural-ice trade shipped New England pond ice as far as India. Artificial refrigeration developed through the century: William Cullen demonstrated a vapour-compression machine in 1755, Jacob Perkins patented one in 1834, and practical commercial systems followed, transforming brewing, meatpacking, and shipping. Household electric refrigerators became common in the 1920s–30s, displacing the icebox.

The vapour-compression cycle

Most refrigerators circulate a refrigerant through four stages: compression, condensation (releasing heat outside), expansion (a sharp drop in pressure and temperature), and evaporation (absorbing heat from the interior). Early refrigerants such as ammonia and CFCs were replaced after CFCs were found to deplete stratospheric ozone; the 1987 Montreal Protocol phased them out in favour of HFCs and, increasingly, low-impact alternatives such as propane and CO₂.

Impact

Refrigeration reshaped diets and cities, allowing perishable foods to travel continents and enabling supermarkets and year-round fresh produce. It also carries environmental costs in energy use and refrigerant leakage, a continuing focus of regulation.
