Olfaction

Schematic of the olfactory system.

Olfaction is the sense of smell, the chemosense by which organisms detect airborne molecules (odorants). Together with taste (gustation), it forms the chemical senses. In humans, olfaction plays a central role in the flavour of food, in the detection of hazards such as smoke and spoiled food, and — through its direct connections to the limbic system — in emotion and memory.

Mechanism

Inhaled odorants dissolve in the mucus covering the olfactory epithelium at the roof of the nasal cavity and bind to receptors on olfactory sensory neurons. Humans possess roughly 400 functional types of olfactory receptors — G protein-coupled receptors whose discovery by Linda B. Buck and Richard Axel earned the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Remarkably, each sensory neuron expresses only one receptor type, and all neurons of a type converge on the same glomeruli in the olfactory bulb, from which signals pass to the piriform cortex and limbic structures, largely bypassing the thalamus on the first relay.

Capability and dysfunction

Although often considered a "minor" sense in humans, olfaction is highly discriminative: a widely cited 2014 study estimated that humans can distinguish more than one trillion olfactory stimuli, a figure debated but indicative of large capacity. Loss of smell (anosmia) and distortion (parosmia) can result from upper respiratory infections — including COVID-19, in which sudden anosmia became a hallmark symptom — head injury, neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's (where smell loss may be an early sign), and aging. Smell training and treatment of the underlying cause are the main therapies.

In other animals

Most mammals are macrosmatic, with smell dominating their sensory world: dogs have up to 300 million olfactory receptors and are trained to detect explosives, disease, and missing persons. Many insects detect pheromones at vanishingly low concentrations with their antennae, and some fish navigate home by the smell of their natal streams.
