The Appalachian Basin is a large foreland sedimentary basin in the eastern United States, lying west of the Appalachian Mountains and extending some 1,300 kilometres (800 mi) from Alabama and Georgia in the south to Lake Ontario and the St. Lawrence lowlands in the north, covering roughly 480,000 square kilometres (185,000 sq mi). It contains one of the thickest and most complete Palaeozoic sedimentary successions in the world and has been a major source of coal, petroleum, and natural gas for nearly two centuries.

Geological development

The basin began forming in the Cambrian with the breakup of the supercontinent Rodinia and the opening of the Iapetus Ocean, which created a passive margin along eastern Laurentia. During the Ordovician through the Permian, three major mountain-building episodes — the Taconic, Acadian, and Alleghanian orogenies — loaded the crust along the margin, causing the adjacent lithosphere to flex downward and creating the foreland basin. Sediment shed from the rising mountains to the east filled the basin, accumulating in places to thicknesses exceeding 12 kilometres (40,000 ft), including deltaic complexes such as the Devonian Catskill delta.

Stratigraphy

The basin fill spans the Cambrian to Permian periods and includes sandstones, shales, limestones, and coal measures. Notable units include the Ordovician Trenton limestone, the Silurian Clinton sandstones and Tuscarora quartzite, the Devonian Oriskany sandstone and the organic-rich Marcellus Shale, and the Pennsylvanian cyclothems of the Allegheny and Pocahontas groups that host the basin's famous bituminous coal seams. The Utica Shale (Ordovician) underlies much of the basin at great depth.

Energy resources

The Appalachian Basin is the birthplace of the modern petroleum industry: Edwin Drake's 1859 oil well near Titusville, Pennsylvania, was drilled into Devonian sandstones of the basin. The region was the leading American coal producer for more than a century, with vast bituminous deposits in Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Ohio, and Kentucky. In the twenty-first century, horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing of the Devonian Marcellus Shale and the Ordovician Utica Shale transformed the basin into one of the world's largest natural gas fields, making it the largest gas-producing region in the United States.
