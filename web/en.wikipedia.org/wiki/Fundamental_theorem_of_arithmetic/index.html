Fundamental theorem of arithmetic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A factor tree for 120 = 2³ × 3 × 5: every integer has exactly one such decomposition.

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic, also called the unique factorization theorem, states that every integer greater than 1 can be represented uniquely as a product of prime numbers, up to the order of the factors. For example,

1200 = 24 · 3 · 52 = 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 5 · 5

and no other factorisation of 1200 into primes is possible, disregarding order. The theorem has two parts: existence (every integer > 1 has at least one prime factorisation) and uniqueness (the factorisation is the same apart from order).

History

Elements of the theorem appear in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC): Book VII, Proposition 30 (Euclid's lemma) is the key step for uniqueness, and Book IX, Proposition 14 is close to the full statement. The first full, explicit statement and proof is due to Carl Friedrich Gauss in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), who noted the assumption of uniqueness had often been used without proof.

Proof outline

Existence is proved by strong induction: if n is prime, it is its own factorisation; if composite, n = ab with a, b < n, and the inductive hypothesis factors a and b. Uniqueness rests on Euclid's lemma: if a prime p divides a product ab, then p divides a or p divides b. Applying the lemma repeatedly shows the primes in any two factorisations of n must match one-for-one.

Generalizations

The theorem explains why 1 is not considered prime: if it were, factorisations would not be unique (1 could appear arbitrarily many times). Abstracted, the theorem says the ring of integers is a unique factorisation domain; Gauss extended the result to the Gaussian integers, while rings such as Z[√−5] fail unique factorisation (6 = 2·3 = (1+√−5)(1−√−5)), a failure that motivated the theory of ideals.

Applications

Prime factorisation underlies the canonical representation of positive rational numbers, computations of gcd and lcm, Euler's totient function, and the security assumptions of the RSA cryptosystem, which relies on the practical difficulty of factoring large integers.
