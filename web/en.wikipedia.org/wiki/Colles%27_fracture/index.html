Colles' fracture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Colles' fracture is a fracture of the distal radius bone in the forearm, close to the wrist, in which the broken end of the radius is displaced backwards (dorsally). It is one of the most common fractures in adults and is classically caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand. The injury was described by the Irish surgeon Abraham Colles in 1814.

Signs and symptoms

The classic appearance is the "dinner fork" or "silver fork" deformity: viewed from the side, the wrist and hand are displaced backwards relative to the forearm. Pain, swelling, bruising and loss of wrist movement are typical. The fracture is especially common in older people with osteoporosis, particularly postmenopausal women, and it often occurs together with a fracture of the ulnar styloid process.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made from the history and examination and confirmed by plain X-rays of the wrist in two planes, which show the distal radial fracture with dorsal displacement and angulation, often with shortening of the radius and intra-articular involvement in some cases. The opposite injury, with the fragment displaced towards the palm (volarly), is called a Smith's fracture.

Treatment

Displaced fractures are usually treated by closed reduction — manipulating the fragments back into position under local, regional or general anaesthesia — followed by immobilisation in a plaster cast for roughly five to six weeks. Unstable, severely displaced or intra-articular fractures may require surgery, such as fixation with percutaneous wires or a volar locking plate. Physiotherapy and hand therapy are commonly used to restore movement and strength, and complications can include malunion, stiffness, median nerve symptoms and complex regional pain syndrome.
