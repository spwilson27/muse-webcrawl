The Book of Gates is an ancient Egyptian funerary text of the New Kingdom, first attested in the reign of Horemheb. It describes the journey of the sun god Ra through the underworld (the Duat) during the twelve hours of the night, and the corresponding judgement and rebirth that lead to his rising at dawn.


Ra's barque passes through a division of the night

Structure

The night is divided into twelve hours, separated by gates — the feature that gives the book its modern name — each guarded by deities and fire-spitting serpents. Ra travels in his barque, accompanied by Sia (perception) and Heka (magic), while gods, the blessed dead, and the peoples of the world appear in registers beside the river of the underworld. Unlike the earlier Amduat, the Book of Gates gives prominent space to the judgement of the dead before Osiris, which occupies the fifth hour. In the final hour Ra is reborn as Khepri, the scarab, and rises into the morning sky.

Sources and copies

The earliest partial copy appears in the tomb of Horemheb; the most complete version was carved on the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I (now in Sir John Soane's Museum, London), and full or near-full versions decorated the tombs of Ramesses I, Ramesses II, and later Ramesside kings in the Valley of the Kings. Portions were also copied in later periods on papyri and sarcophagi.

Significance

Together with the Amduat and the Book of Caverns, the Book of Gates forms the great trilogy of New Kingdom "books of the underworld". Its systematic vision of judgement, reward and punishment, and of the sun's nightly regeneration, was central to Egyptian royal funerary belief and to the hope that the deceased king — and later any justified dead person — would share Ra's daily rebirth.
