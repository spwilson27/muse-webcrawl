Fai chun

Fai chun (揮春) or chunlian in a small, single-character or short-phrase form, is a traditional decoration frequently used during Chinese New Year. People put fai chun on door panels, walls and windows to create a jubilant festive atmosphere, and to express wishes for good luck and prosperity in the coming year. They are typically written in black or gold ink on red paper — red symbolising good fortune and joy — though gold paper with red characters is also used.

Forms and content

Fai chun range from the single character 福 (fu, "good fortune") — famously pasted upside down so that fortune "arrives" (a pun in Chinese) — to four-character idioms and longer phrases wishing for health, wealth, academic success, safety or a flourishing business. Businesses display fai chun with wishes for prosperity, while households choose blessings suited to the family. Vertical couplets (chunlian proper) flank doorways, while the smaller square or rectangular fai chun are placed on doors, rice bins, walls and office desks.

History

The custom descends from the older practice of hanging taofu — peach-wood charms inscribed with the names of guardian deities — to ward off evil. With the spread of paper, these evolved into written New Year couplets and auspicious slips. Handwritten fai chun by calligraphers remain popular in Hong Kong, Taiwan, Guangdong and diaspora communities, alongside mass-printed versions.
