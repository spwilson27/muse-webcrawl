Food miles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Food miles is the distance food is transported from the place where it is produced to the consumer. The term was popularised in the early 1990s by Tim Lang, professor of food policy at City University London, as a way of highlighting the hidden ecological, social and economic costs of the modern food system.

Concept

Globalised supply chains mean ingredients for a single meal may travel many thousands of miles, often by air freight, which produces far more carbon dioxide per tonne than shipping. Food miles became a popular shorthand for the environmental footprint of food and a rallying idea for the local food movement, prompting some retailers to label air-freighted produce and some consumers to favour seasonal, locally grown food.

Criticism

Researchers point out that distance alone is a poor proxy for impact. Transport is often a small share of a food's total emissions compared with production: heating greenhouses to grow tomatoes out of season locally can emit more than importing field-grown ones. Studies such as DEFRA's 2005 report concluded that a simple food-miles indicator is misleading, and that mode of transport (air versus sea), production methods, and the shopper's own journey matter as much as distance. The concept also raises fairness questions for developing-country farmers whose exports are targeted by "buy local" campaigns despite efficient sea freight.

Legacy

Although life-cycle assessment is now preferred by scientists, "food miles" remains influential in public debate and marketing, and helped popularise concern about the carbon footprint of diets.
