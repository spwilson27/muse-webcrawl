Achilles and the tortoise

Achilles and the tortoise is one of the paradoxes of motion described by the Greek philosopher Zeno of Elea in the 5th century BC, intended to support Parmenides' doctrine that motion is impossible. In the paradox, Achilles races a tortoise and gives it a head start; Zeno argues Achilles can never overtake it.

The paradox

Suppose Achilles runs ten times faster than the tortoise and gives it a hundred metres' start. By the time Achilles reaches the tortoise's starting point, the tortoise has moved ten metres ahead. Achilles covers those ten metres, but the tortoise has advanced one metre; and so on indefinitely. Since there are infinitely many such points Achilles must reach, Zeno concludes he can never catch the tortoise — a conclusion at war with everyday experience.

Resolutions

The standard mathematical resolution notes that the infinite sequence of intervals forms a convergent geometric series: the distances and times shrink proportionally and sum to a finite total (in the example, Achilles catches up after 111⅑ metres), so an infinite number of steps can be completed in finite time. Aristotle discussed the paradox by distinguishing potential from actual infinity; philosophers continue to debate supertasks and the structure of space and time raised by Zeno's argument, which survives in Aristotle's Physics since Zeno's own writings are lost.
