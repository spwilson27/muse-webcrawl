Delahaye

Delahaye was a French automotive manufacturing company founded by Émile Delahaye in 1894 in Tours, France. It built automobiles until 1954 and was celebrated for elegant, high-performance cars — most famously the Type 135 and the extravagantly styled Type 165 — as well as racing successes and a substantial production of trucks, fire engines, and military vehicles.

History

Émile Delahaye, an engineer with experience in steam engines and ceramics machinery, built his first cars in the mid-1890s and entered them in early races such as Paris–Marseille–Paris. After his retirement in 1901, the company developed under professional management, producing luxury cars, commercial vehicles, and the well-known V6-engined models of the early twentieth century.

In the 1930s Delahaye achieved its greatest fame. The Type 135 (1935) combined a 3.2–3.6 litre overhead-valve six with independent front suspension and won the Monte Carlo Rally and the 1938 24 Hours of Le Mans. Coachbuilders such as Figoni et Falaschi, Saoutchik, and Chapron clothed Delahayes in some of the most celebrated Art Deco bodies of the era.

After the Second World War, punitive French taxation of large cars damaged the luxury market. Delahaye responded with the utilitarian VLR, a light military reconnaissance vehicle, and merged with Hotchkiss in 1954, ending car production under the Delahaye name shortly thereafter.

Notable models

  • Type 135 — grand touring and competition car, 1935–1952
  • Type 165 — V12 flagship of 1938–1939
  • Type 175 — postwar luxury model
  • Type 235 — final model, 1951–1954
