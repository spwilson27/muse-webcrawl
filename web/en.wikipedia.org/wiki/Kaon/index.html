Kaon

In particle physics, a kaon (/ˈkeɪ.ɒn/), also called a K meson and denoted K, is any of a group of four mesons distinguished by a quantum number called strangeness. In the quark model they are understood to be bound states of a strange quark (or antiquark) and an up or down antiquark (or quark).

Kaons have played a key role in the development of particle physics. Their unexpectedly long lifetimes and unusual decay modes — the "tau–theta puzzle" — led to the discovery of parity violation in the weak interaction, and neutral kaon oscillations led to the discovery of CP violation by James Cronin and Val Fitch in 1964.

History

Kaons were first observed in 1947 by George Rochester and Clifford Butler in cloud-chamber photographs of cosmic rays at the University of Manchester, which showed unexpected V-shaped tracks from particles of intermediate mass. Because they were produced strongly but decayed slowly, they were classed among the "strange particles", and the new quantum number strangeness was introduced by Murray Gell-Mann and others to explain their behaviour.

Neutral kaon mixing and CP violation

The neutral kaons K⁰ and its antiparticle are produced by the strong interaction, but they decay through the weak interaction as two mass eigenstates, KS and KL, with very different lifetimes. A beam of pure K⁰ therefore oscillates into its antiparticle as it travels. In 1964, an experiment at Brookhaven National Laboratory by Cronin and Fitch found that the long-lived KL occasionally decayed into two pions — a mode forbidden if CP symmetry were exact — demonstrating CP violation, a discovery recognised by the 1980 Nobel Prize in Physics.
