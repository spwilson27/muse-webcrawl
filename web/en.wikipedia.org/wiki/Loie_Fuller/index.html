Loie Fuller
Born: Marie Louise Fuller, 15 January 1862, Fullersburg, Illinois
Died: 1 January 1928 (aged 65), Paris
Occupation: Dancer, choreographer, lighting designer

Loie Fuller (born Marie Louise Fuller; January 15, 1862 – January 1, 1928), also known as Loïe Fuller, was an American dancer and a pioneer of both modern dance and theatrical lighting techniques.

Fuller's Serpentine Dance created swirling shapes from vast lengths of silk.

Career

Fuller began her career as a child actress and burlesque performer in the United States. In 1891 she developed the Serpentine Dance, in which she manipulated hundreds of yards of silk costume, extended by wands sewn into the sleeves, under shifting coloured electric lights of her own design. Performing it in Paris at the Folies Bergère from 1892, she became an overnight sensation and an icon of the Art Nouveau movement, depicted by Toulouse-Lautrec, Rodin and many others.

Innovation

Fuller patented numerous stage effects — chemical salts for coloured gels, luminescent dyes, mirrored stages, and her costume and lighting apparatus — and held early patents relating to theatrical lighting. She collaborated with scientists including Marie Curie (whom she asked about radium for a luminous costume), and she experimented with film, producing Le Lys de la vie (1920). Her work moved dance away from narrative ballet toward abstraction, light and movement, influencing Isadora Duncan, who toured with her company, and the whole of modern dance.

Later life

Fuller spent most of her later life in Paris, running a dance school and company, and writing her memoir Fifteen Years of a Dancer's Life (1913). She died of pneumonia in Paris in 1928 and is buried in Père Lachaise Cemetery.
