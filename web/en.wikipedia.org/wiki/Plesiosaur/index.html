Plesiosaurs (order Plesiosauria) were a group of marine reptiles that lived from the latest Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous Period, about 204 to 66 million years ago. They had a distinctive body plan: a broad, turtle-like body, a short tail, and four large flippers with which they "flew" through the water, much as sea turtles and penguins do. The group includes the long-necked plesiosauroids and the short-necked, large-headed pliosauroids.

Discovery

The first complete plesiosaur skeleton was discovered in 1823 by the fossil collector Mary Anning at Lyme Regis, Dorset, and described by William Conybeare, who had named the genus Plesiosaurus in 1821. Its anatomy — a small head on a very long neck, with paddle-like limbs — was so unlike any living animal that some contemporaries suspected a forgery. Plesiosaurs became central exhibits in nineteenth-century palaeontology and featured in the famous Crystal Palace dinosaur sculptures.

Anatomy and lifestyle

Plesiosaurs ranged from about 1.5 metres to giants such as the pliosaur Pliosaurus and the elasmosaurids, some exceeding 10 metres. Long-necked forms probably used their necks to strike at fish and cephalopods, while short-necked pliosaurs were apex predators of other marine reptiles and large fish. Unusually among reptiles, plesiosaurs gave birth to live young — demonstrated by a fossil of Polycotylus containing a single large foetus. Gastroliths (stomach stones) are commonly found with skeletons. Like other Mesozoic marine reptiles, they became extinct in the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event 66 million years ago.

Classification

Plesiosauria is traditionally divided into the long-necked Plesiosauroidea (including Elasmosauridae and Cryptoclididae) and the short-necked Pliosauroidea (including Pliosauridae and the polycotylids), though modern cladistic analyses suggest pliosaurs evolved from within the plesiosauroid radiation.
