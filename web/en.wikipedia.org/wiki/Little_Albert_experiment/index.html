Little Albert experiment

ResearchersJohn B. Watson, Rosalie Rayner
Date1920
InstitutionJohns Hopkins University

The Little Albert experiment was a case study in classical conditioning conducted in 1920 by behaviourist John B. Watson and graduate student Rosalie Rayner at Johns Hopkins University. It demonstrated that emotional responses such as fear could be conditioned in a human infant.

Procedure

"Albert B.", an infant about nine months old at initial testing, showed no fear of a white rat, rabbit, dog, masks, or other stimuli, but cried at a loud noise made by striking a steel bar. At about eleven months, the rat was presented together with the noise. After several pairings Albert cried at the rat alone, and the fear generalised to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat, and a Santa Claus mask.

Ethics and identity

The study is now considered highly unethical: the fear was induced without parental informed consent in the modern sense and was never removed. Albert's identity was long disputed; candidates proposed include Douglas Merritte, who died young, and William Barger. The experiment remains a landmark — and cautionary example — in psychology textbooks, alongside classical conditioning work by Pavlov.
