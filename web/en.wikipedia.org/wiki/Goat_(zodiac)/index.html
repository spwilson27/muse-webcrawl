Goat (zodiac)

Goat
Chinese羊 (yáng)
BranchWei (未), eighth Earthly Branch
Years… 1955, 1967, 1979, 1991, 2003, 2015, 2027 …
Element cycleWood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water (60-year cycle)

The Goat (羊) is the eighth sign in the 12-year cycle of the Chinese zodiac, associated with the Earthly Branch character 未 (wèi). The Chinese character yáng does not distinguish between sheep and goats, so the sign is rendered in English as Goat, Sheep or Ram — an ambiguity that periodically attracts attention in English-language media at the start of such years, as in 2015. The most recent Year of the Goat ran from 19 February 2015 to 7 February 2016; the next begins in 2027.

Years and elements

In the sexagenary cycle, each Goat year also carries one of the five elements: Wood (1955, 2015), Fire (1967, 2027), Earth (1979), Metal (1991) and Water (2003). People born in a given zodiac year are traditionally believed to share the sign's characteristics.

Personality traits (traditional)

Popular astrology describes those born in the Year of the Goat as gentle, mild-mannered, creative, artistic and compassionate, with a love of beauty and a preference for harmony and tranquillity; less flattering attributions include pessimism, indecisiveness, dependence on others and a tendency to worry. In Chinese folklore the goat – especially the kneeling lamb, said to kneel while nursing — symbolises filial piety, and the character 羊 appears in characters connoting goodness and auspiciousness (祥). Tradition holds that Goats are most compatible with Rabbits, Horses and Pigs, and least compatible with Oxen. Such beliefs influence behaviour in parts of East and Southeast Asia, where some couples have historically timed births to avoid or favour particular signs.

In other cultures

The sign is shared, with variations, across the East Asian cultural sphere: in the Vietnamese zodiac the Goat is retained (unlike the Rabbit, which becomes the Cat), while in Japan the sign is read as the Sheep (hitsuji). The Goat is also one of the animals in the Tibetan and Mongolian calendars, which follow related cycles.
