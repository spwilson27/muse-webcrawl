Virology

Virology is the branch of microbiology concerned with the study of viruses — submicroscopic, parasitic particles of genetic material (DNA or RNA) contained in a protein coat — and of virus-like agents such as viroids and prions. It encompasses viral structure, classification and evolution, infection and replication within host cells, viral diseases, and the means of preventing and treating them, including vaccines and antiviral drugs. Those who practise the discipline are called virologists.

History

The field began in the 1890s when Dmitri Ivanovsky showed that sap from tobacco plants with mosaic disease remained infectious after passing through bacteria-proof filters, and Martinus Beijerinck named the new agent a "virus" (from Latin for poison) — a contagium vivum fluidum. Friedrich Loeffler and Paul Frosch demonstrated a filterable cause for foot-and-mouth disease in animals (1898), and Walter Reed's commission identified the first human virus, yellow fever virus (1901). Wendell Stanley crystallized tobacco mosaic virus in 1935, showing viruses sit at the boundary of chemistry and life; the electron microscope revealed their structure in the 1930s–40s, and bacteriophage research by Delbrück, Hershey, and others founded molecular biology. Jonas Salk and Albert Sabin developed polio vaccines in the 1950s, and the twentieth century closed with the identification of HIV and, in 2020, the extraordinarily rapid characterization and vaccine development against SARS-CoV-2.

Structure and classification

A complete virus particle, a virion, consists of a nucleic-acid core surrounded by a protein capsid, sometimes with a lipid envelope bearing glycoprotein spikes. Viruses are classified by the Baltimore classification into seven groups according to genome type and replication strategy (double- or single-stranded DNA or RNA, positive or negative sense, reverse-transcribing), and by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses into realms, families, genera, and species. Genome sizes range from a few kilobases to the giant viruses (e.g. mimiviruses and pandoraviruses) exceeding a megabase.

Replication and host interaction

Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites: they attach to host-cell receptors, enter, uncoat, replicate their genomes using host (and sometimes viral) enzymes, assemble progeny, and exit by budding or lysis. Infection may be acute, latent (as in herpesviruses), chronic (hepatitis B and C), or transforming (oncoviruses such as human papillomavirus and Epstein–Barr virus). Host defences include interferons and adaptive immunity; viruses counter with immune-evasion genes — a co-evolutionary arms race central to the discipline.

Applications

Beyond medicine, viruses serve as tools: bacteriophages in therapy and research, viral vectors in gene therapy and vaccine platforms (adenovirus and mRNA technologies), oncolytic viruses in cancer treatment, and baculoviruses for protein expression. Virology intersects with epidemiology, immunology, structural biology, and bioinformatics, including metagenomic discovery of vast numbers of previously unknown viruses.
