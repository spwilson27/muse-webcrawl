Proteus syndrome

Proteus syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterised by mosaic, patchy and progressive overgrowth of skin, bone, muscle, fatty tissue, and blood and lymphatic vessels. It is named after the Greek god Proteus, who could change his shape. The condition was first described in 1979 by Michael Cohen and formally named in 1983. Joseph Merrick, the "Elephant Man", was long thought to have had neurofibromatosis but is now generally believed to have had Proteus syndrome.

Cause

In 2011, researchers at the National Institutes of Health found that Proteus syndrome is caused by a somatic mosaic mutation in the AKT1 gene — specifically a point mutation (c.49G>A, p.Glu17Lys) arising after fertilisation, so that only some cells of the body carry it. Because the mutation occurs sporadically and in mosaic form, the condition is not inherited. AKT1 encodes a kinase in the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway that regulates cell growth, explaining the abnormal tissue proliferation. The same pathway is disrupted in cancers and in related overgrowth conditions grouped as the PIK3CA-related overgrowth spectrum.

Signs and symptoms

Affected individuals are typically normal at birth, with tumours and overgrowth appearing in the first years of life and progressing disproportionately with age. Features may include asymmetric overgrowth of limbs or the skull, cerebriform connective tissue naevi (especially on the soles), lipomas, vascular malformations, and characteristic facial features. A serious complication is a greatly increased risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, a common cause of premature death.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on strict clinical criteria — mosaic distribution, sporadic occurrence and progressive course — supplemented by genetic testing of affected tissue, since the mutation may not be detectable in blood. Only a few hundred cases have been reported worldwide. There is no cure; management is multidisciplinary, involving orthopaedic and plastic surgery to correct overgrowth, monitoring for tumours and thrombosis, and supportive care. AKT inhibitors developed for cancer are under investigation as targeted treatments.
