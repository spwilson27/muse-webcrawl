Raku ware (楽焼, raku-yaki) is a type of Japanese pottery traditionally used in Japanese tea ceremonies, most often in the form of tea bowls (chawan). It is characterised by being hand-shaped rather than thrown on a wheel, quite porous, and fired at low temperatures with lead glazes; pieces are removed from the kiln while still glowing hot, which produces surfaces and colours that are unpredictable and prized for that reason.

History

Raku ware originated in Kyoto in the late 16th century, in the circle of the tea master Sen no Rikyū. Rikyū favoured simple, rustic tea bowls in the wabi taste, and commissioned the tile-maker Chōjirō to produce bowls modelled by hand. Toyotomi Hideyoshi is said to have presented Chōjirō's son Jōkei with a seal bearing the character 楽 (raku, "ease" or "pleasure"), from which the family name — and the name of the ware — derives.

The Raku family has continued the tradition in Kyoto for over 450 years, with each generation headed by a potter taking the name Raku; the 15th generation head, Kichizaemon, founded the Raku Museum in Kyoto. The two classic types are red raku (low-fired, soft reddish earthenware) and black raku (a dark, often nearly black glaze), both formed by carving a rough shape from a solid block of clay rather than throwing.

Western raku

In the 20th century raku was adopted and transformed by studio potters in the West, notably after Bernard Leach introduced Japanese ceramics to British and American audiences, and by American potter Paul Soldner, who popularised a version involving post-firing reduction: the red-hot vessel is placed in a container with combustible material, producing smoky, crackled and metallic lustre effects quite different from the subdued Japanese originals. "Western raku" is thus a distinct technique sharing mainly the rapid, low-temperature firing and removal of the ware while hot.

Aesthetics

Raku embodies the wabi-sabi aesthetic: irregularity, asymmetry, simplicity and the marks of the making process are valued rather than concealed. Because no two pieces fire alike, each bowl is unique, and small imperfections — warping, crawling glaze, uneven colour — are central to its appreciation in the tea ceremony.
