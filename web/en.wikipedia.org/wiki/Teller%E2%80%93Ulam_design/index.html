Teller–Ulam design

The Teller–Ulam design is the technical concept behind most modern thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs). Named after physicists Edward Teller and Stanisław Ulam, it uses a fission primary stage to compress and ignite a physically separate fusion secondary stage, the key idea being radiation implosion.

Principle

In the design, X-rays from the primary fission explosion are channelled within a casing to uniformly compress and heat fusion fuel (such as lithium deuteride) in the secondary, achieving the extreme temperatures and pressures needed for fusion. The concept was developed at Los Alamos in early 1951 and validated by the Ivy Mike test of 1 November 1952, which yielded 10.4 megatons.

History and secrecy

The concept was independently discovered by other nuclear powers and remains classified in detail. It enabled compact, high-yield weapons and multi-stage designs that underpin modern nuclear arsenals.
