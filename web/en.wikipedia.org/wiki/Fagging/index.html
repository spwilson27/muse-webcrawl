Fagging

A fag performing errands at a 19th-century public school

Fagging was a traditional practice in British public schools and at the universities of Oxford and Cambridge in which junior pupils acted as personal servants to senior pupils. The junior was known as a fag and the senior as a fag-master. Fagging duties typically included cleaning the senior's study, polishing boots, preparing food such as toast and tea, running errands and carrying messages.

History

The system was well established by the 18th and 19th centuries at schools such as Eton, Harrow and Rugby. Defenders argued that it taught humility, discipline and service, and that it gave junior boys a protector; in return for service, fag-masters were expected to shield their fags from bullying and to help them settle into school life.

Critics, including many former pupils, described fagging as institutionalised exploitation that shaded readily into bullying and abuse, since fag-masters held disciplinary power over their fags and could punish them, in some schools including by beating. The practice features prominently in 19th-century school literature, notably Tom Brown's School Days (1857), in which the young Tom is bullied while fagging at Rugby.

Decline

Fagging declined through the 20th century as attitudes to hierarchy and child welfare changed. Most schools abolished the more menial forms of the practice after the Second World War, and it had effectively disappeared from British schools by the late 20th century, surviving mainly in attenuated forms such as new boys performing minor communal duties, or in historical fiction.
