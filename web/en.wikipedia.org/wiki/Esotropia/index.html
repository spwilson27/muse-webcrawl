Esotropia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Esotropia

Field: Ophthalmology, strabismology
Definition: Inward (nasal) deviation of one or both eyes
Opposite: Exotropia (outward deviation)
Risks if untreated: Double vision, amblyopia ("lazy eye") in children
Treatment: Glasses, prism lenses, patching, surgery

Esotropia is a form of strabismus (eye misalignment) in which one or both eyes turn inward toward the nose. The condition can be present constantly or intermittently, and can make a person appear "cross-eyed". It is the opposite of exotropia, in which the eyes deviate outward. Esotropia may be congenital or acquired, and occurs in both children and adults.

Types

Infantile (congenital) esotropia

Infantile esotropia presents within the first six months of life with a large, constant inward deviation. It is often associated with other ocular motor features such as latent nystagmus and dissociated vertical deviation. Treatment usually involves strabismus surgery on the extraocular muscles, typically before the age of two, to allow development of binocular vision.

Accommodative esotropia

The most common form in childhood, accommodative esotropia usually appears between ages two and four in children with significant hyperopia (farsightedness). The effort to focus (accommodation) drives excessive convergence, turning the eyes inward. It is often fully or partly corrected with hyperopic glasses; the portion corrected by glasses is called the refractive accommodative component, and some children have a high accommodative convergence/accommodation (AC/A) ratio managed with bifocals or surgery.

Other forms

Acquired esotropia in adults may arise from decompensated phorias, divergence insufficiency (often in older adults, causing double vision at distance), sixth cranial nerve palsy, thyroid eye disease, or neurological disease. Sensory esotropia occurs when vision in one eye is poor and fusion is lost.

Consequences and treatment

In children, persistent misalignment during the visual system's sensitive period can lead to amblyopia — reduced vision in the turned eye — and loss of stereopsis. Diagnosis uses the cover test and prism measurement of the deviation. Treatment depends on type and may include corrective glasses, prism lenses, patching or atropine penalisation for amblyopia, orthoptic exercises, botulinum toxin injection, and surgery (usually weakening the medial rectus muscles) to realign the eyes.
