Classical order

A classical order is one of the ancient styles of classical architecture, each distinguished by its proportions and characteristic profiles and details, most readily recognised by the type of column employed.

Classical orders
Greek: Doric, Ionic, Corinthian
Roman additions: Tuscan, Composite
Key theorist: Vitruvius

The orders

The three Greek orders are the sturdy Doric, the scrolled Ionic, and the ornate, acanthus-carved Corinthian. Roman practice, codified by Vitruvius, added the plain Tuscan and the hybrid Composite. Each order comprises a column (base, shaft, capital) and the entablature it supports (architrave, frieze, cornice), governed by proportional modules.

History and revival

Systematised in Hellenistic and Roman architecture, the orders were revived in Renaissance treatises by Alberti, Serlio and Vignola, whose Canon of the Five Orders (1562) became the standard pattern-book for Palladian, Baroque and Neoclassical building across Europe and the Americas.

Use

Orders could be superimposed storey over storey, as on the Colosseum, rising from Doric at the base to Corinthian at the top.
