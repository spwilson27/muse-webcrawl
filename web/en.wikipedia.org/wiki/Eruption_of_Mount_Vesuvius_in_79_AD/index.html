Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD

The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD was one of the most catastrophic and famous volcanic eruptions in European history. In the autumn of 79 AD, Vesuvius violently ejected a cloud of super-heated tephra and gases — a Plinian eruption — burying the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum under metres of ash and pumice and killing thousands.

Course of the eruption

After foreshocks, a column rose more than 30 km into the stratosphere around midday, raining pumice on Pompeii to the southeast. Pliny the Younger, watching from Misenum, described the pine-tree-shaped cloud — the origin of the term Plinian. That night the column collapsed, sending pyroclastic surges and flows that destroyed Herculaneum and, the next morning, reached Pompeii, killing those remaining. Pliny the Elder died at Stabiae while commanding a rescue fleet.

Victims and preservation

Ash hardened around bodies, whose decay left cavities that Giuseppe Fiorelli filled with plaster from 1863, creating the famous casts. Recent research using DNA and CT scans has revised ages, sexes and causes of death — mostly asphyxiation, thermal shock and collapsing roofs.

Legacy

Rediscovered in the 18th century, Pompeii and Herculaneum became foundational sites of archaeology, preserving Roman daily life in unique detail. The eruption remains the type example for hazard assessment of Vesuvius, which threatens the modern Naples area today.
