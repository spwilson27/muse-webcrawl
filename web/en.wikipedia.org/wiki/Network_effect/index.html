Network effect

A network effect (also called network externality or demand-side economies of scale) is the phenomenon by which the value or utility a user derives from a good or service depends on the number of users of compatible products. Networks can exhibit direct effects — a telephone is more useful when more people have one — or indirect effects, where growth of one user group benefits another, as with buyers and sellers on a marketplace.

Metcalfe's law

Robert Metcalfe proposed that the value of a telecommunications network is proportional to the square of the number of connected users, an idea later debated and revised (for example to n log n). Positive network effects can create winner-take-all dynamics, tipping points and lock-in, while negative effects — congestion or spam — appear when networks grow unmanaged.

Examples

Classic cases include telephones, fax machines, operating systems, social media platforms, payment card networks and online marketplaces. Businesses pursue network effects through seeding, subsidies to one side of a platform, and standards strategy.
