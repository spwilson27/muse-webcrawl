Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) is an aviation surveillance technology in which an aircraft periodically broadcasts its identity, position, altitude, velocity, and other data, derived principally from satellite navigation, so that it can be tracked by air traffic control ground stations and by other aircraft. Unlike radar, which measures an aircraft's position from the ground, ADS-B is "dependent" on the aircraft's own navigation systems and "automatic" in that it requires no pilot input.

How it works

An ADS-B "Out" installation transmits extended squitter messages on the 1090 MHz transponder frequency (in most of the world) once or twice per second. In the United States, general aviation aircraft operating below 18,000 feet may instead use a Universal Access Transceiver on 978 MHz. ADS-B "In" equipment receives broadcasts from surrounding aircraft, enabling cockpit traffic displays and applications previously possible only with ground radar coverage. Because ground stations are cheap compared with radar, ADS-B extends surveillance to remote and mountainous areas and to low altitudes.

Mandates and adoption

The United States required ADS-B Out in most controlled airspace from 1 January 2020, and the European Union imposed similar equipage requirements. Space-based ADS-B, hosted on the Iridium NEXT satellite constellation by Aireon, has provided global oceanic coverage since 2019. Public flight-tracking websites aggregate ADS-B data from networks of volunteer receivers. The system's transmissions are unencrypted and unauthenticated, which has prompted debate about privacy and spoofing.
