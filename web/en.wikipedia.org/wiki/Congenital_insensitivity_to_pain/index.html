Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP), also known as congenital analgesia, is a rare condition in which a person cannot feel physical pain from birth. It is one of the hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN). Despite the apparent advantage, the condition is dangerous: pain is a protective warning signal, and people with CIP accumulate fractures, burns, self-inflicted oral injuries, joint destruction (Charcot joints) and untreated infections.

Causes

Different genetic forms exist. Loss-of-function mutations in SCN9A, encoding the Nav1.7 voltage-gated sodium channel in nociceptors, cause pain insensitivity with a normal sense of smell variably affected. Mutations in NTRK1 cause HSAN type IV (congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis, CIPA), in which sweating is also absent, causing recurrent fevers. Mutations in PRDM12 cause HSAN type VIII. Most forms are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern.

Presentation

Infants may bite their tongue, lips and fingers once teeth erupt. Children suffer painless fractures that may only be noticed through swelling or limping, corneal injuries from absent protective reflexes, and burns. Intelligence is normal in the SCN9A form but often impaired in CIPA. People with CIP can usually feel pressure, vibration, temperature discrimination to a degree, and visceral sensations, but not painful stimuli such as pinpricks or extreme heat.

Management

There is no cure. Management centres on injury prevention: protective eyewear and footwear, regular inspection of skin and joints, mouth guards, education of caregivers, and prompt treatment of infections. Genetic counselling is offered to affected families.
