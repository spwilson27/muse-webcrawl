Cut (transition)

A cut joins one shot instantly to the next.

In film and video, a cut is an abrupt transition from one shot to another, in which the first shot ends and the second begins instantaneously, with no intermediate frames. It is the most basic and most common type of film transition, and the foundation of film editing: the editor physically or digitally splices shots together, and each junction is a cut.

Because the cut is instantaneous, it is often described as invisible when used within a continuous scene — the viewer's attention is carried across it by continuity of action, eyelines, and sound. Cuts that deliberately break continuity, such as the jump cut, call attention to the edit itself and can create feelings of compression, agitation, or surprise.

Types of cut

Several specialised cuts are named in editing practice. A cutaway interrupts the main action with a related shot before returning; an insert shows a detail of the scene; a match cut joins two shots with a matching shape, action, or composition; a smash cut jumps abruptly between tonally contrasting scenes for shock or comic effect; and the J-cut and L-cut offset the audio transition from the picture transition. Cutting on action — changing shots in the middle of a movement — helps conceal the edit.

History

Early films were single continuous shots. Around the turn of the twentieth century, filmmakers such as George Albert Smith and Edwin S. Porter began joining shots into multi-shot sequences, and D. W. Griffith and the Soviet montage theorists developed the cut into an expressive device. With digital non-linear editing, the cut remains the default transition, executed by simply abutting clips on a timeline.
