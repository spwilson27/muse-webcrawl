The United States Geological Survey (USGS) is a scientific agency of the United States government and part of the Department of the Interior. Founded in 1879, it studies the landscape of the United States, its natural resources, and the natural hazards that threaten it — including earthquakes, volcanoes, floods, droughts, and landslides. Unlike most federal bureaus it has no regulatory mission; it is a fact-finding research organization.

The USGS operates the national streamgaging network of more than 8,000 stations, monitors volcanoes through five volcano observatories, runs the National Earthquake Information Center, produces topographic maps (the historic 7.5-minute quadrangle series and the modern US Topo program), and partners with NASA in the Landsat program of Earth-observation satellites, whose archive since 1972 is made freely available.

History

The survey was created by Congress in 1879, consolidating earlier territorial surveys led by figures such as Ferdinand V. Hayden, George Wheeler, John Wesley Powell, and Clarence King, who became its first director. Powell, the second director, championed scientific study of the arid West. Twentieth-century directors expanded the agency into water resources, geologic mapping, and — after 1979 — biological research.

Programs

Major mission areas include ecosystems, water resources, energy and minerals, environmental health, natural hazards, and core science systems. The USGS also maintains the Geographic Names Information System and the Board on Geographic Names' domestic decisions.
