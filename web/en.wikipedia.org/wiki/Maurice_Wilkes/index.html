Sir Maurice Vincent Wilkes (26 June 1913 – 29 August 2010) was a British computer scientist who designed and built the Electronic Delay Storage Automatic Calculator (EDSAC), one of the first stored-program computers, and pioneered the concept of microprogramming.

Career

Wilkes studied at St John's College, Cambridge, and worked at the Cavendish Laboratory before the Second World War, during which he worked on radar and computing at the Telecommunications Research Establishment. In 1945 he was appointed director of the Mathematical Laboratory (later the Computer Laboratory) at Cambridge. After a visit to the United States in 1946, where he attended lectures by John von Neumann on the EDVAC design, Wilkes returned determined to build a practical stored-program machine.

EDSAC

EDSAC ran its first program on 6 May 1949 — the calculation of a table of squares — making it the first complete, practical stored-program electronic computer to be in regular use. It used mercury delay lines for memory and paper tape for input. EDSAC was used for real scientific and commercial work, and its successor EDSAC 2 arrived in 1958. Wilkes also authored the first textbook on computer programming, The Preparation of Programs for an Electronic Digital Computer (1951), with David Wheeler and Stanley Gill.

Microprogramming

In 1951 Wilkes published the idea of microprogramming: implementing a computer's control unit using a small, fast read-only store of micro-instructions. The concept became a standard technique in processor design and was later recognised with the 1967 Turing Award. He was also a key figure in time-sharing and in the early Cambridge Ring local area network. He was knighted in 2000.
