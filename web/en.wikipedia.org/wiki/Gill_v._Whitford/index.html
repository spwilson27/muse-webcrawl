Gill v. Whitford, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), was a United States Supreme Court case concerning partisan gerrymandering. Democratic voters challenged Wisconsin's 2011 state assembly redistricting plan (Act 43) as an unconstitutional partisan gerrymander, relying in part on the "efficiency gap" measure of wasted votes. The Court unanimously held that the plaintiffs had failed to demonstrate the individualised standing required to challenge the statewide plan, and remanded the case, avoiding a ruling on the merits.

Background

After the 2010 census, Wisconsin's Republican-controlled government drew assembly districts under which Republicans won 60 of 99 seats in 2012 despite receiving a minority of the statewide vote. Twelve Democratic voters sued, and in November 2016 a three-judge district court ruled 2–1 that the map was an unconstitutional partisan gerrymander — the first federal court decision in three decades to strike down a map on that ground. The lower court drew on the efficiency gap, developed by Nicholas Stephanopoulos and Eric McGhee, which counts each party's "wasted" votes (votes for losing candidates plus surplus votes for winners) as a measure of gerrymandering severity.

Decision

Chief Justice John Roberts's opinion held that a plaintiff challenging a statewide plan must show concrete, district-specific injury: a voter must claim that his or her own district had been packed or cracked. Because the plaintiffs' proof addressed the statewide plan rather than their individual districts, the case was vacated and remanded for an opportunity to develop such evidence. The decision left the merits — whether partisan gerrymandering claims are justiciable at all — for another day; the following year, in Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), the Court held such claims to be non-justiciable political questions in federal court.

Significance

Gill is a key episode in the politics of the American two-party system, where control of redistricting lets each party entrench itself. After remand the Wisconsin case was overtaken by Rucho; challenges to gerrymandering subsequently moved to state courts and state constitutions.
