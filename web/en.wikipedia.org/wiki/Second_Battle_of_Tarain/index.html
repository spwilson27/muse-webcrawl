Second Battle of Tarain

Date1192
LocationTarain (modern Taraori), near Karnal, Haryana, India
BelligerentsGhurid Empire vs. Chahamana (Chauhan) Rajputs
ResultDecisive Ghurid victory

The Second Battle of Tarain was fought in 1192 between the Ghurid forces of Muhammad of Ghor (Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad) and the Rajput confederacy led by Prithviraj Chauhan, king of Ajmer and Delhi, on the same plain near Karnal in present-day Haryana where Muhammad had been defeated the previous year. This time the Ghurids won a decisive victory that opened northern India to Muslim conquest and laid the foundations of the Delhi Sultanate.

Background

At the First Battle of Tarain (1191), Prithviraj's host had defeated and wounded Muhammad, who was carried from the field. Rather than pursue or consolidate, the Rajputs spent months reducing the Ghurid-held fort of Tabarhind (Bhatinda). Muhammad spent the intervening year preparing a careful rematch, reportedly purging officers who had fled and rebuilding his army around mounted archers. Contemporary and near-contemporary Persian chroniclers such as Hasan Nizami and Minhaj-i Siraj are the principal sources; later Rajput epic tradition, above all the Prithviraj Raso, embellished the story considerably and is not considered reliable in detail.

The battle

According to the Persian accounts, Muhammad divided his cavalry into divisions of mounted archers with orders to harass the Rajput army, feign flight, and avoid close combat, wearing down the larger but slower Rajput host through the day. When the Rajputs were exhausted and disordered, he launched a final charge with his fresh reserve of some 12,000 armoured horsemen, which broke the Rajput army. Prithviraj was captured while fleeing and was executed. Estimates of the armies' sizes in the sources are greatly exaggerated, but the Rajput force was probably the larger.

Aftermath

The victory was followed by the Ghurid occupation of Ajmer and Delhi. Muhammad returned to Ghazni leaving his slave-general Qutb al-Din Aibak in charge of the Indian conquests; further victories at Chandawar (1194) extended Ghurid power to the Ganges valley. After Muhammad's assassination in 1206, Aibak founded the Mamluk (Slave) dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate, which would rule northern India for three centuries. The battle is consequently regarded as one of the turning points of Indian history.
