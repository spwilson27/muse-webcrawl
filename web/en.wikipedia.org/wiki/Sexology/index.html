Sexology

Sexology is the scientific study of human sexuality, including human sexual interests, behaviours, and functions. The term sexology does not generally refer to the non-scientific study of sexuality, and sexologists apply tools from many academic fields, including biology, medicine, psychology, epidemiology, sociology, and criminology.

History

Precursors include the Kama Sutra and other classical erotic manuals, but sexology as a scientific discipline arose in the 19th century. Richard von Krafft-Ebing's Psychopathia Sexualis (1886) catalogued sexual behaviour from a forensic-psychiatric perspective. Pioneers of a more liberal approach included Magnus Hirschfeld, who founded the Institut für Sexualwissenschaft in Berlin in 1919 (destroyed by the Nazis in 1933), and Havelock Ellis, whose Studies in the Psychology of Sex appeared from 1897.

In the mid-20th century, Alfred Kinsey's large-scale interview studies (Sexual Behavior in the Human Male, 1948, and …Female, 1953) and the laboratory research of William Masters and Virginia Johnson (Human Sexual Response, 1966) reshaped public and scientific understanding. Shere Hite's reports and the work of later researchers extended study of sexual orientation, response, and dysfunction.

Fields of study

Contemporary sexology covers sexual development, sexual orientation and gender identity, sexual relationships, sexual physiology and response cycles, contraception and reproductive health, sexual dysfunction and sex therapy, and the prevention of sexually transmitted infections. Professional bodies include the World Association for Sexual Health and various national societies; sexologists work in research, clinical practice, education, and public health.
