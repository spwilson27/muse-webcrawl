The House of Nassau (German: Haus Nassau) is a diversified aristocratic dynasty in Europe. It is named after the lordship associated with Nassau Castle, located in present-day Nassau, Rhineland-Palatinate, Germany. The family arose in the 11th century with Count Dudo of Laurenburg, and over the centuries its various branches came to rule numerous territories in the Holy Roman Empire, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg.

Origins and branches

Dudo of Laurenburg (died c. 1123) is considered the founder of the house; his descendants took their name from Nassau Castle, built around 1100. In the mid-13th century the family divided into two main lines, the Walramian and Ottonian lines, named for counts Walram II and Otto I. The Walramian line produced Adolf of Nassau, King of the Romans (1292–1298), and ruled territories including Nassau-Weilburg, Nassau-Wiesbaden, and Saarbrücken. The Ottonian line held lands north of the Lahn, including Siegen, Dillenburg, and Breda in the Low Countries.

Orange-Nassau

The Ottonian line ascended to European prominence when René of Châlon, Prince of Orange, left his principality to his cousin William of Nassau-Dillenburg — William the Silent — in 1544. William led the Dutch Revolt against Spain and became the founder of the Dutch Republic's stadtholderate. His descendants, the House of Orange-Nassau, provided the sovereigns of the Netherlands: after the Napoleonic era, William I became the first King of the Netherlands in 1815, and the Dutch royal house descends from this line to the present day.

Luxembourg

Another branch, Nassau-Weilburg, inherited the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg in 1890 when the personal union with the Netherlands ended because Luxembourg's Salic law required a male heir. Grand Duke Adolphe of Nassau-Weilburg founded the line from which the present Grand Ducal family of Luxembourg descends.
