Paul Graham (born 13 November 1964) is an English-American computer scientist, writer and entrepreneur. He is known for co-founding Viaweb, one of the first hosted e-commerce platforms, which was sold to Yahoo in 1998; for co-founding the startup accelerator Y Combinator in 2005; for his widely read essays on programming, startups and technology; and for his work on Lisp programming languages, including the dialects Arc and Bel.

Early life and education

Graham was born in Weymouth, England, and grew up in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, where his father worked as a nuclear physicist. He studied philosophy at Cornell University, graduating in 1986, and earned a master's degree and a PhD in computer science from Harvard University in 1990, with further study in painting at the Rhode Island School of Design and the Accademia di Belle Arti in Florence.

Viaweb and Yahoo

In 1995 Graham co-founded Viaweb with Robert Morris and Trevor Blackwell. Viaweb let users build online stores through a web browser — software written in Lisp and running on the server, a novel approach at the time. In 1998 Yahoo acquired Viaweb for about $49 million in stock, renaming it Yahoo Store. Graham's account of building a startup in Lisp, "Beating the Averages," became a classic essay on competitive advantage in software.

Y Combinator

In 2005 Graham, Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris and Trevor Blackwell founded Y Combinator, a seed accelerator that funded batches of startups with small standard investments in exchange for equity, providing mentorship and a demo day for investors. The model was widely imitated worldwide; Y Combinator's portfolio has included Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe, Reddit, Doordash and Coinbase. Graham stepped back from day-to-day leadership in 2014, handing the presidency to Sam Altman.

Writing and Lisp work

Graham's essays — collected in Hackers and Painters (2004) and widely circulated online — address startup strategy ("Do Things that Don't Scale," "How to Make Wealth"), the hierarchy of disagreement, and the qualities of good programmers. His earlier books On Lisp (1993) and ANSI Common Lisp (1995) are standard texts on the language. In 2008 he released Arc, a Lisp dialect designed for exploratory programming, and in 2019 he published the specification of Bel, a foundational Lisp written in itself.
