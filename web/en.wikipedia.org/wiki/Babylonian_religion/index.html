Babylonian religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Babylonian religion is the religious practice of the Babylonians of ancient Mesopotamia, running from the early dynasties of the second millennium BC until the decline of Babylonian civilization in the early centuries AD. It developed out of earlier Sumerian religion and shared much of its pantheon, mythology and ritual with the related Assyrian religion of northern Mesopotamia.

Pantheon

The Babylonian pantheon was large and headed, after Babylon's rise to supremacy under Hammurabi (18th century BC), by Marduk, the city's patron god, who supplanted the Sumerian Enlil as king of the gods in Babylonian theology. Other principal deities included Ea (Sumerian Enki), god of wisdom and sweet waters; Shamash (Utu), god of the sun and justice; Sin (Nanna), god of the moon; Ishtar (Inanna), goddess of love and war; Adad, the storm god; and Nabu, Marduk's son, god of writing and scribes, whose cult grew greatly in the first millennium BC.

Mythology

The Enūma Eliš, the Babylonian creation epic, recounts how Marduk defeated the primordial sea-goddess Tiamat, fashioned heaven and earth from her body, and created humanity from the blood of the rebel god Kingu to serve the gods. It was recited during the New Year (Akitu) festival in Babylon. Other major works include the Epic of Gilgamesh, the myth of Adapa, and the descent of Ishtar to the underworld.

Cult and temples

Religion centred on the temple households of the gods, great estates staffed by priests who offered daily food, drink and clothing to the divine statues. Babylon's principal temple was the Esagila, dedicated to Marduk, beside the great ziggurat Etemenanki. The high point of the year was the Akitu festival, during which the king "took the hand of Marduk" and the gods' statues processed through the city.

Babylonians practised extensive divination — astrology, extispicy (inspection of entrails), and dream interpretation — and employed exorcists and lamentation priests to counter disease and evil spirits. Babylonian astral religion and its zodiac were highly influential on later Greek, Jewish and Islamic traditions.

Decline

The cults continued under Persian, Greek and Parthian rule, but temple institutions withered over the early centuries AD; the last datable cuneiform texts are from the 1st century AD, by which time the ancient religion had effectively died out.
