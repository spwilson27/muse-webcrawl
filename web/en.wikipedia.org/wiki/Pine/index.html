A pine is any conifer tree of the genus Pinus in the family Pinaceae. Pines are evergreen, resinous trees (rarely shrubs) native throughout most of the Northern Hemisphere, from the Arctic Circle to Central America, North Africa and Southeast Asia. They are among the most ecologically and commercially important trees in the world.

Pines bear needles in bundles (fascicles) of two, three or five, and woody seed cones.

Description

Pines are long-lived: a Great Basin bristlecone pine (Pinus longaeva) named Methuselah is nearly 5,000 years old, among the oldest known non-clonal organisms on Earth. Their leaves are of several kinds, the most distinctive being long needles borne in clusters called fascicles, wrapped at the base in a sheath; the number of needles per fascicle (two, three or five) is a key identification character. Pines are monoecious, producing separate male pollen cones and female seed cones; seeds are usually winged and wind-dispersed, though some species—such as those producing pine nuts—rely on birds. Many pines are adapted to fire, with thick bark, serotinous cones that open after burning, or the ability to resprout.

Distribution and ecology

Pines dominate vast forests: the boreal taiga of Eurasia and North America, Mediterranean pinewoods, and mountain forests worldwide. They are often pioneer species colonising poor, dry or disturbed soils, and they form ectomycorrhizal associations with fungi essential to their nutrition.

Uses

Pines are a primary source of softwood timber and pulp; their wood is used in construction, furniture and paper. Other products include resin and turpentine, pine tar, pine nuts from species such as the stone pine and pinyon pines, and essential oils. Many species are planted as ornamentals and as Christmas trees (notably Scots pine and Norway spruce's frequent substitute). Monterey pine (Pinus radiata), native to a tiny area of California, is the most widely planted pine in the world, forming plantation forestry across Chile, New Zealand and Australia.
