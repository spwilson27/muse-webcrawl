Lazarus syndrome

Lazarus syndrome, also known as autoresuscitation, is the spontaneous return of circulation after failed attempts at cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and after resuscitation efforts have ceased. Named after Lazarus of Bethany, who in the New Testament was raised from the dead by Jesus, the phenomenon has been documented in the medical literature since the early 1980s, though it is believed to be under-reported.

Incidence and causes

Reported cases typically involve a delay of several minutes — usually within ten minutes — between the end of CPR and the spontaneous return of a pulse and breathing. In most documented instances, patients ultimately did not survive for long, but some recovered fully.

Proposed mechanisms include:

  • Delayed action of drugs administered during resuscitation, which reach the central circulation only after chest compressions stop.
  • Relief of dynamic hyperinflation (auto-PEEP): excessive ventilation pressures built up during CPR can impede venous return; when compressions and ventilation cease, pressures fall and circulation can resume.
  • Reversal of myocardial stunning and transient electrolyte disturbances, such as hyperkalemia resolving over time.

Implications

Because of the Lazarus syndrome, clinicians are advised to monitor patients for several minutes — commonly at least ten — after resuscitation is stopped before death is formally declared. The phenomenon also has implications for organ donation protocols, which require careful confirmation of death.
