Eid al-Fitr

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Crowds in festive dress leaving a mosque after Eid prayers in the morning]
Crowds in festive dress leaving a mosque after Eid prayers in the morning

Eid al-Fitr is an Islamic holiday marking the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting (Sawm). Celebrated on the first day of Shawwal, the tenth month of the Islamic calendar, it begins with a special congregational prayer, the giving of obligatory alms (Zakat al-Fitr), festive meals, new clothes, gifts for children and visits to family. It is one of the two canonical feasts of Islam, the other being Eid al-Adha.

Observance

The festival starts when the crescent moon of Shawwal is sighted — producing the perennial variation of dates between countries and communities ('moon wars' between astronomical calculation and local sighting). After dawn, Muslims perform ghusl, wear their best clothes and eat something sweet before the Eid prayer in mosques or open grounds, having first paid Zakat al-Fitr so the poor can feast too. Greetings include 'Eid Mubarak' ('blessed Eid').

Customs worldwide

Food defines the holiday regionally: sheer khurma in South Asia, kahk biscuits in Egypt, ketupat in Southeast Asia, baklava across the Middle East. Three days are public holidays in many Muslim-majority countries; in the diaspora, Eid is the principal occasion for communal visibility, with mass prayers in stadiums and parks. Returning home for Eid (mudik in Indonesia; balik kampung in Malaysia) is among the world's largest annual migrations.

Religious meaning

Eid al-Fitr thanks God for the strength to complete Ramadan's fast and celebrates forgiveness, charity and communal renewal; visiting graves of relatives, reconciling quarrels and distributing sweets are customary expressions of its theology of gratitude.
