The thumb is the first digit of the hand, next to the index finger. When a person is standing in the medical anatomical position (where the palm is facing forward), the thumb is the outermost digit. The Medical Latin name is pollex (compare hallux for the big toe), and the corresponding adjective is pollical.

Structure

Unlike the other four digits, the thumb consists of only two phalanges — proximal and distal — rather than three. Its metacarpal bone joins the trapezium of the carpus at the carpometacarpal joint, a saddle joint that permits the wide range of motion underlying opposition: the ability to touch the thumb pad to the pads of the other fingers. Opposition makes the precision grip possible and is a defining capability of the human hand. Nine muscles act on the thumb, including the thenar muscles that form the fleshy eminence at its base.

Evolution

Fully opposable thumbs are characteristic of primates, and the length and musculature of the human thumb are considered adaptations related to tool use and manipulation. Some other animals, such as pandas, have evolved thumb-like structures from wrist bones (sesamoids) that serve similar grasping functions.

Clinical significance

Conditions affecting the thumb include osteoarthritis of the carpometacarpal joint, De Quervain's tenosynovitis, trigger thumb, skier's thumb (injury to the ulnar collateral ligament), and congenital anomalies such as triphalangeal thumb and thumb duplication (preaxial polydactyly).

In culture

The thumb figures in gestures — thumbs-up and thumbs-down signals of approval or disapproval — and in expressions such as "rule of thumb". Hitchhikers traditionally signal with an extended thumb.
