Microcredit is the extension of very small loans (microloans) to impoverished borrowers who typically lack collateral, steady employment or a verifiable credit history and are therefore excluded from traditional banking. It forms part of the wider field of microfinance, which also includes savings, insurance and payment services for low-income clients.

Origins

Although informal lending and credit cooperatives have long histories, modern microcredit is strongly associated with Bangladesh in the 1970s. Muhammad Yunus, an economics professor at the University of Chittagong, began lending small sums from his own pocket to poor villagers — famously $27 divided among 42 women in the village of Jobra in 1976 — and in 1983 founded the Grameen Bank. Grameen's model lent to groups of five women whose members were jointly responsible, achieving high repayment rates. Yunus and the bank jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.

Spread and variations

Microcredit institutions spread worldwide, including BRAC and ASA in Bangladesh, BancoSol in Bolivia, and thousands of NGOs, credit unions and commercial microfinance banks. The United Nations declared 2005 the International Year of Microcredit. Interest rates are typically higher than in conventional banking because small loans are costly to administer, which has prompted criticism and regulation in some markets. Digital and mobile lending later extended the model to borrowers reached entirely by phone.

Impact and criticism

Randomised evaluations summarised in the 2010s found that microcredit modestly expands business creation and occupational choice but has, on average, only small effects on household income, consumption, education and women's empowerment. Critics have highlighted over-indebtedness crises, such as in Andhra Pradesh in 2010, and cases of aggressive collection. Supporters argue that access to credit nonetheless provides valuable financial resilience and opportunity for the poor.
