Wildlife smuggling is the illegal trade in wild animals, plants and products derived from them across national borders. It is one of the largest forms of transnational organised crime — estimates of its annual value range from several billion to over US$20 billion — and a major driver of species declines, ranking with habitat loss among the principal threats to biodiversity.

Forms of the trade

Smuggled wildlife falls into several broad markets. Live animals are trafficked for the exotic pet trade (parrots, reptiles, primates), for zoos and collectors, and for use in traditional medicine. Animal products include elephant ivory, rhinoceros horn, pangolin scales, tiger bone and skins, bear bile, seahorses and shark fins. Plants and timber — rosewood, orchids, cacti and rare hardwoods — are also heavily traded. Smugglers conceal specimens in luggage, mislabel shipments, bribe officials, route goods through transit countries, and increasingly sell through online marketplaces and social media. Mortality in transit is often very high.

Impacts

Unsustainable harvesting has pushed species such as pangolins (the most trafficked mammals in the world), many tortoises and freshwater turtles, African elephants and several rhino and tiger populations toward critical endangerment. The trade also spreads disease — wildlife trafficking is implicated in the movement of zoonotic pathogens — undermines legal, regulated trade, deprives source countries of revenue, and is linked in some regions to corruption and armed groups.

Legal framework and enforcement

The principal international instrument is the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES, signed 1973), which regulates trade in more than 40,000 listed species through a permit system and outright bans for Appendix I species. National laws such as the US Endangered Species Act and Lacey Act, and EU wildlife trade regulations, implement these controls. Enforcement involves customs and border agencies, specialist wildlife crime units, Interpol and the UN Office on Drugs and Crime; the International Consortium on Combating Wildlife Crime coordinates joint work. Demand-reduction campaigns, forensic identification of seized products (DNA barcoding, isotope analysis), and financial-crime investigations targeting trafficking networks are growing parts of the response.
