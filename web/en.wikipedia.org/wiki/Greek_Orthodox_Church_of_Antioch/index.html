Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch

The Greek Orthodox Church of Antioch, officially the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch and All the East (Arabic: بطريركية أنطاكية وسائر المشرق للروم الأرثوذكس), is an autocephalous Greek Orthodox church within the communion of Eastern Orthodoxy. It claims succession to the community founded in Antioch by the apostles Peter and Paul, and it is one of the ancient pentarchy patriarchates. Its adherents, traditionally called Melkites in the historical sense, are largely Arab Christians, and its liturgical languages are Arabic and Greek.

History

Antioch was one of the great centres of early Christianity; according to the Acts of the Apostles it was at Antioch that the followers of Jesus were first called "Christians". After the Council of Chalcedon (451), the church in Antioch divided, and the Chalcedonian line of patriarchs continued alongside the non-Chalcedonian church known today as the Syriac Orthodox Church. The patriarchate endured centuries of Byzantine, Arab, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman rule; in the 14th century the patriarchal seat was transferred to Damascus, where it remains.

In 1899 the patriarchate elected its first Arab patriarch after a long period of ethnic Greek incumbents, reflecting the Arab character of its faithful. The church today has archdioceses in Syria, Lebanon and the Middle East, and large diaspora archdioceses in North and South America, Europe and Australia. Its theological school is associated with the University of Balamand in Lebanon.
