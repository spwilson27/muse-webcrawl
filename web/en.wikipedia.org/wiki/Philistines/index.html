Philistines

The Philistines (Hebrew: פְּלִשְׁתִּים, Pəlīštīm) were an ancient people who lived on the south coast of Canaan during the Iron Age. Their polity, the Philistine pentapolis of Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron, formed part of the eastern Mediterranean world. The Philistines are known from the Hebrew Bible as frequent adversaries of the Israelites, as well as from Egyptian records identifying them among the "Sea Peoples" who appeared in the region in the 12th century BCE. The name "Palestine" derives from their name.

Origins

Egyptian inscriptions of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu record the Peleset among the Sea Peoples defeated around 1175 BCE and settled in strongholds in Canaan. Their early material culture—locally made Mycenaean-style pottery, Aegean-type hearths and loom weights, and consumption of pork—points to an origin in the Aegean world. Ancient DNA from Ashkelon (published 2019) found a southern-European-related genetic component in early Iron Age burials that dissipated through intermarriage within about two centuries.

History

By the 11th century BCE the five Philistine cities were governed by lords (seranim) acting in concert. Excavations at Ekron (Tel Miqne) reveal a major olive-oil industry; Ashkelon was a great port. In the 8th–7th centuries BCE the Philistine cities were vassals of Assyria and then Egypt. Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon destroyed Ashkelon in 604 BCE and deported its population, ending distinct Philistine identity, though the regional name survived.

In the Bible

The Hebrew Bible portrays the Philistines as Israel's chief enemy during the period of the Judges and the early monarchy: Samson fights them; they capture the Ark of the Covenant and kill King Saul at Mount Gilboa; the young David defeats their champion Goliath of Gath. These narratives reflect genuine Iron Age rivalry, although their details are theological as well as historical.

Culture and language

The Philistines spoke a language that left few inscriptions (the Ekron inscription is in a Canaanite dialect); by the Persian period they had adopted local languages and deities such as Dagon and Baal-Zebub. Their religion, cuisine, and architecture show a blend of Aegean inheritance and Levantine adoption.

Legacy of the name

After the Bar Kokhba revolt, Rome renamed Judaea "Syria Palaestina," perpetuating the Philistine name in the region's designation. In modern usage, "philistine" also became a derogatory term for a person hostile to art and culture, popularised in English by Matthew Arnold in the 19th century—a usage unrelated to the ancient people's actual culture.
