The University of Göttingen, officially the Georg August University of Göttingen (German: Georg-August-Universität Göttingen), is a public research university in the city of Göttingen, Germany. Founded in 1734 by George II, King of Great Britain and Elector of Hanover, and opened in 1737, it became one of the leading universities of the Enlightenment and a byword for excellence in mathematics and the natural sciences.

History

Göttingen was planned as a modern, relatively liberal university with a famous library. In the 19th century Carl Friedrich Gauss made it a centre of mathematics; later Felix Klein and David Hilbert turned it into the world capital of the subject, attracting students from across Europe and America. Bernhard Riemann, and in physics Max Born, Werner Heisenberg and J. Robert Oppenheimer, continued the tradition. The dismissal of Jewish and dissenting scholars after 1933 devastated the university's mathematical and physics schools. In 1837 the protest of the "Göttingen Seven", including the Brothers Grimm, against the abrogation of Hanover's constitution became a celebrated defence of academic freedom.

Present day

The university today has 13 faculties and works closely with nearby Max Planck Institutes and the German Primate Center. Together with its partner institutions it forms the Göttingen Campus. Its library, the Göttingen State and University Library, is one of Germany's largest.
