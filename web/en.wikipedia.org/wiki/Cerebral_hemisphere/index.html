The vertebrate cerebrum (brain) is formed by two cerebral hemispheres that are separated by a groove, the longitudinal fissure. The brain can thus be described as being divided into left and right cerebral hemispheres. Each of these hemispheres has an outer layer of grey matter, the cerebral cortex, that is supported by an inner layer of white matter. In eutherian (placental) mammals, the hemispheres are linked by the corpus callosum, a very large bundle of nerve fibres; smaller commissures connect them in other vertebrates.

Structure

The surface of each hemisphere is folded into ridges (gyri) and grooves (sulci), increasing the area of cortex. Each hemisphere is conventionally divided into four lobes — frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital — named for the skull bones above them, plus the deeply placed insular cortex. Deep within each hemisphere lie subcortical structures including the hippocampus and the basal ganglia, and the lateral ventricle.

Lateralisation

Although the hemispheres are broadly symmetrical, many functions are lateralised: in most people the left hemisphere is dominant for language (Broca's and Wernicke's areas) and for right-handed motor control, while the right hemisphere is relatively specialised for spatial and visuospatial processing. Each hemisphere primarily controls and receives sensation from the opposite (contralateral) side of the body. Studies of "split-brain" patients, whose corpus callosum has been severed to treat epilepsy, revealed that the hemispheres can function with considerable independence when disconnected.

Development

The hemispheres develop from the prosencephalon (forebrain) of the embryonic neural tube, expanding dramatically in mammals and especially in primates, where cortical folding and the expansion of association areas underpin advanced cognition.
