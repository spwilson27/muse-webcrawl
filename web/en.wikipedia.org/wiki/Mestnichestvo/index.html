Mestnichestvo

Mestnichestvo (Russian: Ме́стничество, IPA: [ˈmʲesʲnʲɪtɕɪstvə], from mesto, "place") was a feudal precedence system in Russia from the 15th to the 17th centuries. Under it, appointments to military, administrative and court positions — and even seating at the tsar's table — were determined by the comparative honour of a boyar's family and the posts held by his ancestors, rather than by merit or the ruler's favour.

Operation

Genealogical books (razryadnye knigi) recorded service appointments, and nobles could sue in "place" disputes (mestnicheskie dela) if assigned a post beneath a rival of lesser ancestral rank. A claimant who refused service under a "lesser" man could be punished, but the system protected aristocratic families against sudden demotion and limited the tsar's freedom to promote outsiders.

Abolition

The system increasingly hampered military effectiveness, as commanders could not be placed over others of higher genealogical standing. In 1682 a zemsky sobor under Fyodor III abolished mestnichestvo, and the genealogical books were ceremonially burned. Merit-based service was later formalised under Peter the Great's Table of Ranks (1722).
