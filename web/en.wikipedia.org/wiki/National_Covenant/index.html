National Covenant

The National Covenant was a Scottish covenant signed on 28 February 1638 in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh, by Scotland's nobles, lairds, ministers and burghers. Drawn up in response to Charles I's attempts to impose Anglican forms of worship — above all Archbishop Laud's new Scottish prayer book of 1637 — it bound its signatories, the Covenanters, to defend the Presbyterian Church of Scotland and the "true religion" while professing loyalty to the king. It became the manifesto of the movement that fought the Bishops' Wars and reshaped the politics of the three kingdoms.

Background and contents

Charles I and Archbishop William Laud sought uniformity of worship across England, Scotland and Ireland. The introduction of the Scottish Book of Common Prayer in July 1637 provoked riots in Edinburgh — tradition credits a market woman, Jenny Geddes, with throwing her stool at the minister in St Giles' Cathedral — and a flood of petitions. The Covenant, drafted by the lawyer Archibald Johnston of Wariston and the minister Alexander Henderson, combined the Negative Confession of 1581, a recital of statutes against "popery", and a bond pledging mutual defence of religion, the king's authority rightly understood, and the laws and liberties of the kingdom. Copies were carried across Scotland and signed by tens of thousands, in some cases, legend says, in blood.

Consequences

Charles refused to accept the Covenant and prepared war. The General Assembly of 1638 abolished episcopacy in Scotland, and the Bishops' Wars of 1639–1640 ended with Scottish armies occupying Newcastle and the king forced to summon the Long Parliament in England — a key step toward the English Civil War. In 1643 the Covenanters extended their programme through the Solemn League and Covenant with the English Parliament. After the Restoration, subscription to the Covenant was made treason, and its defenders — the Cameronians and others — suffered in the persecutions remembered as the Killing Times, until Presbyterianism was re-established at the Revolution of 1688–89.
