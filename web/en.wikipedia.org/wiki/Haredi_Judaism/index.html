Haredi Judaism (Hebrew: חֲרֵדִי, romanised Ḥaredi, "one who trembles at [God's] word") is a branch of Orthodox Judaism characterised by strict adherence to Jewish law (halakha) and traditions, and resistance to modern secular culture and values. Its members are often called ultra-Orthodox in English, a term many Haredim consider pejorative.

Beliefs and lifestyle

Haredi life centres on Torah study, prayer and the commandments. Full-time study in yeshivas and kollels by adult men is highly valued, supported by women's employment and communal charity. Haredi communities tend toward insularity: distinctive dress (black hats and coats for men; modest dress for women), Yiddish or Hebrew in daily life, separate gender roles, limited engagement with secular media, and residence in close-knit neighbourhoods.

Groups and demography

The Haredi world comprises Hasidic dynasties (including Satmar, Belz, Ger and Chabad-Lubavitch), non-Hasidic Lithuanian (Litvak) communities descended from the Misnagdim, and Sephardi and Mizrahi Haredim. With high fertility rates — often six or more children per family — Haredim are the fastest-growing segment of world Jewry, constituting roughly 13–14% of Israel's population and large communities in New York, London and Antwerp. Their political parties (such as United Torah Judaism and Shas in Israel) are significant coalition actors, and issues like military conscription exemptions for yeshiva students are perennially contentious.
