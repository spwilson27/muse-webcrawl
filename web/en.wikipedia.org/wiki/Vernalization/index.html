Vernalization

Vernalization
DefinitionInduction of flowering by prolonged cold
Effective temperaturesTypically 1–7 °C for weeks
Key genesFLC, VRN1, VRN2, FT
Occurs inWinter cereals, biennials, many perennials

Vernalization (from Latin vernus, "of the spring") is the acquisition of the ability to flower through exposure to the prolonged cold of winter. Many plants of temperate regions — winter varieties of wheat, barley and rye, biennials such as cabbage, carrot and foxglove, and many perennials — will not flower, or flower only belatedly and unevenly, unless they first experience weeks of low temperature. The requirement ensures that flowering is timed for spring rather than a mild spell in autumn.

Physiology and mechanism

The cold is perceived mainly in dividing cells, particularly the shoot apex, and must usually last several weeks at roughly 1–7 °C. The response is quantitative: longer cold gives faster, more complete flowering. The vernalized state is stable through many mitotic divisions (a form of cellular memory) but is reset in the next generation. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, flowering is repressed by the gene FLOWERING LOCUS C (FLC); prolonged cold triggers epigenetic silencing of FLC through Polycomb-mediated histone modification (trimethylation of histone H3 lysine 27), releasing the floral activator FT (florigen) to respond to spring day lengths. In cereals an analogous circuit centres on the VRN1 and VRN2 genes.

Agriculture and history

Farmers have long distinguished spring-sown cereals from winter-sown ones requiring cold. In the 1920s–30s the Soviet agronomist Trofim Lysenko promoted "yarovization" — pre-chilling seed — and wrongly claimed the acquired state was hereditary; Lysenkoism, enforced politically in the USSR, retarded Soviet genetics for decades. Today, vernalization requirements guide cultivar choice and sowing dates, and horticulturalists chill bulbs, seeds and biennial crops artificially to control flowering. Warming winters raise concerns that some crops and orchard species may receive insufficient chill.
