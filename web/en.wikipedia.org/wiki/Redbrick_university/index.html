Redbrick university

A redbrick university was originally one of the nine civic universities founded in the major industrial cities of England in the 19th and early 20th centuries. However, the term is now used more broadly to describe any British university founded between the late 19th and mid-20th centuries, distinguishing them from the older "ancient" universities and collegiate institutions such as Oxford and Cambridge.

Origin of the term

The term was coined by Edgar Allison Peers, a professor of Spanish at the University of Liverpool, writing under the pseudonym "Bruce Truscot" in Red Brick University (1943). It referred to the Victorian Gothic and red Accrington brick used in the civic university buildings, notably Liverpool's Victoria Building (1892), whose terracotta-red brick gave the name to the whole movement.

The civic universities

The original civic university colleges grew out of the industrial cities' need for technical, medical and scientific education for local students. They include the institutions that became the universities of Birmingham, Bristol, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester and Sheffield, together with what are now Newcastle University, the University of Reading and the University of Nottingham in some usages. Many were founded with civic philanthropy from industrialists and admitted students regardless of religion or background, in contrast to the older universities.

Characteristics

Redbrick universities were traditionally strong in engineering, medicine, science and commerce, oriented toward practical knowledge and local industry, and mostly non-collegiate and campus-based within their cities. Most are members of the Russell Group of research-intensive universities.
