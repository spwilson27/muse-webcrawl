German alphabet

The German alphabet is the Latin alphabet as used for writing the German language. In its basic form it consists of the 26 standard Latin letters, supplemented by four special characters: the umlauted vowels Ä, Ö, Ü and the sharp S, ß (Eszett). Whether the umlaut letters and ß count as separate letters of the alphabet varies by context; in dictionary ordering the umlauts are usually treated as equivalent to the plain vowels (or to ae, oe, ue), and ß is filed as "ss".

The letters and their pronunciation

German letter names are mostly straightforward: A (ah), B (beh), C (tseh), D (deh), E (eh), F (ef), G (geh), H (hah), I (eeh), J (yot), K (kah), L (el), M (em), N (en), O (oh), P (peh), Q (koo), R (er), S (es), T (teh), U (oo), V (fau), W (veh), X (iks), Y (üpsilon), Z (tset). Several letters sound different from their English counterparts: W is pronounced like English "v", V usually like "f", J like "y", and Z like "ts".

Umlaut and Eszett

The umlaut dots developed from a small superscript e written above a, o, and u in medieval manuscripts; when the characters Ä, Ö, Ü cannot be used, they are replaced by the digraphs Ae, Oe, Ue. The letter ß represents a voiceless "s" sound after long vowels and diphthongs; in Switzerland and Liechtenstein it has been abandoned in favour of "ss". A capital form, ẞ, was officially admitted into German orthography in 2017. Handwritten German was long written in blackletter (Fraktur) and the Kurrent/Sütterlin cursive scripts, until Latin ("normal") script was prescribed in 1941.

Letter frequency and use

The most frequent letter in German text is E, followed by N and I; compound words and the capitalization of all nouns are notable features of written German but belong to orthography rather than the alphabet itself. The alphabet song taught to German children follows the same tune as the English "ABC song" with the umlauts and ß appended.
