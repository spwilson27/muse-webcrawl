James Prescott Joule (24 December 1818 – 11 October 1889) was an English physicist and brewer, born in Salford, Lancashire. Joule studied the nature of heat and discovered its relationship to mechanical work, work that led to the concept of energy conservation and the first law of thermodynamics. The SI unit of energy, the joule, is named in his honour.

Experiments

Tutored partly by John Dalton, Joule began experimenting in the brewery cellar of his family business. In 1840 he formulated Joule's first law: the heat produced by an electric current in a conductor is proportional to the resistance times the square of the current. He then investigated whether mechanical work could be converted into heat at a fixed rate. In his famous paddle-wheel experiments, falling weights drove paddles in an insulated vessel of water, and Joule measured the tiny temperature rise, obtaining a value for the mechanical equivalent of heat — about 772 foot-pounds per British thermal unit — close to the modern figure (4.186 joules per calorie). His 1845 paper "On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat" was at first coolly received; after William Thomson (later Lord Kelvin) took up his results at the 1847 British Association meeting, recognition followed. With Kelvin he later studied the Joule–Thomson effect, the cooling of a gas on free expansion, which became the basis of refrigeration technology.

Legacy

Joule's measurements established that heat is a form of energy rather than a substance, overthrowing the caloric theory and laying the experimental foundation of thermodynamics alongside the work of Mayer, Helmholtz, and Clausius. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1850 and served as president of the British Association in 1873.
