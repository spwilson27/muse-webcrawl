Ayllu

RegionAndes (present-day Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador)
LanguagesQuechua, Aymara
BasisKinship group holding land communally
Key practicesAyni (reciprocal labour); minka (collective work); mita (labour tribute)

The ayllu is the traditional form of community organisation in the Andean region of South America, particularly among Quechua and Aymara peoples. An ayllu is a group of families, understood as kin descended from a common ancestor, who work land held in common and share reciprocal labour obligations. Ayllus predated the Inca Empire, were incorporated into its administrative system, survived the Spanish colonial period, and remain a living form of social organisation in highland Peru and Bolivia.

Organisation

Members of an ayllu held usufruct rights to parcels (tupus) allocated by the community according to family size, while pastures, water, and other resources were held collectively. Reciprocity structured ayllu life: ayni denotes balanced exchange of labour between households, and minka (minca) collective work for communal benefit. Ayllus were typically organised into dual moieties — an upper (hanan) and lower (hurin) half — reflecting a wider Andean pattern of dualism. Under the Inca, ayllus owed labour tribute (mita) to the state, which administered them through curacas (local lords) and organised conquered peoples into decimal administrative units while leaving the ayllu itself intact.

Verticality and exchange

As described by anthropologist John Murra, ayllus and the ethnic polities they composed often controlled "vertical archipelagos" — discontinuous colonies at several ecological levels (coast, high valleys, puna pasture, and eastern slopes) — allowing direct access to products such as maize, potatoes, wool, and coca without markets.

After the conquest

Spanish colonial rule reworked ayllus into reducciones and subjected them to the colonial mita for mines such as Potosí, yet ayllus persisted as the basis of indigenous community identity and land claims. In modern Bolivia and Peru, ayllus have been legally recognised within systems of indigenous self-government and communal land titling, and the term is used by contemporary indigenous movements as a symbol of pre-colonial political organisation.
