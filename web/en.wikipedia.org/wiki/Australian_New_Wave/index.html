Australian New Wave

The Australian New Wave, also known as the Australian film revival, was an era of resurgence in Australian filmmaking beginning in the 1970s. After decades in which local feature production had almost ceased, a combination of government support, new film schools and film festivals revived the industry, producing films that won international acclaim and launched major careers.

Origins

Key institutional steps included the establishment of the Australian Film Development Corporation in 1970, the Australian Film Commission in 1975, state film corporations, and the Australian Film, Television and Radio School (opened 1973). Prime Minister John Gorton and later the Whitlam government championed a distinctively Australian cinema as a matter of national culture. Early successes ranged from the bawdy "ocker" comedies, such as The Adventures of Barry McKenzie (1972) and Alvin Purple (1973), to acclaimed art films.

Major films and filmmakers

The movement's landmark works include Peter Weir's Picnic at Hanging Rock (1975) and The Last Wave (1977); Fred Schepisi's The Chant of Jimmie Blacksmith (1978); Gillian Armstrong's My Brilliant Career (1979); George Miller's Mad Max (1979), which became a worldwide phenomenon and one of the most profitable films ever made relative to its budget; Bruce Beresford's Breaker Morant (1980); and Gallipoli (1981). Actors such as Mel Gibson, Judy Davis, Jack Thompson and Bryan Brown gained international recognition.

Legacy

The New Wave established a sustainable national film industry and a body of films exploring Australian landscape, history and identity. The generous 10BA tax concessions introduced in 1981 changed the industry's financing and are often taken to mark the end of the New Wave period. Many of its directors and stars went on to prominent careers in Hollywood.
