Ruku (Japanese: 流久) was a post town (shukuba) in Japan during the Edo period. Post towns were settlements established at intervals along the great highways of the Tokugawa shogunate, where travellers could rest, change horses, and find food and lodging at officially regulated inns (honjin and hatago).

History

During the Edo period (1603–1868), the shogunate maintained a network of five great routes — the Gokaidō — of which the Tōkaidō, linking Edo (modern Tokyo) with Kyoto, was the most important. Lesser routes and their stations served regional traffic between castle towns and the capital. Post towns such as Ruku owed their existence to this system: the sankin-kōtai policy, which required feudal lords (daimyō) to travel regularly to Edo with large retinues, guaranteed a steady demand for porters, horses, and accommodation.

Each post station was obliged to maintain a set number of porters and pack horses for official transport, administered through an office known as the ton'ya-ba. Villages surrounding a station shared in the transport duty, and the prosperity of a shukuba rose and fell with the volume of traffic on its road.

Decline

With the Meiji Restoration and the coming of the railways in the late nineteenth century, the post-town system was abolished and many small stations lost their function and faded into ordinary villages. Like other minor shukuba, Ruku survives today mainly as a place name and in the records of Edo-period itineraries and travel guides that listed the stations of the highways.
