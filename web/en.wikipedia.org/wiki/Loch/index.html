Loch

Loch (Scottish Gaelic: loch, pronounced [l̪ˠɔx]) is the Scottish Gaelic word for a lake or a sea inlet. In Scotland most lakes are called lochs (the notable exception being the Lake of Menteith, the only body of water regularly called a lake), while in Ireland the corresponding word is lough.

Loch Lomond, the largest lake in Great Britain by surface area.

Usage

The word applies both to freshwater lakes and to long, narrow sea inlets, which are sometimes called "sea lochs". Familiar examples include Loch Ness, Loch Lomond, Loch Tay and Loch Awe among freshwater lochs, and Loch Linnhe, Loch Fyne and Loch Long among sea lochs. Loch Lomond is the largest by surface area; Loch Ness holds the greatest volume of water; Loch Morar is the deepest.

Etymology and distribution

The term comes from Insular Celtic languages and is cognate with Welsh llwch and Cornish logh. In Irish it is written loch in modern Irish but anglicised as lough, as in Lough Neagh and Lough Corrib. The word has been borrowed into other languages for Scottish-style lakes, and it also appears in Manx as logh. Small lochs are sometimes called lochans.

Engineering

Many Scottish lochs are used as reservoirs and for hydroelectric generation; some, such as Loch Katrine, supply drinking water to Glasgow. Sea lochs have been used for fish farming, naval anchorages and, historically, for herring fisheries.
