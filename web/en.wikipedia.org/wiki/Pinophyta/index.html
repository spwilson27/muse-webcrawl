The Pinophyta, commonly known as the conifers, are a division of vascular land plants containing a single extant class, Pinopsida. They are gymnosperms, bearing their seeds in cones rather than enclosed in fruit. Conifers include many of the world's tallest, largest, and longest-lived trees.

Characteristics

Most conifers are evergreen trees with needle-like or scale-like leaves adapted to cold or dry conditions. Their wood consists largely of tracheids and contains resin canals. Reproduction is by separate male (pollen) and female (seed) cones, usually on the same tree; pollination is by wind. Seeds are often dispersed by wind, though some are spread by birds and mammals.

Distribution and ecology

Conifers dominate the boreal forests (taiga) of the Northern Hemisphere and mountain forests worldwide, and occur naturally on every continent except Antarctica. They are ecologically important as habitat and as carbon stores, and economically vital as sources of softwood timber, pulp, resin, and turpentine.

Evolution

Conifers originated in the late Carboniferous period, more than 300 million years ago, and were among the dominant land plants of the Mesozoic era. Living groups are divided into families including Pinaceae (pines, spruces, firs), Cupressaceae (cypresses, junipers, redwoods), Araucariaceae, Podocarpaceae, and Taxaceae (yews).
