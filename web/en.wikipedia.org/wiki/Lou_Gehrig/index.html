Henry Louis Gehrig (19 June 1903 – 2 June 1941) was an American professional baseball first baseman who played 17 seasons in Major League Baseball for the New York Yankees. Nicknamed "the Iron Horse", he is renowned for his hitting prowess, his record streak of 2,130 consecutive games played, and the poise with which he faced the amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) that ended his career and life — a disease now commonly called "Lou Gehrig's disease" in North America.

Career

Gehrig, the son of German immigrants, starred at Columbia University before signing with the Yankees in 1923. He became the regular first baseman in 1925 — the start of his consecutive-games streak — and formed with Babe Ruth the heart of "Murderers' Row", the legendary 1927 Yankees lineup. Gehrig batted behind Ruth, drove in 1,995 runs (still among the highest totals ever), hit 493 home runs, won the American League Triple Crown in 1934, and was MVP in 1927 and 1936. The Yankees won six World Series titles during his career.

Illness and farewell

Early in the 1939 season Gehrig's performance collapsed; on 2 May he removed himself from the lineup in Detroit, ending his streak at 2,130 games (a record that stood until Cal Ripken Jr. surpassed it in 1995). At the Mayo Clinic he was diagnosed with ALS, an incurable degenerative disease. On 4 July 1939, at a ceremony between games of a doubleheader at Yankee Stadium, Gehrig delivered his famous farewell speech, declaring, "Today I consider myself the luckiest man on the face of the earth." He was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame that December by a special vote.

Death and legacy

Gehrig died on 2 June 1941, aged 37. His number 4 was the first uniform number retired in American professional sports. He remains the archetype of durability and grace in athletics, and his name is inseparably linked with the fight against ALS.
