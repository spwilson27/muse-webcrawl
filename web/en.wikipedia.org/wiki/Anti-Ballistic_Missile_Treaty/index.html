The Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty (ABM Treaty) was an arms control agreement between the United States and the Soviet Union on limiting anti-ballistic missile systems used in defending areas against ballistic missile-delivered nuclear weapons. Signed by Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev on 26 May 1972 as part of the SALT I accords, it entered into force on 3 October 1972 and for three decades underpinned the strategic doctrine of mutual assured destruction by keeping both superpowers deliberately vulnerable to retaliation.

Terms

Each side was initially permitted two ABM deployment areas — one around its capital and one around an ICBM field — each limited to 100 interceptor missiles and associated radars. A 1974 protocol reduced this to one site each: the USSR defended Moscow, while the US completed the Safeguard site at Grand Forks, North Dakota, only to close it in 1976 as not cost-effective. The treaty banned nationwide defences, sea-, air-, space- and mobile land-based ABM systems, and established the Standing Consultative Commission to handle compliance questions.

Withdrawal

The treaty's ban on space-based and theatre defences conflicted with the Reagan-era Strategic Defense Initiative in the 1980s and with later US national missile defence plans. Arguing that the treaty blocked defence against "rogue state" missiles, President George W. Bush gave notice of withdrawal in December 2001; the United States withdrew on 13 June 2002, the first US withdrawal from a major arms control treaty. Russia condemned the move, and subsequent years saw the deployment of US interceptors in Alaska and California and the erosion of other arms control agreements.
