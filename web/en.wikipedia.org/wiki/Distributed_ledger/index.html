Distributed ledger

A distributed ledger is replicated and synchronised across a network of participants.

A distributed ledger (also called a shared ledger, or distributed ledger technology, DLT) is a database that is consensually shared and synchronised across multiple sites, institutions, or geographies, accessible by multiple people. Unlike a centralised database, a distributed ledger has no single central administrator; instead, participants maintain copies of the records and agree on updates through a consensus algorithm. This design allows transactions to have public "witnesses", making a cyberattack on a single copy ineffective against the whole system.

Characteristics

Distributed ledgers are typically append-only stores in which records are added according to agreed rules. The underlying technology assures that once data is stored, it cannot be altered retroactively without the alteration of subsequent records and the collusion of the network. Records are often secured using public-key cryptography, and each participant ("node") holds an identical copy of the ledger.

Types

Distributed ledgers may be permissionless, allowing anyone to participate and validate transactions, or permissioned, where access is restricted to authorised participants. The best-known type of distributed ledger is the blockchain, which underlies cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin; other designs use directed acyclic graphs or hash-linked lists.

Applications

Beyond cryptocurrency, distributed ledgers have been explored for supply-chain tracking, trade finance, identity management, land registries and smart contracts. Adoption outside financial speculation remains limited, and the technology has attracted criticism over scalability, energy use and governance.
