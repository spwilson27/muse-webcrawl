Singh

Singh (IPA: /sɪŋ/) is a title, middle name or surname that means "lion" in various South Asian communities. Derived from the Sanskrit siṃha (lion), it has in the past been used as a martial or royal surname by several warrior communities of the Indian subcontinent, including Rajputs. Since 1699 it has been the name borne by all baptised male Sikhs: Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, instructed every Sikh man to adopt the name Singh and every Sikh woman the name Kaur ("princess"), as an expression of equality and rejection of caste distinctions.

Etymology and history

The Sanskrit word siṃha appears in ancient Indian royal titles and names — for example, in the Gupta-era coinage and in names such as Simhavarman. In medieval India, forms of "Singh" (Simha, Sinha) were widely used by Kshatriya and Rajput lineages, and the name spread through much of northern and western India as an honorific denoting valour.

Use among Sikhs

At the founding of the Khalsa at Anandpur Sahib in 1699, Guru Gobind Singh gave the name Singh to the first five initiates, the Panj Pyare, and took the name himself (from Gobind Rai to Gobind Singh). For initiated (Khalsa) Sikh men, Singh is obligatory as a middle or last name; many non-initiated people of Sikh heritage also bear it. Because so many Sikhs share the name, individuals are usually distinguished by their personal and family names.

Other usage

Singh remains common among Hindu Rajputs and other communities in India and Nepal, and in the diaspora worldwide. Variants include Sinha in Bihar and Bengal and Sing in some transliterations. The corresponding Thai royal vocabulary and Sri Lankan "Sinha" (as in the Sinhalese ethnonym, "lion people") share the same Sanskrit root.
