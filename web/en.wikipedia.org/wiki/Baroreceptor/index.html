Baroreceptor

TypeMechanoreceptor (sensory neuron)
SensesBlood pressure (vessel wall stretch)
LocationsCarotid sinus, aortic arch
ReflexBaroreflex

Baroreceptors (or baroceptors) are mechanoreceptors located in blood vessels near the heart that provide the brain with information about blood volume and blood pressure by detecting the level of stretch of the vessel wall. The principal baroreceptors are in the carotid sinus, at the bifurcation of the common carotid arteries, and in the wall of the aortic arch.

Function

When arterial pressure rises, the vessel walls stretch and baroreceptor firing increases. Signals travel via the glossopharyngeal nerve (from the carotid sinus) and the vagus nerve (from the aortic arch) to the nucleus tractus solitarius in the medulla. The brainstem responds by increasing parasympathetic outflow and decreasing sympathetic outflow, slowing the heart, reducing contractility, and dilating vessels — thereby lowering the pressure. A fall in pressure produces the opposite response.

The baroreflex

This negative feedback loop, the baroreflex, buffers short-term changes in blood pressure, for example on standing up, when blood pools in the legs. Failure of the reflex can cause orthostatic hypotension and fainting. Baroreceptors adapt ("reset") over hours to days, so they regulate acute changes rather than the long-term set point of blood pressure, which depends mainly on renal fluid balance.

Clinical relevance

Carotid sinus hypersensitivity can cause syncope in response to pressure on the neck. Electrical baroreflex activation therapy — implanted stimulation of the carotid sinus — has been investigated as a treatment for resistant hypertension and heart failure.
