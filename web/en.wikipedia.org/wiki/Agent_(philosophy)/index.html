Agent (philosophy)

In philosophy, an agent is a being with the capacity to act — to bring about changes in the world intentionally. Agency is the exercise of this capacity, and its study spans ethics, metaphysics, philosophy of mind, and philosophy of action. Human beings are the paradigm agents, though questions arise about the agency of animals, groups, organizations, and artificial systems.

Action theory

Philosophers of action ask what distinguishes actions from mere events or bodily movements. On influential accounts, such as Donald Davidson's, an action is behavior caused and rationalized by the agent's beliefs and desires. Others emphasize intentions, plans, or the agent's guidance of their own conduct. Harry Frankfurt distinguished persons by their capacity for second-order volitions — desires about their own desires.

Moral agency

A moral agent is an entity that can be held morally responsible for its actions — able to discern right from wrong and to act on that understanding. Moral agency is usually linked to free will: if agents could not have done otherwise, or if their acts are fully determined, the basis for praise and blame is questioned. A moral patient, by contrast, is an entity toward which moral agents may owe duties.

Collective and artificial agency

Recent philosophy examines whether corporations, states, and other groups can be agents in their own right, with beliefs and intentions not reducible to those of members. Parallel debates concern artificial intelligence: whether autonomous systems can possess agency, and who bears responsibility for their behavior.
