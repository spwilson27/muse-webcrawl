Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928

The Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928 (18 & 19 Geo. 5. c. 12) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that extended the voting franchise to all women over the age of 21, granting women electoral equality with men for the first time.

Citation18 & 19 Geo. 5. c. 12
Introduced bySir William Joynson-Hicks, Home Secretary
Royal assent2 July 1928
StatusRepealed (superseded by later legislation)

Background

The Representation of the People Act 1918 had enfranchised women aged over 30 who met a property qualification — some 8.4 million women — while all men over 21 (and servicemen over 19) could vote. The disparity was widely seen as unjustifiable, and Stanley Baldwin's Conservative government committed itself to equalising the franchise. The bill passed with broad support, though some Conservatives feared the effect of millions of younger women voters, dubbed "flapper voters" by the press.

Effects

The Act added about 5 million women to the electoral register, making women a majority of the electorate (about 52.7%). The first general election held under equal franchise was that of 30 May 1929 — the "Flapper Election" — which produced a hung parliament with Labour as the largest party. Nancy Astor had been the first woman to sit in the Commons (1919); in 1929 fourteen women were elected.
