Insomnia

Insomnia is a sleep disorder in which people have trouble sleeping. It may involve difficulty falling asleep (sleep-onset insomnia), difficulty staying asleep through the night (sleep-maintenance insomnia), or waking too early, despite adequate opportunity for sleep, together with daytime consequences such as fatigue, low energy, difficulty concentrating, mood disturbance, and decreased performance. Insomnia can be short-term (acute), lasting days or weeks, often triggered by stress or life events, or long-term (chronic), occurring at least three nights a week for three months or more.

Causes and risk factors

Insomnia is associated with stress, irregular sleep schedules, poor sleep habits, mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression, physical illness and pain, medications, caffeine, alcohol, and other substances. Hyperarousal — heightened physiological and cognitive activation — is a central mechanism in chronic insomnia. It is more common in women and increases with age.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily based on the patient's history; sleep diaries may be kept for one to two weeks. Standardized questionnaires and, where other disorders such as sleep apnoea are suspected, polysomnography or actigraphy may be used.

Management

The recommended first-line treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which combines sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive restructuring, relaxation, and sleep-hygiene education, and produces durable benefits. Sleep hygiene measures include keeping regular sleep times, a dark and cool bedroom, and avoiding caffeine, alcohol, and screens late in the day. Medications such as benzodiazepine receptor agonists ("Z-drugs"), melatonin receptor agonists, and dual orexin receptor antagonists may be used for short periods or in selected patients, balancing benefit against dependence and side effects. Notably, alcohol, though sedating, fragments sleep and is not recommended as a sleep aid.
