Infantry fighting vehicle

A modern IFV combines troop transport with direct fire support.

An infantry fighting vehicle (IFV), also known as a mechanized infantry combat vehicle (MICV), is a type of armoured fighting vehicle used to carry infantry into battle and provide direct-fire support. With the former Soviet Union's adoption of the BMP-1 in the 1960s, IFVs spread worldwide and became a fixture of modern mechanised armies.

IFVs are similar to armoured personnel carriers (APCs), designed to transport a section or squad of infantry, but differ in having much greater firepower: typically a rapid-fire autocannon of 20–40 mm calibre in a turret, often supplemented by anti-tank guided missiles and coaxial machine guns. Firing ports, once common so that embarked infantry could shoot from inside, have largely disappeared from modern designs in favour of heavier armour.

History

The concept emerged from Second World War halftracks and postwar APCs such as the American M113 and Soviet BTR series, which were intended as battlefield taxis. The West German HS.30 and especially the Soviet BMP-1 (introduced in 1966) added a turreted gun and the ability to fight alongside tanks, with low silhouettes and amphibious capability. Western counterparts followed, including the German Marder, French AMX-10P, British Warrior, and American M2 Bradley, the latter fielded in the 1980s with a 25 mm chain gun and TOW missiles.

Design trade-offs

IFV design balances firepower, protection, mobility and troop capacity. Armour has grown steadily heavier in response to mines, improvised explosive devices and top-attack weapons, with modular add-on armour and, increasingly, active protection systems. Wheeled IFVs such as the Boxer and Patria AMV offer road mobility and lower running costs, while tracked vehicles retain superior cross-country performance.
