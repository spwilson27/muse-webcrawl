Sidney Joseph Bechet (May 14, 1897 – May 14, 1959) was an American jazz saxophonist, clarinetist, and composer. He was one of the first important soloists in jazz — recording with Louis Armstrong and others in the early 1920s — and was arguably the first notable jazz saxophonist. His forceful delivery, wide vibrato, and emotional intensity on the soprano saxophone and clarinet made him one of the defining voices of early jazz.

Life and career

Born into a middle-class Creole family in New Orleans, Bechet taught himself clarinet and played in local bands as a teenager. He toured widely, reached London in 1919 with Will Marion Cook's Southern Syncopated Orchestra, and performed in Paris, where the Swiss conductor Ernest Ansermet famously praised him in print as an artist of genius — one of the earliest serious critical assessments of a jazz musician.

Bechet recorded extensively in the 1920s, including sessions with Armstrong, and with his own New Orleans Feetwarmers in the 1930s. In the late 1930s and 1940s he was a central figure of the traditional-jazz revival, recording for Blue Note and composing the tune "Petite Fleur."

He settled permanently in France in the early 1950s, where he was enormously popular — "Petite Fleur" became a major hit there — and he married in a celebrated ceremony in Antibes. He died of lung cancer in Garches on his 62nd birthday, in 1959.

Legacy

Bechet's playing influenced generations of saxophonists and clarinetists. A statue of him stands in Juan-les-Pins, France, and his autobiography Treat It Gentle remains a classic of jazz literature.
