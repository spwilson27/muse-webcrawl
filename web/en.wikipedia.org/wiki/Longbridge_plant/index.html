The Longbridge plant is a motor vehicle manufacturing complex in the Longbridge area of Birmingham, England. Founded by Herbert Austin, who began building cars there in 1905–06 in a former print works, it grew into one of the largest car factories in Europe and for a century was the symbolic heart of the British motor industry.

History

Under the Austin Motor Company the plant produced the best-selling Austin Seven (from 1922) and expanded enormously during the Second World War, when it built aircraft (including Fairey Battles and Lancaster bombers), aero engines and munitions, employing tens of thousands. After the 1952 merger into the British Motor Corporation, Longbridge became BMC's principal site and birthplace of the Mini (1959), designed by Alec Issigonis, as well as the 1100/1300 and the Allegro. Through the British Leyland era it was frequently in the news for industrial disputes. Later landmarks included the Austin Metro (1980) and the Rover 75 (1999).

MG Rover collapse and aftermath

Car production ended abruptly when MG Rover collapsed in April 2005 with the loss of some 6,000 jobs at the plant. The site's Chinese owners, Nanjing Automobile and later SAIC, retained a reduced footprint; MG Motor UK subsequently assembled and then engineered cars at Longbridge, launching the MG6 there in 2011, though final assembly ended in 2016, leaving design, engineering and headquarters functions. Much of the historic factory was demolished and the surrounding land redeveloped as a new town centre with housing, shops, a college and technology park.
