Heat treating

Heat treating (or heat treatment) is a group of industrial, thermal and metalworking processes used to alter the physical, and sometimes chemical, properties of a material. The most common application is metallurgical, where heating and cooling are used to make metals harder, tougher, softer, or more corrosion-resistant, without changing the shape of the part. Heat treatment is applied to steel, aluminium alloys, copper alloys, titanium and glass.

Principles

The properties of a metal depend on its crystal (micro)structure — the phases present, their grain size, and their distribution. Heating a steel above its critical temperature converts its structure to austenite; the rate and manner of subsequent cooling determine whether the austenite transforms to soft pearlite, intermediate bainite, or very hard martensite. Diffusion, precipitation and phase transformations underpin most heat-treating effects, and are charted on time–temperature transformation (TTT) diagrams.

Principal processes

  • Annealing — heating and slow cooling to soften metal, relieve internal stresses and improve ductility and machinability.
  • Quenching — rapid cooling in water, oil or air to form hard martensite in steel; often leaves the part brittle.
  • Tempering — reheating quenched steel to a moderate temperature to trade some hardness for much greater toughness.
  • Normalising — heating and air-cooling to refine grain structure and homogenise properties.
  • Case hardening — enriching only the surface with carbon (carburising) or nitrogen (nitriding) so the part has a hard, wear-resistant skin over a tough core, as in gears.
  • Precipitation hardening (age hardening) — used for aluminium, nickel and stainless alloys, in which fine precipitates formed during ageing impede dislocation motion and strengthen the metal.

Practice

Heat treatment is carried out in furnaces with precisely controlled temperature and atmosphere — vacuum, inert gas, or controlled carbon potential — to prevent scaling and decarburisation. Distortion and cracking during quenching are major practical concerns, managed by part design, quench severity and fixturing. The craft descends from ancient smithing: blacksmiths hardened sword edges and tempered them by colour long before the underlying metallurgy was understood.
