Illusory truth effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Repetition increases perceived truth.

The illusory truth effect (also known as the illusion of truth effect, validity effect, truth effect, or the reiteration effect) is the tendency to believe false information to be correct after repeated exposure. This phenomenon was first identified in a 1977 study at Villanova University and Temple University. When truth is assessed, people rely on whether the information is in line with their understanding or if it feels familiar. The first condition is logical, as people compare new information with what they already know to be true. Repetition makes statements easier to process relative to new, unrepeated statements, leading people to interpret the "processing fluency" as a signal for truth.

Original research

The effect was first named and defined following the results in a study from 1977 by Lynn Hasher, David Goldstein, and Thomas Toporowski at Villanova and Temple Universities. Participants rated their confidence in the truth of 60 plausible statements every two weeks; some statements were repeated across sessions. Participants rated repeated statements as more true than new statements, even though the experimenters had told them the statements might be false. Subsequent experiments by other researchers replicated the effect across varied materials and populations.

Mechanisms

Two main mechanisms have been proposed. Processing fluency: repeated statements are easier to read, hear, and comprehend, and this ease is misattributed to truth. Familiarity: mere exposure creates a feeling of knowing that is confused with prior verification. Robert Zajonc's mere-exposure effect is closely related. The effect persists even when people are warned about it, when statements contradict knowledge, and — notably — even for statements participants were initially told were false, though prior knowledge and the plausibility of a statement moderate its size: repetition moves judgements most for ambiguous claims.

Related phenomena

The illusory truth effect is related to the availability heuristic, the continued influence effect (misinformation that persists after correction), and hindsight bias. It helps explain why advertising slogans, political talking points, urban legends, and propaganda gain credibility through repetition. In the era of social media, algorithmic amplification can expose users to the same false claims many times, strengthening the effect.

Counters

Research suggests that accuracy prompts — briefly directing attention to accuracy before sharing — reduce the spread of misinformation online. Corrections are more effective when they supply a coherent alternative explanation rather than a bare retraction, and "prebunking" or inoculation (warning people about manipulation techniques in advance) can build resistance. However, repeating a myth in order to debunk it carries a small risk of boosting familiarity, so fact-checks often lead with the true fact.
