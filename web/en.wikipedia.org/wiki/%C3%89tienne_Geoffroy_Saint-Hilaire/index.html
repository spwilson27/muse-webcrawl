Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire

Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire (15 April 1772 – 19 June 1844) was a French naturalist who established the principle of "unity of composition" in animal anatomy — the idea that all animals are built on a single structural plan. A professor at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris, he accompanied Napoleon's expedition to Egypt and famously debated Georges Cuvier in 1830 over the organisation of animal bodies, a landmark episode in the history of evolutionary thought.

Career

Geoffroy studied theology, law, and medicine before turning to natural history. In 1793, aged 21, he was appointed professor of zoology at the newly reorganised Muséum in Paris, where he helped build its menagerie. He joined the scientific commission of Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798–1801), collecting extensively and later organising the collections described in the Description de l'Égypte.

Unity of composition

Geoffroy argued that the same anatomical elements recur across species, variously developed, reduced, or fused — his "principle of connections" held that the relative positions of organs are constant even when their forms and functions differ. His Philosophie anatomique (1818–1822) extended this to monstrosities, founding the study of teratology, and he suggested that species might change over time under environmental influence, an early transformist position.

The 1830 debate

In 1830 Geoffroy clashed publicly with Cuvier at the Academy of Sciences after endorsing a paper comparing vertebrates with cephalopod molluscs. Cuvier defended four separate body plans and functional explanation; Geoffroy defended a single plan modified by descent of form. The debate, witnessed across Europe, is seen as a precursor to evolutionary biology.
