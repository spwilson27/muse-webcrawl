Gleaning

Gleaning is the act of collecting leftover crops from farmers' fields after they have been commercially harvested or of collecting crops that are not economically profitable to harvest. It is a practice described in the Hebrew Bible that became a legally enforced entitlement of the poor in a number of Christian kingdoms. In the modern era, gleaning is organized by food recovery and food bank organizations that collect surplus and unsalable food for redistribution to people in need.

Biblical and historical background

The Book of Leviticus and the Book of Deuteronomy instruct landowners to leave the corners of their fields and fallen produce for the poor and the stranger; the Book of Ruth tells of Ruth gleaning in the fields of Boaz. In England, gleaning was treated as a customary right of the poor until a 1788 court judgment held that it was not a right at common law, although the practice persisted in many parishes.

Modern practice

Modern gleaning networks, such as the Society of St. Andrew in the United States and Feedback's Gleaning Network in the United Kingdom, mobilize volunteers to harvest produce that would otherwise go to waste. Gleaning is also associated with movements such as dumpster diving and Freeganism, and with efforts to reduce food waste.

In art

The practice was famously depicted by Jean-François Millet in his 1857 painting The Gleaners, and by Agnès Varda in her 2000 documentary The Gleaners and I.
