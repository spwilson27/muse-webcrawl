California School

The California School is a term used for a loose movement of American watercolor painting that flourished from the 1930s to the 1950s, particularly in Southern California. Its painters — among them Millard Sheets, Emil Kosa Jr., Phil Dike, Barse Miller and Dong Kingman — applied the transparent watercolor technique to large-scale scenes of everyday regional life.

Characteristics

Working in a style influenced by American Scene painting and the Regionalist movement, California School artists painted busy harbours, farms, city streets, beaches and industrial landscapes directly and spontaneously, favouring bold composition and strong light. Unlike easel watercolorists working small, they often painted half-sheet and full-sheet works outdoors.

Context and influence

Many of the artists also worked in the Hollywood film studios as illustrators and art directors, and taught in art schools such as the Chouinard Art Institute. The school helped establish watercolor as a major medium in the American West, and its members played a role in the development of the later California watercolor and plein air traditions.
