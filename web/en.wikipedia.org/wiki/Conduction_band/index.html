Conduction band

In solid-state physics, the conduction band is the range of electron energies above the valence band in which electrons are free to move through a material and contribute to electrical conduction. In a solid, the discrete energy levels of individual atoms broaden into continuous bands; the highest band containing electrons at absolute zero is the valence band, and the next band above it is the conduction band.

Band gap and conductivity

The energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band is the band gap. In metals the bands overlap or are partly filled, so electrons conduct freely. In semiconductors the gap is small (about 1.1 eV in silicon), and thermal energy or doping can promote electrons into the conduction band, leaving mobile holes in the valence band. In insulators the gap is large (several eV), so virtually no electrons reach the conduction band.

Carrier behaviour

Electrons in a nearly empty conduction band behave as free particles with an effective mass determined by the curvature of the band. Their density depends exponentially on temperature and band gap in intrinsic semiconductors, and is controlled by donor doping in extrinsic ones. The band structure — including whether the conduction-band minimum aligns in momentum with the valence-band maximum (direct vs. indirect gap) — governs optical absorption and emission, fundamental to devices such as LEDs and solar cells.
