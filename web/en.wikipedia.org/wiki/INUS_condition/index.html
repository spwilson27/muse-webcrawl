INUS condition

An INUS condition is, in the philosophy of causation, an "insufficient but non-redundant part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition". The concept was introduced by the Australian philosopher J. L. Mackie in his 1965 paper "Causes and Conditions" and developed in his book The Cement of the Universe (1974).

Definition

A condition C is an INUS condition for an effect E when C by itself is not sufficient for E, but C is a necessary (non-redundant) part of some complex of conditions that is sufficient for E, while that whole complex is not itself necessary, because other complexes could also produce E. Mackie's standard example is a house fire: a short circuit is insufficient on its own (it also needs flammable material and oxygen), non-redundant within the complex that actually produced the fire, while the whole complex is unnecessary because a fire could equally have been caused by lightning or arson.

Significance

The INUS analysis was an influential attempt to give a regularity-based account of causation that accommodates multiple and overdetermining causes. It avoids requiring causes to be strictly necessary or strictly sufficient, and it influenced later work on causal modelling, law and epidemiology, where risk factors are often described in similar component-cause terms.
