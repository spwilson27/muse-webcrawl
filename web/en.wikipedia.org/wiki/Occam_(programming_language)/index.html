Occam (programming language)

Occam is a concurrent programming language developed in the early 1980s at Inmos, the British semiconductor company, as the native language of the transputer microprocessor. Named after the philosopher William of Ockham and his principle of parsimony ("Occam's razor"), the language is deliberately minimal in design.

Design

Occam is built on Tony Hoare's Communicating Sequential Processes (CSP) calculus. A program consists of processes that run in parallel and communicate over channels — unbuffered, synchronous connections carrying typed messages. Three primitive constructs structure programs: SEQ for sequential execution, PAR for parallel execution, and ALT for choosing among alternative communications (a guarded choice). Indentation, rather than braces or keywords, determines block structure, an unusual feature occam shares with its contemporary design philosophy of textual simplicity.

Because channels are point-to-point and processes share no memory, occam programs mapped naturally onto networks of transputers, each chip with its own memory connected by fast serial links.

Versions and legacy

Occam 1 (1983) supported the original transputer; occam 2 (1987) added floating-point types, functions, and multi-dimensional arrays; the later occam-π (pi) extended the language with mobile channels and processes, influenced by the π-calculus. Although the transputer failed commercially and Inmos was absorbed into SGS-Thomson, occam's channel model proved durable: it strongly influenced the design of concurrency in the Go language and renewed academic interest in CSP-based programming.
