In mathematics, a Dirichlet problem is the problem of finding a function which solves a specified partial differential equation in the interior of a given region, taking prescribed values on the boundary of the region. It is named after Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, and is most classically posed for Laplace's equation: find a harmonic function u in a domain Ω with u = g on ∂Ω.

History

The problem arose from physics: the equilibrium temperature distribution in a plate whose edge temperatures are controlled, or the electrostatic potential inside a region with fixed boundary potential. Dirichlet proposed solving it by minimising an energy integral — the "Dirichlet principle" — but Karl Weierstrass showed the argument had a gap, since an infimum need not be attained. David Hilbert later rehabilitated the method around 1900, and the modern theory uses the direct method in the calculus of variations and Sobolev spaces.

Existence and regularity

For domains with sufficiently regular boundaries — those satisfying an exterior cone condition — continuous boundary data admit a unique solution, obtainable by Perron's method of subharmonic functions. Certain irregular boundary points, such as the tip of Lebesgue's spine, can fail to be regular: the solution may not attain the prescribed boundary value there. By the maximum principle, solutions of the homogeneous problem are unique.

Solution methods

Explicit solutions exist for special geometries: the Poisson integral formula solves the problem on a disk or ball. General domains are treated by Green's functions, separation of variables, conformal mapping in two dimensions, or numerically by finite element and boundary element methods.

Related problems

Prescribing the normal derivative instead of the value gives the Neumann problem; prescribing a combination gives the Robin problem. The Dirichlet problem generalises to elliptic equations broadly, and appears throughout potential theory, heat conduction and minimal surface theory.
