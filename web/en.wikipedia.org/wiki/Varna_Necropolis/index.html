Varna Necropolis

The Varna Necropolis is a burial site of the Chalcolithic (Copper Age) Varna culture, located in the western industrial zone of Varna, Bulgaria, near Lake Varna. Dating to roughly 4560–4450 BC, it is internationally famous as the source of the oldest known worked gold artefacts in the world, predating the goldwork of Egypt and Mesopotamia.

Grave 43, the richest burial, contained gold ornaments, a sceptre and copper tools.

Discovery and excavation

The site was discovered accidentally in 1972 when excavator operator Raycho Marinov unearthed gold objects while digging a cable trench. Archaeological excavations under Ivan Ivanov continued through the 1970s and 1980s, revealing some 294 graves over an area of about 7,500 square metres; roughly a third of the estimated cemetery area remains unexcavated.

The graves

Burials took several forms: men laid out on their backs, women in a crouched (hocker) position, and a number of symbolic cenotaphs containing no skeleton but rich grave goods, including clay masks with gold appliqués on the face. About 62 graves contained gold objects, totalling more than 3,000 artefacts weighing over 6 kilograms — including beads, rings, bracelets, breastplates, diadems and sceptres. Grave 43, an adult male of about 40–50 years, alone held some 990 gold items weighing about 1.5 kg, more gold than had previously been found in all other contemporary sites combined.

Significance

The extreme concentration of wealth in a few graves is among the earliest clear archaeological evidence for pronounced social hierarchy and hereditary status in prehistoric Europe. Alongside gold, the graves contained copper tools, flint blades of exceptional length, obsidian, and bracelets made from the shell of the Mediterranean mollusc Spondylus, indicating long-distance exchange networks. The finds are displayed at the Varna Archaeological Museum, whose exhibition is popularly known as the "oldest gold in the world".
