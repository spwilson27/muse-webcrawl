Aggadah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aggadic legends are often illustrated in illuminated manuscripts such as the Passover Haggadah

Aggadah (Aramaic: אַגָּדָה, "telling" or "narration"; plural aggadot) is the non-legalistic part of classical rabbinic literature, including parables, legends, folklore, anecdotes, historical narratives, moral maxims, and theological speculation. It stands in contrast to halakha, the legal material of the Talmud and related works.

Aggadah appears throughout the Talmud and the midrashic compilations. Roughly speaking, a substantial portion of the Babylonian Talmud is aggadic material, and whole midrashic collections, such as Genesis Rabbah and Leviticus Rabbah, are predominantly aggadic.

Content and character

Aggadic literature covers a wide range of themes: expansions of biblical narratives filling in gaps in the scriptural account; stories about rabbis and their deeds; teachings about God, creation, providence, and the world to come; eschatology and messianic expectations; ethics and piety; and folk wisdom expressed in proverbs and parables. Famous aggadic motifs include the legends of Abraham smashing his father's idols, the exploits and wisdom of King Solomon, and accounts of the martyrdom of Rabbi Akiva.

Interpretation

Already in the medieval period, authorities debated how literally aggadah should be taken. Maimonides distinguished those who accept aggadic statements at face value, those who reject them entirely, and those who understand them as parables conveying philosophical and ethical truths — the approach he endorsed. The Maharal of Prague and others developed symbolic readings, and modern scholarship treats aggadah as a window into the religious imagination and social world of late antique Judaism.

The Haggadah

The term is related to, but distinct from, the Haggadah of Passover, the text recited at the seder, which itself incorporates much aggadic material about the Exodus from Egypt.
