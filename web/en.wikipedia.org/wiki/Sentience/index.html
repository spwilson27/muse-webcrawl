Sentience

Sentience

Sentience is the ability to experience feelings and sensations. In philosophy, the term is often used for the capacity to have subjective experiences — to feel pleasure, pain, and other states — as distinct from sapience (wisdom or self-awareness) and from consciousness more broadly. A sentient being is one for which there is "something it is like" to be that being, in the phrase associated with philosopher Thomas Nagel.

In philosophy

The concept traces to Enlightenment thinkers. Jeremy Bentham famously argued in 1789 that the morally relevant question about animals is not "Can they reason?" nor "Can they talk?" but "Can they suffer?" In contemporary ethics, sentience is the central criterion in Peter Singer's utilitarian case for animal welfare and in much of the animal rights literature, since the capacity to suffer or enjoy is taken to ground moral interests.

In science

Scientists study sentience through behavioural and neurological evidence: nociception, motivational trade-offs, self-protective behaviour, and neural structures analogous to those supporting feeling in humans. The 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness asserted that a wide range of animals, including mammals, birds, and octopuses, possess the neurological substrates of consciousness. The question of sentience in invertebrates, fish, and artificial systems remains an active research area.

Sentience and law

Legal recognition of animal sentience has grown: the Treaty of Lisbon (2009) requires the European Union to pay regard to the welfare of animals as sentient beings, and countries including the United Kingdom have enacted animal sentience legislation establishing committees to scrutinise policy for its effects on sentient animals.
