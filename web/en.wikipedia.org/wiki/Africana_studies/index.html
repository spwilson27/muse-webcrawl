Africana studies

Africana studies is the interdisciplinary academic field devoted to the study of the histories, cultures, and politics of peoples of African descent worldwide — in Africa, the Americas, the Caribbean, and Europe. It is known variously as Black studies, African American studies, and African diaspora studies, and draws on history, literature, sociology, political science, economics, and the arts.

History

Although Black intellectuals such as W. E. B. Du Bois and Carter G. Woodson had long studied African and African American life, Black studies emerged as an institutional field in the late 1960s through student activism, including the 1968–69 strike at San Francisco State University, which produced the first Black studies department in the United States. Programs spread across American universities in the 1970s and later to institutions internationally.

Approaches

The field examines slavery and its legacies, colonialism, liberation movements, race and identity, and the cultural production of the African diaspora. Theoretical currents include Afrocentricity associated with Molefi Kete Asante, Black feminism, and diaspora frameworks influenced by Paul Gilroy's The Black Atlantic. Temple University established the first doctoral program in the field in 1988.
