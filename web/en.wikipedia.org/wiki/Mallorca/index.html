Mallorca

Mallorca is the largest island of Spain's Balearic Islands in the western Mediterranean, and a major European holiday destination. Its capital, Palma, holds about half the island's roughly 900,000 inhabitants. Beyond the resorts, Mallorca offers the Serra de Tramuntana mountain range (a UNESCO cultural landscape), hill towns such as Valldemossa and Sóller, and Palma's vast Gothic cathedral, La Seu, rising above the old harbour.

History

Prehistoric talaiot builders, Phoenicians, Romans (who founded Palma in 123 BC), Vandals, Byzantines and Moors all held Mallorca; under Muslim rule Madina Mayurqa prospered. James I of Aragon conquered the island in 1229–31, and for a time a cadet Kingdom of Majorca existed under his younger son. Catalan is the island's own language alongside Spanish. In the Spanish Civil War Mallorca became a Nationalist base (and an Italian air base for raids on the Republican mainland, including Barcelona) after a failed Republican landing in 1936. Mass tourism from the 1960s — today well over ten million visitors a year — transformed the island's economy and landscape, and now fuels local debate about housing and sustainability.

Geography and culture

The Tramuntana range runs along the north-west coast, its dry-stone terraces and olive groves centuries old; the fertile central plain (Es Pla) and the gentler Llevant complete the island. Chopin and George Sand wintered famously (and unhappily) at Valldemossa in 1838–39; Robert Graves made Deià his home. Palma's cathedral, begun in 1229 on the mosque site, had its interior remodelled in part by Antoni Gaudí in the early 1900s.
