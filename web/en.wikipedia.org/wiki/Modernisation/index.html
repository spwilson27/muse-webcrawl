Modernisation

Modernisation (also spelled modernization) is the process of social change whereby less developed societies acquire the characteristics of more developed societies. In the social sciences, modernization theory holds that traditional societies will develop as they adopt more modern practices, progressing through stages of economic growth, urbanization, industrialization, democratization and secularization.

The concept draws on the work of classical sociologists such as Émile Durkheim, Max Weber and Ferdinand Tönnies, who analysed the transition from traditional to modern societies. In the post-war period, theorists such as Walt Rostow formalised modernization into stages of growth, and the theory strongly influenced development policy and foreign aid in the mid-twentieth century.

Characteristics

Processes associated with modernisation include industrialization, urbanization, increasing literacy and mass education, the growth of mass media, the extension of political participation, the differentiation of social institutions, and a shift from ascribed to achieved status. Technological modernization, in a narrower sense, refers to the upgrading of equipment, infrastructure and processes in industry and public services.

Criticism

Modernization theory has been criticised for ethnocentrism, for assuming a single linear path of development modelled on Western societies, and for neglecting the effects of colonialism and global inequality — critiques formalised in dependency theory and world-systems theory. Later scholars have proposed revised frameworks, such as theories of multiple modernities, which allow that different societies modernise in culturally distinct ways.
