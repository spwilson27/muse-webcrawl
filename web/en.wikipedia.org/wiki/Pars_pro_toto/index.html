Pars pro toto

Pars pro toto
Latin: "a part [taken] for the whole"
Type: Figure of speech; form of synecdoche
Inverse: Totum pro parte

Pars pro toto (Latin for "a part for the whole") is a figure of speech in which a part of something is used to refer to the whole of it, or a specific part or aspect is used to represent a broader entity. It is a type of synecdoche, and the inverse is totum pro parte, in which the whole stands for a part.

Examples

  • "All hands on deck" — hands (part) for the sailors (whole).
  • "Sails" for ships, as in "a fleet of fifty sail".
  • "The Kremlin" for the Russian government, and "the Crown" for the monarchy — bordering on metonymy.
  • Counting people as "heads", as in "per head" or a "head count".
  • Naming a country by its capital city, as in "Washington announced…" for the United States government.

Usage and related terms

The device is common in poetry, rhetoric, journalism, and everyday language, because a vivid, concrete part often evokes the whole more forcefully than an abstract name. In classical rhetoric, pars pro toto is grouped with synecdoche generally, alongside totum pro parte ("England won the World Cup" — the country standing for its team). In linguistics and cognitive semantics it is analysed as a form of metonymic reference within an "idealised cognitive model".
