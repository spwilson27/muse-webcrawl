Timber framing

Timber framing is a method of building structures using heavy, squared-off timbers connected by large wooden pegs driven through mortise and tenon joints, rather than the sawn dimensional lumber and nails of modern platform framing. The structural skeleton — posts, beams, braces, and rafters — carries the building's loads, so the frame can be raised as a series of pre-assembled cross-sections called bents before the walls are filled in.

History

Timber framing has been practised for thousands of years wherever large trees were available. It produced the half-timbered houses of medieval Europe (German Fachwerk, French colombage), the temples and palaces of Japan and China, and the great barns and covered bridges of colonial North America. In England, cruck frames and box frames were standard for vernacular building until brick and stone displaced them. Industrialisation and the spread of cheap nails and sawmill lumber made the labour-intensive technique rare by the late nineteenth century.

Construction

Frames traditionally use green oak or other durable hardwood in Europe and softwoods such as Douglas fir in North America. Members are laid out with scribing techniques, cut with saws, chisels, and boring machines, and test-fitted on the ground. Joints are secured with tapered hardwood pegs, often of oak or locust, drawing the joint tight as they are driven home. Spaces between timbers were historically filled with wattle and daub, brick nogging, or stone; modern frames more often wrap the skeleton in structural insulated panels, keeping the timbers exposed inside.

Revival

From the 1970s a revival movement, led by craftsmen such as Tedd Benson in the United States, returned timber framing to custom homebuilding, aided by computer-aided design and CNC joinery machines. Traditional hand-cut frames remain a niche craft taught through guilds and workshops.
