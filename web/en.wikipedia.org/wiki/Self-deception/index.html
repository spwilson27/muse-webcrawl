Self-deception

Self-deception is a process of denying or rationalizing away the relevance, significance, or importance of opposing evidence and logical argument. It involves convincing oneself of a truth (or lack of truth) so that one does not reveal any self-knowledge of the deception. Everyday examples include the smoker who discounts the evidence on health risks, the partner who overlooks signs of infidelity, and the student who attributes failure entirely to bad luck.

The paradoxes

Philosophers have long noted two puzzles. The static paradox: how can one person simultaneously believe p and believe not-p, as self-deception seems to require? The dynamic paradox: deliberately deceiving oneself seems to require knowing the truth in order to hide it — yet a deceiver who knows the plan cannot be fooled by it, just as one cannot tickle oneself by surprise. Solutions divide into intentionalist accounts, which posit partitions within the mind (so one part can deceive another), and deflationary accounts, notably Alfred Mele's, which argue that self-deception requires no intention to deceive oneself at all: motivationally biased treatment of evidence — selective attention, selective evidence-gathering, and unfavourable interpretation of unwelcome data — is sufficient.

Evolutionary and psychological accounts

Robert Trivers proposed that self-deception evolved in the service of deceiving others: by believing our own falsehoods we display fewer of the cues of conscious lying and thus deceive more convincingly. Psychological research links self-deception to positive illusions, unrealistic optimism, and the above-average effect, which may support confidence and mental health in moderation but also risk-taking and poor decisions. In psychopathology, denial is treated as a defence mechanism in psychoanalytic theory. The concept is related to wishful thinking, motivated reasoning, cognitive dissonance, and bad faith in existentialist philosophy.
