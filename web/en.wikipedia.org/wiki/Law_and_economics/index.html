Law and economics

Law and economics, or the economic analysis of law, is the application of economic theory — particularly microeconomics and the concept of efficiency — to the analysis of legal rules and institutions. It seeks both to explain how legal rules affect behaviour (positive analysis) and to evaluate which rules are socially desirable (normative analysis).

Origins

Although Adam Smith and Jeremy Bentham anticipated elements of the field, modern law and economics began around 1960 with Ronald Coase's article "The Problem of Social Cost" (source of the Coase theorem), Guido Calabresi's work on accident law, and Gary Becker's extension of economics to crime and discrimination. The movement was centred at the University of Chicago, where Richard Posner's Economic Analysis of Law (1973) became its leading treatise, and the Journal of Law and Economics and Journal of Legal Studies its main outlets.

Key ideas and influence

Core concepts include the idea that people respond to legal sanctions as prices, that common-law rules tend toward efficiency, and that the purpose of remedies such as damages can be understood as internalising externalities at least cost. The field has strongly influenced antitrust, tort, contract, and corporate law, and judicial education in the United States. Critics dispute the priority given to efficiency over distributive justice and the assumption of rational behaviour, criticisms partly addressed by behavioural law and economics.
