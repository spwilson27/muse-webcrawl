First Anglo-Sikh war

The First Anglo-Sikh war was fought between the Sikh Empire and the British East India Company in 1845 and 1846 around the Ferozepur district of Punjab. It resulted in a British victory and the partial subjugation of the Sikh kingdom, which lost territory, accepted a British resident at Lahore and had its army reduced, though it survived until it was annexed after the Second Anglo-Sikh War in 1849.

Background

After the death of Maharaja Ranjit Singh in 1839, the Sikh Empire fell into disorder, with a succession of short-lived rulers and assassinations. Real power passed to the Khalsa Army, whose soldiers' panchayats (regimental committees) alarmed the Lahore Durbar. The British meanwhile concentrated troops and bridging equipment near the Sutlej frontier. In December 1845 the Sikh army crossed the Sutlej into territory it regarded as its own; the British treated the crossing as an invasion, and Governor-General Sir Henry Hardinge declared war on 13 December.

Campaign

The war consisted of a series of hard-fought pitched battles: Mudki (18 December 1845), Ferozeshah (21–22 December), a costly night battle in which the British under Sir Hugh Gough came close to defeat, Baddowal (21 January 1846), Aliwal (28 January), where Sir Harry Smith won a celebrated victory, and finally Sobraon (10 February 1846), where the British stormed the Sikh entrenchments across the Sutlej in the bloodiest battle of the war. British losses were heavy — the Khalsa's artillery and discipline made the Sikhs among the most formidable opponents the Company faced in India — but disunity and the treachery or lethargy of the Sikh commanders Lal Singh and Tej Singh repeatedly undermined their army.

Treaty of Lahore

By the Treaty of Lahore (9 March 1846), the Sikh state ceded the fertile Jalandhar Doab between the Beas and Sutlej rivers, accepted a war indemnity, reduced its army, and admitted a British resident, Henry Lawrence, at Lahore. Kashmir was sold separately to Gulab Singh, who became Maharaja of Jammu and Kashmir. Later in 1846 the Treaty of Bhairowal made the British resident's authority during the minority of Maharaja Duleep Singh effectively supreme.
