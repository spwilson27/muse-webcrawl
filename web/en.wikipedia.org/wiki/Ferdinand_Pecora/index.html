Ferdinand Pecora (January 6, 1882 – December 7, 1971) was an American lawyer and judge of Sicilian descent, best known as chief counsel to the U.S. Senate Banking and Currency Committee's 1933–34 investigation of Wall Street — the Pecora Commission — whose revelations of financial abuses helped produce the landmark securities legislation of the New Deal.

Early life and career

Pecora was born in Nicosia, Sicily, and emigrated to the United States as a child. Growing up in poverty in New York, he left school young, worked his way through night studies, and was admitted to the New York bar in 1911. He became an assistant district attorney in New York County, gaining a reputation as a formidable prosecutor of fraud, and later entered private practice.

The Pecora hearings

Appointed chief counsel in January 1933, Pecora transformed a faltering inquiry into a sensation. Under his cross-examination, leading bankers admitted practices that shocked the public: National City Bank's Charles E. Mitchell acknowledged selling worthless securities and avoiding income tax; J.P. Morgan Jr. revealed "preferred lists" of influential clients offered stock at discount prices; and Albert Wiggin of Chase National Bank admitted short-selling his own bank's stock during the crash. Photographs of a publicity-seeking midget seated on Morgan's lap during a recess became an iconic image of the era.

The hearings built the political case for the Glass–Steagall Act (1933), the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, which created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).

Later life

Pecora served briefly as one of the first SEC commissioners under Joseph P. Kennedy, then became a justice of the New York Supreme Court, serving from 1935 to 1950. He ran unsuccessfully for mayor of New York City in 1950. He published a memoir of the investigation, Wall Street Under Oath, in 1939.
