Panorama

A panorama (from Greek πᾶν "all" and ὅραμα "view") is any wide-angle view or representation of a physical space, whether in painting, drawing, photography, film or a three-dimensional model. The word was coined in the late eighteenth century by the English painter Robert Barker to describe his innovative cylindrical paintings of Edinburgh and London, exhibited in purpose-built rotundas where visitors stood on a central platform surrounded by a 360-degree image.

History

Panoramic rotundas became a craze in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, presenting battle scenes, city views and exotic landscapes to mass audiences, and they are considered a precursor of the cinema. Painters such as Pierre Prévost and later battle-panorama specialists created monumental works, some of which survive in museums today.

Photography

Panoramic photography captures an elongated field of view, with aspect ratios of 2:1 or more. Early approaches used rotating cameras and slit shutters; modern digital methods stitch overlapping frames together, either in-camera or with software, to produce single wide images. Smartphones commonly offer an automatic panorama mode that sweeps the scene. 360-degree panoramas can be projected as spherical images and explored interactively, as in street-level mapping services.
