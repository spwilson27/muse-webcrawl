Cohort (military unit)

Cohort
Latin: cohors (pl. cohortes)
Typical strength: about 480 men
Composition: 6 centuries of 80
Parent unit: Legion (10 cohorts)

A cohort (Latin cohors) was a standard tactical military unit of the Roman army composed of several centuries. Following the reforms traditionally attributed to Gaius Marius in the late second century BC, the cohort replaced the maniple as the principal tactical subunit of the legion, and ten cohorts made up a legion.

Organisation

A standard cohort consisted of six centuries of 80 men each, for a total of about 480 soldiers, commanded by the senior of its six centurions (the pilus prior). The first cohort of a legion was larger — from the late first century AD it comprised five double-strength centuries of about 160 men, totalling roughly 800, and included the legion's most senior centurion, the primus pilus, as well as the legionary eagle standard.

Cohorts were numbered (I–X) and their centuries retained titles recalling the old manipular order — hastati, principes, and pili — even though the tactical distinctions between those troop types had disappeared.

Auxiliary cohorts

The auxilia, the non-citizen supporting troops of the imperial army, were also organised in cohorts. An auxiliary cohors peditata had about 480–500 infantry; a cohors equitata mixed infantry with a small cavalry contingent; and double-strength units were styled cohors milliaria (about 800–1,000 men). Auxiliary cohorts were frequently named for the province or people from which they were first recruited — for example, cohorts of Tungrians, Batavians, and Syrians served on Hadrian's Wall.

Other uses

The term was also applied to units in Rome itself, including the Praetorian Guard (organised in cohorts), the urban cohorts who policed the city, and the cohortes vigilum who served as fire brigade and night watch. The word survives in modern usage for a group sharing a common characteristic, as in a demographic cohort or a cohort study.
