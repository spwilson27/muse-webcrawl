Zajal

Zajal (Arabic: زجل) is a traditional form of oral strophic poetry in colloquial Arabic, declaimed or sung, which originated in al-Andalus (Islamic Iberia) and spread throughout the Arab world. In its best-known modern forms it is performed as a sung debate between poets in Lebanon, Palestine, Syria and Egypt.

Origins

The zajal developed in Muslim Spain, where the strophic forms muwashshah and zajal flourished from the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Its most famous Andalusian practitioner was Ibn Quzmān (c. 1080–1160) of Córdoba, whose dīwān of zajals in colloquial Andalusi Arabic is the genre's founding monument. Scholars have debated the extent to which these Iberian strophic forms influenced the troubadour lyric of neighbouring Christian Europe.

Structure and performance

A zajal is built of strophes with a recurring refrain, in metres looser than those of classical Arabic poetry and in a register close to everyday speech, allowing wordplay, humour and topical allusion. In the Levantine tradition, a zajal evening (haflat zajal) features rival choirs of poets, accompanied by percussion instruments such as the tablah and daf, improvising verses in turn — praising their own side, satirising their opponents, and addressing themes from love to politics — while the chorus repeats the refrain after each singer.

Recognition

Lebanese zajal was inscribed in 2014 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognised as a living art of improvised sung poetry central to social gatherings, weddings and festivals.
