Begonia

A cultivated begonia, showing the typically asymmetric leaves.

Begonia is a genus of perennial flowering plants in the family Begoniaceae. With roughly 2,000 accepted species, it is one of the largest genera of flowering plants. Begonias are native to moist subtropical and tropical climates of South and Central America, Africa and southern Asia, and are widely grown as ornamental plants.

Description

Begonias are terrestrial (sometimes epiphytic) herbs or undershrubs. Their leaves are characteristically asymmetric — the feature behind the former generic name usage and a common identification mark. Plants are monoecious, bearing separate male and female flowers on the same plant; flowers are often showy with coloured tepals, and the fruit is a winged capsule containing numerous tiny seeds.

Cultivation

The genus was named by Charles Plumier in honour of Michel Bégon, a French governor of Saint-Domingue and patron of botany, and adopted by Linnaeus. Horticulturally, begonias are grouped into types such as cane, rhizomatous, rex, tuberous and wax (semperflorens) begonias, and thousands of cultivars are grown for foliage or flowers in gardens, hanging baskets and as houseplants. Most require warmth, humidity and protection from frost.
