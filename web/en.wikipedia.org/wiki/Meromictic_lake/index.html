A meromictic lake has layers of water that do not intermix. In ordinary (holomictic) lakes, seasonal temperature changes cause the water column to mix at least once a year, but in meromictic lakes a stable density gradient — usually caused by a difference in salinity or dissolved chemicals — prevents complete mixing. The term was coined in 1935 by the Austrian limnologist Ingo Findenegg.

Layers

A meromictic lake is divided into three layers:

  • The mixolimnion, the surface layer, which circulates freely like the water of an ordinary lake and exchanges gases with the atmosphere.
  • The chemocline, a thin transition zone where the concentration of dissolved substances changes rapidly with depth.
  • The monimolimnion, the deep layer, which does not mix with the surface. It is typically denser, often saltier, and usually lacks dissolved oxygen.

Causes of meromixis

Meromixis can arise in several ways. In ectogenic meromixis, an external event — such as seawater intruding into a freshwater lake, or a landslide — introduces dense water at depth. In crenogenic meromixis, saline springs feed dense water into the lake bottom. In biogenic meromixis, the decomposition of organic matter accumulates dissolved salts in the deep water over time. Deep, small-surface lakes sheltered from wind, such as crater lakes, are especially prone to meromixis.

Consequences

Because the monimolimnion never mixes, it becomes anoxic and accumulates nutrients, hydrogen sulfide, methane and carbon dioxide. Bacteria, including photosynthetic purple sulfur bacteria at the chemocline, form distinctive communities. The undisturbed sediments of meromictic lakes preserve fine annual layers (varves), making them valuable archives for paleoclimate research.

If the stored gases of a meromictic lake are suddenly released — a limnic eruption — the result can be catastrophic. The 1986 eruption of Lake Nyos in Cameroon released a cloud of carbon dioxide that killed more than 1,700 people. Degassing pipes have since been installed at Nyos and Lake Monoun to vent the gas safely.

Examples

  • Lake Nyos and Lake Monoun, Cameroon
  • Lake Kivu, on the Rwanda–DR Congo border
  • The Black Sea, the world's largest meromictic basin
  • Green Lake and Fayetteville Green Lake, New York, United States
  • Lake Cadagno, Switzerland
  • Soap Lake, Washington, United States
