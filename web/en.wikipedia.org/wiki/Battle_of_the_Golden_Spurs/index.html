Battle of the Golden Spurs

The Battle of the Golden Spurs (Dutch: Guldensporenslag), also called the Battle of Courtrai, was fought on 11 July 1302 near Kortrijk (Courtrai) in Flanders. An army of Flemish urban militias — mostly weavers, fullers and other guildsmen fighting on foot — defeated a royal French army built around its heavy cavalry, one of the most celebrated medieval victories of infantry over knights.

Background

Philip IV of France had annexed Flanders in 1300 and installed an unpopular governor. On the night of 17–18 May 1302, Flemish rebels in Bruges massacred the French garrison in the "Bruges Matins." The cities rose and assembled an army which took position outside Kortrijk, behind ditches and marshy streams, under leaders including Pieter de Coninck and Jan Breydel.

The battle

The French commander, Robert of Artois, ordered his infantry forward, then impatiently recalled them to let the knights win the glory. The cavalry charges foundered in the wet, ditch-cut ground before the Flemish pike-and-goedendag formations. Artois himself was killed, along with a large part of the French chivalry; the Flemings, ordered to take no prisoners for ransom, collected some 500 pairs of golden spurs from the dead knights and hung them as trophies in the Church of Our Lady in Kortrijk.

Legacy

The battle checked French annexation of Flanders and became a founding myth of Flemish identity; 11 July is the official holiday of the Flemish Community. Together with Bannockburn (1314) and Morgarten (1315), it is often cited in the "infantry revolution" of the fourteenth century.
