Paralytic shellfish poisoning

SpecialtyEmergency medicine, toxicology
CauseSaxitoxin and related toxins from dinoflagellates, accumulated in bivalve shellfish
SymptomsPerioral tingling, numbness, weakness, paralysis, respiratory failure
OnsetWithin 30 minutes to a few hours of ingestion
TreatmentSupportive care, mechanical ventilation; no specific antidote

Paralytic shellfish poisoning (PSP) is one of the four recognised syndromes of illness caused by eating shellfish contaminated with marine biotoxins, alongside diarrhetic, neurotoxic, and amnesic shellfish poisoning. PSP is caused primarily by saxitoxin and a family of related compounds produced by certain dinoflagellates (such as Alexandrium species, Gymnodinium catenatum, and Pyrodinium bahamense) and some cyanobacteria. Filter-feeding bivalves — clams, mussels, oysters, and scallops — accumulate the toxins without being harmed themselves.

Bivalve shellfish can accumulate saxitoxin during algal blooms ("red tides").

Mechanism

Saxitoxin is a potent neurotoxin that blocks voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve and muscle cells, preventing the propagation of action potentials. The result is a descending flaccid paralysis. Saxitoxin is heat-stable, so cooking does not reliably destroy it, and it is tasteless, so contaminated shellfish cannot be detected by appearance or flavour.

Symptoms

Symptoms usually begin within 30 minutes to a few hours of eating contaminated shellfish. Early signs include tingling and numbness around the lips and tongue spreading to the face, neck, and extremities, a sensation of floating, headache, nausea, and vomiting. In severe cases, progressive muscle weakness leads to paralysis and respiratory failure, which is the usual cause of death. Patients typically remain conscious and aware. Most survivors recover fully within days as the toxin is excreted; mortality in outbreaks has ranged widely, and modern respiratory support has greatly reduced fatality rates.

Epidemiology and prevention

PSP occurs worldwide in temperate and tropical coastal waters, typically associated with harmful algal blooms ("red tides"). Prevention relies on regulatory monitoring programmes that test shellfish beds for toxin levels and close harvest areas when saxitoxin exceeds the common regulatory limit of 80 µg per 100 g of shellfish meat. Recreational harvesters are advised to observe local closures. There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive, with mechanical ventilation for respiratory paralysis.

History

Outbreaks have been recorded for centuries; a notable 1793 incident during Captain George Vancouver's expedition to British Columbia sickened his crew after eating mussels. Saxitoxin was isolated and characterised in the 20th century, and it has been studied as a potential chemical weapon — it is listed under the Chemical Weapons Convention.
