Valdivian temperate rain forest

The Valdivian temperate rain forest is an ecoregion on the west coast of southern South America, in Chile and extending into Argentina. It lies between roughly 37° and 48° south latitude, on the western slopes of the Andes and the Chilean Coastal Range. It is part of the Neotropical realm and is recognised as one of the world's few temperate rain forests and a biodiversity hotspot.

Climate and flora

The region has a temperate oceanic climate with abundant year-round rainfall, from about 1,000 mm to over 5,000 mm annually in exposed coastal mountains. The forests are dominated by evergreen angiosperms — coihue and other southern beeches (Nothofagus), laurels, myrtles and the conifers alerce (Fitzroya cupressoides) and Guaitecas cypress — with dense understoreys of bamboo (Chusquea), ferns and climbing plants. Alerce trees can live more than 3,000 years, among the longest-lived trees on Earth. Many plant lineages are Gondwanan relicts, related to the flora of New Zealand and Tasmania.

Fauna

Characteristic animals include the pudú (one of the world's smallest deer), the monito del monte (a relict marsupial), Darwin's fox, the kodkod cat, and endemic birds such as the chucao tapaculo and Magellanic woodpecker.

Conservation

Much of the lowland forest has been cleared for agriculture, forestry plantations and firewood, and large areas were burned during the colonial period. Protected areas include Pumalín Douglas Tompkins National Park, Alerce Costero National Park and Valdivian Coastal Reserve in Chile, and Los Alerces National Park in Argentina. The ecoregion, together with the Juan Fernández Islands, is designated a biodiversity hotspot because of its high endemism and habitat loss.
