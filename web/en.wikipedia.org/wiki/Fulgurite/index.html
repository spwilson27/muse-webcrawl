Fulgurite

Fulgurites, commonly called "fossilised lightning", are natural tubes or crusts of glass formed when lightning strikes suitable material such as sand, soil or rock. The name comes from the Latin fulgur, meaning "thunderbolt".


A sand fulgurite

Formation

A lightning bolt heats the material along its path to temperatures approaching 30,000 K, far above the melting point of silica (about 1,710 °C). As the discharge penetrates sand, it vaporises and fuses the grains along a branching channel a few centimetres wide, leaving a hollow, glass-lined tube that records the path of the bolt. Fulgurites in rock (exogenic fulgurites) form glassy crusts on surfaces struck by lightning.

Types and occurrence

Sand fulgurites are fragile tubes that can extend metres below the surface; the longest recorded specimen approached 5 metres. They are found in deserts, beaches and mountaintops worldwide. Fulgurites consist largely of the high-pressure/high-temperature silica polymorph lechatelierite and preserve information about past lightning activity and palaeoenvironmental conditions; they have been used to date periods of desert aridity.
