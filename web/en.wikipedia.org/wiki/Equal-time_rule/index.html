Equal-time rule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The equal-time rule — formally, the equal opportunities requirement of Section 315 of the Communications Act of 1934 — is a United States law requiring broadcast stations that allow a legally qualified candidate for public office to "use" their facilities to afford equal opportunities to all other candidates for the same office. It is administered by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). Despite its name, the rule does not require a station to give free time: if a candidate buys or is given time, opponents must be allowed to obtain comparable time on comparable terms.

Broadcast appearances by candidates can trigger equal-opportunity obligations for US stations.

Provisions

The requirement traces to the Radio Act of 1927 and was carried into Section 315 of the 1934 Act. Its key features:

  • Trigger: any "use" of a station by a legally qualified candidate — an appearance by the candidate, including in entertainment programming — obliges the station to offer equivalent opportunities to opposing candidates who request them within seven days.
  • No censorship: a station may not censor the content of candidate programming, and is shielded from liability for what candidates say.
  • Rates: during defined pre-election windows, candidates must be charged the station's "lowest unit rate" for comparable time.
  • Scope: the rule applies to broadcast licensees, and in modified form to cable systems; it does not apply to the internet.

Exemptions

Congress amended Section 315 in 1959 — after an FCC ruling that even brief news clips of a candidate triggered equal time — to exempt appearances in bona fide newscasts, news interviews, news documentaries (where the candidate's appearance is incidental), and coverage of bona fide news events such as debates and conventions staged by others. A 1975 FCC ruling extended protection to broadcaster-sponsored debates. These exemptions mean that most everyday political news coverage does not trigger the rule, while entertainment appearances still can: the rule has famously been implicated when candidates hosted shows such as Saturday Night Live, prompting stations to offer comparable time to rival candidates.

Distinctions

The equal-time rule is often confused with the Fairness Doctrine, a separate, repealed FCC policy (1949–1987) that required coverage of controversial issues of public importance and contrasting viewpoints; Section 315 instead concerns only candidate appearances. It is also distinct from the FCC's reasonable access rule (Section 312(a)(7)), which obliges stations to sell or give some access to federal candidates, and from the "Zapple doctrine", a quasi-equal-time requirement for supporters of candidates that the FCC discontinued applying in 2014.
