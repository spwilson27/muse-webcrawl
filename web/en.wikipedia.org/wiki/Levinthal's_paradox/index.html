Levinthal's paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Levinthal's paradox is a thought experiment in the theory of protein folding, first articulated by Cyrus Levinthal in 1969. It observes that if a protein folded by randomly sampling all possible conformations of its polypeptide chain, it would take an astronomically long time to reach its native state — far longer than the age of the universe — yet real proteins fold in milliseconds to seconds. The paradox therefore shows that proteins cannot fold by random search.

The calculation

Consider a small protein of 100 amino acid residues. If each residue can adopt roughly three conformations, the chain has on the order of 3100 ≈ 5 × 1047 possible conformations. Even sampling each in the shortest physically meaningful time (around 10−13 seconds, a molecular vibration), an exhaustive search would take some 1027 years. Levinthal himself used larger per-residue estimates, reaching figures around 10127 conformations.

Resolution

The paradox is resolved by recognising that folding is not a random search. Folding is guided by the energy landscape of the protein: local interactions form secondary structures, partial folds bias subsequent steps, and the process funnels the chain toward its low-energy native conformation. Levinthal himself proposed that proteins fold along specific pathways; modern theory describes this in terms of a funnel-shaped folding funnel on the energy landscape, often assisted in the cell by molecular chaperones. The paradox remains a standard illustration of why biased, hierarchical search processes can solve problems that brute-force search cannot.
