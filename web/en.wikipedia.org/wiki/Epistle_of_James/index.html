Epistle of James

The Epistle of James is a book of the New Testament. It is one of the seven "catholic" (general) epistles and is traditionally attributed to James the Just, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church. The letter is addressed "to the twelve tribes scattered among the nations" and consists mainly of practical exhortations on Christian conduct rather than doctrinal exposition.

Contents

Across five chapters, James teaches on enduring trials, the danger of the tongue, care for widows and orphans, warnings against favouritism toward the rich, humility, prayer, and patience. Its most famous passage insists that "faith without works is dead" (James 2:26), arguing that genuine faith necessarily expresses itself in action. The letter also contains the instruction that the sick should call the elders to pray and anoint them with oil (James 5:14), a basis for the sacrament of anointing of the sick.

Authorship and reception

Scholars debate whether the letter was written by James the Just before his death in 62 AD, or pseudonymously later in the first or early second century; its polished Greek and sparse references to Jesus are often cited in the debate. Martin Luther famously called it an "epistle of straw" because he saw its teaching on works as conflicting with Paul's doctrine of justification by faith, though modern scholarship generally reads the two authors as addressing different questions.
