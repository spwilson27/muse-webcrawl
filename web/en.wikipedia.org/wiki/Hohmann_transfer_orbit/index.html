Hohmann transfer orbit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A diagram of an elliptical Hohmann transfer orbit carrying a spacecraft from a low circular orbit to a higher one]
A diagram of an elliptical Hohmann transfer orbit carrying a spacecraft from a low circular orbit to a higher one

In Orbital mechanics, a Hohmann transfer orbit is an elliptical orbit used to transfer between two circular orbits of different radii in the same plane, using two engine impulses. Conceived by the German engineer Walter Hohmann in 1925, it is the most fuel-efficient two-impulse transfer between orbits — the workhorse manoeuvre of mission design, used from geostationary insertions to interplanetary voyages such as the Mars transfer window.

The manoeuvre

The spacecraft burns prograde at periapsis to enter an ellipse whose apoapsis touches the target orbit, coasts half an orbit, then burns again to circularise. The price of its economy is time (half the transfer ellipse's period — about 8.5 months from Earth to Mars), which is why faster, costlier trajectories are chosen for crewed or time-critical missions.

Applications and limits

Hohmann transfers govern satellite deployment (the standard route to geostationary orbit via a transfer ellipse), rendezvous planning and textbook interplanetary design; departures and arrivals add hyperbolic legs in practice. Real missions refine the ideal with plane changes (expensive at high speed), gravity assists and low-thrust spirals for electric propulsion, where the Hohmann solution does not apply.
