Satchel Paige

Leroy Robert "Satchel" Paige (July 7, 1906 – June 8, 1982) was an American baseball pitcher who spent most of his career in the Negro leagues before becoming, at about age 42, the oldest rookie in Major League Baseball history with the Cleveland Indians in 1948. Widely regarded as one of the greatest pitchers of all time, he was famed for his fastball, longevity, showmanship and aphorisms — among them "Don't look back. Something might be gaining on you."

Early life and nickname

Born in Mobile, Alabama, Paige earned his nickname as a boy carrying bags (satchels) at the railway station. After a spell in a reform school, where he learned to pitch, he turned professional in the 1920s.

Negro leagues and barnstorming

Paige starred for teams including the Birmingham Black Barons, Pittsburgh Crawfords and Kansas City Monarchs, with whom he won the 1942 Negro World Series. His barnstorming tours and exhibition duels with white stars such as Dizzy Dean and Bob Feller built a national legend; Joe DiMaggio called him the best pitcher he ever faced. He also pitched in Mexico and the Dominican Republic, where in 1937 he led the Ciudad Trujillo team assembled by dictator Rafael Trujillo.

Major League career

Signed by Bill Veeck's Cleveland Indians in July 1948, Paige went 6–1 with a 2.48 ERA and helped Cleveland win the pennant and World Series. He later pitched for the St. Louis Browns, making All-Star teams in 1952 and 1953 at about age 46, and made a final one-game appearance for the Kansas City Athletics in 1965 at around 59, pitching three scoreless innings.

Legacy

Paige was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1971, the first player inducted primarily for his Negro leagues career. His exact won-lost records remain uncertain, but his stature as a folk hero and master pitcher is secure.
