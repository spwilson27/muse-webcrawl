Hittite language

Hittite (natively nešili, "in the language of Neša") is the extinct language of the Hittites, a people of Bronze Age Anatolia who created an empire centred on Hattusa in north-central Turkey. Written in cuneiform, it is the earliest securely attested member of the Indo-European language family and the principal language of the Anatolian branch.

Discovery and decipherment

Thousands of clay tablets from Hattusa were excavated in the early twentieth century. In 1915–1917 the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný deciphered the language and demonstrated its Indo-European character, famously from a sentence containing watar ("water") and a word for eating. A large corpus of royal annals, treaties, laws, rituals and myths survives.

Classification

Hittite belongs to the Anatolian branch, together with Luwian, Palaic and later Lycian and Lydian. Anatolian preserves some very archaic features — notably consonants written ḫ that are thought to continue the "laryngeals" reconstructed for Proto-Indo-European — while lacking some categories, such as a distinct feminine gender, found in other branches.

Grammar

Hittite is an inflected language with two genders (common and neuter), two numbers (singular and plural), and a case system including nominative, accusative, genitive, dative-locative, ablative and instrumental. Verbs conjugate in present and preterite tenses, active and middle voices, with two conjugation classes (the mi- and hi-conjugations). Word order is typically subject–object–verb, and the language makes extensive use of sentence-connecting particles.

History

Scholars distinguish Old Hittite (c. 1650–1500 BCE), Middle Hittite (c. 1500–1430) and New Hittite (c. 1430–1180). After the fall of the empire around 1180 BCE, the language died out as a written medium, though related Luwian dialects persisted in the Neo-Hittite states of Syria and southeastern Anatolia.
