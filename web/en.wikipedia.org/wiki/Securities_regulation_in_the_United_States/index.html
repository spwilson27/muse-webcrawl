Securities regulation in the United States

Securities regulation in the United States is the field of law and regulation governing the issuance and trading of securities — stocks, bonds, and other investment instruments — with the aims of protecting investors, maintaining fair and efficient markets, and facilitating capital formation. The principal federal regulator is the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).

History

Before the federal system, regulation existed mainly at the state level through so-called "blue sky laws," the first of which was enacted by Kansas in 1911. The stock market crash of 1929 and the ensuing Great Depression exposed widespread abuses and led Congress to enact the foundational federal statutes during the New Deal.

Principal federal statutes

  • Securities Act of 1933 — governs the initial issuance of securities, requiring registration and disclosure through a prospectus.
  • Securities Exchange Act of 1934 — regulates secondary trading, exchanges, and broker-dealers, and created the SEC.
  • Trust Indenture Act of 1939 — regulates debt securities and indentures.
  • Investment Company Act of 1940 and Investment Advisers Act of 1940 — regulate mutual funds and investment advisers.
  • Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 — enacted after the Enron and WorldCom scandals, it tightened corporate governance and accounting oversight, creating the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board.
  • Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 — a broad financial reform following the 2008 financial crisis, extending regulation to derivatives and systemic risk.

Regulatory structure

The SEC oversees public companies, securities exchanges, broker-dealers, and investment advisers. Self-regulatory organizations such as FINRA operate under SEC supervision. State regulators continue to enforce blue sky laws alongside the federal regime. The New Deal statutes define "security" broadly, and the Supreme Court's Howey test determines whether an arrangement is an "investment contract" subject to regulation.
