Plate glass university

The modernist architecture of the plate glass universities, such as the University of East Anglia, gave the group its name.

Plate glass universities are a group of universities in the United Kingdom established or promoted during the 1960s. The term derives from their modern architectural design, which often used wide expanses of plate glass in steel or concrete frames, contrasting with the (mainly Victorian) red-brick universities and the older universities of Oxford and Cambridge. The term was coined by Michael Beloff in his 1968 book The Plate Glass Universities.

The universities

The institutions most often included are the universities founded following the recommendations of the Robbins Report of 1963 and the expansion policy of the University Grants Committee: the University of Sussex (chartered 1961), the University of East Anglia (1963), the University of York (1963), the University of Essex (1964), the University of Kent (1965), the University of Lancaster (1964), and the University of Warwick (1965), together with the University of Stirling (1967) in Scotland and the New University of Ulster (1968). Keele University (founded 1949, chartered 1962) is sometimes treated as a forerunner.

Characteristics

The plate glass universities were planned as campus institutions, often on greenfield sites at the edges of historic cities, with buildings by leading modernist architects — for example Denys Lasdun at East Anglia, with its distinctive ziggurats. Academically they were innovative, introducing interdisciplinary schools of study, modular courses, American-style majors and minors, and new subjects such as environmental sciences, development studies and film studies. Several quickly acquired strong reputations; Warwick in particular developed close links with business and industry.
