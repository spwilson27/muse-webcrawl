Phylogeny

A phylogenetic tree representing the evolutionary relationships among organisms.

A phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms, and phylogenetics is the study of relationships among groups of organisms based on their evolutionary history. These relationships are inferred from molecular sequencing data and morphological data, and are commonly represented as a branching diagram called a phylogenetic tree.

Methods

Traditional phylogenetics relied on morphological characters — the presence or absence of structures, their shapes and development — to group organisms. Since the late 20th century, molecular phylogenetics has dominated, using DNA, RNA and protein sequences. Sequences are aligned, and trees are estimated using methods such as maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference.

The resulting groupings ideally reflect common descent: a monophyletic group (clade) contains an ancestor and all of its descendants, whereas paraphyletic and polyphyletic groupings do not. The classification system developed by Willi Hennig, called cladistics or phylogenetic systematics, requires taxa to be clades.

History

The term "phylogeny" derives from the German Phylogenie, introduced by Ernst Haeckel in 1866, who also popularised the idea that "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny" — a notion now considered an oversimplification. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by common descent provided the theoretical basis for phylogenetic trees; Darwin's only figure in On the Origin of Species (1859) is a branching diagram of descent.

Applications

Phylogenies underpin biological classification, and are used in fields from ecology and conservation to epidemiology (tracking the spread of viruses), forensics and the study of language evolution by analogy.
