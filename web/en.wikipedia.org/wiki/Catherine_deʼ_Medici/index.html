Catherine de' Medici (Italian: Caterina de' Medici; 13 April 1519 – 5 January 1589) was an Italian noblewoman of the Medici family who became Queen of France as the wife of King Henry II. As the mother of three French kings — Francis II, Charles IX, and Henry III — she exercised great influence during their reigns, acting as regent for Charles IX and remaining a dominant figure at court through the French Wars of Religion.

Early life and marriage

Catherine was born in Florence, the only child of Lorenzo II de' Medici, Duke of Urbino, and Madeleine de la Tour d'Auvergne. Both parents died within weeks of her birth, and she was raised by relatives and in convents; during the siege of Florence in 1529–1530 the orphaned girl was held hostage by the republican government that had expelled her family. In 1533, at the age of fourteen, she was married to Henry, Duke of Orléans, the second son of King Francis I, in a match arranged by her great-uncle Pope Clement VII, who died the following year — leaving Catherine, in the eyes of the French court, a bride whose connections had lost much of their value.

For the first decade of the marriage Catherine produced no children, and her position was precarious, especially as Henry's devotion to his mistress Diane de Poitiers was public knowledge. After consulting physicians — the couple are traditionally said to have been helped by the court doctor Jean Fernel — she bore the first of ten children in 1544. When Henry became king in 1547, Catherine was crowned queen consort, though real influence at court lay with Diane and the great noble factions.

Regency and power

Henry II died in 1559 from a jousting wound, and was succeeded by the sickly Francis II, husband of Mary, Queen of Scots; real power lay with Mary's uncles, the Guise family. When Francis died in 1560, Catherine became regent for her ten-year-old son Charles IX and moved to the center of power, which she retained, in varying degrees, for the rest of her life. Seeking to preserve the monarchy amid the rivalry of the Catholic Guise faction and the Protestant (Huguenot) leadership of the Bourbon and Coligny families, she pursued a policy of compromise, issuing the Edict of January 1562, which granted limited toleration to Protestants, and later the Peace of Amboise (1563).

Wars of Religion and St. Bartholomew's Day

Compromise repeatedly failed, and from 1562 France descended into a series of civil wars between Catholics and Huguenots. Catherine negotiated marriages to secure the dynasty, most famously wedding her daughter Margaret to the Protestant Henry of Navarre in August 1572. Days after the wedding, following a failed assassination attempt on the Huguenot leader Admiral Coligny, the court — with Catherine's involvement, whose extent historians still debate — sanctioned the killing of the Huguenot leadership then gathered in Paris. The killings spiraled into the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre, in which thousands of Protestants were murdered in Paris and the provinces. The massacre fixed Catherine's dark reputation in Protestant historiography, where she was long portrayed as an Italian poisoner and disciple of Machiavelli.

Later years and legacy

Charles IX died in 1574 and was succeeded by Catherine's favorite son, Henry III, for whom she continued to travel, negotiate, and govern during his absences and illnesses. She died at Blois on 5 January 1589, weeks after the assassination of the Duke of Guise on Henry's orders and months before Henry himself was assassinated, ending the Valois line. Modern historians generally assess Catherine more sympathetically than the "black legend" allowed: she ruled in defense of her sons' inheritance amid confessional violence, factionalism, and fiscal crisis that no policy could easily master.

Catherine was also one of the great patrons of the arts in sixteenth-century France. She built and embellished palaces including the Tuileries, staged magnificent court festivals and ballet de cour — the Ballet Comique de la Reine (1581) is a landmark in the history of ballet — and is credited in popular tradition with introducing Italian culinary and sartorial refinements to France, though many such attributions are exaggerated.
