Radial nerve

From: Posterior cord (C5–T1)
Innervates: Triceps brachii, brachioradialis, extensors of wrist and fingers, supinator, anconeus
To: Superficial branch, deep branch / posterior interosseous nerve

The radial nerve is a nerve in the human body that supplies the posterior portion of the upper limb. It arises from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, with fibres from spinal roots C5 to T1, and is the largest branch of the plexus.

Course and function

The nerve descends behind the axillary and brachial arteries, winds around the humerus in the radial (spiral) groove, pierces the lateral intermuscular septum, and divides near the elbow into a superficial sensory branch and a deep motor branch (the posterior interosseous nerve). It supplies the triceps brachii, anconeus, brachioradialis and the extensor muscles of the forearm, and provides sensation to much of the posterior arm and forearm and the dorsum of the lateral hand.

Clinical significance

The nerve is vulnerable where it lies against the humeral shaft. Compression in the spiral groove causes "Saturday night palsy" — typically after sleeping with the arm draped over a chair — producing wrist drop and loss of finger extension with relatively preserved triceps function. Mid-shaft humeral fractures can also injure the nerve. Most compression injuries recover spontaneously over weeks to months.
