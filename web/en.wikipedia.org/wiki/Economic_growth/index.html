Economic growth

[Image: a construction skyline with cranes over a growing city at dawn]

Economic growth is an increase in the production of goods and services in an economy over time, conventionally measured as the percentage rate of increase in real GDP (or GDP per capita). Sustained growth, historically recent — beginning with the Industrial Revolution — compounds: at 2% a year incomes double in about 35 years, turning poor societies rich within lifetimes.

Sources and theory

Growth comes from accumulating factors (capital, labour, human capital), from better technology and organisation, and from institutions that make investment worthwhile. The Solow model (1956) showed capital accumulation alone runs into diminishing returns, leaving technological progress as the engine of long-run per-capita growth; endogenous growth theory (Romer, Lucas) explains innovation itself through research, education and incentives. Developing countries may grow faster by adopting existing technology — the convergence hypothesis — though many remain in a 'middle-income trap'. Growth's benefits and limits are debated: it correlates with longer lives, literacy and falling Poverty, while raising questions of distribution, resource use and climate — the degrowth and green-growth debates of macroeconomics.
