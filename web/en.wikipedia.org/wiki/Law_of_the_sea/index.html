Law of the sea

The law of the sea is a body of international law governing the rights and duties of states in their use of the world's oceans. Its modern foundation is the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), concluded in 1982 and in force since 1994, often described as a "constitution of the oceans".

Maritime zones defined by UNCLOS.

Maritime zones

UNCLOS defines a series of zones measured from coastal baselines: internal waters, a territorial sea of up to 12 nautical miles over which the coastal state has sovereignty, a contiguous zone to 24 nautical miles, an exclusive economic zone (EEZ) of up to 200 nautical miles in which the coastal state has sovereign rights to resources, and the continental shelf. Beyond national jurisdiction lie the high seas, open to all states, and the deep seabed ("the Area"), whose resources are the common heritage of mankind.

History

The doctrine of freedom of the seas, articulated by Hugo Grotius in Mare Liberum (1609), dominated for centuries before the expansion of coastal-state claims in the 20th century led to the codification conferences of 1958, 1960, and 1973–1982.
