Cardiac tamponade
SpecialtyCardiology; emergency medicine
SymptomsShortness of breath, weakness, lightheadedness, chest pain

In tamponade, fluid in the pericardial sac compresses the heart and prevents it from filling.

Cardiac tamponade is a medical emergency in which fluid accumulating in the pericardium — the sac surrounding the heart — compresses the heart and prevents it from filling properly, causing a sharp drop in cardiac output and obstructive shock. It may develop rapidly, as in trauma or aortic dissection, or gradually, as with malignant or uraemic effusions.

Signs and symptoms

The classic Beck's triad consists of low blood pressure, distended jugular veins, and muffled heart sounds. Other features include shortness of breath, tachycardia, and pulsus paradoxus, an abnormally large fall in systolic blood pressure (over 10 mmHg) during inspiration. On electrocardiography, large effusions may show electrical alternans; chest X-ray can show an enlarged, globular cardiac silhouette.

Causes

Causes include metastatic cancer, pericarditis (infectious, autoimmune or post-myocardial infarction), uraemia, hypothyroidism, chest trauma, rupture of the heart or aorta, and complications of cardiac procedures or surgery.

Diagnosis and treatment

Echocardiography is the key diagnostic test, showing the effusion together with diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle and exaggerated respiratory variation in blood flow. Definitive treatment is drainage of the fluid, usually by ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis with a needle inserted below the sternum; recurrent or loculated effusions may require a surgical pericardial window. Intravenous fluids can temporarily support blood pressure while drainage is arranged.
