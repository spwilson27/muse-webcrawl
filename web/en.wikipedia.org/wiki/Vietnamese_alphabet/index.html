The Vietnamese alphabet (chữ quốc ngữ, "national language script") is the modern writing system for the Vietnamese language, based on the Latin script with additional diacritics. It uses 29 letters and a rich system of diacritical marks to indicate vowel quality and the six tones of Vietnamese.

Vietnamese text illustrating the alphabet's multiple stacked diacritics.

History

Vietnamese was historically written with Chinese characters (chữ Hán) and the indigenous derivative script chữ Nôm. In the 17th century, Catholic missionaries—notably the Portuguese Francisco de Pina and the French Jesuit Alexandre de Rhodes, who published the first Vietnamese–Portuguese–Latin dictionary in 1651—developed a Latin-based orthography for evangelization. Under French colonial rule the script was promoted in administration and education, and in the 20th century it became universal, credited with aiding mass literacy campaigns.

Letters and diacritics

The alphabet's 29 letters are: a, ă, â, b, c, d, đ, e, ê, g, h, i, k, l, m, n, o, ô, ơ, p, q, r, s, t, u, ư, v, x, y. Letters f, j, w, and z appear only in loanwords. Seven letters are modified by diacritics (ă, â, đ, ê, ô, ơ, ư), and five tone marks—grave (huyền), acute (sắc), hook above (hỏi), tilde (ngã), and dot below (nặng), plus the unmarked ngang tone—can stack on vowels, as in the word ệ. Digraphs and trigraphs such as ch, gh, ng/ngh, ph, th, and tr represent single consonant sounds.
