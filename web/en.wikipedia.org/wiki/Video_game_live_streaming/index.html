Video game live streaming is the practice of broadcasting oneself playing a video game in real time over the internet to a live audience, typically accompanied by commentary and interaction with viewers through chat. It emerged as a major entertainment medium in the early 2010s and became a central part of video game culture, with top streamers attracting audiences comparable to television broadcasts.

A streamer at a typical broadcasting setup with microphone, camera, and gameplay capture.

History

The roots of streaming lie in earlier "let's play" videos and early streaming sites. Justin.tv, launched in 2007, hosted gaming broadcasts before spinning off its gaming section as Twitch in 2011. Twitch grew rapidly, and Amazon acquired it in 2014 for approximately $970 million. Competing services followed, including YouTube Gaming, Microsoft's Mixer (closed in 2020), and later Kick. By the late 2010s, major esports finals and individual streamers regularly drew concurrent audiences in the millions.

Formats and streamers

Streaming formats include casual playthroughs, competitive and professional play, speedrunning, game development streams, and "just chatting" sessions. Personalities such as Ninja (Tyler Blevins), who rose to mainstream fame playing Fortnite in 2018, demonstrated the celebrity potential of the medium. VTubers, who perform through animated avatars, became a significant subgenre, particularly in Japan and later worldwide.

Economics

Streamers earn revenue from channel subscriptions, viewer donations and tips, advertising, sponsorships, and merchandise. Platforms typically take a share of subscription revenue. The largest streamers sign exclusivity deals worth millions of dollars, while the vast majority of broadcasters stream to small audiences as a hobby.

Impact

Live streaming reshaped game marketing, with developers designing games for watchability and paying streamers for launch coverage. It also influenced game design through audience-participation experiments such as Twitch Plays Pokémon (2014), in which thousands of viewers collectively controlled a single game.
