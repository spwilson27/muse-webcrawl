Natalism

Natalism (also called pronatalism or the pro-birth position) is an ideology or policy position that promotes the reproduction of human life as an important objective of being alive, and high birth rates more generally. Natalism in public policy typically seeks to create financial and social incentives for populations to reproduce, such as offering tax incentives that reward having and supporting children.

Motives

Pro-natalist positions arise from several motives:

  • Religious – many religions encourage procreation; the Catholic Church, for example, promotes the bearing of children within marriage, and the biblical command to "be fruitful and multiply" is cited by several faiths.
  • Demographic and economic – governments facing below-replacement fertility and ageing populations adopt pro-natalist policies to sustain their workforces, pension systems, and ethnic or national population shares.
  • Philosophical – the view, discussed in population ethics, that bringing happy lives into existence is good.

Policy examples

Historical and modern examples include France's family allowances and politique familiale; the Soviet Union's "Mother Heroine" awards; Singapore's shift from "Stop at Two" to incentives for larger families; Hungary's extensive family tax benefits introduced in the 2010s; and measures such as paid parental leave, subsidised childcare, and "baby bonuses" in many European and East Asian states responding to very low fertility rates. In the 2020s, pronatalist advocacy also became a visible movement among technology and political figures concerned about population decline.

Opposition

Natalism is opposed by antinatalism, which holds that coming into existence is a harm, and is criticised by some environmentalists and feminists who argue that policies should prioritise reproductive autonomy and that population growth has ecological costs.
