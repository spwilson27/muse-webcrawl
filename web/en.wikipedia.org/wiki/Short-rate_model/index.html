Short-rate model

A short-rate model, in mathematical finance, describes the evolution of interest rates through the dynamics of the instantaneous spot rate r(t) — the "short rate" — typically as a stochastic differential equation. From the model, prices of bonds and interest-rate derivatives are derived. Short-rate models contrast with forward-rate frameworks such as the Heath–Jarrow–Morton model, which model the entire yield curve directly.

One-factor models

In the Vasicek model (1977), the short rate follows a mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, giving closed-form bond prices but allowing negative rates. The Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model (1985) uses a square-root diffusion that keeps rates non-negative. The Hull–White model adds a time-dependent drift so the model fits the observed initial term structure exactly. Other classic models include Rendleman–Bartter, Dothan, and Black–Derman–Toy, the latter a binomial-tree model used widely in practice.

Multi-factor and market models

Multi-factor models add further state variables to capture yield-curve twists. Because short-rate models imply a specific initial curve (or are calibrated to it), alternatives such as the LIBOR market model (Brace–Gątarek–Musiela) became popular for pricing caps and swaptions in terms of observable forward rates.

Calibration

Model parameters are estimated from historical data or calibrated to market prices of liquid instruments; the two approaches can give different parameters because the models are specified under the risk-neutral measure for pricing.
