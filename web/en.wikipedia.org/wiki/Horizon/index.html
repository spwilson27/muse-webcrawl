Overview

The horizon is the line where Earth's surface appears to meet the sky. For an observer standing at sea level it lies only about 5 kilometres away — a direct consequence of the planet's curvature. From higher up, the horizon recedes: the distance in kilometres is roughly 3.57 times the square root of the observer's height in metres, so from an airliner at 10,000 metres the horizon is some 350 km away.

The progressive disappearance of ships hull-first over the horizon was one of the classic everyday proofs of Earth's sphericity, known since antiquity.

In astronomy and navigation

Astronomers distinguish the visible horizon from the true (celestial) horizon, the plane through Earth's centre parallel to the observer's horizon; altitudes of stars are measured from it, with a small correction — the dip of the horizon — for the observer's height. Navigators taking celestial sights with a sextant apply the same dip correction, and allow for refraction, which bends light and lifts the apparent horizon slightly.

The polar circles are defined by the Sun's behaviour at the horizon: on the Arctic Circle the Sun just touches the horizon at midnight on the June solstice without setting.
