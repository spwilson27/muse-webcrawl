Indian Gaming Regulatory Act

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA; Pub. L. 100-497, 25 U.S.C. §§ 2701–2721) is a 1988 United States federal law that establishes the jurisdictional framework governing gambling on Indian reservations. It was enacted following the Supreme Court's decision in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians (1987), which held that states could not regulate gaming on tribal lands where the state permitted similar gaming.

Classes of gaming

IGRA divides gaming into three classes. Class I covers traditional and social games under exclusive tribal jurisdiction. Class II covers bingo and similar games, regulated by tribes with oversight by the National Indian Gaming Commission, which the Act created. Class III covers casino-style gaming such as slot machines and blackjack, permitted only under a tribal–state compact negotiated with the state in which the tribe's lands lie.

Impact

The Act provided the legal foundation for the growth of tribal gaming into an industry generating tens of billions of dollars in annual revenue, funding tribal government services, infrastructure and economic diversification, while remaining a subject of debate over sovereignty, revenue sharing and the scope of state negotiating obligations.
