Inverse limit

In mathematics, the inverse limit (also called the projective limit) is a construction that allows one to "glue together" several related objects, the precise gluing process being specified by morphisms between the objects. Inverse limits can be defined in any category, although their existence depends on the category in question.

The inverse limit is a special case of the concept of a limit in category theory. By duality, the inverse limit is the dual notion of a direct limit.

Definition

An inverse system consists of a collection of objects indexed by a directed set, together with morphisms (called bonding maps) from each object to objects with smaller indices, satisfying natural compatibility conditions. The inverse limit of such a system, when it exists, is an object together with morphisms to each object of the system, universal among all such cones.

lim← Xi = { (xi) ∈ ∏ Xi : xi = fij(xj) whenever i ≤ j }

In the category of sets, the inverse limit is realised as the subset of the Cartesian product consisting of all "compatible sequences".

Examples

  • The ring of p-adic integers is the inverse limit of the rings Z/pnZ with the natural projection maps.
  • The profinite completion of a group is an inverse limit of finite quotient groups.
  • A solenoid in topology is the inverse limit of circles under repeated covering maps.

Derived functors

For inverse systems of abelian groups indexed by the natural numbers, the inverse limit functor is left exact but not right exact; its first derived functor, written lim1, measures the failure of exactness and vanishes for systems satisfying the Mittag-Leffler condition.
