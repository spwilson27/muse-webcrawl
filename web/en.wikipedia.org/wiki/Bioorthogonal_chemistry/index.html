Bioorthogonal chemistry

A bioorthogonal "click" reaction labels a target biomolecule without disturbing the cell.

The term bioorthogonal chemistry refers to any chemical reaction that can occur inside of living systems without interfering with native biochemical processes. The term was coined by Carolyn Bertozzi in 2003. Bioorthogonal reactions enable the study of biomolecules such as glycans, proteins and lipids in real time in living systems without cellular toxicity.

Requirements

A bioorthogonal reaction must proceed under physiological conditions (aqueous medium, near-neutral pH, body temperature), be selective for its introduced functional groups over the thousands of native groups in a cell, give high yields at low concentrations, and use reagents that are non-toxic and metabolically stable.

Key reactions

The field began with the Staudinger ligation, in which an azide reacts with an engineered phosphine. Bertozzi's group then developed strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC), a copper-free version of click chemistry in which a strained cyclooctyne reacts rapidly with an azide — avoiding the toxicity of the copper catalyst used in the classic CuAAC reaction. Later additions include inverse electron-demand Diels–Alder reactions between tetrazines and strained alkenes, among the fastest bioorthogonal reactions known.

Applications

Bioorthogonal chemistry is used to image glycans and other biomolecules in cells and whole animals, to profile newly synthesised proteins, for activity-based protein profiling, in targeted drug delivery and antibody–drug conjugates, and in pretargeted radioimmunotherapy. Bertozzi shared the 2022 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Morten Meldal and K. Barry Sharpless for the development of click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry.
