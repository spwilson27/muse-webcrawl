Helen of Troy, also known as Helen of Sparta, is a figure in Greek mythology said to have been the most beautiful woman in the world. She was believed to have been the daughter of Zeus and Leda, and was the sister of Clytemnestra, Castor and Polydeuces (Pollux), and Philonoe. She was married to King Menelaus of Sparta, but eloped (or was abducted) with Prince Paris of Troy, resulting in the Trojan War when the Achaeans set out to reclaim her. Helene in Greek means "torch" or "corposant".

Mythology

Birth

In most accounts Helen was the daughter of Zeus, who, in the form of a swan, seduced or assaulted her mother Leda. Helen was hatched from an egg. Her brothers Castor and Pollux, the Dioscuri, were likewise born of Leda, one mortal and one divine.

Suitors and marriage

When Helen came of age, princes from all over Greece sought her hand. Her stepfather Tyndareus, fearing violence among the rejected suitors, made them all swear an oath to defend Helen's chosen husband against any who quarrelled with him. Menelaus, brother of Agamemnon, was chosen (or chose) and married Helen, becoming king of Sparta on Tyndareus's abdication.

Abduction by Paris

Paris, son of King Priam of Troy, came to Sparta under the judgement of Paris: Aphrodite had promised him the most beautiful woman in the world as a bribe for awarding her the golden apple of discord. Helen left Sparta with Paris — whether willingly, seduced, or abducted varies by account — taking much of Menelaus's treasure. Her departure caused the Trojan War.

The Trojan War

Menelaus invoked the oath of Tyndareus, and the Greek princes sailed to Troy to demand Helen's return. The war lasted ten years. Helen, watching the battles from the walls of Troy, identified the Greek heroes for Priam. After Paris's death she was married to his brother Deiphobus. At Troy's fall, Menelaus reclaimed her; despite intending to kill her, he was overcome by her beauty and took her back to Sparta, where, in the Odyssey, she appears presiding over Menelaus's court, having received the drug nepenthe in Egypt.

Cult and legacy

Helen was worshipped as a goddess in Sparta and elsewhere; her cult was associated with beauty, vegetation, and seafaring (as sister of the Dioscuri, protectors of sailors). Her beauty became proverbial: Christopher Marlowe's Doctor Faustus calls her "the face that launched a thousand ships." She remains one of the most depicted figures of classical myth in art and literature.
