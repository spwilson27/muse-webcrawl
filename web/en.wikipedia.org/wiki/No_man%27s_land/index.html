No man's land

No man's land is land that is unoccupied or is under dispute between parties who leave it unoccupied out of fear or uncertainty. The term is especially associated with the contested ground between opposing trenches in the First World War.

History of the term

The phrase dates to medieval England, where "nonesmanneslond" referred to land outside the north wall of London used for executions. It was used for disputed borderlands and, in the 1900s, adopted for military ground between front lines.

First World War

On the Western Front, no man's land ranged from a few dozen metres to several hundred metres wide, cratered by shells and crossed by barbed wire. Soldiers feared crossing it under machine-gun fire; the Christmas truce of 1914 famously saw troops meet in no man's land.

Other uses

The term also applies to places such as the Korean Demilitarized Zone, the former inner German border strip, and Bir Tawil between Egypt and Sudan. In everyday speech it denotes any in-between or neglected space, and in sport a disputed or unclaimed area of play.
