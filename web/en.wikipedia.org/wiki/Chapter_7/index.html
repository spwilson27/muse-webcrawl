Chapter 7

Chapter 7 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, titled "Liquidation", is the most common form of bankruptcy in the United States. In a Chapter 7 case, a trustee is appointed to collect and sell the debtor's non-exempt assets and distribute the proceeds to creditors, after which most remaining debts of an individual debtor are discharged. Businesses also use Chapter 7 when ceasing operations, though corporations receive no discharge.

Process

The case begins with a petition, schedules of assets and liabilities, and a statement of financial affairs. Filing imposes an automatic stay stopping most collection activity. A trustee reviews the case, presides over the meeting of creditors (the "341 meeting"), and liquidates any assets not protected by federal or state exemptions — in practice most individual cases are "no-asset" cases in which unsecured creditors receive nothing. An individual debtor typically receives a discharge a few months after filing.

Eligibility and the means test

Since the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005, individual debtors with primarily consumer debts must pass a means test: if their income exceeds the state median and they are deemed able to repay a meaningful amount, the case may be dismissed or converted to Chapter 13. An individual cannot receive a Chapter 7 discharge if they received one in a case filed within the previous eight years.

Non-dischargeable debts

Certain obligations survive Chapter 7, including most taxes, domestic support obligations, most student loans, debts incurred by fraud, and debts for death or personal injury caused by drunk driving.
