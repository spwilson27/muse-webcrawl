Hōne Heke

Hōne Heke Pōkai
Born: c. 1807/1808, Pakaraka, New Zealand
Died: 7 August 1850, Kaikohe
Iwi: Ngāpuhi
Known for: First signatory of the Treaty of Waitangi; cutting down the flagstaff at Kororāreka

Hōne Heke Pōkai (c. 1807/1808 – 7 August 1850) was a Māori chief of the Ngāpuhi iwi in northern New Zealand and a leading figure of the early colonial period. Educated at the Church Missionary Society school at Kerikeri, he was baptized a Christian and became literate in te reo Māori. He was the first chief to sign the Treaty of Waitangi on 6 February 1840 — yet within four years he led armed resistance against the Crown in the conflict known as the Flagstaff War.

The flagstaff

Heke grew disillusioned as British authority expanded, Māori trade declined, and the capital moved from Kororāreka (Russell) to Auckland. Viewing the Union Jack flying on Maiki Hill as a symbol of Māori subjugation — and contrasting it with his understanding of the Treaty as a partnership — he had the flagstaff cut down in July 1844. The government re-erected it; Heke cut it down again in January 1845, a third time days later, and a fourth time on 11 March 1845 in an attack on Kororāreka that began the Northern War (Flagstaff War), fought alongside his ally Te Ruki Kawiti.

The Northern War

Heke and Kawiti proved formidable opponents in the bush warfare that followed, fighting at Puketutu, Ōhaeawai — where Māori trench-and-bunker defenses inflicted heavy casualties on British troops — and Ruapekapeka in January 1846. Heke was badly wounded at Ōhaeawai. Though the war ended without a clear British victory, Heke afterwards made peace, famously writing to Governor Grey that God made this country for us and it cannot be taken by the sword.

Legacy

Heke died of tuberculosis in 1850 at Kaikohe. He remains a potent symbol of Māori assertion of tino rangatiratanga (sovereignty) and of the contested meaning of the Treaty of Waitangi. The flagstaff at Kororāreka still flies the United Tribes flag and the Union Jack, and a replacement staff was later gifted by his descendants.
