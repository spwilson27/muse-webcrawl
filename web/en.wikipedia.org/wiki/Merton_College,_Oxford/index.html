Merton College (in full: House or College of Scholars of Merton in the University of Oxford) is one of the constituent colleges of the University of Oxford in England. Its foundation can be traced to the 1260s when Walter de Merton, chancellor to Henry III and later to Edward I, first drew up statutes for an independent academic community and established endowments to support it. The college statutes were finalised in 1274.

History

Merton claims to be the oldest college in Oxford or Cambridge; the claim is contested, but Merton's statutes of 1264 are the earliest clearly establishing a self-governing college on the modern model, and its Mob Quadrangle is the oldest quadrangle in either university. The college library, dating from 1373, is the oldest library in England still in continuous use.

In the 14th century Merton was associated with the "Merton Calculators", a group of scholars including Thomas Bradwardine and William Heytesbury who pioneered mathematical approaches to physics. During the English Civil War, Merton was the only Oxford college to side with Parliament, and its buildings were requisitioned for Charles I's court.

People

Notable Mertonians include William Harvey, John Wycliffe (a fellow), T. S. Eliot, J. R. R. Tolkien (Merton Professor of English Language and Literature), Frederick Soddy, and several Nobel laureates. Women were first admitted in 1979.

Traditions

Merton holds an annual Time Ceremony, in which members walk backwards around Fellows' Quadrangle drinking port while the clocks change, jokingly to maintain the space-time continuum.
