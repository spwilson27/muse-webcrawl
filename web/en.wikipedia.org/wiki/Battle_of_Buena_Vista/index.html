Battle of Buena Vista

The Battle of Buena Vista (23 February 1847), known in Mexico as the Battle of Angostura, was a major battle of the Mexican–American War. A much-reduced United States force of about 4,700 men, mostly volunteers under General Zachary Taylor, defended a strong position in the mountain pass of La Angostura near the hacienda of Buena Vista, south of Saltillo, against a Mexican army of some 15,000–20,000 under General Antonio López de Santa Anna. After two days of severe fighting, Santa Anna withdrew during the night of 23–24 February. The victory made Taylor a national hero in the United States and helped propel him to the presidency in 1848.

Background

After taking Monterrey in September 1846, Taylor's Army of Occupation was stripped of most of its regulars for Winfield Scott's expedition against Veracruz. Learning of this, Santa Anna raised a large army at San Luis Potosí and marched north across the desert — losing thousands to hardship en route — to crush Taylor before Scott could threaten Mexico City. Taylor advanced from Saltillo and occupied the Angostura pass on the advice of his engineers, including Captain Robert E. Lee, whose reconnaissance helped select the position.

Battle

Skirmishing began on 22 February; Santa Anna, whose demand for surrender Taylor refused ("Double your files and give them to General Taylor" anecdotes aside), launched his main assault on the morning of 23 February. Mexican columns turned the American left and at points broke through toward Buena Vista itself, threatening Taylor's supply depot and hospitals, but US artillery — notably the batteries of Braxton Bragg, Thomas Sherman and George Thomas — and timely counterattacks by the Mississippi Rifles under Jefferson Davis and Indiana and Arkansas volunteers restored the line. The famous, probably apocryphal, order "Give them a little more grape, Captain Bragg" entered American folklore. With his men exhausted and short of food, Santa Anna retreated south under cover of darkness, claiming victory in his dispatches but abandoning the campaign.

Aftermath

Buena Vista was the last major battle in northern Mexico; Taylor's army remained on the defensive thereafter while Scott's campaign decided the war. The battle cemented the reputations of many officers who later became prominent in the American Civil War, and it remains one of the most studied engagements of the war in Mexican historiography, where the near-success at Angostura is emphasised.
