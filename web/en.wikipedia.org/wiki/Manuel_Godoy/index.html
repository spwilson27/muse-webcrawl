Manuel de Godoy y Álvarez de Faria (12 May 1767 – 4 October 1851) was the First Secretary of State (chief minister) of Spain from 1792 to 1797 and again from 1801 to 1808. A favourite (valido) of King Charles IV and Queen María Luisa, he accumulated enormous power and unpopularity, and received the title Prince of the Peace (Príncipe de la Paz) after negotiating the Peace of Basel with revolutionary France in 1795.

Rise to power

Born to a minor noble family in Badajoz, Godoy joined the Royal Bodyguard and came to the attention of the future Charles IV and María Luisa. After Charles became king in 1788, Godoy's advancement was meteoric: by 1792, aged 25, he was chief minister. He was made Duke of Alcudia and married María Teresa de Borbón, a cousin of the king, in 1797.

Government and fall

Godoy directed Spain's policy through the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars: war with France (1793–1795), then alliance with France by the Treaty of San Ildefonso (1796), war with Britain, and victory alongside France in the War of the Oranges against Portugal (1801). In 1807 he signed the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau with Napoleon, allowing French troops to enter Spain to partition Portugal — the prelude to Napoleon's occupation of Spain itself. The resulting scandal provoked the Tumult of Aranjuez in March 1808, in which a mob sacked Godoy's palace; he was dismissed and imprisoned, while Charles IV abdicated. Godoy spent the rest of his life in exile, dying in Paris in 1851.

Legacy

Godoy remains one of the most reviled figures in Spanish history, symbolising court favouritism and the disasters that opened the Peninsular War, though historians have also credited him with administrative and cultural patronage, including support for Goya.
