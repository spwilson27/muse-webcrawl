Japanese embassy hostage crisis

The Japanese embassy hostage crisis began on 17 December 1996 in Lima, Peru, when 14 members of the Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement (MRTA) seized the residence of the Japanese ambassador during a celebration of Emperor Akihito's birthday, taking hundreds of diplomats, officials and businesspeople hostage. The siege lasted 126 days — one of the longest hostage crises of the modern era — and ended on 22 April 1997 when Peruvian special forces stormed the residence through secretly dug tunnels, rescuing 71 of the 72 remaining hostages. All 14 hostage-takers, one hostage and two commandos were killed.

The seizure

On the evening of 17 December 1996, MRTA guerrillas led by Néstor Cerpa Cartolini blasted through a wall adjoining the residence during a reception attended by some 600 guests, including the Japanese ambassador Morihisa Aoki, Peruvian ministers, generals and the mother and brother of President Alberto Fujimori. The MRTA demanded the release of imprisoned comrades (some 400–450 prisoners), safe passage to the jungle, and a change in the government's neoliberal economic policies.

The siege

Over the following weeks the guerrillas released most hostages — women, the elderly and eventually all but 72 men deemed most valuable. Negotiations, mediated at times by the Red Cross's Michel Minnig, the Catholic archbishop Juan Luis Cipriani and Canadian ambassador Anthony Vincent (himself a released hostage), deadlocked: Fujimori refused to free MRTA prisoners, fearing a precedent, though his government quietly prepared a military solution. Hostages communicated with security forces by hidden radios and notes, reporting the captors' routines; authorities played loud military music and drilled troops nearby to mask the sound of tunnel-digging.

Operation Chavín de Huántar

On 22 April 1997, 140 Peruvian commandos assaulted the residence through tunnels dug beneath it — the operation named for the ancient Chavín de Huántar site, famous for its underground galleries. Explosions and a rapid assault caught the MRTA off guard during their daily indoor football game; within minutes the residence was secured. One hostage, Supreme Court justice Carlos Giusti, died of a heart attack aggravated by a leg wound; two commandos were killed; all 14 guerrillas died, some, according to later investigations and testimony, possibly after surrendering — allegations that led to prolonged legal proceedings in Peru and review by the Inter-American human rights system.

Aftermath

The successful rescue dramatically boosted Fujimori's popularity and effectively destroyed the MRTA as an organisation. The operation is still studied by special forces worldwide, and a replica of the residence built for training survives; the original building was later demolished and the site turned into a memorial space.
