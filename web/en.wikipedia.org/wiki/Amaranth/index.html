Amaranth is a cosmopolitan group of annual or short-lived perennial plants in the genus Amaranthus. Some species are grown as leaf vegetables, as ornamentals with spectacular flower plumes, or as pseudocereals valued since pre-Columbian times in Mesoamerica. The name comes from the Greek amarantos, "unfading", a reference to the long-lasting flowers.


Love-lies-bleeding (Amaranthus caudatus) in flower

Grain amaranth

Three species — Amaranthus caudatus, A. cruentus and A. hypochondriacus — were domesticated in the Americas and formed, along with maize and beans, a staple of Aztec and Inca agriculture. The Aztecs called the seed huauhtli; it was paid as tribute in enormous quantities and used in ritual foods. Spanish colonial authorities suppressed its cultivation because of its role in indigenous religion, and the crop nearly vanished before a revival began in the 1970s–80s. Amaranth grain is gluten-free, rich in protein for a plant food (about 13–14%), and unusual among grains in containing ample lysine. It is eaten popped (as in Mexican alegría sweets), milled into flour, or cooked like quinoa.

Leaf amaranth

Many species, including A. tricolor and A. viridis, are cultivated as leafy greens across Asia, Africa and the Caribbean, where they are known as callaloo, Chinese spinach, or bayam. The leaves are a good source of vitamins A and C, calcium and iron.

Weeds and ecology

Other species are aggressive agricultural weeds: Palmer amaranth (A. palmeri) and waterhemp (A. tuberculatus) have evolved resistance to multiple herbicides, including glyphosate, and pose serious problems in North American soybean and corn production. Amaranths are C4 plants, highly tolerant of heat and drought.

Cultural significance

Because its flower does not wither, amaranth symbolised immortality in Greek tradition. Its prominence as an ancient, resilient food has made it a flagship of the "ancient grains" movement in health food markets.
