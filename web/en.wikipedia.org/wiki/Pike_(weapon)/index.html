A pike is a very long thrusting spear formerly used in warfare, principally as an anti-cavalry weapon but also against infantry. Very unlike the shorter spears commonly used by foot soldiers, the pike is rarely a single-handed weapon, and was not intended to be thrown. Pikes used in Europe during classical and medieval times measured between 3 and 7.5 metres (10 and 25 feet). The weapon had a small steel spearhead on a long wooden shaft, typically of ash.

Popular with the armies of both classical and early-modern Europe, the pike fell out of use after the widespread adoption of the bayonet in the late 17th and early 18th centuries, a change that converted every musketeer into a potential pikeman.

Design

The pike resembled a long spear with an extremely long shaft and a small piercing blade. The shaft near the head was often reinforced with metal strips called langets, which protected it from being cut by swords. Medieval pikes commonly measured around 3–4 metres (10–14 ft), while those of the 17th-century pike square could reach 6–7.6 metres (20–25 ft). The longer the weapon, the harder it was to wield, since it had to be braced against the ground or held by a well-drilled soldier in a dense formation.

History

Antiquity

The Macedonian sarissa, introduced under Philip II of Macedon and carried to fame by his son Alexander the Great, was a pike of roughly 4–6.5 metres used by the phalanx. Its great length allowed several ranks of points to project in front of the formation, presenting an almost impenetrable hedge of spearheads.

Medieval period

Scottish infantry used long spears in the tight schiltron formations of the Wars of Scottish Independence, famously at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314. Flemish footmen at the Battle of the Golden Spurs (1302) likewise used long spears and the goedendag to defeat French cavalry.

Renaissance and early modern period

The weapon's reputation was established by the Swiss mercenaries of the late 15th century, whose aggressive pike columns defeated the heavy cavalry of Charles the Bold of Burgundy. Landsknecht mercenaries from Germany adopted the weapon, and Spanish tercios combined pikemen with arquebusiers and later musketeers in the pike and shot formations that dominated European battlefields through the Thirty Years' War. In the English Civil War, pikemen still made up a large fraction of infantry strength.

Decline

The introduction of the plug bayonet, and then the socket bayonet around the turn of the 18th century, allowed musketeers to defend themselves against cavalry without a separate pike arm. France abolished the pike in 1703 and most other armies followed; Sweden under Charles XII was a notable holdout. The word survives in expressions such as "pike square" and in ceremonial use.
