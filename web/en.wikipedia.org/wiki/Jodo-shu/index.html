Jōdo-shū (浄土宗, "The Pure Land School") is a branch of Pure Land Buddhism derived from the teachings of the Japanese ex-Tendai monk Hōnen (1133–1212). It is among the largest denominations of Japanese Buddhism, alongside its offshoot Jōdo Shinshū, founded by Hōnen's disciple Shinran.

Doctrine

Hōnen taught that in the present degenerate age of mappō (the latter days of the Dharma), human beings cannot attain enlightenment through their own efforts ("self-power," jiriki). Salvation comes instead through reliance on the "other-power" (tariki) of Amida Buddha, whose Original Vow promises birth in his Pure Land to all who sincerely recite the nembutsu — "Namu Amida Butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha"). Hōnen advocated the exclusive practice (senju) of the nembutsu, while still affirming the value of moral conduct in daily life.

History

Hōnen's teaching spread rapidly among commoners and aristocrats alike, provoking persecution: in 1207 his disciples were executed or exiled and Hōnen himself was banished to Shikoku. After his death, his followers organized the school around temples such as Chion-in in Kyoto (his mausoleum site) and Zōjō-ji in Edo/Tokyo. During the Tokugawa period Jōdo-shū enjoyed shogunal patronage as the Tokugawa family's temple affiliation.

Organization today

The school is organized into several branches (with the Chinzei branch, descending through Hōnen's disciple Benchō, the largest), counts thousands of temples across Japan, and maintains missions overseas. Its head temple is Chion-in in Kyoto's Higashiyama district.
