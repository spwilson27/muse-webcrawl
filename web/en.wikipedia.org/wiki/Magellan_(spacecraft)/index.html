Magellan (spacecraft)

The Magellan spacecraft was a robotic space probe launched by NASA on 4 May 1989 aboard Space Shuttle Atlantis (mission STS-30) to map the surface of Venus using synthetic-aperture radar. Named after the Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan, it was the first interplanetary mission launched from the Space Shuttle and the first deep-space probe launched by the United States in almost eleven years.

Mission

Venus's permanent cloud cover makes optical imaging of its surface impossible, so Magellan mapped the planet with radar. Arriving in a near-polar elliptical orbit in August 1990, it completed three 243-day mapping cycles, imaging about 98% of the surface at resolutions around 100–300 metres and measuring a global altimetry and gravity dataset. The spacecraft reused hardware from earlier missions — including a Voyager-era bus and the 3.7 m high-gain antenna from Voyager — to keep costs down.

Findings

Magellan's maps revealed a strikingly young surface, apparently resurfaced by volcanism roughly 300–600 million years ago, with thousands of volcanoes, vast lava plains, pancake-shaped volcanic domes and long sinuous lava channels, but no evidence of Earth-style plate tectonics. Impact craters are few and randomly distributed, consistent with planet-wide resurfacing.

End of mission

In 1993 Magellan performed the first aerobraking manoeuvre by a deep-space probe, using Venus's atmosphere to circularise its orbit for gravity mapping. In October 1994, during the "Windmill" experiment measuring atmospheric forces, controllers deliberately lowered the orbit; contact was lost on 13 October 1994 and the spacecraft burned up in the Venusian atmosphere.
