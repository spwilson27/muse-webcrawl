National Historic Sites of Canada (French: Lieux historiques nationaux du Canada) are places that have been designated by the federal Minister of the Environment, on the advice of the Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada (HSMBC), as being of national historic significance. Parks Canada, a federal agency, manages the National Historic Sites program. As of recent counts, there are more than 970 National Historic Sites, of which roughly 170 are administered directly by Parks Canada; the remainder are owned or administered by other levels of government or private entities.

Related designations recognize persons and events of national historic significance. Together, sites, persons, and events form the three pillars of the national commemoration program in Canada. National Historic Sites are found in every province and territory, and include battlefields, fur trade posts, lighthouses, industrial complexes, archaeological sites, historic districts, and buildings associated with important themes in Canadian history.

History

The origins of the program lie in the early twentieth century, when growing public interest in heritage preservation, encouraged by local historical societies and the example of preservation movements in the United States and Europe, prompted federal action. In 1914 the Commission of Conservation drew attention to the need to preserve historic places, and in 1917 Fort Anne in Annapolis Royal, Nova Scotia became the first site acquired by the federal government for historic preservation purposes.

In 1919 the Advisory Board for Historic Preservation — soon renamed the Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada — was established to advise the government on commemoration. The board's early recommendations concentrated heavily on the fur trade, military history, and the political history of the colonial period. Over the following decades the program broadened: after the Second World War, and especially from the 1970s onward, designations increasingly addressed social, economic, women's, Indigenous, and multicultural history, as well as architecture and industrial heritage.

Designation process

Any member of the public, organization, or government may request that a place be considered for designation. Submissions are researched by Parks Canada historians and evaluated by the Historic Sites and Monuments Board of Canada, an advisory body whose members are appointed from each province and territory. The board recommends designations to the Minister responsible for Parks Canada, who makes the final decision. A commemorative plaque, cast in bronze and mounted on a cairn, is typically erected at the site to mark the designation.

Designation is commemorative in nature. For sites not owned by the federal government, national historic site status does not by itself impose legal protection or restrictions on the owner, although many designated places are protected under provincial or municipal heritage legislation.

Administration and protection

Parks Canada administers approximately 170 National Historic Sites as part of its network of protected heritage places, which also includes national parks and national marine conservation areas. Sites administered by Parks Canada are managed under the Parks Canada Agency Act and associated policies to protect their commemorative integrity — the condition in which the resources that symbolize a site's national significance survive sound and unimpaired.

The federal government also supports the preservation of non-federal sites through cost-sharing programs, and heritage railway stations, heritage lighthouses, and federal heritage buildings are addressed under related federal heritage policies and statutes such as the Heritage Railway Stations Protection Act and the Heritage Lighthouse Protection Act.

Notable sites

Among the best-known National Historic Sites are the fortifications of Quebec City, the Rideau Canal in Ottawa, the fur trade post of Lower Fort Garry in Manitoba, L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland and Labrador — the only confirmed Norse site in North America — and the Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in Alberta. Several National Historic Sites are also inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, including Old Town Lunenburg, the Historic District of Old Quebec, and the Rideau Canal.
