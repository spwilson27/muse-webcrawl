Hermaphroditus (Ancient Greek: Ἑρμαφρόδιτος) is a figure of Greek mythology, the child of Hermes and Aphrodite — the two names combined in his own. In the best-known account, in Ovid's Metamorphoses, Hermaphroditus was a beautiful youth whose body was merged with that of the nymph Salmacis, so that he became a single being of both sexes. His name is the origin of the term "hermaphrodite".

Mythology

According to Ovid, Hermaphroditus was raised by naiads on Mount Ida. At fifteen he wandered to Caria and came to the crystal-clear pool of the nymph Salmacis. Salmacis desired him and, when he entered the water to bathe, leapt in and clung to him, praying to the gods that they should never be separated. The gods granted the prayer literally: the two were fused into one form, "neither woman nor man, yet both". Hermaphroditus, weakened, then cursed the pool so that any man who bathed in it would likewise emerge softened and half-man.

Worship and representations

Hermaphroditus appears in Greek religion from at least the 4th century BC, especially in Cyprus and Athens; Theophrastus refers to superstitious observances before his shrines on fixed days of the month. In art he is a recurring subject of Hellenistic and Roman sculpture, most famously the Sleeping Hermaphroditus, a reclining figure — copied in several versions, including examples in the Louvre and the Uffizi — whose body presents female forms from one side and male anatomy from the other.

Legacy

The name gave rise to the biological term hermaphroditism for organisms possessing both male and female reproductive organs, and the figure has been a longstanding subject in art, literature and discussions of sex and gender in the classical world.
