Teleprinter

A teleprinter (also called a teletypewriter, teletype, or TTY) is an electromechanical device that sends and receives typed messages through a communications channel, usually a pair of wires. Teleprinters formed the terminals of telex networks, carried news-wire copy into newspaper offices, and later served as the first interactive terminals for computers.

History

Printing telegraphs date to the 1840s and 1850s (House, Hughes). The practical start-stop teleprinter emerged from work by Donald Murray, who adapted the Baudot code into the Murray code around 1901, and by the Morkrum and Kleinschmidt companies in the United States, whose machines evolved into the Teletype Corporation's famous models. The five-bit Baudot–Murray code let a simple mechanism print one character per keystroke at about 45–50 baud.

Operation

Each keystroke is serialized into a start bit, five data bits, and a stop bit, transmitted to the receiving machine, which decodes the combination and prints the character on paper. Many machines included a paper-tape punch and reader, allowing messages to be composed offline, stored, and relayed — the "torn-tape" switching used in military and airline networks.

Computing legacy

Because they were rugged, available, and already understood serial communication, teleprinters — especially the Teletype Model 33 ASR — became the standard console for minicomputers and early Unix systems in the 1960s and 1970s. Their legacy persists in computing: the Unix device name tty, default line lengths of 80 columns, control characters such as carriage return and line feed, and the ASCII code's design all trace back to teleprinter practice.
