Proprietary trading

Proprietary trading (also "prop trading") occurs when a financial firm trades stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, derivatives, or other instruments with its own money — its own capital at risk — for its own profit, rather than earning commissions by executing trades on behalf of clients. When a bank or trading firm engages in proprietary trading, it seeks to profit directly from market movements, arbitrage, or statistical strategies, and it bears the associated gains and losses itself.

[Image: Prop trading floor]

Types of firms and strategies

Dedicated proprietary trading firms (such as large electronic market-making and high-frequency trading firms) trade only their own capital and typically do not take client business. Strategies include market making, index and statistical arbitrage, event-driven and merger arbitrage, relative-value trades, and high-frequency trading. Compensation at prop firms is usually a share of trading profits.

Banks and the Volcker Rule

Before the 2008 financial crisis, major investment banks ran substantial proprietary trading desks whose profits — and occasional large losses — contributed to debates about conflicts of interest and risk to depositors and taxpayers. In the United States, the Volcker Rule, part of the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 (named after former Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker), generally prohibits deposit-taking banks from proprietary trading, with exemptions for market making, underwriting, hedging, and trading in government securities. Banks responded by closing or spinning off prop desks, many of whose traders migrated to hedge funds and independent prop firms.

Retail "prop firms"

A separate online industry markets funded-account "challenges" to retail traders: customers pay a fee to trade a simulated account against profit targets, with successful traders notionally receiving payouts from the firm's capital. Regulators in several jurisdictions have warned that many such firms' business models depend on challenge fees rather than trading profits, and some have been shut down as unregistered or fraudulent operations.
