Anatomical votive

An anatomical votive is a type of votive offering in the shape of a part of the human body, dedicated at a healing sanctuary or shrine in the hope of, or in gratitude for, the cure of an ailment affecting that part. Anatomical votives are known from many cultures of the ancient Mediterranean world, including the Greek, Etruscan and Roman worlds, and comparable objects continue to be offered in some modern religious traditions.

History

In ancient Greece, worshippers presented model body parts at sanctuaries of the healing god Asclepius, such as the Asclepieia at Epidaurus, Athens and Corinth. The Corinth Asclepieion produced large numbers of terracotta arms, legs, hands, feet, ears and other parts dating chiefly to the fourth century BC.

In Italy, anatomical votives in terracotta became extremely common between the fourth and first centuries BC, deposited in Etruscan, Latin and Roman sanctuaries. Finds include heads, limbs, eyes, ears, internal organs such as uteri and intestines, and models of polyvisceral plaques showing the opened torso. Hundreds of thousands of such objects survive, and they constitute a major source of evidence for ancient medicine, disease and religious practice.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether anatomical votives were offered before treatment, in hope of a cure, or after recovery, as thank-offerings (ex-votos). The parts represented plausibly reflect the conditions that brought worshippers to the sanctuary, giving historians of medicine indirect evidence about the prevalence of injuries, eye and ear diseases, fertility concerns and other complaints in ancient populations.

Modern parallels

The tradition continues in the milagros of Latin American folk Catholicism and the tamata of Greek Orthodoxy — small metal plaques stamped with body parts, offered at shrines of saints.
