Bandwagon effect

Bandwagon effect
"Jumping on the bandwagon"
FieldPsychology, political science, economics
TypeCognitive bias / social influence
RelatedHerd behaviour, conformity, social proof, network effects

The bandwagon effect is a psychological phenomenon in which people do or believe things because many other people do or believe the same. The probability of an individual adopting a belief, product, or behaviour increases with the proportion of others who have already done so, irrespective of the underlying evidence. It is a form of herd behaviour and is closely related to conformity and social proof.

Origin of the term

The expression comes from nineteenth-century American politics, when circus bandwagons — wagons carrying the band in a circus parade — were adopted by campaigners, most famously in the 1848 presidential campaign of Zachary Taylor. Candidates rode a decorated bandwagon to attract attention, and supporters were urged to "jump on the bandwagon" and join the winning side. By the early twentieth century the phrase had become a general term for following the majority.

In politics

In elections, the bandwagon effect describes voters rallying to the candidate perceived to be leading, for example after favourable opinion-poll results or early primary victories — a dynamic that can make campaigns self-reinforcing. The opposite phenomenon, support for the perceived loser, is called the underdog effect. Because of concerns about bandwagon effects, some countries restrict the publication of opinion polls shortly before voting. The related "spiral of silence" theory holds that people who believe their views are in the minority become less willing to express them, further exaggerating the perceived majority.

In consumer behaviour and culture

Marketers exploit the bandwagon effect through testimonials, "best-seller" labels, and displays of popularity. In fashion, music, and technology, adoption can cascade through network effects and information cascades: once a product or style passes a threshold of adopters, remaining consumers adopt it largely because others have. The effect also appears in medicine and finance, and it is one mechanism behind speculative bubbles.
