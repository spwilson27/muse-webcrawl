Battle of Thermopylae

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The monument of Leonidas at Thermopylae, a bronze statue of the Spartan king]

The Battle of Thermopylae was fought in August or September 480 BC at the narrow coastal pass of Thermopylae ('the Hot Gates') in central Greece, between an alliance of Greek city-states led by King Leonidas I of Sparta and the invading Persian Empire of Xerxes I. For three days a small Greek force — famously including Leonidas' 300 Spartan royal guards — held the pass against the vastly larger Persian army, until a local man, Ephialtes, showed the Persians a mountain path that outflanked the position. Leonidas dismissed most of the army and died with his Spartans, 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans. The stand became the supreme Greek legend of heroic sacrifice.

The battle

The pass, in 480 BC perhaps 15 metres wide at its narrowest in places, negated Persian numbers: Greek hoplites rotated in the phalanx and shattered assaults by Medes and the elite Immortals. A storm simultaneously wrecked part of the Persian fleet off Magnesia. On the third day, informed that Hydarnes' Immortals were descending the Anopaia path behind them, Leonidas held a council: most allies withdrew (whether dismissed or deserting is argued), while the Spartans, Thespians and Thebans remained and died fighting, Leonidas among them — allegedly fulfilling an oracle that Sparta must lose a king or fall.

Aftermath and legend

Xerxes desecrated Leonidas' body, advanced through Boeotia and burned Athens; within weeks Salamis reversed the campaign's logic. Simonides' epitaph for the Spartans — 'Go tell the Spartans, passerby, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie' — fixed Thermopylae in cultural memory. Modern scholarship stresses that the Greeks expected to hold longer (the pass was a delaying action for the fleet at Artemisium), that thousands of other Greeks fought and many died there, and that helots and perioikoi who also fell go largely uncommemorated — but the core of the story, drawn from Herodotus, is sound.
