The California gold rush (1848–1855) was a gold rush that began on 24 January 1848, when gold was found by James W. Marshall at Sutter's Mill in Coloma, California. The news brought approximately 300,000 people to California from the rest of the United States and abroad — the "forty-niners" of 1849 — and transformed the territory, which the United States had acquired from Mexico days after the discovery, under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

The rush

Word spread slowly at first; San Francisco's newspapers and the merchant Samuel Brannan, who famously paraded a vial of gold through the town, ignited the rush in mid-1848. Confirmation by President Polk in December 1848 triggered the great migration of 1849, by sea around Cape Horn or across Panama, and overland by wagon. San Francisco grew from a village of about 1,000 into a city of some 25,000 within two years, its harbour choked with abandoned ships whose crews had deserted for the goldfields.

Mining

Early miners recovered placer gold from streams with pans, rockers, and sluices, and the surface deposits were rich enough that a lucky miner could earn many times a labourer's wage. As placers were exhausted, mining turned corporate and destructive: hydraulic mining washed away whole hillsides with water cannons until it was curtailed by the Sawyer decision of 1884, and hard-rock quartz mining and dredging followed. Most individual prospectors ended poorer than they began; merchants — Levi Strauss, Wells Fargo, and the "Big Four" railroad magnates among them — made the durable fortunes.

Consequences

California's non-native population leapt from roughly 14,000 to some 300,000 within a few years, enabling statehood in 1850 as a free state under the Compromise of 1850. The rush was catastrophic for Native Californians, whose population collapsed from about 150,000 to 30,000 through disease, displacement, violence, and massacres, some state-sanctioned. Foreign miners faced discriminatory taxes and expulsions; Chinese immigrants, who formed a large mining workforce, were targets of violence and later of exclusion laws. The environmental scars — mercury contamination, diverted rivers, mountains of tailings — remain visible today. Gold production peaked in 1852 at about $81 million.
