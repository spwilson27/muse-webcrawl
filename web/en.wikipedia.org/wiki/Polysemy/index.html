Polysemy

Polysemy (from Greek polys, "many", and sema, "sign") is the capacity for a word, phrase or sign to have multiple related meanings. A word with several related senses is called polysemous. For example, the verb "to get" can mean "procure", "become", or "understand"; the noun "head" can denote a body part, the top of an object, the leader of an organisation, or the foam on beer — senses that speakers perceive as extensions of one another.

Polysemy and homonymy

Polysemy is usually distinguished from homonymy, in which a single written or spoken form corresponds to two unrelated words with different origins — for example "bank" (financial institution) and "bank" (side of a river), or "bat" (animal) and "bat" (sports equipment). In practice the boundary is fuzzy: dictionaries decide whether to give senses separate entries (homonymy) or numbered senses within one entry (polysemy), sometimes guided by etymology and sometimes by speakers' intuitions of relatedness.

How polysemy arises

Polysemy develops through processes of semantic change:

  • Metaphor: the "foot" of a mountain; the "head" of a department.
  • Metonymy: "the Crown" for the monarchy; a "dish" for the food served in it.
  • Generalisation and specialisation: broadening or narrowing of an original sense.
  • Regular polysemy: systematic alternations, such as animal/meat ("chicken", "lamb") or container/content ("I drank the whole bottle").

In linguistics and computing

Polysemy is a central topic in lexical semantics and cognitive linguistics, where theorists such as Charles Ruhl and Dirk Geeraerts have debated whether senses are stored as discrete lists or derived from a single abstract meaning. In computational linguistics, word-sense disambiguation — selecting the intended sense of a polysemous word in context — is a long-standing problem for machine translation and information retrieval.
