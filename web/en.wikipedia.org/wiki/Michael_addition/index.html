Michael addition

The Michael addition (or Michael reaction) is the nucleophilic addition of a carbanion or another nucleophile to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound containing an electron-withdrawing group. It belongs to the larger class of conjugate additions and is one of the most powerful methods for mild formation of C–C bonds. The reaction is named after Arthur Michael, who reported it in 1887.

Mechanism

A base deprotonates the Michael donor — typically a doubly stabilized carbon acid such as a malonic ester or nitroalkane — to give an enolate or related anion. This nucleophile attacks the β-carbon of the Michael acceptor (an enone, enoate, or similar alkene bearing an electron-withdrawing group), forming a new carbon–carbon bond and an enolate, which is then protonated to give the 1,5-dicarbonyl-type product.

Scope and variants

Beyond carbon nucleophiles, amines (aza-Michael), thiols (thia-Michael), and phosphines can add conjugately. Catalytic asymmetric Michael additions, including organocatalytic versions, are widely used in synthesis; the Robinson annulation combines a Michael addition with an intramolecular aldol reaction to build six-membered rings.
