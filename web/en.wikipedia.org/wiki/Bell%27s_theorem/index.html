Bell's theorem
Proposed byJohn Stewart Bell, 1964
FieldQuantum mechanics, foundations of physics
ConsequenceNo local hidden-variable theory can reproduce all quantum predictions

Bell's theorem is a term encompassing a number of closely related results in physics, all of which determine that quantum mechanics is incompatible with local hidden-variable theories given some basic assumptions. It was introduced by physicist John Stewart Bell in his 1964 paper "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox".

Background

The EPR paradox (1935) argued quantum mechanics must be incomplete: measurements on entangled particles seemed to imply either "spooky action at a distance" or pre-existing hidden properties. Bell showed these alternatives could be distinguished experimentally: any theory that is both local and realistic must satisfy certain statistical bounds — Bell inequalities — which quantum mechanics predicts will be violated.

Experiments

The CHSH form of the inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969) made testing practical. Experiments by Alain Aspect in the early 1980s and, in 2015, several "loophole-free" Bell tests confirmed violations of the inequalities, in agreement with quantum mechanics. Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics for this experimental work on quantum entanglement.

Interpretation

Exactly what Bell's theorem rules out — locality, realism, or the conjunction, with escape routes such as superdeterminism — remains debated among interpretations of quantum mechanics. The theorem underpins modern quantum information science, including device-independent quantum cryptography.
