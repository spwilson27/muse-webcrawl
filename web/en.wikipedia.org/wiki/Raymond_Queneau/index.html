Raymond Queneau (February 21, 1903 – October 25, 1976) was a French novelist, poet and editor, celebrated for his wit, his experiments with language, and his use of mathematical structures in literature. He was a co-founder, with the mathematician François Le Lionnais, of the Oulipo group in 1960.

Life and work

Born in Le Havre, Queneau studied philosophy at the Sorbonne and moved in surrealist circles in the 1920s before breaking with André Breton. His first novel, Le Chiendent (1933), already showed his interest in formal construction and spoken French. He worked for many years at the publishing house Gallimard, becoming director of the prestigious Encyclopédie de la Pléiade.

His most famous books include Exercices de style (1947), which retells a trivial anecdote in ninety-nine different styles; Zazie dans le Métro (1959), a comic novel about a foul-mouthed young girl visiting Paris, later filmed by Louis Malle; and Cent mille milliards de poèmes (1961), a book of ten sonnets whose cut-up lines can be recombined to yield 1014 possible poems.

Oulipo

Queneau described Oulipo members as "rats who build the labyrinth from which they plan to escape". The group explored constrained writing — lipograms, palindromes, combinatorial structures — and greatly influenced later experimental literature in France and abroad.
