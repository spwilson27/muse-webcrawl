A pendulum clock is a clock that uses a pendulum, a swinging weight, as its timekeeping element. Invented by Christiaan Huygens in 1656 and patented the following year, it was the most accurate timekeeping technology in the world for nearly 300 years, until the quartz clock of the 1930s. Pendulum clocks were the standard timekeepers of homes, factories, and observatories, and were used to set all other clocks.

History

Galileo Galilei discovered the isochronism of the pendulum — that its period is nearly independent of amplitude — around 1602, and late in life designed a pendulum-regulated clock, built after his death by his son. Huygens, working independently, had the first working model made by clockmaker Salomon Coster in 1656. His design reduced error from roughly 15 minutes per day (verge clocks) to about 15 seconds per day.

Mechanism

The pendulum swings with a period determined almost entirely by its length: T = 2π√(L/g). A "seconds pendulum" about 0.994 m long has a two-second period. The escapement — originally Huygens' crutch design, later the anchor escapement (c. 1670) and the deadbeat escapement (1715) — both counts the swings and gives the pendulum a small push each swing to replace energy lost to friction and air resistance, powered by falling weights or a mainspring.

Improvements

The main source of error was thermal expansion of the pendulum rod, countered by mercury pendulums (Graham, 1721) and gridiron pendulums (Harrison, 1726), and later by low-expansion alloys such as invar. The Shortt–Synchronome clock (1921), in which a free "master" pendulum was synchronised with a slave clock, kept time to about a second per year and was the observatory standard until quartz.
