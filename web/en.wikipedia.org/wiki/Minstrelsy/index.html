Minstrelsy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Minstrelsy is the art and occupation of the minstrel, a medieval entertainer who performed music, song, storytelling, juggling and poetry, typically in the courts of nobility and in castles, taverns and marketplaces across Europe. Minstrels were professional performers of the Middle Ages, flourishing roughly from the 11th to the 15th centuries, when printed music and changing court culture gradually displaced them.

Origins and role

The word derives from the Latin ministerialis, meaning a servant or official. Early minstrels were attached to royal and noble households, where they provided entertainment at feasts, celebrated the deeds of their patrons in song, and sometimes carried messages or gathered news as they travelled. Others were itinerant, moving between towns and fairs.

Minstrels overlap with, but are often distinguished from, troubadours and trouvères — the poet-composers of southern and northern France who composed their own lyric verse — and from jongleurs, who were more purely acrobats and entertainers. In practice these categories blurred: a minstrel might sing songs composed by troubadours, play instruments such as the lute, vielle, harp and shawm, recite romances, and perform tricks.

Organisation and decline

In England, royal minstrels enjoyed livery and wages, and by the later Middle Ages minstrels formed guilds in many towns to regulate the profession. The term also survives in the name of the minstrels' galleries found in great halls. Minstrelsy declined from the 16th century as professional theatre companies, printed ballads and court orchestras took over its functions; the word later took on new meanings in Britain and America, notably in the very different tradition of the 19th-century minstrel show.
