Christian Science is a religious movement founded by Mary Baker Eddy in 19th-century New England. Its teachings, set out in Eddy's book Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures (1875), hold that reality is entirely spiritual: God is infinite Mind, and matter, sin, sickness, and death — though apparently real to human perception — are errors of "mortal mind" that can be overcome through prayer and a correct understanding of God. Christian Science is best known for its practice of spiritual healing in place of conventional medicine.

History

Eddy dated her discovery of Christian Science to 1866, when she reported recovering rapidly from a serious fall after reading an account of one of Jesus's healings. She went on to teach her system — influenced in her early development by the mental healer Phineas Quimby, though the extent of that influence is debated — and published Science and Health in 1875. In 1879 she and her students chartered the Church of Christ, Scientist, in Boston. The movement grew rapidly in the late 19th century, especially among women, and in 1908 Eddy founded the newspaper The Christian Science Monitor, which became a respected secular news organization. Membership peaked in the mid-20th century and has since declined substantially.

Beliefs and practice

Christian Science theology is metaphysically idealist: God, synonymous with Mind, Spirit, Soul, Principle, Life, Truth, and Love, is all-in-all, and creation is God's perfect spiritual reflection. Because sickness is understood as an error of belief rather than a material condition, adherents typically turn first to prayer — their own or that of a Christian Science practitioner — rather than medical treatment. Services in branch churches consist of readings from the Bible and Science and Health following a weekly Lesson-Sermon studied by members; there is no ordained clergy, and services are led by elected Readers. Churches maintain public reading rooms where the literature is available.

Controversy

The movement's reliance on spiritual healing has generated persistent controversy, particularly in cases where children died of treatable conditions after medical care was withheld; several such cases led to prosecutions in the United States, and most jurisdictions now limit religious exemptions from child medical-neglect laws. The church maintains that adherents are free to choose medical care and points to what it regards as a long record of healings. Christian Science is also sometimes confused with Scientology, an unrelated organization.
