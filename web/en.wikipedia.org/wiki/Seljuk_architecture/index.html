Seljuk architecture

Seljuk architecture refers to the building traditions developed under the Seljuk Turks, who ruled large parts of Persia, Mesopotamia, Syria, and Anatolia between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. It combined Persian, Central Asian, and local traditions and profoundly influenced the later architecture of the Ottoman Empire and Iran.

Great Seljuk architecture (Iran)

Under the Great Seljuks of Iran, mosque architecture was transformed by the adoption of the four-iwan plan, arranged around a central courtyard, which became canonical in Persian mosques — the Friday Mosque of Isfahan is the outstanding example. Brick was the prestige material, often laid in elaborate decorative patterns, complemented by carved stucco and glazed tile. Seljuk architects also developed monumental tomb towers, such as the Gonbad-e Qabus, and refined the use of muqarnas (stalactite vaulting).

Anatolian Seljuk architecture

The Seljuk Sultanate of Rum in Anatolia built primarily in stone. Its most distinctive monuments include great caravanserais along the trade routes — the Sultan Han near Aksaray among the largest — madrasas with richly carved portals in Konya, Kayseri, and Sivas, and mosques such as the Great Mosque and hospital of Divriği, a UNESCO World Heritage Site famed for its exuberant stone carving. Anatolian Seljuk portals (taç kapı) rose well above the façade and were framed by minarets, often in pairs, built of brick with glazed turquoise decoration.

Legacy

Seljuk forms — the four-iwan mosque, the monumental portal, muqarnas vaulting, and glazed tilework — passed into Ilkhanid, Timurid, and Ottoman practice and remain central to the architectural identity of Iran and Turkey.
