The door-in-the-face technique is a compliance method in social psychology: a persuader first makes a large request that the respondent will almost certainly refuse — metaphorically slamming a door in the persuader's face — then follows it with the smaller, target request. The retreat is perceived as a concession, and the norm of reciprocity, together with a perceptual contrast effect, makes the respondent significantly more likely to comply than if the small request were made alone.

Original experiment

Robert Cialdini and colleagues demonstrated the effect in 1975: students asked first to volunteer two hours a week for two years as counsellors at a juvenile detention centre (universally refused) and then asked to chaperone a single two-hour zoo trip agreed at about 50%, versus about 17% of those asked only for the zoo trip. Meta-analyses confirm a modest but reliable effect, strongest when the same person makes both requests, the requests are prosocial, and there is no delay between them.

Explanations and applications

Two accounts dominate: reciprocal concessions (negotiation norms oblige each side to match retreats) and social responsibility/guilt. The technique is used in fundraising, sales negotiations and plea bargaining, and is the mirror image of the foot-in-the-door technique, in which a small initial request paves the way for a larger one.
