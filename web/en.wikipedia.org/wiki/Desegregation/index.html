Desegregation

Desegregation is the process of ending the separation of two groups, usually on the basis of race, and reversing policies of segregation. The term is used most often in connection with the United States, where it describes the dismantling of legally enforced racial segregation in schools, public facilities, transport, housing and the armed forces during the twentieth century, largely as a result of the civil rights movement, federal court decisions and legislation.

United States

Racial segregation in the American South was entrenched under the doctrine of "separate but equal" upheld by the Supreme Court in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). The armed forces were the first major institution to be desegregated by federal action: in 1948 President Harry S. Truman issued Executive Order 9981, mandating equality of treatment in the military. In education, the landmark ruling in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) declared state-sponsored segregation of public schools unconstitutional, though implementation was resisted — sometimes violently, as in the Little Rock Crisis of 1957 — and proceeded slowly, often only under court-supervised desegregation orders and busing plans.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 outlawed segregation in public accommodations and employment, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 removed barriers to political participation. Courts continued to supervise school desegregation for decades, and debates continue over de facto segregation arising from housing patterns and school district boundaries.

Elsewhere

The term is also applied to the ending of other systems of enforced separation, including South Africa's apartheid regime, dismantled in the early 1990s, and to gender desegregation of institutions and professions.
