Ajax the Great

Ajax (Ancient Greek: Αἴας, romanized: Aíās) is a Greek mythological hero, the son of King Telamon and Periboea, and the half-brother of Teucer. He plays an important role in the Trojan War and is portrayed in Homer's Iliad and in the Epic Cycle as a towering warrior of immense strength, second only to Achilles among the Achaean heroes. He is commonly distinguished from the lesser Ajax, son of Oileus, by the epithets Ajax the Great, Greater Ajax, or Ajax Telamonianus (son of Telamon).

In the Iliad

In the Iliad, Ajax is the tallest and strongest of the Greeks, a massive defensive bulwark famously described as the "bulwark of the Achaeans". He carries a huge shield of seven layers of oxhide faced with bronze, said to have been made by Tychius. Ajax fights Hector twice: their first duel, fought by lot after Hector's challenge, ends at nightfall with an exchange of gifts; in their later encounters Ajax repeatedly drives Hector back and defends the Greek ships, at one point fighting off the Trojans almost single-handed with a great ship's pike. After Achilles withdraws from battle, Ajax, with Odysseus and Phoenix, forms the embassy sent to persuade him to return, and after Achilles' death Ajax carries his body back to the ships under covering fire from Odysseus.

Contest for the arms of Achilles and death

Ajax's death is not in the Iliad but was told in the Epic Cycle (the Aethiopis and Little Iliad) and is the subject of Sophocles' tragedy Ajax. The arms of Achilles were to be awarded to the greatest Greek hero; the contest was decided between Ajax and Odysseus, and the arms were given to Odysseus. Dishonoured and enraged, Ajax resolved to kill the Greek leaders, but Athena drove him mad, and he slaughtered flocks of sheep and cattle in the belief they were his enemies. Coming to his senses and overwhelmed by shame, he killed himself by falling on the sword he had received from Hector. In Sophocles' play, Ajax's wife Tecmessa and his brother Teucer plead for his burial, which the Atridae initially forbid until Odysseus himself intervenes.

Cult and legacy

Ajax was worshipped as a hero on Salamis, where a temple and festival, the Aianteia, were held in his honour, and at Athens, where the tribe Aiantis was named after him and his statue stood in the marketplace. His son Eurysaces and descendant Philaeus were claimed as ancestors by Athenian families, including that of Miltiades and Alcibiades. Ajax remained a byword for steadfast, unglamorous courage in later literature.
