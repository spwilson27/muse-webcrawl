Flipped classroom

In a flipped classroom, direct instruction moves to individual study and class time is used for active learning.

A flipped classroom is a type of blended learning that aims to increase student engagement and learning by having pupils complete readings at home and work on live problem-solving during class time. This pedagogical style moves activities, including those that may have traditionally been considered homework, into the classroom. With a flipped classroom, students watch online lectures, collaborate in online discussions, or carry out research at home while engaging in concepts in the classroom with the guidance of a mentor.

In the traditional model of classroom instruction, the teacher is typically the central focus of a lesson and the primary disseminator of information during the class period. In a flipped classroom, the teacher's role changes towards that of a coach or adviser, encouraging students in individual inquiry and collaborative effort.

History

Precursors include the work of Eric Mazur at Harvard in the 1990s, who developed peer instruction. The term gained wide currency after 2007, when Colorado chemistry teachers Jonathan Bergmann and Aaron Sams began recording their lectures for absent students and realised the model could be generalised. Salman Khan and Khan Academy popularised video-based instruction from 2006 onwards, and by the 2010s flipped learning had become a major movement in educational technology, formalised by the Flipped Learning Network's "four pillars" (F-L-I-P): flexible environment, learning culture, intentional content and professional educator.

Approach

Typically, short video lectures (often 5–15 minutes) are assigned before class, sometimes accompanied by quizzes. Class time is then devoted to exercises, projects and discussion, allowing the teacher to help students who struggle rather than lecturing to the whole group. Variants include in-class flipping, where stations rotate within the classroom, and mastery-based flipping, in which students must demonstrate understanding before moving on.

Evidence and criticism

Research findings are mixed. Meta-analyses find small positive effects on achievement in some contexts, particularly in STEM and health education, but results depend heavily on implementation. Critics note that the model assumes all students have reliable internet access and a suitable home study environment, may increase screen time and workload, and can fail if students arrive unprepared. Supporters argue that active learning in class, rather than the videos themselves, drives the benefits.
