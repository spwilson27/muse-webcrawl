Kleine–Levin syndrome (KLS) is a rare neurological disorder characterised by recurrent episodes of excessive sleep (hypersomnia) associated with cognitive and behavioural changes. During episodes, patients may sleep for 15 to 21 hours per day, waking only to eat and use the bathroom, and may display apathy, confusion, derealisation, hyperphagia and disinhibition. Between episodes, sleep, cognition and behaviour are typically normal.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes usually begin abruptly, sometimes following an infection, alcohol intake, sleep deprivation or head trauma, and last from a few days to several weeks. Patients are difficult to rouse, and when awake appear dazed and apathetic, with slowed speech and memory problems. Compulsive eating (megaphagia) and hypersexuality occur in a subset of patients. The disorder most often begins in adolescence, affects males more than females, and episodes tend to become less frequent with age, frequently resolving within a decade or so.

Causes

The cause is unknown. Proposed mechanisms involve dysfunction of the hypothalamus and thalamus; functional imaging during episodes has shown hypoperfusion of thalamic and frontotemporal regions. Infectious or autoimmune triggers have been suggested, and genetic associations, including with the TRANK1 region, have been reported, but no definitive mechanism has been established.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on the recurrent pattern of hypersomnia with cognitive or behavioural disturbance and normal functioning between episodes, after excluding other causes of hypersomnia such as narcolepsy, depression and metabolic disorders. Polysomnography and imaging are usually normal outside episodes.

Treatment

There is no cure; management centres on supportive care and safety during episodes. Stimulants such as modafinil may reduce sleepiness in some patients, and lithium and certain other agents have been reported to reduce episode frequency in case series. Watchful waiting is often appropriate because the condition tends to remit spontaneously.

History

Precursor cases were described in the 19th and early 20th centuries; Willi Kleine reported a series of cases in 1925, and Max Levin described the combination of hypersomnia with pathological hunger in 1929–1936. Macdonald Critchley consolidated the syndrome's description in 1942 and gave it its eponymous name.
