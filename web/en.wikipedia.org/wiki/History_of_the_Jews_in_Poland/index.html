The history of the Jews in Poland dates back over a thousand years. For centuries Poland was home to the largest Jewish community in the world, a major center of Jewish culture and scholarship, until the community was almost entirely destroyed in the Holocaust.

Early history and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

Jewish merchants and settlers arrived in Polish lands by the 10th–11th centuries. The Statute of Kalisz (1264) granted Jews legal protection and communal autonomy, and later privileges encouraged migration from Germany and Bohemia. In the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, Jewish communities flourished; the Council of Four Lands coordinated communal affairs, and yeshivas in cities such as Kraków and Lublin made Poland a heartland of rabbinic learning. By the 17th century, an estimated three-quarters of world Jewry lived in the Commonwealth.

Partitions and the 19th century

The partitions of Poland (1772–95) divided Polish Jewry among Russia, Prussia, and Austria, subjecting most to the Russian Pale of Settlement and discriminatory laws. Poland became a cradle of Hasidism and of the Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment), as well as of early Zionism and the socialist Bund.

Interwar Poland

The Second Polish Republic had some 3.3 million Jews—about ten percent of the population—with vibrant Yiddish and Hebrew culture in Warsaw, Łódź, Vilnius, and Lwów, alongside rising antisemitism and economic boycotts.

The Holocaust and aftermath

Nazi Germany occupied Poland in 1939 and built the major extermination camps—Auschwitz, Treblinka, Bełżec, Sobibór, Chełmno, and Majdanek—on occupied Polish soil. Roughly three million Polish Jews, about ninety percent, were murdered. Postwar emigration, pogroms such as Kielce (1946), and the antisemitic campaign of 1968 reduced the community to a small remnant; since 1989 a modest cultural revival has taken place.
