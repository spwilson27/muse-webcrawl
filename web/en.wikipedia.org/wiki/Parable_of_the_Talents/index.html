The Parable of the Talents (also known as the Parable of the Minas or the Parable of the Pounds) is one of the parables of Jesus. It appears in two of the Synoptic Gospels: Matthew 25:14–30 and Luke 19:12–27 (where it is known as the Parable of the Minas). In the parable, a master entrusts his property to three servants before going on a journey; on his return he rewards the servants who invested and multiplied what they were given, and punishes the one who hid his money in the ground.

The master entrusts his wealth to three servants before departing.

The narrative (Matthew)

In Matthew's version, a man going on a journey calls his servants and entrusts to them his property: to one he gives five talents, to another two talents, and to another one talent, "to each according to his ability." A talent was a very large sum of money — a unit of weight in silver worth roughly twenty years of a labourer's wages.

"He who had received the five talents went at once and traded with them, and he made five talents more. So also he who had the two talents made two talents more. But he who had received the one talent went and dug in the ground and hid his master's money." — Matthew 25:16–18

After a long time the master returns and settles accounts. The first two servants present double what they received and are commended: "Well done, good and faithful servant... enter into the joy of your master." The third servant returns only the original talent, accusing the master of being "a hard man, reaping where you did not sow." The master rebukes him as a "wicked and slothful servant," orders the talent taken from him and given to the servant with ten, and casts him "into the outer darkness." The parable concludes with the saying: "For to everyone who has will more be given, and he will have an abundance. But from the one who has not, even what he has will be taken away."

Luke's version: the Parable of the Minas

In Luke, the story is framed differently: a nobleman travels to a distant country to receive a kingdom, and his citizens send a delegation after him saying they do not want him to reign over them. He gives ten servants one mina each (a much smaller sum, about three months' wages). On his return as king, he rewards the faithful servants with authority over cities and orders his enemies slain. The Lukan version is told near Jericho as Jesus approaches Jerusalem, apparently to correct the expectation that the kingdom of God would appear immediately.

Interpretation

The parable is commonly interpreted as a teaching about stewardship: the gifts and abilities God gives are to be used productively in his service rather than buried out of fear, and an account will be required. It is also read eschatologically, as a picture of faithful watchfulness during the delay of Christ's return, consistent with its place in Matthew's Olivet Discourse between the Parable of the Ten Virgins and the judgment of the sheep and goats.

The English word talent, meaning a natural aptitude or skill, derives its modern sense largely from this parable, via the interpretation of the money as God-given abilities. Some modern scholars and theologians have offered alternative readings, noting the third servant's description of the master and interpreting the parable as a critique of exploitative economic practices, though the traditional stewardship reading remains dominant.
