Restoration (Spain)

The Restoration (Spanish: Restauración) is the period in Spanish history from the proclamation of Alfonso XII as king in December 1874, ending the First Spanish Republic, until the proclamation of the Second Spanish Republic on 14 April 1931. The period is named for the restoration of the Bourbon dynasty to the Spanish throne.

The turno pacífico

The Restoration system was principally designed by Antonio Cánovas del Castillo, leader of the Conservative Party. Its central mechanism was the turno pacífico ("peaceful alternation"), under which the two dynastic parties — Cánovas's Conservatives and Práxedes Mateo Sagasta's Liberals — alternated in power. Elections were routinely rigged through the influence of local political bosses known as caciques, so that the incoming government effectively won elections that were arranged before they were held. The Constitution of 1876 provided the legal framework for the system and remained in force, with interruptions, for nearly half a century.

Crisis and decline

The system survived the assassination of Cánovas in 1897 and the disaster of 1898, when Spain lost Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines in the Spanish–American War, but it came under mounting strain in the early twentieth century. Regionalism in Catalonia and the Basque Country, labor unrest, and military intervention in politics eroded its foundations. After the 1923 coup of Miguel Primo de Rivera, the monarchy supported a dictatorship that lasted until 1930; the failure of the subsequent "dictablanda" of General Berenguer discredited Alfonso XIII, and municipal elections in April 1931 were treated as a plebiscite on the monarchy, leading to the king's departure and the proclamation of the republic.
