Hoplite

Hoplites (Greek: ὁπλίται, hoplítai) were citizen-soldiers of ancient Greek city-states who were primarily armed with spears and shields. Hoplite soldiers utilised the phalanx formation in order to be effective in war with fewer soldiers. The formation discouraged the soldiers from acting alone, for this would compromise the formation and minimise its strengths. The hoplites were represented mainly by free citizens — propertied farmers and artisans — who were able to afford a linen or bronze armour suit and weapons, estimated at three months' pay for an average citizen.

Equipment

The hoplite's defining item was the hoplon (or aspis), a large concave shield about one metre across, made of wood faced with bronze and held by a double grip — the forearm through a central band (porpax) and the hand on a cord (antilabē) — which let the shield cover both its bearer and the man to his left. Body armour included a helmet (famously the Corinthian type), a cuirass of bronze or layered linen, and greaves. The primary weapon was the dory, a spear of about 2.5–3 metres with an iron head and a bronze butt-spike; the short iron xiphos served as a sidearm. The full panoply could weigh 20–30 kg.

Tactics

Hoplites fought in the phalanx, a dense block typically eight ranks deep, advancing to crash into the enemy line in a collective shove (ōthismos) followed by spear-work over the shield rims. Battles were often short and decisive by modern standards, fought on flat ground both sides tacitly accepted. Sparta's professional long-serving hoplites were the most feared; Thebes under Epaminondas broke Spartan supremacy at Leuctra (371 BC) with a deepened left wing. In the fourth century BC, lighter troops such as Iphicrates' peltasts and Philip II of Macedon's sarissa-armed phalanx progressively eclipsed the classical hoplite.

Society

Hoplite equipment was self-provided, so service and political citizenship were closely linked: the "hoplite franchise" underpinned the broadening of political rights in cities such as Athens. The hoplite ideal of standing fast in the ranks became a central theme of Greek civic ideology and elegiac poetry, as in the verse of Tyrtaeus.
