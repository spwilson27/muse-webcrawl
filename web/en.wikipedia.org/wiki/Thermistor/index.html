Thermistor

Thermistor
Type: temperature-dependent resistor
Name: portmanteau of "thermal" and "resistor"
Types: NTC (negative temperature coefficient), PTC (positive temperature coefficient)
Materials: sintered metal oxides (NTC); doped barium titanate, polymers (PTC)
Applications: temperature sensing, inrush current limiting, overcurrent protection, thermostats

A thermistor is a type of resistor whose resistance varies sharply and predictably with temperature. Much more sensitive than ordinary resistors or resistance thermometers, thermistors are widely used as temperature sensors, current limiters, and self-resetting protective devices. The word is a blend of thermal and resistor; the behaviour was first noted by Michael Faraday in silver sulfide in 1833, and practical devices were developed at Bell Laboratories in the 1930s by Samuel Ruben.

NTC thermistors

Negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistors decrease in resistance as temperature rises, typically by 3–5 percent per degree Celsius near room temperature — about ten times the sensitivity of a platinum resistance thermometer. They are usually made of sintered semiconducting metal oxides (such as oxides of manganese, nickel, cobalt, and copper) formed into beads, discs, or chips. Their resistance–temperature relationship is strongly nonlinear and is commonly described by the Steinhart–Hart equation, which gives accuracies of a fraction of a degree over useful ranges. NTC devices serve in digital thermometers, medical probes, automotive and HVAC sensors, battery monitoring, and as inrush-current limiters, since their initially high cold resistance falls as they warm up under load.

PTC thermistors

Positive temperature coefficient (PTC) thermistors increase in resistance with temperature. "Switching" PTC devices made from doped polycrystalline barium titanate show a sudden, enormous resistance jump at a characteristic Curie temperature. They are used as self-resetting fuses and overcurrent protectors, motor-starting elements, and self-regulating heating elements. Polymer PTC devices ("polyfuses") perform the same protective role in consumer electronics and batteries.

Characteristics and use

Thermistors are inexpensive, small, rugged, and highly sensitive, but they are nonlinear, have a more limited temperature range than thermocouples or RTDs (commonly about −55 °C to +150 °C for NTC types), and require care about self-heating: the measurement current itself warms the sensor, which can be a source of error — or, exploited deliberately, the basis of flow and level sensors.
