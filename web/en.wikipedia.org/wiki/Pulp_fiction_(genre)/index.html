Pulp fiction is the fiction published in the cheap American magazines — the "pulps" — of the first half of the twentieth century, named for the coarse wood-pulp paper on which they were printed, in contrast to the smooth-paper "slicks". The term has come to denote the racy, violent, fast-paced popular fiction the pulps contained: hard-boiled detective stories, science fiction, horror, westerns and adventure tales. Many foundational writers of genre fiction began in the pulps.

History

The first recognisably pulp magazine, Frank Munsey's Argosy (converted to all-fiction on pulp paper in 1896), demonstrated that cheap fiction could sell in huge numbers. Through the 1910s and 1920s, publishers such as Street & Smith issued dozens of titles, often devoted to a single character (the "hero pulps" — The Shadow, Doc Savage) or a single genre. The 1920s and 1930s were the pulp heyday: hundreds of titles competed on newsstands at ten cents a copy, paying writers a cent a word or less.

Paper shortages in the Second World War, rising costs and competition from paperback books, comics and television killed the format; the last major pulps folded in the 1950s. Their contents, however, migrated wholesale into the post-war paperback boom.

Genres and writers

Detective and crime

Black Mask magazine defined hard-boiled detective fiction: Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler both began there, creating the Continental Op, Sam Spade and the style that fed film noir.

Science fiction and fantasy

Hugo Gernsback's Amazing Stories (1926) coined "science fiction"; the field's writers and fandom grew within the pulps. Weird Tales published H. P. Lovecraft, Robert E. Howard (creator of Conan the Barbarian) and Clark Ashton Smith; Edgar Rice Burroughs's Tarzan and John Carter stories appeared in the general adventure pulps.

Other genres

Westerns (Zane Grey's successors, Max Brand), aviation and war stories, romance, and the notorious "spicy" and "weird menace" (shudder) pulps of the 1930s filled out the racks.

Legacy

"Pulp" now also describes a style — lurid, breakneck, plot-driven — invoked by works such as Quentin Tarantino's film Pulp Fiction (1994). Scholars and collectors value the pulps as the seedbed of modern genre fiction, and many pulp stories have been reprinted in scholarly and popular editions.
