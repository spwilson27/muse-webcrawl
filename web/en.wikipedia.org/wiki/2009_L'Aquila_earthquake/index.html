The 2009 L'Aquila earthquake was a magnitude 6.3 earthquake that struck near the city of L'Aquila in the Abruzzo region of central Italy in the early hours of 6 April 2009. It killed 309 people, injured more than 1,500 and destroyed or damaged tens of thousands of buildings, making it Italy's deadliest earthquake since the 1980 Irpinia earthquake.

Event and damage

The main shock occurred at 03:32 local time, following months of minor foreshocks, and was felt across central Italy, including Rome. L'Aquila's historic centre — its cathedral, churches and the university's student dormitory, where eight students died — was devastated, as were outlying villages such as Onna and Paganica. Modern reinforced-concrete buildings that collapsed attracted particular scrutiny for poor construction and materials. Tens of thousands of residents were housed in tent camps and hotels for months.

Trial of the scientists

In a case that drew worldwide attention, six Italian scientists and one government official on the National Commission for the Forecast and Prevention of Major Risks were tried for manslaughter over statements made at a 31 March 2009 meeting, after which a civil protection official had described the seismic swarm as "favourable" and not dangerous. In October 2012 all seven were convicted and sentenced to six years in prison; on appeal in 2014 the scientists were acquitted, and Italy's Court of Cassation confirmed the acquittals in 2015, leaving only a reduced conviction for a civil protection official. The verdicts sparked debate about the communication of scientific uncertainty.

Reconstruction

Prime Minister Silvio Berlusconi's government promoted an emergency housing programme (the C.A.S.E. projects), criticised for cost and isolation, while restoration of L'Aquila's historic centre proceeded slowly and remained incomplete many years later. A state funeral at which Pope Benedict XVI later visited the city drew national attention to the disaster.
