Nominative–accusative language

A nominative–accusative language is a language in which the single argument (subject) of an intransitive verb and the agent (subject) of a transitive verb are treated grammatically alike (both in the nominative case), while the patient (object) of a transitive verb is treated differently (accusative case). This is the most common morphosyntactic alignment worldwide and is found in most European languages, including English.

Comparison with ergative–absolutive alignment

The contrasting system is ergative–absolutive alignment, in which the intransitive subject patterns with the transitive object (absolutive) rather than the transitive agent (ergative). In a nominative–accusative language, by contrast:

  • The dog (nom.) ran.
  • The dog (nom.) chased the cat (acc.).

Marking

Nominative–accusative alignment may be marked by case inflections on nouns (as in German, Russian or Japanese), by word order, or by agreement; English largely uses word order and pronoun case (I/me, she/her). Some languages mix alignments depending on tense, aspect or clause type.
