Opinio juris sive necessitatis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Opinio juris
Type: Element of customary international law
Field: Public international law
Source: Article 38(1)(b), ICJ Statute
Meaning: "An opinion of law or necessity"
The Statute of the International Court of Justice, whose Article 38 lists international custom "as evidence of a general practice accepted as law" among the sources of international law.

Opinio juris sive necessitatis ("an opinion of law or necessity") is the subjective element of customary international law: the belief by a state that a particular practice is required by, or consistent with, a legal obligation. Together with general state practice, it is one of the two elements that must be present for a rule of customary international law to arise.

The two-element test

Article 38(1)(b) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice directs the Court to apply "international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law". Courts and scholars read this as requiring (1) an objective element — a general and consistent practice of states — and (2) a subjective element, the opinio juris: the practice must be carried out in the belief that it is legally required or permitted, rather than from mere courtesy, convenience, or habit.

Evidence

Evidence of opinio juris may be found in states' official statements, diplomatic correspondence, national legislation, decisions of national courts, military manuals, and votes and statements in international organisations. Resolutions of the United Nations General Assembly may contribute to, or evidence, opinio juris, although they are not binding of themselves.

Key cases

In the North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969), the International Court of Justice held that acts must "amount to a settled practice" and be carried out "in such a way as to be evidence of a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law requiring it". In Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (1986), the Court inferred opinio juris in part from states' attitudes toward General Assembly resolutions. The Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion (1996) also discussed the concept in relation to the prohibition of the use of force.

Criticism

Scholars have noted a logical difficulty, sometimes called the "chronological paradox": if a practice must be accompanied by a belief that it is already law, it is hard to explain how a new customary rule can ever emerge, since the belief would initially be mistaken. A common answer is that states may act from a belief that the practice is becoming law, or from a sense of legal duty broadly understood.
