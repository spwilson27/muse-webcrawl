Wide-body aircraft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Passengers in a bright twin-aisle wide-body cabin]
Inside a wide-body cabin.

A wide-body aircraft (twin-aisle aircraft) is a jet airliner with a fuselage diameter wide enough for two passenger aisles, typically 5.6–6.6 metres across, seating seven to ten abreast in economy. The Boeing 747 created the category in 1970, driven by Pan Am\'s demand for capacity and by the economics of carrying freight in the belly hold. Wide-bodies dominate long-haul flying; types include the 777, 787, A330, A350 and the double-deck Airbus A380.

Origins

In the 1960s, Boeing, Douglas and Lockheed raced to build \'jumbos\': Boeing\'s 747 won with its raised cockpit and freight potential, while the DC-10 and Lockheed TriStar (both 1971) served medium routes. Wide-bodies let airlines slash seat-mile costs and carry containerised cargo below deck.

Design features

Two aisles speed boarding and service; underfloor holds take LD3 containers side by side (or LD2/LD1 on smaller types). Modern wide-body twins use composite wings and structures, bleedless systems (787) and engines of over 100,000 lbf thrust (GE9X for the 777X).

Market

Four-engine wide-bodies (747, A380) declined as twins matched their range and beat their economics; the A350 and 777 families lead new sales, with freighters (777F, 747-8F) a major segment. Wide-bodies also serve dense short routes in Asia.
