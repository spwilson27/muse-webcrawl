Foot-in-the-door technique

The technique is named after the door-to-door salesman's practice of preventing a door from being closed.

The foot-in-the-door technique (or FITD) is a compliance tactic that aims at getting a person to agree to a large request by first setting them up by having that person agree to a modest request. The theory is based on the principle of consistency: once a person has complied with a small request, they are more likely to comply with a larger related request, so as to remain consistent with their self-image as helpful or committed.

The original study

The technique was demonstrated by Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser in a 1966 experiment. Householders who had first agreed to a small request — answering a few questions or displaying a tiny sign about safe driving — were far more likely, when approached later, to agree to a much larger request (installing a huge, ugly "Drive Carefully" billboard in their front yard) than householders approached with the large request directly: about 76% complied in one condition versus roughly 17–20% of controls.

Explanations

The leading explanation is self-perception theory: having agreed to the small request, people infer that they are the kind of person who supports the cause, and this altered self-concept motivates later compliance. Commitment and consistency, as popularised by Robert Cialdini in Influence, provide a related account. Meta-analyses confirm a real but modest effect, which is strongest when the initial request is pro-social, when the two requests are similar, and when no external incentive (such as payment) undermines the person's sense of free choice.

Applications

The technique is used in fundraising (small initial donations paving the way for larger ones), marketing and sales, health behaviour change, and online design, where agreeing to a small step (creating an account, accepting a free trial) increases the odds of larger commitments. It contrasts with the door-in-the-face technique, which begins with an extreme request likely to be refused and retreats to a smaller one.
