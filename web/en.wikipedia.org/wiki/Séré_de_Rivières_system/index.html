The Séré de Rivières system was an ensemble of fortifications built across France from 1874 onwards, named after its designer, the French general and engineer Raymond Adolphe Séré de Rivières (1815–1895). Built in response to the French defeat in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, it formed rings of detached forts around strategic cities and a barrier of fortress-lines along the new eastern frontier.

Design

Rather than continuous city walls, the system used polygonal forts of masonry and earth, spaced to support one another with artillery fire, overlooking road and rail approaches. The strongpoints of Verdun, Toul, Épinal and Belfort anchored the frontier line, linked by curtain forts and intervals defended by field armies. The adoption of high-explosive shells in the 1880s (the "torpedo shell crisis") rendered masonry obsolete and forced costly upgrades with reinforced concrete and steel armoured turrets, as at the later forts around Verdun.

First World War

The forts saw heavy action in 1914–18. Some, like Liège's and Namur's Belgian counterparts (of related concept), fell quickly to super-heavy German howitzers; others became legendary during the Battle of Verdun in 1916, where Fort Douaumont was captured by a small German party and Fort Vaux resisted heroically under Major Raynal. The system influenced later French thinking that culminated in the Maginot Line.
