The Tribe of Levi is one of the Twelve Tribes of Israel, traditionally descended from Levi, the third son of Jacob and Leah. Unlike the other tribes, the Levites received no contiguous territory in the Land of Israel; instead, they were set apart for religious service, assisting the priests at the sanctuary and later at the Temple in Jerusalem. The priestly family within the tribe, the Kohanim, claimed descent from Aaron, brother of Moses.

Biblical narrative

According to the Hebrew Bible, Levi was one of the founders of the Israelite nation. In the Book of Genesis, Levi and his brother Simeon are rebuked by Jacob for their violent revenge against Shechem after the rape of their sister Dinah. Jacob's deathbed blessing in Genesis 49 predicts that Levi's descendants will be "scattered in Israel" — a prophecy the biblical authors read as fulfilled by the Levites' dispersed settlement pattern.

During the Exodus, the Levites distinguished themselves, in the biblical account, by their zeal after the incident of the golden calf (Exodus 32), rallying to Moses and executing judgment on the idolaters. As a reward, the tribe was consecrated to divine service. The Book of Numbers records that the Levites were taken in place of the firstborn of Israel and assigned duties around the Tabernacle: transporting it, guarding it, and assisting the priests.

Divisions and duties

The Levites were divided into three clans named for Levi's sons: the Gershonites, the Kohathites, and the Merarites. The Kohathites included the family of Aaron, from whom the priests (Kohanim) were drawn, as well as the families of Moses and of the prophet Samuel. Non-priestly Levites served as musicians, gatekeepers, treasurers, judges, and teachers; the Books of Chronicles describe David organizing them into courses for Temple service, including the celebrated singers Asaph, Heman, and Jeduthun.

Levitical cities

Because the tribe held no tribal allotment — "the LORD is their inheritance," as Deuteronomy puts it — the Levites were granted 48 cities scattered among the other tribes, together with surrounding pasture land (Numbers 35; Joshua 21). Six of these were designated cities of refuge, where those guilty of manslaughter could flee from the avenger of blood. The Levites were supported by tithes of produce and livestock from the other tribes.

In later Judaism

After the Babylonian exile and the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the practical functions of the Levites largely lapsed, but the status continued to be hereditary in traditional Judaism. In Orthodox and Conservative synagogue practice, a Levite is called second to the Torah reading (after a Kohen) and traditionally washes the hands of the priests before the priestly blessing. The surnames Levi, Levy, Halevi, Levin, and similar often indicate Levitical descent, though not invariably.
