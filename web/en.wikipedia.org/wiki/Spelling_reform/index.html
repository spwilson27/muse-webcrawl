Spelling reform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reformers have long sought to make spelling match pronunciation

A spelling reform is a deliberate, often officially sanctioned change of spelling rules for a language, generally intended to make spelling more regular, more phonetic, or easier to learn. Reforms range from small adjustments of individual words to complete changes of script.

English

English spelling is famously irregular, and reform attempts date back centuries. Noah Webster's American dictionaries (early nineteenth century) established many enduring American spellings such as color for colour and center for centre. In 1906 the Simplified Spelling Board, funded by Andrew Carnegie, persuaded President Theodore Roosevelt to order some 300 simplified spellings in United States government documents, though Congress resisted and the order was soon dropped. More radical proposals have included George Bernard Shaw's advocacy that led to the Shavian alphabet, and systems such as Cut Spelling and SR1.

Other languages

Many languages have undergone successful state-led reforms. Germany's spelling reform of 1996 (revised in 2004 and 2006) adjusted rules across the German-speaking countries; the Netherlands revises official spelling periodically. Some reforms replace the writing system itself, most famously Turkey's adoption of a Latin-based alphabet in 1928. Simplified Chinese characters, promulgated in the 1950s, are an analogous regularisation of a logographic script.

Arguments

Supporters cite literacy, ease of learning and the mismatch between speech and writing. Opponents point to the disruption of existing literature, the loss of etymological information, dialect differences that make a single "phonetic" spelling impossible, and the entrenchment of current conventions.
