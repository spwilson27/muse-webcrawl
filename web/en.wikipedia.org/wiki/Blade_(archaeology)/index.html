Blade (archaeology)
DefinitionStone flake at least twice as long as it is wide, with near-parallel edges
MaterialFlint, chert, obsidian and other fine-grained stone
Produced byBlade core reduction (direct or indirect percussion, pressure flaking)
Prominent fromUpper Paleolithic; also Mesoamerican prismatic blades

In archaeology, a blade is a specialised type of lithic flake that is at least twice as long as it is wide, with parallel or sub-parallel edges and a regular cross-section. Blades are struck from prepared stone cores using techniques that allow many standardised blanks to be produced from a single piece of raw material.

Production

Blade production begins with shaping a core — often prismatic or conical — with a prepared striking platform and guiding ridges. Blades are then removed in series by indirect percussion with a punch, or, in later and especially Mesoamerican industries, by pressure flaking. The resulting blanks are efficient: a blade core yields far more cutting edge per unit of stone than earlier flake technologies.

Use and significance

Blades could be used as knives with little modification, or retouched into end-scrapers, burins, backed blades and microliths, and set into hafts as composite tools. Systematic blade production is a hallmark of Upper Paleolithic industries such as the Aurignacian and Gravettian in Europe. In pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, prismatic obsidian blades — razor-sharp and mass-produced by specialist craftspeople — served as knives, razors and ritual bloodletting instruments, and were traded over long distances.
