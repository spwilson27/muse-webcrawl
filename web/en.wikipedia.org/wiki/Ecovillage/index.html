An ecovillage is a traditional or intentional community whose goal is to become more socially, culturally, economically and ecologically sustainable. Ecovillages aim to produce most of their food, energy and material needs locally, minimise their ecological footprint, and regenerate their environments, while maintaining a high quality of life. A widely cited definition by Robert Gilman (1991) describes an ecovillage as a "human-scale, full-featured settlement, in which human activities are harmlessly integrated into the natural world in a way that is supportive of healthy human development and can be successfully continued into the indefinite future."

History

The modern movement drew on the communal experiments of the 1960s–70s — Findhorn in Scotland (founded 1962), Auroville in India (1968), The Farm in Tennessee (1971) — together with permaculture (co-founded by Bill Mollison) and green politics. The term "ecovillage" was popularised by Robert and Diane Gilman in their 1991 report Eco-Villages and Sustainable Communities for the Gaia Trust. In 1995 the Global Ecovillage Network (GEN) was founded at a conference at Findhorn, linking thousands of communities; GEN now has consultative status with the UN Economic and Social Council and works with the UN Institute for Training and Research on community-based climate adaptation.

Principles

GEN identifies dimensions of sustainability that ecovillages work across:

  • Ecological — organic local food production, renewable energy, natural building (straw-bale, cob, timber), water recycling, habitat restoration and permaculture design.
  • Social — shared governance (often consensus or sociocracy), conflict facilitation, shared meals and facilities, diversity and inclusivity.
  • Cultural — ritual, art, celebration, mindfulness and connection to place; many communities have a spiritual strand (Findhorn, Damanhur) while others are secular.
  • Economic — local currencies or exchange systems, cooperative businesses, social enterprise, and sharing of cars, tools and land.

Studies (such as those by the Global Ecovillage Research Project and residents' "Community Sustainability Assessment") suggest established ecovillages can achieve ecological footprints roughly half the national average of their host countries, largely through shared infrastructure, low meat diets, and reduced car dependence, though results vary and systematic measurement is limited.

Examples

  • Findhorn Ecovillage, Moray, Scotland — one of the oldest; known for its Living Machine water treatment and low-footprint housing.
  • Auroville, Tamil Nadu, India — a large international township (target population 50,000; actual residents a few thousand) founded by Mirra Alfassa ("The Mother") in the Sri Aurobindo tradition.
  • Ecovillage at Ithaca, New York — cohousing neighbourhoods noted for very low energy use.
  • Sieben Linden, Saxony-Anhalt, Germany — straw-bale houses and largely vegan self-provisioning.
  • Svanholm and Christiania (Denmark), Torri Superiore (Italy), Dancing Rabbit (Missouri), Crystal Waters (Australia).
