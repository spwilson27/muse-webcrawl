Coercive control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coercive control is a pattern of behaviour, not a single incident

Coercive control is a pattern of controlling behaviours used by one person to dominate another, typically an intimate partner, through intimidation, isolation, degradation and regulation of everyday life. The concept was developed by the American sociologist Evan Stark, whose 2007 book Coercive Control: How Men Entrap Women in Personal Life reframed much intimate-partner abuse not as a series of discrete assaults but as a continuing "liberty crime" akin to hostage-taking, in which the victim's autonomy is progressively stripped away.

Characteristics

Behaviours associated with coercive control include isolating the victim from friends, family and support; monitoring movements, communications and spending (increasingly through technology such as location tracking and spyware); controlling money, food, sleep, clothing and access to transport or work; threats, humiliation and "gaslighting"; micro-regulation of daily routines backed by punishment; and harm or threats toward children, pets or property. Physical violence may be frequent, occasional, or absent — Stark argued that injury-centred definitions of domestic abuse miss the primary harm, which is entrapment.

Legal recognition

England and Wales created a specific offence of controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship in section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015, punishable by up to five years' imprisonment; the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 extended recognition and removed the cohabitation requirement. Scotland's Domestic Abuse (Scotland) Act 2018 criminalises a course of abusive behaviour toward a partner or ex-partner and is widely cited as model legislation. Ireland (2018), France, and several Australian states (notably New South Wales, with an offence commenced in 2024, and Queensland) have also enacted coercive-control offences, following campaigns after high-profile murders such as that of Hannah Clarke and her children in 2020. In the United States the concept is incorporated into some state family-law and domestic-violence statutes, including definitions in California and Connecticut ("Jennifer's Law", 2021).

Debate

Supporters argue that pattern-based offences let police intervene before abuse escalates to homicide. Critics raise concerns about evidential difficulty, over-criminalisation, misidentification of the primary aggressor, and disproportionate effects on marginalised communities.
