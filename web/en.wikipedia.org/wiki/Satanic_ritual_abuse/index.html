Satanic ritual abuse

Satanic ritual abuse (SRA), also called the Satanic panic, was a moral panic that spread through the United States and other English-speaking countries in the 1980s and early 1990s. It consisted of widespread allegations that organised networks of Satanists were ritually abusing children in day-care centres and secret ceremonies. Despite thousands of accusations and numerous prosecutions, investigations — including an FBI review by agent Kenneth Lanning (1992) — found no evidence of the alleged organised conspiracies, and the episode is now widely regarded by scholars and law-enforcement bodies as a moral panic driven by suggestive interviewing, recovered-memory therapy and media amplification.

Origins

The panic is usually dated to the 1980 book Michelle Remembers, in which Michelle Smith and her psychiatrist Lawrence Pazder described alleged recovered memories of ritual abuse; its claims were later discredited. Fundamentalist religious movements, anti-cult networks and some therapists spread the framework through seminars for police and social workers.

Major cases

The McMartin preschool trial in California (1983–1990) became the longest and most expensive criminal trial in US history and ended with no convictions. Other prominent cases included Kern County, Minnesota's Jordan cases, the West Memphis Three (later released), and in Britain the Orkney and Rochdale cases of 1990–91, where children were removed from their homes on allegations that collapsed. Many convictions obtained in the era were later overturned.

Aftermath and analysis

Official inquiries in the US, UK, Netherlands and elsewhere concluded there was no corroborating evidence for intergenerational Satanic cult networks. Psychologists linked the phenomenon to false-memory formation, moral panic theory and the day-care sex-abuse hysteria of the period. The panic left lasting damage: ruined careers, wrongful imprisonments and diverted child-protection resources, and it remains a standard case study in sociology and law.
