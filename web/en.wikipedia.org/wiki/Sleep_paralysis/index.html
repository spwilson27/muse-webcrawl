Sleep paralysis

Sleep paralysis is a state, during waking up or falling asleep, in which a person is conscious but unable to move or speak. Episodes last from seconds to a few minutes and result from the normal muscle atonia of REM sleep persisting into wakefulness. Many people also experience vivid hypnagogic or hypnopompic hallucinations — a sensed presence, a figure on the chest, floating sensations — accompanied by intense fear.

Between roughly 8% and 30% of people experience sleep paralysis at least once in their lives, with higher rates among students and people with disrupted sleep. Risk factors include sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedules, sleeping on one's back, stress, and conditions such as narcolepsy, in which isolated sleep paralysis is one of the classic tetrad of symptoms.

Mechanism

During REM sleep the brainstem actively inhibits motor neurons to prevent acting out dreams. Sleep paralysis occurs when consciousness is restored before this inhibition lifts, while dream imagery intrudes into waking perception.

Cultural interpretations

Throughout history, sleep paralysis has been interpreted through local folklore: the "old hag" of Newfoundland, kanashibari in Japan, the incubus and succubus of medieval Europe, jinn attacks, and in recent decades, alien abduction accounts. Henry Fuseli's 1781 painting The Nightmare is a famous artistic rendering of the experience.

Management

There is no specific cure for isolated episodes; reassurance, regular sleep, and treating underlying sleep disorders reduce frequency. In distressing recurrent cases, certain antidepressants that suppress REM sleep are sometimes used.
