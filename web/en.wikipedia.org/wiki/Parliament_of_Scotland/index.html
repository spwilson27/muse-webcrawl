Parliament of Scotland

TypeUnicameral (Three Estates)
Established13th century (c. 1235 first recorded)
Disbanded1 May 1707 (Acts of Union)
SeatParliament House, Edinburgh (from 1639); previously various towns
SuccessorParliament of Great Britain; (modern) Scottish Parliament (est. 1999)

The Parliament of Scotland (Scots: Pairlament o Scotland) was the legislature of the Kingdom of Scotland from the early 13th century until 1707, when it merged with the Parliament of England to form the Parliament of Great Britain under the Acts of Union. Unlike the bicameral English parliament, it was unicameral: clergy, nobility, and burgh commissioners — the Three Estates — sat together in a single chamber, along with shire commissioners added in the 16th century.

Parliament House, Edinburgh (built 1632–39), now home to the Court of Session.

History

Parliaments grew out of the king's feudal council; an assembly identifiable as a parliament is recorded in 1235. Burgh representatives were first summoned in 1326 (the parliament of Cambuskenneth), when taxation was required. Through the 15th and 16th centuries parliament's role expanded, though its agenda was controlled by the Lords of the Articles, a steering committee whose dominance became a major political grievance. The Scottish Reformation Parliament of 1560 abolished papal authority. During the Covenanting period (1639–51) parliament asserted exceptional authority, abolishing the Lords of the Articles and legislating without royal assent. After the Restoration the committee was restored, but in 1690 it was abolished for good, and the 1690s–1700s saw an assertive parliament pass measures such as the Act of Security (1704).

The Union

By the Act of Union 1707, passed by the Scottish parliament amid intense controversy, economic pressure (including the failure of the Darien scheme and the English Alien Act), and allegations of bribery, the parliament voted itself out of existence; its last sitting was on 25 March 1707, and the Union took effect on 1 May 1707. Lord Chancellor Seafield famously remarked it was "the end of an auld sang." Scotland thereafter sent 45 MPs and 16 representative peers to Westminster.

Legacy

Parliament House in Edinburgh survives as the seat of Scotland's supreme courts. The name and institutional memory were revived when the modern Scottish Parliament was established in 1999 following the Scotland Act 1998, sitting first in the General Assembly Hall and from 2004 in its new building at Holyrood.
