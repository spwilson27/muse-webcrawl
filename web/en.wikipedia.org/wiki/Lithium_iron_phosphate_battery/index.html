Lithium iron phosphate battery

Prismatic LFP battery cells

The lithium iron phosphate battery (LiFePO4 battery), or LFP battery, is a type of lithium-ion battery using lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) as the cathode material and a graphitic carbon anode. LFP batteries are known for their thermal and chemical stability, long cycle life, and low cost, at the expense of somewhat lower energy density than nickel-based lithium-ion chemistries.

History

LiFePO4 was identified as a cathode material in 1996 by Akshaya Padhi, Kirakodu Nanjundaswamy, and John Goodenough at the University of Texas at Austin. Its intrinsic electrical conductivity is low, but this was overcome by carbon coating and reducing particle size, enabling commercialisation in the 2000s by companies such as A123 Systems and BYD.

Advantages and disadvantages

LFP batteries offer excellent safety — the strong P–O covalent bonds in the phosphate cathode resist oxygen release, making thermal runaway much less likely than with cobalt or nickel-rich cathodes. They tolerate full charge, operate over a wide temperature range, and can sustain many thousands of charge cycles, giving a long service life. Iron and phosphate are abundant and cheap, and the chemistry contains no cobalt or nickel, easing supply-chain and ethical concerns.

The main drawbacks are lower nominal voltage (3.2 V versus 3.7 V for many other lithium-ion cells) and lower energy density, which made LFP initially unsuitable for some space-constrained uses. Improvements such as cell-to-pack designs (e.g., BYD's Blade Battery) have narrowed the gap.

Applications

LFP has become dominant in entry-level electric vehicles (including many Tesla and Chinese EVs), stationary energy storage, electric buses, solar home systems, marine and RV house batteries, and backup power. By the early 2020s it accounted for a large and growing share of the global EV battery market, particularly in China.
