Health effects from noise

Noise health effects are the health consequences of regular exposure, to consistent elevated sound levels. Elevated workplace or environmental noise can cause hearing impairment, tinnitus, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, annoyance and sleep disturbance. Changes in the immune system and birth defects have also been attributed to noise exposure.

Hearing loss

Noise-induced hearing loss is a permanent sensorineural loss caused by damage to the hair cells of the cochlea. Prolonged exposure above about 85 dB(A) (an 8-hour time-weighted average) progressively increases risk; occupational standards from bodies such as OSHA and NIOSH set exposure limits and require hearing conservation programmes. Sudden impulse noise (e.g. gunfire, explosions) can cause immediate acoustic trauma.

Cardiovascular and other effects

Chronic environmental noise — particularly road traffic and aircraft noise — is associated in epidemiological studies with raised blood pressure, increased risk of myocardial infarction and stroke, and metabolic effects. Proposed mechanisms involve stress responses, including cortisol and catecholamine release and sleep fragmentation. Night-time noise disrupts sleep architecture even without awakening, and long-term sleep disturbance is linked to impaired cognition and children's learning, notably reading delay in children exposed to high aircraft noise around schools (the RANCH study).

Burden and regulation

The World Health Organization has estimated that at least one million healthy life years are lost annually in Western Europe from traffic-related noise, and its 2018 Environmental Noise Guidelines recommend average road-traffic noise below 53 dB Lden. Regulation takes the form of occupational exposure limits, land-use planning around airports, vehicle and tyre noise standards, and noise mapping under instruments such as the EU Environmental Noise Directive.
