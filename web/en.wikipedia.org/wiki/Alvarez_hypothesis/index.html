Alvarez Hypothesis

The Alvarez hypothesis proposes that the mass extinction that killed the non-avian dinosaurs and many other species at the end of the Cretaceous period, about 66 million years ago, was caused by the impact of a large asteroid or comet with the Earth. Proposed in 1980 by physicist Luis Alvarez, his geologist son Walter Alvarez, and chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, it is now the widely accepted explanation for the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) extinction event.

The impact of a roughly 10 km asteroid is held to have caused the K–Pg mass extinction.

The iridium evidence

Studying a thin clay layer at the K–Pg boundary near Gubbio, Italy, the Alvarez team found concentrations of the rare element iridium many times higher than normal crustal levels. Because iridium is abundant in asteroids but rare in Earth's crust, they proposed a global fallout layer from a giant impact, and estimated an impactor about 10 km across. The iridium layer was subsequently found at boundary sites worldwide, along with shocked quartz and impact spherules.

The crater

In 1991 the buried Chicxulub crater on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula — about 180 km in diameter — was identified and dated to the time of the extinction, providing the "smoking gun." The impact would have ejected vast quantities of sulfur-rich debris and dust into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight, collapsing photosynthesis and food chains, and causing a global "impact winter," compounded by wildfires and acid rain.

Debate and acceptance

The hypothesis was initially controversial; some paleontologists favored gradual causes, notably the massive Deccan Traps volcanism in India, as the primary driver, and debate continues over the relative contributions. Nonetheless, impact is now regarded by most scientists as the principal cause of the extinction of roughly 75% of Earth's species at the boundary.
