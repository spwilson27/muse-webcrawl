Tree of the knowledge of good and evil

In Judaism and Christianity, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Hebrew: עֵץ הַדַּעַת טוֹב וָרָע, 'éts hadda'ath tov vará') is one of two specific trees in the story of the Garden of Eden in Genesis 2–3, along with the tree of life. God forbade Adam to eat of its fruit; after Adam and Eve ate it anyway—tempted by the serpent—they were expelled from the garden, an event known as the Fall of man.

The narrative

Genesis 2 places the tree in the midst of the garden and records the command: "of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die." In Genesis 3 the serpent tells Eve that eating will open their eyes and make them "as gods, knowing good and evil." The couple eat, become aware of their nakedness, and are banished so that they cannot also eat from the tree of life and live forever.

Interpretations

The phrase "good and evil" has been read as a merism meaning knowledge of everything, as moral discernment, or as knowledge reserved to God. Jewish commentary often understands the forbidden fruit test as one of obedience; Christian theology connects the act with original sin. The identity of the fruit is unspecified in the text—traditions have proposed the fig, grape, wheat, or (in Western art, partly through a Latin pun on malum) the apple.

In art and culture

The tree, usually depicted with the serpent coiled around it, is among the most frequently represented images of Western art, from medieval manuscripts to Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling.
