Derandomization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Derandomization is the process of converting a randomised algorithm into a deterministic one, or of reducing the amount of randomness an algorithm uses, while preserving its performance. It is a central theme of computational complexity theory, connected with the question of whether randomness actually adds power to computation — for polynomial-time algorithms, the question of whether P = BPP.

Approaches

Common derandomization techniques include:

  • Conditional probabilities: choosing random bits one at a time to fix them so that the expected success of the remaining choices stays high, turning probabilistic existence proofs (the probabilistic method) into deterministic constructions
  • Pairwise and limited independence: many algorithms need far less randomness than fully independent bits
  • Pseudorandom generators (PRGs): deterministic functions that stretch a short truly random seed into a long string indistinguishable to efficient tests. The Nisan–Wigderson generator shows that if certain hard functions exist, then BPP = P
  • Expander graphs and random walks: replacing many random bits by a walk on an expander, used for example in the derandomisation of undirected connectivity (Reingold's theorem, SL = L)

Notable results

The AKS primality test (2002) gave a deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for a problem previously solved efficiently only with randomness, and is frequently cited as a landmark derandomization. Many complexity theorists conjecture that P = BPP — that randomness can always be removed at polynomial cost — because sufficiently strong circuit lower bounds would imply strong pseudorandom generators, a "hardness versus randomness" trade-off. Derandomization techniques have also yielded deterministic approximation algorithms and explicit combinatorial constructions such as expanders and error-correcting codes.
