Liang dynasty

The Liang dynasty (502–557), also known as the Southern Liang, was an imperial dynasty of China during the Northern and Southern dynasties period. It was founded by Xiao Yan, Emperor Wu, who deposed the last emperor of the Southern Qi in 502 and ruled from Jiankang (modern Nanjing). The Liang was the third and longest-lived of the southern dynasties, and under its early decades southern China enjoyed stability, economic prosperity, and a flourishing Buddhist culture.

Emperor Wu

Emperor Wu reigned for 47 years, an unusually long tenure. A scholar and poet, he was a devout Buddhist who built temples, sponsored translations of sutras, prohibited animal sacrifice in imperial rites, and several times "gave himself" to a monastery, requiring his court to ransom him back with vast donations. His patronage made Jiankang one of the great Buddhist centres of East Asia, and exchanges with India and the northern Wei flourished. Late in his reign, however, he tolerated corruption among imperial princes and neglected military defence of the Huai frontier.

Hou Jing's rebellion and collapse

In 548 the renegade Eastern Wei general Hou Jing, who had been received as a defector, rose in rebellion, besieged Jiankang, and starved the capital into surrender in 549. Emperor Wu died in captivity, and Hou Jing ruled through puppet emperors before proclaiming his own short-lived Han dynasty. The general Wang Sengbian and Chen Baxian eventually crushed the rebellion, but the Liang had been shattered; rival Liang courts persisted in the west, where a rump "Later Liang" survived under Northern Zhou and Sui protection until 587. Chen Baxian seized the throne in 557, founding the Chen dynasty.

Culture

The Liang court produced the Wen Xuan, the great anthology of literature compiled under Crown Prince Xiao Tong (昭明太子), and the literary criticism of Liu Xie and Zhong Rong matured in this period. The imperial tomb sculptures south of Nanjing — winged stone beasts and inscribed pillars — are masterpieces of southern dynasties art.
