Thermography

Infrared thermography (IRT), thermal video, or thermal imaging, is a process where a thermal camera converts infrared radiation emitted from an object into an electronic signal, which is then processed to produce a thermal image on a video monitor. The calculated heat variations are displayed in various colours or shades of grey. Thermography allows identification of environmental and physical conditions through characterization of heat patterns.

All objects with a temperature above absolute zero emit infrared radiation, and the amount of radiation increases with temperature. Thermographic cameras detect radiation in the infrared range of the electromagnetic spectrum (roughly 9,000–14,000 nanometres or 9–14 μm) and produce images of that radiation, called thermograms. Since infrared radiation is emitted by all objects with a temperature above absolute zero according to the black body radiation law, thermography makes it possible to see one's environment with or without visible illumination.

History

Infrared radiation was discovered in 1800 by the astronomer Sir William Herschel, who found a type of invisible radiation in the spectrum beyond red light while studying sunlight passing through a prism. The first practical thermal imaging cameras were developed for military use during and after the Second World War, and the technology was gradually declassified for civilian applications in the 1950s and 1960s.

Applications

Thermography is used in a wide range of fields:

  • Building diagnostics — detecting heat loss, insulation defects, air leakage, and moisture intrusion in structures.
  • Predictive maintenance — identifying overheating electrical connections, motors, and mechanical components before failure.
  • Medicine — as an adjunct screening tool, including breast thermography.
  • Firefighting — seeing through smoke, locating hotspots, and finding people in burning buildings.
  • Law enforcement and military — night vision, surveillance, and target acquisition.
  • Process monitoring — checking temperatures in manufacturing, such as steel, glass, and cement production.

Types of devices

Thermal imagers fall into two main categories: cooled infrared detectors, which operate at cryogenic temperatures and offer higher sensitivity and resolution, and uncooled detectors, typically based on microbolometer arrays, which are cheaper and smaller but less sensitive. Modern uncooled cameras have largely closed the performance gap for many commercial applications.
