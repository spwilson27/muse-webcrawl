Subfornical organ

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The subfornical organ (SFO) is a small sensory structure in the brain, located on the underside of the fornix near the interventricular foramina at the roof of the third ventricle. It is one of the circumventricular organs — regions where the blood–brain barrier is incomplete, allowing neurons to directly sample the composition of the blood.

Function

Because its capillaries are fenestrated, the subfornical organ can detect circulating signals that cannot cross the blood–brain barrier elsewhere. It is best known for its sensitivity to angiotensin II, a peptide hormone of the renin–angiotensin system. When angiotensin II rises — as it does when blood volume or pressure falls — SFO neurons are activated and drive responses that restore fluid balance: the sensation of thirst, salt appetite, drinking behaviour, release of the antidiuretic hormone vasopressin, and increased sympathetic outflow raising blood pressure.

The SFO also responds to other circulating factors, including relaxin, leptin, and inflammatory mediators, and it is implicated in fever generation, energy balance, and cardiovascular regulation. It projects to the median preoptic nucleus, the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus, and other autonomic centres, forming, together with the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis, a key sensory network for body-fluid homeostasis.

Clinical relevance

Abnormal signalling through the subfornical organ has been implicated in forms of hypertension, particularly salt-sensitive and neurogenic hypertension, and in the excessive drinking seen in some disorders of fluid balance.
