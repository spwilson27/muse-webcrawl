Matthias Grünewald (c. 1470 – 1528) was a German Renaissance painter of religious works, regarded as one of the greatest painters of his era. His real name was Mathis Gothart; the name "Grünewald" derives from a 17th-century biographical error by Joachim von Sandrart. Almost alone among major artists of his time, he largely ignored the classical revival and Italianate ideals of the High Renaissance, working instead in a late-medieval style intensified by extraordinary emotional expressiveness and dramatic colour.

Life

Little is documented of Grünewald's life. He worked in and around Würzburg, Frankfurt, Aschaffenburg and Mainz, served as court painter and engineer to the archbishops of Mainz, and undertook hydraulic engineering work as well as painting. His career suffered during the German Peasants' War (1524–25), with which he had Lutheran sympathies; he left Mainz service and died at Halle in 1528. Only a small body of work — around ten paintings and some 35 drawings — survives with secure attribution.

The Isenheim Altarpiece

His masterpiece is the Isenheim Altarpiece (1512–1516), painted for the monastery of St. Anthony at Isenheim in Alsace, now in the Unterlinden Museum in Colmar. The Antonite monks specialised in treating skin diseases including ergotism ("St Anthony's fire"), and the altarpiece's graphic imagery — above all its harrowing Crucifixion, with Christ's lacerated, plague-marked body — was intended to console and offer hope of healing to suffering patients. Its other wings include the luminous Annunciation, the Resurrection, the Madonna and Child with a concert of angels, and the Temptation of St Anthony.

Reception

Forgotten for centuries, Grünewald was rediscovered in the late 19th and early 20th centuries and became a hero of the German Expressionists, who saw in his distortions, saturated colour and emotional intensity a precursor of modern art. Paul Hindemith's opera and symphony Mathis der Maler (1934–38) dramatise his life amid the turmoil of the Reformation era.
