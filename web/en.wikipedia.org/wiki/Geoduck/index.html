Geoduck

Geoduck
Scientific name: Panopea generosa
Family: Hiatellidae
Native range: Pacific coast of Canada and the United States
Lifespan: up to 168 years

The Pacific geoduck (Panopea generosa) is a species of very large saltwater clam in the family Hiatellidae. The common name is derived from a Lushootseed (Nisqually) word meaning "dig deep". Native to the coastal waters of the eastern North Pacific Ocean from Alaska to Baja California, the geoduck is one of the largest burrowing clams in the world, notable for its very long siphon ("neck") and its exceptional longevity.

Description

The shell of an adult geoduck ranges from 15 to over 20 cm (6 to 8 in) in length, but the extremely long siphons make the clam appear much larger; larger specimens can weigh over 7 kg (15 lb). The siphon cannot be retracted into the shell, giving the animal its distinctive appearance. Geoducks are among the longest-living animals, with the oldest recorded specimen aged 168 years; females produce enormous numbers of eggs over a lifetime spanning a century or more.

Habitat and life cycle

Geoducks burrow into muddy and sandy substrates in the low intertidal and subtidal zones, extending their siphons to the surface to filter-feed on phytoplankton. They broadcast spawn in late spring and summer; the larvae drift for several weeks before settling and burrowing. Growth is rapid at first and slows with age.

Fishery and cuisine

The geoduck supports a valuable commercial fishery in Washington State and British Columbia, with much of the harvest exported to Asia, where the clam is regarded as a delicacy, eaten as sashimi or in hot pot. Geoduck is also farmed in intertidal aquaculture operations. The animal is the mascot of The Evergreen State College in Olympia, Washington.
