Sir James Clark Ross (15 April 1800 – 3 April 1862) was a British Royal Navy officer and polar explorer known for his expeditions to both the Arctic and Antarctic. In 1831 he located the North Magnetic Pole, and between 1839 and 1843 he commanded the expedition that discovered the Ross Sea, the Ross Ice Shelf, and Victoria Land in Antarctica.

Early career and Arctic service

Ross entered the navy in 1812 under his uncle Sir John Ross, serving on Arctic voyages searching for the Northwest Passage in 1818 and on four expeditions under William Edward Parry between 1819 and 1827. On the 1829–1833 expedition of his uncle John Ross aboard the Victory, James Clark Ross led the sledge party that reached the North Magnetic Pole on 1 June 1831, on the Boothia Peninsula, raising the Union Flag there.

Antarctic expedition

From 1839 to 1843 Ross commanded HMS Erebus and HMS Terror on an expedition to conduct magnetic observations in the southern hemisphere and explore the Antarctic. He discovered the Ross Sea, Victoria Land, and the active volcanoes Mount Erebus and Mount Terror (named for his ships), and charted the great ice barrier later known as the Ross Ice Shelf, reaching a farthest south of about 78°10′S. The expedition's scientific results, including Joseph Dalton Hooker's botany of the southern islands, were published over many years.

Later life

Ross was knighted in 1843 and promoted to rear admiral in 1856. In 1848 he commanded one of the first expeditions sent to search for Sir John Franklin's lost Arctic expedition, wintering at Port Leopold without success. The Ross Sea, Ross Island, the Ross Ice Shelf, James Ross Island, and the lunar crater Ross are named in his honour; the British polar research ship RRS Sir David Attenborough succeeded earlier vessels named for him, including RRS James Clark Ross.
