In statistics, a contingency table (also known as a cross tabulation or crosstab) is a type of table in a matrix format that displays the (multivariate) frequency distribution of the variables. They are heavily used in survey research, business intelligence, engineering, and scientific research. They provide a basic picture of the interrelation between two or more variables and can help find interactions between them.

Structure

Each cell of the table represents the count (frequency) of observations falling into the corresponding combination of categories of the row and column variables. The row and column totals are called marginal totals. The simplest case, a 2 × 2 table, cross-classifies a sample by two binary variables, as in the classic example of treatment (drug vs. placebo) against outcome (recovered vs. not). Tables may be extended to r × c arrays and to multi-way tables combining three or more variables.

Measures of association

Various statistics summarize association in a contingency table, including the odds ratio, relative risk, the phi coefficient and Cramér's V. For ordinal variables, measures such as Kendall's tau and Goodman and Kruskal's gamma exploit the ordering of categories. Marginal homogeneity and symmetry are of interest in square tables arising from paired data.

Tests of independence

Pearson's chi-squared test compares observed counts with the counts expected under the hypothesis that the row and column variables are independent; expected counts are computed from the marginal totals. For small samples, Fisher's exact test gives an exact p-value based on the hypergeometric distribution. The G-test (likelihood-ratio test) is an alternative with a similar asymptotic chi-squared distribution. Log-linear models generalize the analysis to multi-way tables.
