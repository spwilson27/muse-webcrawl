Legal death

[Image: Death certificate]

Legal death is the recognition under the law of a jurisdiction that a person is dead. It determines when a person's legal rights and obligations end: their estate may be distributed, a marriage is dissolved, insurance and pension benefits become payable, and their organs may become eligible for donation. Legal death is usually evidenced by a death certificate issued after certification of death by a medical practitioner or coroner.

Criteria

Historically, death was determined by the irreversible cessation of breathing and circulation. Advances in resuscitation, mechanical ventilation and organ transplantation in the mid-twentieth century made that standard insufficient, leading to the concept of brain death: the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. The Harvard Medical School committee report of 1968 and, in the United States, the Uniform Determination of Death Act (1981) established that an individual is dead if they have sustained either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain. Most jurisdictions worldwide have adopted some version of these criteria, although diagnostic protocols vary.

Presumed death

Where a person has disappeared and is believed dead but no body has been recovered, courts may issue a declaration of presumed death after a statutory period of unexplained absence — traditionally seven years at common law, though the period varies by jurisdiction and may be shorter in cases of specific peril (such as a shipwreck or air crash). Such declarations allow the missing person's affairs to be settled; rare cases of people "returning from the dead" create complex legal problems involving remarriage and property.

Related concepts

Legal death is distinct from civil death, a historical penalty by which a person lost their legal rights while still alive, and from clinical death, a medical term for the cessation of blood circulation and breathing, which may be reversible. Disputes over the determination of death have arisen in high-profile cases involving patients maintained on life support, raising ethical and religious questions about the boundary between life and death.
