Riemann–Roch theorem

The Riemann–Roch theorem is an important theorem in mathematics, specifically in complex analysis and algebraic geometry, for the computation of the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions with prescribed zeroes and poles on a compact Riemann surface. It is named after Bernhard Riemann and his student Gustav Roch, who proved the complementary part of the formula in his 1865 dissertation.

Statement

For a divisor D on a compact Riemann surface X of genus g, the theorem states:

l(D) − l(K − D) = deg(D) + 1 − g,

where l(D) is the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions whose poles are no worse than those allowed by D, K is the canonical divisor, and deg(D) is the degree of the divisor. Riemann proved the inequality ≥, and Roch supplied the remaining correction term.

Consequences and generalizations

The theorem connects analytic properties of a curve (spaces of functions) with purely topological data (the genus) and is fundamental in the classification of algebraic curves. Consequences include Riemann's existence bounds for functions with given poles and criteria for a curve to be embedded in projective space. Twentieth-century generalizations — the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem for morphisms of schemes, and the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, of which Riemann–Roch can be viewed as a special case — are pillars of modern algebraic geometry and mathematical physics.
