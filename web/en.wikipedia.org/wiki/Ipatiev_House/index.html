Ipatiev House

The Ipatiev House (Russian: Дом Ипатьева) was a wooden and brick house in Yekaterinburg, Russia, where the former Russian Emperor Nicholas II, his family, and several retainers were held prisoner and murdered by Bolshevik revolutionaries on the night of 16–17 July 1918. The house belonged to the mining engineer Nikolai Ipatiev, from whom it was requisitioned in April 1918 and renamed the "House of Special Purpose" (Дом особого назначения).

The imperial family's captivity

Nicholas, Alexandra, and their daughter Maria arrived at the house on 30 April 1918 after transfer from Tobolsk; the remaining children joined them on 23 May. The family and four attendants lived on the upper floor under strict guard, with windows painted white and a high double palisade erected around the property. The commandant Yakov Yurovsky took over in early July with orders to prepare the family's removal.

The murders

In the early hours of 17 July 1918, fearing the approach of White forces during the Russian Civil War, Yurovsky and a detachment of guards woke the prisoners, led them to a ground-floor room, and shot them. Eleven people died: Nicholas II, Alexandra, their five children (Olga, Tatiana, Maria, Anastasia, and Alexei), the physician Eugene Botkin, the maid Anna Demidova, the cook Ivan Kharitonov, and the valet Alexei Trupp.

Demolition and legacy

The house later housed a museum and regional party archives. In 1977, on the orders of the Politburo and supervised by local party chief Boris Yeltsin, the house was demolished to prevent it becoming a site of pilgrimage. After the collapse of the Soviet Union the site became a place of commemoration; the Church on the Blood in Honour of All Saints was built there and consecrated in 2003. Nicholas II and his family were canonized as passion bearers by the Russian Orthodox Church, and their remains were interred in St. Catherine's Chapel of the Peter and Paul Cathedral in Saint Petersburg in 1998.
