Straits of Dover

The Straits of Dover (French: Pas de Calais) are the narrowest part of the English Channel, between south-east England and northern France. At their narrowest the straits are about 33.3 km (20.7 miles) wide, from Dover to Cap Gris-Nez, and they form the boundary between the Channel and the North Sea. The Channel Tunnel runs beneath them.

Geography and geology

The straits cut through chalk: the White Cliffs of Dover face matching cliffs in France, remnants of a chalk ridge that once joined Britain to mainland Europe. Geologists believe catastrophic megafloods from a glacial lake in the North Sea basin — possibly around 450,000 and 200,000 years ago — scoured the strait's floor and finally severed the land bridge, making Britain an island. The seabed is shallow, with dangerous sandbanks such as the Goodwin Sands off Deal.

Shipping

The straits are among the busiest shipping lanes in the world, with several hundred vessel movements a day between the Atlantic approaches and the ports of northern Europe. The world's first traffic separation scheme was established here in 1967; the Channel Navigation Information Service at Dover and its French counterpart at Cap Gris-Nez coordinate movements. Cross-Channel ferries between Dover and Calais and Dunkirk still cross above the tunnel traffic.

History and swimming

The straits were the invasion route feared in 1588 (Spanish Armada), 1805 (Napoleon) and 1940 (Operation Sea Lion), and were heavily contested in both world wars — the Dover Patrol guarded them in 1914–18. Swimming the Channel across the straits, first achieved by Captain Matthew Webb in 1875, remains a famous endurance feat.
