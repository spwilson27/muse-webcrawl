Photon bunching

Photon bunching is the tendency of photons from a thermal (chaotic) light source to arrive in clusters, so that the probability of detecting two photons within a very short time interval is enhanced compared with photons arriving independently at random. It is a manifestation of the bosonic character of photons and of the intensity fluctuations of thermal light.

The second-order correlation function of thermal light peaks at zero delay, g²(0) = 2.

Correlation function

Bunching is quantified by the second-order correlation function g²(τ). For an ideal single-mode thermal source, g²(0) = 2, decaying to 1 for delays longer than the coherence time. Coherent laser light has g²(τ) = 1 at all delays (Poissonian statistics), while a single-photon source shows the opposite behaviour, photon antibunching, with g²(0) approaching 0.

Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect

The effect was first measured by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Twiss in the 1950s using intensity interferometry. Their observation that photon detections at two separated detectors were correlated caused controversy until it was explained by the quantum theory of light: photons, being bosons, exhibit enhanced probability of occupying the same state. Hanbury Brown and Twiss used the correlation to measure the angular diameters of stars, an application revived in modern intensity interferometers.

Applications

Photon bunching underlies photon correlation spectroscopy, in which the timescale of the decay of g²(τ) reveals the coherence time of the source or the diffusion of scattering particles. It also sets noise limits in optical measurements with thermal light and is a routine diagnostic for classifying light sources as thermal, coherent or nonclassical.
