British Israelism

British Israelism (also called Anglo-Israelism) is a pseudohistorical belief system whose adherents hold that people of Great Britain (and, by extension, other peoples of north-western Europe and their diasporas) are direct lineal descendants of the Ten Lost Tribes of Israel. The idea is not supported by mainstream historical, archaeological, or genetic scholarship, and it is regarded as pseudohistory by academics.

History

Precursors of the doctrine appeared in the 17th century, but British Israelism as an organised movement dates from the 19th century, with works such as John Wilson's Our Israelitish Origin (1840) and the energetic organising of Edward Hine. It spread through the British Empire and to the United States, where it was promoted by figures such as Charles Fox Parham and later Herbert W. Armstrong, whose Worldwide Church of God taught a version of the doctrine until the church repudiated it in the 1990s.

Beliefs and claims

Adherents typically trace the lost tribes through migrations across the Caucasus and Europe, use speculative etymologies (for example, deriving "British" from the Hebrew berith, "covenant", and ish, "man"), and interpret the British monarchy as continuing the Davidic line. Mainstream scholars reject these claims, pointing to the lack of linguistic or genetic evidence connecting the British to ancient Israelites.

Legacy

In the United States, a hostile variant of the doctrine influenced the Christian Identity movement, which recast the ideas in explicitly racist forms condemned by mainstream churches. The British-Israel World Federation continues to promote the original doctrine today.
