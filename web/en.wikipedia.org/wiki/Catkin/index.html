Catkin

A catkin (or ament) is a slim, cylindrical cluster of flowers with inconspicuous or no petals, typically found on wind-pollinated trees and shrubs such as birches, oaks, willows, alders, poplars and hazels. Catkins usually hang downward and shed or receive pollen by wind.

TypeInflorescence (ament)
PollinationWind (anemophily)
ExamplesBirch, oak, willow, hazel, alder

Description

Catkins are unisexual: a plant may bear male and female catkins on the same individual (monoecious, as in birch and oak) or on separate plants (dioecious, as in willows and poplars). Male catkins are longer and release clouds of pollen in spring, often before the leaves emerge; female catkins are shorter and develop into fruits or seed-bearing cones after fertilisation.

Ecology

Early-spring catkins are an important pollen and nectar source for emerging bees and other insects, and the seeds of catkin-bearing trees feed birds and mammals. Goat willow ("pussy willow") catkins are a familiar seasonal ornament. See also inflorescence.
