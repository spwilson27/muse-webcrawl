Language isolate

A language isolate is a language with no demonstrable genealogical (or "genetic") relationship with other languages; in other words, it is a language family comprising a single language. Isolates are often the sole survivors of once larger families whose other members have died out, or languages that have always been small and isolated. Well-known examples include Basque in Europe, Korean (often classified as an isolate or as the largest member of the small Koreanic family), Sumerian in the ancient Near East, and Burushaski in northern Pakistan.

Isolates versus unclassified languages

A language isolate should be distinguished from an unclassified language: an isolate is well documented, and repeated attempts to relate it to other languages have failed, whereas an unclassified language may simply lack sufficient data — often because it is extinct and poorly attested — to permit classification. Some languages have moved between categories as research progressed; for example, Japanese is sometimes grouped with the nearly extinct Ainu languages or treated as the largest member of the Japonic family alongside the Ryukyuan languages.

How isolates arise

Isolates typically arise through extinction: a language family spread over a region is gradually replaced by an expanding neighbour, leaving one remnant language in a refuge area such as mountains or islands. Basque, spoken in the western Pyrenees, is the only surviving pre-Indo-European language of western Europe; its extinct relative Aquitanian is known from inscriptions. In other cases a language may have been separated from its relatives for so long that the comparative method can no longer recover the connection.

Sign languages

Some sign languages are also isolates. Village sign languages that arise in small communities with high rates of hereditary deafness — such as Adamorobe Sign Language in Ghana or Kata Kolok in Bali — may have no relationship to the national sign languages around them.
