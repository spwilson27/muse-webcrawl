Gelatin (or gelatine) is a translucent, colourless, flavourless food ingredient and industrial material derived from collagen obtained from animal body parts, chiefly pig skin, cattle hides, and bones. It is commonly used as a gelling agent in food, pharmaceuticals, photography, and cosmetics.

Dried gelatin sheets and a moulded gelatin dessert.

Production

Gelatin is produced by partial hydrolysis of collagen. Raw materials are pre-treated with acid (Type A gelatin) or alkali (Type B) to break cross-links, then extracted with hot water in successive batches, concentrated, and dried into sheets, granules, or powder. Its gelling strength is graded by Bloom value, typically between 50 and 300 Bloom.

Uses

In food, gelatin sets desserts such as jelly and panna cotta, stabilises marshmallows, gummy candies, yoghurt and ice cream, and clarifies juices and wine. It forms the shells of pharmaceutical capsules and is used in medical products including plasma expanders and wound dressings. Gelatin emulsions historically carried the light-sensitive silver halides in photographic film and paper. Because it is animal-derived, it is not suitable for vegans; common substitutes include agar, pectin, and carrageenan.

Properties

Gelatin gels are thermo-reversible: they set on cooling and melt on warming, at temperatures close to body temperature, which gives gelatin-set foods their characteristic melt-in-the-mouth texture. Although a protein, gelatin is nutritionally incomplete, lacking the essential amino acid tryptophan.
