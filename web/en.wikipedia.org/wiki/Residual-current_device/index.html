Residual-current device

A residual-current device (RCD), residual-current circuit breaker (RCCB) or ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) is an electrical safety device that quickly breaks an electrical circuit with leakage current to ground. It is designed to disconnect the circuit whenever it detects that the current flowing in the live and neutral conductors is unbalanced — indicating current leaking through an unintended path, such as through a person to earth — thereby reducing the risk of electric shock.

Operating principle

In a healthy circuit the currents in the live and neutral conductors are equal and opposite. The RCD passes both conductors through a sensing transformer; if some current returns via earth instead of the neutral, the imbalance produces a signal that triggers a relay to open the contacts, typically within 20–40 milliseconds. Devices for personal protection commonly trip at a residual current of 30 mA, and 10 mA devices are used in especially sensitive applications such as hospitals and swimming pools. Unlike a fuse or circuit breaker, an RCD does not primarily protect against overloads or short circuits, although combined devices (RCBOs) provide both functions.

Types and terminology

In North America the same function is provided by the ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI), often built into receptacles in bathrooms, kitchens and outdoor locations. RCDs are classified by the waveforms they can detect (types AC, A, F and B), by whether they are fixed or portable, and by their time-delay characteristics; selective (S-type) devices allow discrimination in installations with several RCDs in series.
