Standby power

Standby power
Also calledVampire power, phantom load, standby loss, leaking electricity
Typical shareRoughly 5–10% of residential electricity use
Main sourcesExternal power supplies, TVs, set-top boxes, chargers, appliances with clocks and remote receivers

Many devices draw power continuously while nominally switched off.

Standby power, also called vampire power, phantom load, or leaking electricity, refers to the electric power consumed by electronic and electrical appliances while they are switched off (but are designed to draw some power) or in standby mode. This only occurs because some devices claim to be switched off but are actually in a different state than physiological off, with functions such as remote-control receivers, clocks, and instant-on circuitry remaining energised.

Although the draw of an individual device is small — commonly between 0.5 and 10 watts — the aggregate is substantial: studies estimate that standby consumption accounts for roughly 5–10% of residential electricity use in developed countries, equivalent to the output of dozens of large power stations worldwide.

Sources

External power supplies ("wall warts") draw current whenever plugged in, even with no device attached or when the device's battery is fully charged. Televisions, set-top boxes, and game consoles maintain sensors listening for a remote control or network wake signal; older cable boxes could draw nearly as much in standby as in use. Appliances with clocks or displays (microwave ovens, coffee makers), computers in sleep mode, and devices with soft power switches all contribute. Because standby loads run continuously — 8,760 hours a year — a one-watt load consumes about 8.76 kWh annually.

Regulation and initiatives

The International Energy Agency's "One Watt Initiative", launched in 1999, aimed to reduce standby power in all devices to one watt or less by 2010. Several jurisdictions adopted binding rules: the European Union's Ecodesign regulation (No 1275/2008) limited standby consumption of household and office equipment to 1 W, tightening to 0.5 W from 2013, with separate rules for networked standby. California adopted appliance efficiency standards covering standby draw, and programmes such as Energy Star set standby limits for labelled products. As a result, standby draw per device has fallen markedly, though the growing number of networked devices has partly offset the gains.

Reducing standby power

Consumers can cut standby losses by unplugging devices and chargers, using switched power strips or "smart" strips that cut power to peripherals when a main device is off, and choosing low-standby models. Plug-in energy meters make the loads visible. Some standby draw is functional — enabling remote start, network availability, and clocks — so policy generally targets wasteful baseline consumption rather than eliminating standby altogether.
