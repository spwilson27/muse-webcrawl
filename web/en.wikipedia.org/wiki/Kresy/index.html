Kresy

Interwar Poland: the Kresy (eastern borderlands) shaded (image placeholder)

Kresy Wschodnie (Polish pronunciation: [ˈkrɛsɨ ˈfsxɔdɲɛ]), or simply Kresy — the "Eastern Borderlands" — is a term used in Poland for the eastern part of the territory of the Second Polish Republic (1918–1939) lying east of the Curzon Line, which Poland lost to the Soviet Union in 1945. The Kresy comprise lands now divided between western Ukraine (Galicia and Volhynia), western Belarus and south-eastern Lithuania, including the historically Polish cities of Lwów (now Lviv), Wilno (now Vilnius), Grodno, Stanisławów, Tarnopol, Łuck, Brześć and Nowogródek. For Poles the term carries deep cultural and nostalgic weight: the Kresy were the setting of much of Polish literature and the homeland of figures such as Adam Mickiewicz and Józef Piłsudski.

Etymology and extent

Kresy means "edges" or "borderlands" in Polish. Before the partitions of Poland in the late 18th century the term could refer to the wild south-eastern frontier of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (the "Wild Fields" of Ukraine). After 1918 it came to denote the eastern voivodeships of the reborn republic — a large, rural, ethnically mixed region whose population was variously Polish, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Jewish, Lithuanian and others, with Poles strongest in the cities and parts of the countryside.

History

The region's borders were settled by the Polish–Soviet War and the Treaty of Riga (1921). In the interwar period the Polish state Polonised administration and schooling and promoted Polish military settlement (osadnicy), fuelling tensions with Ukrainian and Belarusian national movements, notably the OUN in Galicia and Volhynia. The Kresy remained among Poland's poorest regions, "Polska B" in the contemporary division of the country into a more developed west and a poorer east.

War and loss

The Soviet Union invaded and annexed the Kresy in September 1939 under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, deporting hundreds of thousands of Polish citizens to Siberia and Kazakhstan; the NKVD murdered some 22,000 Polish officers and officials in the Katyń massacre of 1940, many of them reservists from the Kresy. Germany occupied the region in 1941–44; the Holocaust destroyed its Jewish communities, and in 1943–44 Ukrainian nationalist forces massacred tens of thousands of Poles in Volhynia and Eastern Galicia. At Yalta and Potsdam the Allies confirmed the Curzon Line as Poland's eastern border; between 1944 and 1946 roughly 1.2–1.5 million Poles were "repatriated" westward, many resettled in the former German territories Poland gained in the west, while Ukrainians and Belarusians remaining in the new Poland were deported in turn, culminating in Operation Vistula (1947).

Memory

The Kresy live on in Polish culture through literature (Mickiewicz's Pan Tadeusz opens in the Lithuanian marches), memoir, song, societies of Kresy natives (Kresowiacy) and their descendants, and museums such as the Ossolineum, moved from Lwów to Wrocław after the war. Commemoration of the Volhynia massacres and Katyń remains politically sensitive in Polish–Ukrainian and Polish–Russian relations.
