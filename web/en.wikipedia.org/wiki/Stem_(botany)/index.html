Stem (botany)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The stem is the main structural axis of a vascular plant, bearing leaves, buds and reproductive structures, and conducting water, minerals and sugars between roots and leaves. Stems range from soft green annual shoots to the massive trunks of trees, whose woody stems add a new growth ring each year.

Structure and growth

Stems grow at tips (apical meristems) and, in woody plants, thicken by a lateral cambium producing xylem (wood) inward and phloem (inner bark) outward. Vascular bundles in young stems contain xylem and phloem; nodes bear leaves and buds, and internodes space them apart. Underground stems — rhizomes, tubers (potato), bulbs and corms — store food and spread plants vegetatively.

Uses

Stems give timber and paper, fibres (flax, hemp), sugar (sugarcane), vegetables (asparagus, celery, kohlrabi) and medicines (quinine bark). Girdling — removing a ring of bark — kills trees by starving roots, a fact used and lamented in forestry; grafting joins stems of different plants into one.
