Masolino da Panicale (nickname meaning "little Tom"; c. 1383 — c. 1447) was an Italian painter of the early Renaissance period. Born Tommaso di Cristoforo Fini in Panicale in Valdelsa, he was one of the first painters to bridge the graceful world of International Gothic painting and the new, more naturalistic art of the Renaissance.

Life and career

Masolino probably trained in Florence, possibly in the workshop of Lorenzo Ghiberti, and tradition holds that he worked as Ghiberti's assistant on the first set of doors for the Florence Baptistery. By 1423 he had joined the Florentine painters' guild. That year he painted the Madonna of Humility for a patron in Bremen, one of his earliest surviving works, which still shows the sinuous lines of the Gothic style.

In 1424 Masolino began his celebrated collaboration with the much younger Masaccio. Together they painted the frescoes of the Brancacci Chapel in Santa Maria del Carmine, Florence, a cycle commissioned by Felice Brancacci. Masolino painted the Temptation of Adam and Eve and the Preaching of Saint Peter, among other scenes, while Masaccio produced the more revolutionary companion panels. The contrast between Masolino's lyrical elegance and Masaccio's weighty realism is one of the most studied in art history.

The two also collaborated on the altarpiece for Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome and on a fresco cycle, now destroyed, for the Branda Chapel in San Clemente. In 1425 Masolino left Florence for Hungary, where he spent about two years in the service of the Italian condottiere Pippo Spano, returning in 1427.

Later works

After Masaccio's early death in 1428, Masolino continued to work in Rome and then in Castiglione Olona in Lombardy, where Cardinal Branda da Castiglione commissioned him to decorate an entire complex. His frescoes there — in the collegiate church, the baptistery (1435) and the cardinal's palace — are among the most poetic landscapes of the Italian Renaissance, with delicate, dream-like figures set against luminous hills and water.

Masolino's masterpiece of panel painting is often considered the Founding of Santa Maria Maggiore, the central panel of the dismantled Colonna Altarpiece, whose other panels are scattered in museums including the National Gallery in London and the Philadelphia Museum of Art. His later style absorbed some of Masaccio's advances in perspective and modelling but never abandoned its courtly grace. Masolino died in Florence around 1447.
