Clothing industry

The clothing industry, or garment industry, encompasses the design, manufacture, marketing, and sale of apparel. Together with textiles it forms one of the world's oldest and largest industrial sectors, employing tens of millions of people — a majority of them women — across global supply chains.

[Image: Rows of machinists sewing garments in a clothing factory]

Structure

The industry runs from fibre production and textile mills through design, pattern-making, cutting, and sewing (the labour-intensive "cut-make-trim" stage) to branding, logistics, and retail. Major garment-exporting countries include China, Bangladesh, Vietnam, India, and Turkey. Brands and retailers in consuming countries typically outsource production to contractors, competing on price, speed, and compliance.

History

Ready-to-wear clothing expanded with the sewing machine in the 19th century; the industry was central to early industrialisation in Britain and New England and notorious for sweatshop conditions, exemplified by the 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in New York. Production migrated through the 20th century to lower-wage countries in Asia and Latin America.

Issues

The 2013 Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh, which killed more than 1,100 garment workers, intensified scrutiny of factory safety and led to the Bangladesh Accord. The rise of "fast fashion" — rapid, low-cost turnover of styles by retailers such as Zara, H&M, and Shein — has been criticised for low wages, excessive waste, and a large environmental footprint; the industry is a significant source of greenhouse-gas emissions, water use, and microplastic pollution. Responses include extended producer responsibility laws, resale and rental models, and traceability initiatives.
