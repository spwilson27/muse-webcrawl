The Oradour-sur-Glane massacre was a massacre of civilians carried out by a company of the Nazi German Waffen-SS on 10 June 1944 in the village of Oradour-sur-Glane in German-occupied France during the Second World War. The unit, belonging to the 2nd SS Panzer Division Das Reich, killed 643 people, including 247 women and 205 children, and destroyed the village. It remains one of the most notorious war crimes committed in Western Europe during the war.

Background

In June 1944, the Das Reich division was moving north from southern France toward Normandy to oppose the Allied landings of 6 June. It had already committed atrocities en route, including the hangings at Tulle on 9 June. French Resistance activity in the region, including the reported kidnapping of an SS officer, preceded the attack on Oradour, though the precise motivation remains debated by historians.

The massacre

On the afternoon of 10 June, SS troops sealed off the village and ordered all inhabitants to assemble in the fairground, ostensibly for an identity check. The men were separated and taken to barns, where they were shot and the buildings set alight. The women and children were locked in the church, which was set on fire; those who tried to escape through the windows were shot. Only a handful of villagers survived, including Marguerite Rouffanche, who escaped from the church.

Aftermath and memorial

After the war, the French government of Charles de Gaulle ordered that the ruins be preserved as a permanent memorial and museum; a new village was built nearby. In 1953, a controversial trial at Bordeaux convicted a number of the surviving perpetrators, but Alsatian conscripts among them were amnestied, causing lasting bitterness. The preserved ruins, including burnt-out cars, the church, and personal effects left in place, now receive hundreds of thousands of visitors each year.
