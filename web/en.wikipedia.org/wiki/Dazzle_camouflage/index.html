Dazzle camouflage

Dazzle camouflage, also known as razzle dazzle (in the United States) or dazzle painting, is a type of ship camouflage used extensively in World War I, and to a lesser extent in World War II and afterwards. Credited to the British marine artist Norman Wilkinson, it consists of complex patterns of geometric shapes in contrasting colours, interrupting and intersecting each other.

Purpose

Unlike other forms of camouflage, the intention of dazzle is not to conceal but to make it difficult to estimate a target's range, speed, and heading. A U-boat captain looking through a periscope had only seconds to judge a ship's course before firing a torpedo that took minutes to arrive; dazzle patterns — with fake bow waves, distorted superstructures, and clashing stripes — were meant to corrupt that judgement, and to make it ambiguous which end of the ship was the bow. Zoologist John Graham Kerr had independently proposed disruptive camouflage for warships, and the idea parallels the dazzling effect of zebra stripes.

Adoption and effectiveness

The British Admiralty adopted dazzle in 1917, and the United States Navy followed in 1918, painting hundreds of merchant and naval vessels. Whether it measurably reduced sinkings was disputed even at the time: ships painted in dazzle were sunk at similar rates to unpainted ones, though crews strongly believed in it, and the morale effect was considered valuable. In World War II, improved rangefinders, aircraft, and radar reduced its usefulness, but dazzle schemes were still applied, and the concept influenced later disruptive pattern material for uniforms and vehicles.

Cultural legacy

The striking appearance of dazzle ships influenced modern art — the Vorticists and Cubists recognised a kinship with their own work — and dazzle patterns have been revived in art installations, fashion, and in "CV dazzle" makeup intended to frustrate facial recognition.
