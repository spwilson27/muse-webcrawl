Swiss German
Native name: Schwiizerdütsch
Region: German-speaking Switzerland
Language family: Indo-European → Germanic → High German → Alemannic
ISO code: gsw

Swiss German (German: Schweizerdeutsch; Alemannic: Schwiizerdütsch) is the collective name for the Alemannic German dialects spoken in the German-speaking part of Switzerland and in some Alpine communities in northern Italy. It is spoken by roughly five million people.

Use and diglossia

Switzerland's German-speaking cantons exhibit a classic diglossia: Swiss German dialects are the everyday spoken language in virtually all informal and most formal oral settings — including much broadcasting, schools and even parliament — while Standard Swiss German, a variety of Standard German, is used for writing, formal speeches and news bulletins. Unlike in Germany or Austria, the dialect is not stigmatised and is spoken across all social classes.

Characteristics

Swiss German dialects differ from Standard German in pronunciation, vocabulary and grammar. Notable features include the retention of Middle High German vowels (e.g. Huus "house" for Standard Haus), the absence of the simple past tense in most dialects, diminutives in -li, and numerous loanwords from French and Italian, such as Mercí for thanks and Velo for bicycle. Dialects vary considerably between regions — the dialects of Bern, Zurich, Lucerne and Basel are readily distinguishable — but are mutually intelligible with some effort.

Writing

There is no official orthography for Swiss German. It is written informally in text messages, advertisements and some literature, usually with ad-hoc spellings based on pronunciation; published dialect literature uses systems such as Eugen Dieth's Schwyzertütschi Dialäktschrift.
