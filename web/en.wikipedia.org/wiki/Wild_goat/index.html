The wild goat (Capra aegagrus) is a wild species of goat that ranges over Europe, Asia Minor, and Central Asia as far as Pakistan. It is the ancestor of the domestic goat (Capra aegagrus hircus). In the wild, goats live in herds of up to 500 individuals; males are solitary for much of the year. Female goats give birth to one to three kids in spring after a gestation period of around 170 days.

The best-known subspecies is the bezoar ibex (Capra aegagrus aegagrus), also called the bezoar goat, which inhabits mountainous regions from Turkey to Pakistan. Other subspecies include the Sindh ibex and the Chiltan ibex of Pakistan, and the kri-kri of Crete, which was long thought to be a distinct subspecies but is now considered a feral descendant of early domestic goats.

Taxonomy

The wild goat was described by Johann Christian Polycarp Erxleben in 1777. Its scientific name derives from the Latin capra (she-goat) and the Greek aigagros (wild goat). Genetic studies have confirmed that the domestic goat descends primarily from the bezoar ibex, with domestication occurring roughly 10,000 years ago in the Zagros Mountains region of western Iran, in the vicinity of the Neolithic site of Ganj Dareh.

Distribution and habitat

Wild goats occur in mountainous terrain, generally between 500 and 4,200 metres in elevation, across a band stretching from the Aegean islands and Anatolia through the Caucasus, Iraq, Iran, Turkmenistan and Afghanistan to Pakistan. They prefer steep, rocky slopes and cliffs that offer escape terrain from predators such as leopards, wolves and golden eagles.

Behaviour and ecology

Wild goats are diurnal herbivores, grazing grasses in the summer and browsing shrubs and trees in winter. During the rut, which peaks in late autumn, males fight for access to females by clashing their large scimitar-shaped horns. Both sexes bear horns, though those of males are much larger, reaching over a metre along the curve in old billies.

Relationship with humans

The wild goat is the principal progenitor of all domestic goat breeds. Its horns were historically prized, and the species' name is associated with bezoar stones — concretions found in the stomachs of ruminants that were once believed to be universal antidotes to poison.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN owing to habitat degradation, competition with livestock, poaching and hybridisation with domestic goats. Populations persist in protected areas such as Kirthar National Park in Pakistan and several reserves in Iran and Turkey.
