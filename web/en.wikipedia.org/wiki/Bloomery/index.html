Bloomery

A bloomery is a type of metallurgical furnace once used widely for smelting iron from its oxides. The bloomery was the earliest form of smelter capable of reducing iron ore, and its product — a spongy mass of iron and slag called a bloom — was refined into wrought iron by repeated hammering. Bloomeries were used from the early Iron Age until they were largely displaced by blast furnaces in the late medieval and early modern periods.

Process

A bloomery consists of a clay or stone shaft, often about a metre high, with one or more tuyères through which air is blown — by natural draft in a chimney-type design, or by bellows. Charcoal and iron ore are charged from the top. At temperatures around 1,200 °C, carbon monoxide from the charcoal reduces the ore to metallic iron, but the temperature stays below iron's melting point (1,538 °C), so the iron collects as a solid, porous bloom intermingled with molten slag. The bloom is removed, reheated and hammered to expel slag, yielding wrought iron.

History

Bloomery smelting spread across Europe, Africa, and Asia; variants include the Catalan forge of southern Europe and the Japanese tatara used to produce tamahagane steel for swords. In Europe, larger water-powered bloomeries developed in the Middle Ages before the blast furnace, which produces liquid cast iron, took over from roughly the 15th century onward.
