Induction furnace

An induction furnace is an electrical furnace in which heat is applied by induction heating of metal. The advantage of the induction furnace is a clean, energy-efficient and well-controlled melting process compared to most other means of metal melting. Capacities range from less than one kilogram to one hundred tonnes, and induction furnaces are widely used in modern foundries to melt iron, steel, copper, aluminium and precious metals, because they produce no arc or combustion pollution.

Operation

A water-cooled coil of copper tubing, carrying alternating current, surrounds a crucible or forms the furnace body. The alternating magnetic field induces eddy currents in the conductive charge inside, and the metal's electrical resistance converts this current to heat (Joule heating). In ferromagnetic materials, hysteresis losses add heat below the Curie temperature. The induced currents also stir the molten bath electromagnetically, promoting uniform temperature and alloy mixing.

Types

Coreless furnace

In a coreless induction furnace, the coil surrounds a refractory-lined crucible. Operating frequency is chosen according to charge size and material — smaller furnaces run at higher frequencies, from a few hundred hertz up to hundreds of kilohertz, typically supplied by solid-state inverters.

Channel furnace

A channel (core-type) furnace has an iron core linking the primary coil to a loop of molten metal that acts as a short-circuited secondary. Channel furnaces are efficient for holding and superheating large quantities of metal but require a molten heel to start.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include precise temperature control, low melt loss, no contamination from electrodes or fuel, and quiet, clean operation. Disadvantages include limited slag refining capability (slags stay relatively cold), refractory lining wear in coreless units, and the need for clean, well-sized charge material for efficient coupling.
