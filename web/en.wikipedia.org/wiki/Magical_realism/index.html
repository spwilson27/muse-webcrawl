Magical realism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A village street where butterflies drift in impossible golden swarms around a woman hanging laundry, utterly unremarked by passersby]

Magic realism (or magical realism) is a style and genre of fiction in which magical or fantastical elements appear in an otherwise realistic, mundane setting, narrated in a matter-of-fact tone that refuses to mark them as strange. The term, first used of painting in the 1920s and adapted by Alejo Carpentier for Latin American fiction ('lo real maravilloso', 1949), became the name of the Latin American Boom's signature mode and then a global literary language.

Its canonical texts are Gabriel García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), Isabel Allende's The House of the Spirits (1982) and, in its wider family, Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children (1981), Toni Morrison's Beloved (1987) and the works of Jorge Luis Borges and Juan Rulfo.

Definition and disputes

The mode's key is the narrator's deadpan: ghosts at the kitchen table, ascensions to heaven with the laundry, rains of four years — reported with the same calm as births and wars, because in the worlds depicted, myth and history share one reality. Critics dispute its borders (with fabulism, surrealism, the fantastic) and its politics: for postcolonial writers the mode dignifies indigenous and colonised cosmologies against imported realism; to detractors it became a marketing label flattening Latin America into exoticism — a charge the Boom's own heirs have debated for decades.

Global reach

From Macondo the mode spread through world fiction — Rushdie's India, Morrison's Ohio, Ben Okri's Nigeria, Haruki Murakami's Japan, Angela Carter's Britain — becoming the default literary dialect for histories in which the marvellous is itself a fact. Its influence on film (from Like Water for Chocolate to Pan's Labyrinth) and on the contemporary novel's DNA is now total.
