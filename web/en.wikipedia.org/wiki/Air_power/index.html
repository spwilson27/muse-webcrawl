Air power

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Air power or airpower consists of the application of military aviation, military strategy and aerial technologies relating to the use of aircraft and other flying machines in warfare. Airpower includes the national means of conducting such warfare, including the intersection of transport and warcraft. Military aircraft include bombers, fighters, transports, reconnaissance aircraft and many other varieties. "Air power" may also refer to a nation's total military aviation assets.

History

Military use of aircraft began with observation balloons and grew rapidly in the First World War, when aeroplanes were used for reconnaissance, air-to-air combat and tactical bombing. Thinkers such as Giulio Douhet, Hugh Trenchard and Billy Mitchell argued between the wars that air power, particularly strategic bombing of an enemy's cities and industry, could be independently decisive. The Second World War saw air power become central: air superiority was a prerequisite for operations such as the Normandy landings, carrier aviation decided battles in the Pacific, and long-range bombing campaigns were waged against Germany and Japan.

Roles

Modern air power roles include gaining and maintaining control of the air (air supremacy and air superiority), strategic and interdiction strikes, close air support of ground forces, airlift, aerial refuelling, reconnaissance, intelligence, surveillance, and search and rescue. Since the late 20th century, precision-guided munitions, stealth aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles have transformed how these roles are performed, allowing much smaller forces to achieve effects that once required massed formations.

Theory

Air power theory addresses how air forces should be organised and employed. John Boyd's OODA loop, John Warden's "five rings" model of the enemy as a system, and doctrines of parallel warfare have influenced Western air forces. Critics note that air power alone has rarely compelled an enemy to concede without complementary land and sea power, a debate running from the Combined Bomber Offensive to the Kosovo War of 1999.
