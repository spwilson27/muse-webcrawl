Date28 October – 8 November 1647
LocationSt Mary's Church, Putney, London

SubjectAn Agreement of the People; the franchise

The Putney Debates were a series of discussions held in late October and early November 1647 among the officers and elected representatives ("Agitators") of the New Model Army at the Church of St Mary the Virgin, Putney, in the midst of the English Civil War. Recorded in shorthand by secretary William Clarke, the debates considered the Levellers' proposed constitution, An Agreement of the People.

The arguments

The central question was who should vote. Colonel Thomas Rainsborough argued: "For really I think that the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live as the greatest he; and therefore truly, sir, I think it's clear, that every man that is to live under a government ought first by his own consent to put himself under that government." Henry Ireton countered that political rights belong only to those with a "permanent fixed interest" in the kingdom—landowners—warning that universal suffrage would undermine property itself.

Outcome

The debates were adjourned without agreement as the army leadership moved to restore discipline; a rendezvous at Corkbush Field on 15 November suppressed incipient mutiny. The Clarke Papers transcript, rediscovered in the 19th century, is regarded as one of the founding documents of modern democratic thought.

Legacy

The debates are commemorated at St Mary's Church, where Rainsborough's words are inscribed. Historians and political theorists continue to cite Putney as an early, remarkably articulate assertion of government by consent.
