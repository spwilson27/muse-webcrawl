Recitative

Recitative is a style of delivery in which a singer is allowed to adopt the rhythms and delivery of ordinary speech. Recitative does not repeat lines as formally composed songs do. It resembles sung ordinary speech more than a formal musical composition.

Recitative is used in opera, oratorio, and cantata to carry the plot forward between arias and ensemble numbers. Its defining feature is that it follows the natural inflections of speech rather than imposing a strict metrical structure, making it the principal vehicle for dialogue and narrative in Baroque and Classical vocal music.

Types

Two main types are distinguished. Recitativo secco ("dry recitative") is accompanied only by a basso continuo, typically a harpsichord or cello, giving the singer great rhythmic freedom. Recitativo accompagnato (or strumentato) is accompanied by the full orchestra and is reserved for moments of heightened emotion or dramatic importance.

History

Recitative emerged around 1600 with the Florentine Camerata and composers such as Jacopo Peri and Claudio Monteverdi, whose L'Orfeo (1607) is among the earliest surviving operas. The style, called stile rappresentativo, aimed to revive the sung drama of ancient Greece. Through the Baroque era, recitative became the standard means of advancing an opera's plot, alternating with da capo arias that expressed a single emotion. In the later 18th century, reformers such as Christoph Willibald Gluck sought to integrate recitative more fully with aria and chorus.
