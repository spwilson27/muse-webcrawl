Wireless telegraphy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wireless telegraphy — often shortened to "wireless" in early usage — is the transmission of telegraph signals by radio waves. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries it was the first practical application of radio: before voice broadcasting existed, radio communication meant Morse code sent by an operator keying a transmitter on and off, producing pulses of damped radio waves — dots and dashes — detected as clicks in the receiver's headphones.

Pioneered in the 1890s by Guglielmo Marconi and others building on the work of Hertz, Lodge, Popov and Tesla, wireless telegraphy was rapidly adopted for ship-to-shore and transoceanic communication. Its value at sea was famously demonstrated in the rescue coordination during the sinking of the Titanic in 1912, after which radio operators and equipment became compulsory on passenger ships.

History

Heinrich Hertz demonstrated radio waves in 1887. Marconi, experimenting at his family's estate near Bologna in 1895, extended ranges from metres to kilometres, moved to England, and in December 1901 claimed the first transatlantic reception, at St. John's, Newfoundland, of the letter "S" sent from Poldhu, Cornwall. Commercial transatlantic service began in 1907.

Technology

Early transmitters used spark gaps, producing broadband damped waves; receivers used coherers, then magnetic and crystal detectors, and later vacuum tubes. Continuous-wave transmitters — the Poulsen arc, the Alexanderson alternator, and eventually vacuum-tube oscillators — allowed narrower signals and, crucially, radiotelephony. Operators, nicknamed "Sparks", became a distinct profession aboard ships.

Maritime use

Shipping lines and navies adopted wireless for safety and commerce. The distress call CQD gave way to SOS (adopted internationally in 1908). The Republic collision of 1909 and the Titanic disaster of 1912, in which nearby ships were alerted by wireless, spurred legislation mandating 24-hour radio watches.

Military use

In the First World War wireless linked headquarters, ships and aircraft, prompting the growth of signals intelligence — Britain's Room 40 famously intercepted German naval transmissions. By the Second World War radio telegraphy was supplemented and partly supplanted by voice radio and teleprinter networks.

Decline

Radiotelegraphy persisted for maritime safety into the satellite era; the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System replaced the Morse watch in 1999. Morse survives in amateur radio, where "CW" (continuous wave) remains a popular mode.
