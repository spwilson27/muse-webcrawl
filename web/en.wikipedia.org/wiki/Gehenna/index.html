Gehenna is the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew Gēʾ Hinnōm, the "Valley of Hinnom" (or Valley of the Son of Hinnom), a ravine south-west of Jerusalem. In Jewish and Christian tradition the physical valley became a symbol of divine judgement, and in the New Testament "Gehenna" is the most common term rendered as "hell".

The valley in the Hebrew Bible

The valley marked a boundary between the tribes of Judah and Benjamin (Joshua 15:8). It gained a sinister reputation as the site of Topheth, where, according to Jeremiah and 2 Kings, some kings of Judah sacrificed children to Molech; King Josiah defiled the shrine to end the practice (2 Kings 23:10). Jeremiah prophesied the valley would become a place of slaughter and burial in judgement.

In Judaism

In rabbinic literature, Gehinnom developed into a place (or state) of purification and punishment for the wicked after death, generally limited to twelve months, after which most souls ascend, though the utterly wicked were held to remain or be destroyed. It functions more like purgatory than an eternal hell in much Jewish thought.

In the New Testament

Jesus uses "Gehenna" repeatedly in the Synoptic Gospels, describing it as a place of unquenchable fire where both soul and body can be destroyed (Matthew 10:28), associated with the "worm that does not die". The Epistle of James also uses the word metaphorically. Its imagery merged in later Christian tradition with Sheol/Hades and the lake of fire of Revelation.

Modern valley

Today the Wadi er-Rababi (Valley of Hinnom) is a parkland valley below Mount Zion, with Ottoman-era tombs, a monastery and walking paths adjoining Jerusalem's Old City.
