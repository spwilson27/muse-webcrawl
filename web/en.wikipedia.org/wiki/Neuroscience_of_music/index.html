Neuroscience of music

The neuroscience of music is the scientific study of brain-based mechanisms involved in the cognitive processes underlying music. These processes include listening to music, performing music, composing music, reading music, writing music, and ancillary activities. It also is increasingly concerned with the brain basis for musical aesthetics and musical emotion.

Musical processing in the brain

Music engages widely distributed networks of the brain. Basic acoustic features are processed in the auditory cortex, while rhythm and meter involve motor areas including the basal ganglia and cerebellum. Melodic expectation relies on frontal and temporal regions, and emotional responses to music recruit the limbic system and reward circuitry such as the nucleus accumbens. Studies of patients with brain damage, such as the acquired condition amusia, have helped reveal how these components can dissociate.

Training and plasticity

Musical training is a powerful model of neuroplasticity. Professional musicians show enlarged cortical representations for sound and fingers, differences in the corpus callosum, and superior auditory-motor integration compared with non-musicians. Research also explores potential transfer effects of music training to language and cognitive skills, as well as therapeutic uses of music in rehabilitation after stroke and in conditions such as Parkinson's disease.
