Lifecasting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lifecasting is the process of creating a three-dimensional copy of a living human body, through the use of molding and casting techniques. Lifecasting is most often used to create a copy of the face or a part of the body, but in recent practice whole-body lifecasts are also made. It is an art form with roots in ancient funerary and portraiture traditions, and today is practised by sculptors, special-effects artists, prosthetists and hobbyists.

History

The practice descends from the death masks of ancient Egypt and Rome, in which a plaster or wax impression was taken of the features of the deceased. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, life masks — taken from the living — became a fashionable means of preserving the likeness of notable figures; life masks of Abraham Lincoln, made by Leonard Volk in 1860 and by Clark Mills in 1865, are among the most famous examples. In the twentieth century, artists such as George Segal and Duane Hanson brought body casting into fine art, while the film industry adopted the technique for creating prosthetic appliances, animatronics and stunt doubles.

Process

A lifecast begins with a mold taken directly from the model. Common molding materials include alginate — a seaweed-derived compound that sets quickly and releases easily — and skin-safe platinum-cure silicone rubber, which captures finer detail and can be reused. Because alginate molds are fragile and begin to shrink within hours, a positive cast in plaster, resin or silicone is usually poured promptly. For face casting, the model breathes either through the nostrils (kept clear with straws or cotton) or through a snorkel-like tube, and the material is applied in sections with a plaster bandage mother mold to hold its shape. The finished cast may then be refined, painted, and mounted for display.

Safety

Because the model's skin, eyes and airways are in contact with the molding material, lifecasting requires care: only skin-safe materials should be used, the model must be given an unmistakable distress signal, and a release agent is applied to skin and hair beforehand. Full-body and full-head casts are considered advanced work and are normally performed by experienced teams.
