Diagonalizable matrix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Eigendirections of a diagonalizable linear map]

In linear algebra, a square matrix A is called diagonalizable or non-defective if it is similar to a diagonal matrix, i.e., if there exists an invertible matrix P and a diagonal matrix D such that

P−1AP = D,   or equivalently   A = PDP−1.

Such a P is not unique, but its columns form a basis of eigenvectors of A, and the diagonal entries of D are the corresponding eigenvalues. A linear map T on a finite-dimensional vector space is diagonalizable if the space has a basis consisting of eigenvectors of T.

Characterization

The fundamental theorem of diagonalizability states that the following are equivalent for an n × n matrix A over a field F:

  • A is diagonalizable over F.
  • The sum of the dimensions of the eigenspaces of A equals n.
  • For every eigenvalue, its geometric multiplicity equals its algebraic multiplicity.
  • The minimal polynomial of A splits into distinct linear factors over F.

Over the complex numbers, almost all matrices are diagonalizable: the defective matrices form a set of measure zero, though they include important examples such as nonzero nilpotent matrices.

Examples

Orthogonal projections, rotations of three-dimensional space (over ℂ), and all real symmetric and complex Hermitian matrices are diagonalizable — the latter two by the spectral theorem, using a unitary or orthogonal change of basis. The matrix [[1,1],[0,1]] is not diagonalizable: its only eigenvalue is 1 with a one-dimensional eigenspace; it is a Jordan block.

Applications

Diagonalization makes matrix powers, exponentials, and linear recurrences easy to compute: from A = PDP−1 follows Ak = PDkP−1. It is used in solving systems of linear differential equations, in Markov chain analysis, in principal component analysis, and throughout quantum mechanics, where Hermitian operators represent observables.
