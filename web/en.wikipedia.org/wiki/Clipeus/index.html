Clipeus

In the Roman world, a clipeus (Latin: clipeus, plural clipei) was a large oval or round shield used by Roman soldiers. The term is also used in art history and other fields by extension.

Military use

The clipeus was the shield of the early Roman legionary, derived from the Greek aspis (hoplon) carried by hoplites. It was made of wood covered with layers of hide or linen and faced with bronze, with a central metal boss. Over time the curved rectangular scutum displaced the round clipeus as standard infantry equipment, though the clipeus remained in use by cavalry and in ceremonial contexts.

In art

In Roman and later art, a clipeus is a round medallion framing a portrait bust — for example the imagines clipeatae, shield-portraits of ancestors or emperors. The motif passed into early Christian, medieval and Renaissance art, appearing in sarcophagi, mosaics and architecture.

Other uses

In entomology, the clypeus (from the same Latin root) is the plate on the lower part of an insect's face, above the labrum, to which the labrum is attached. Similar shield-shaped structures in other organisms share the name.
