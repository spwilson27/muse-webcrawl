A crossbow bolt is a projectile fired from a crossbow. Historically, bolts were typically shorter than conventional arrows, though modern designs vary, and some are essentially indistinguishable from arrows in length. A bolt with a square-shaped head was often called a quarrel, from the Old French carrel and the Latin quadrellus, "four-sided."

Because a crossbow stores far more energy in a much shorter power stroke than a hand bow, its projectiles must be stiff and strong enough to withstand the violent acceleration at release. Bolts also rest on the crossbow's rail rather than being drawn against a flexible shaft, so they are not arched around the bow in the "archer's paradox" fashion of bow-shot arrows.

History

Medieval crossbow bolts were usually made of wood with iron heads, and at roughly 30–40 cm were much shorter and heavier relative to their length than war arrows. Their mass and stiff construction helped them penetrate mail and, at short ranges, plate armor. The quarrel's four-sided pyramidal head was designed for punching through armor, while broad flat heads were used for hunting. Crossbowmen carried bolts in a quiver at the hip. Finds of bolt heads are common at battle sites and castle excavations across Europe.

Construction

A bolt consists of a shaft, a point (head), fletching (vanes or feathers), and a nock. Traditional shafts were turned or split wood; today they are most often aluminium or carbon-fiber tubes, chosen by spine (stiffness) to match the crossbow's draw weight. Modern hunting bolts are commonly 16–22 inches long and weigh, with a 100-grain point, around 400 grains (about 26 g) in total. Some bolts are flat-nocked (a plain squared end against the string) while others use half-moon or capture nocks.

Heads

Field points are conical target points. Hunting broadheads have two or more razor blades, either fixed or mechanical (expanding on impact). Historical heads included armor-piercing bodkin-like quarrel heads, crescent heads said to be used against rigging or horses, wooden blunts for small game and for recovering the bolt, and incendiary heads carrying combustible material.

Fletching and nocks

Because bolts travel a short distance at high speed, their vanes are small — typically 2–4 inches long. Medieval fletching was sometimes made of leather, parchment, or thin wood rather than feathers. Unlike arrows, bolts traditionally lacked a deep string nock, since the string simply pushes against the squared end of the shaft held in the crossbow's lock; modern designs add shaped nocks to align the string consistently.

Modern use

Manufacturers and many regulations today call crossbow projectiles "arrows," and the term "bolt" is retained mostly for shorter projectiles (especially for pistol crossbows) and in historical contexts. Bolts are used in target shooting and, where legal, big-game hunting; their short length and the crossbow's compact power stroke make the combination effective at short-to-moderate ranges.
