Second Red Scare

Periodc. 1947 – late 1950s
CountryUnited States
Key figuresJoseph McCarthy, J. Edgar Hoover, Richard Nixon, Roy Cohn
Principal institutionsHouse Un-American Activities Committee, Senate Internal Security Subcommittee, FBI

The Second Red Scare was a period of intense anti-communist suspicion and political repression in the United States, roughly from 1947 through the late 1950s, at the onset of the Cold War. It is closely associated with McCarthyism, named after Senator Joseph McCarthy, whose unsubstantiated accusations of communist infiltration ruined careers across government, entertainment, academia, and labor.

Origins

Shocks of 1949–1950 — the Soviet Union's first atomic test, the communist victory in China, and the outbreak of the Korean War — together with espionage cases such as Alger Hiss and Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, convinced many Americans that domestic communists posed a grave threat. President Harry Truman's 1947 loyalty program (Executive Order 9835) subjected federal employees to loyalty screening; the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) investigated Hollywood, producing the Hollywood Ten contempt cases and the studio blacklist. The McCarran Internal Security Act of 1950 required communist organizations to register, and state governments and private employers operated their own loyalty oaths and purges.

McCarthy and the climax

McCarthy's February 1950 Wheeling speech, claiming to hold a list of communists in the State Department, launched him to prominence. As chairman of the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations from 1953, with chief counsel Roy Cohn, he pursued alleged subversives in the Voice of America, overseas libraries, and the U.S. Army. The televised Army–McCarthy hearings of spring 1954 exposed his methods to the public; in December 1954 the Senate censured him, and his influence collapsed. Edward R. Murrow's See It Now broadcast of March 1954 is remembered as a turning point in media coverage.

Effects and legacy

Thousands lost jobs or were blacklisted on the basis of associations, anonymous accusations, or refusal to testify; some were imprisoned under the Smith Act. The scare narrowed American political debate, chilling labor activism and left-wing organizing, though its legal excesses were progressively rolled back by Supreme Court decisions from 1956 onward, notably Yates v. United States (1957). "McCarthyism" endures as a byword for reckless, evidence-free political accusation.
