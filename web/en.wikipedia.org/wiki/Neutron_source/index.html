Neutron source

A neutron source is any device that emits neutrons, irrespective of the mechanism used to produce them. Neutron sources vary widely in size and intensity, from hand-held radioisotope sources emitting a few thousand neutrons per second to large research facilities producing fluxes above 1015 neutrons per square centimetre per second. They are used in physics research, industry, medicine, and security.

Types

Radioisotope sources

Small sealed sources exploit nuclear reactions. In an (α,n) source such as americium–beryllium, alpha particles from a decaying actinide strike beryllium nuclei and knock out neutrons. Californium-252 emits neutrons directly by spontaneous fission and is widely used to start up reactors and calibrate detectors. Photoneutron sources combine a gamma emitter with beryllium or heavy water.

Neutron generators

Compact sealed tubes accelerate deuterium or tritium ions into a metal target, producing neutrons through the D–D or D–T fusion reactions; the D–T reaction yields energetic 14.1 MeV neutrons. Such generators are used in oil-well logging, explosives detection, and materials analysis.

Reactors and spallation sources

Research reactors provide intense continuous neutron beams for scattering experiments. Spallation sources — such as ISIS in the United Kingdom, the SNS at Oak Ridge, and the European Spallation Source — bombard heavy-metal targets with high-energy protons, ejecting dozens of neutrons per proton. These facilities support neutron diffraction, spectroscopy, imaging, and muon science.

Applications

Neutrons probe matter in ways X-rays cannot: they are sensitive to light elements such as hydrogen, distinguish neighbouring elements, and carry a magnetic moment for studying magnetism. Industrial uses include neutron activation analysis, moisture gauging, boron neutron capture therapy in medicine, and detection of fissile material.

Safety

Neutrons are deeply penetrating and biologically damaging; sources require shielding, typically with hydrogen-rich materials such as water, paraffin, or concrete, and neutron-absorbing boron or cadmium. Portable sources are strictly regulated because of their radioactivity and security implications.
