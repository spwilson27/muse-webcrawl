Dogmatic theology, also called dogmatics, is the branch of theology concerned with the systematic exposition and defence of the official doctrines — the dogmas — of a religious tradition, especially within Christianity. It presents the truths of faith as an ordered whole, drawing on scripture, tradition and the teaching authority of the church.

Scope

Dogmatics typically treats, in sequence, the doctrine of God, the Trinity, creation, Christology, soteriology, pneumatology, ecclesiology, the sacraments and eschatology. In Roman Catholic theology it is distinguished from moral theology and from fundamental theology, which addresses the grounds of faith itself; the term is closely related to, and in many contexts overlaps, systematic theology, though "dogmatics" emphasises commitment to the confessed doctrines of a particular church.

History

The discipline took shape in the post-Reformation era, when Catholic and Protestant theologians produced comprehensive systems of doctrine; Melanchthon's Loci Communes (1521) is an early landmark. In the 20th century Karl Barth's monumental Church Dogmatics redefined the field for Protestant theology, insisting that dogmatics is the church's self-examination of its talk about God against the standard of revelation.
