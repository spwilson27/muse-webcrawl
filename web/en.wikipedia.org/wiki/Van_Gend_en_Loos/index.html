Van Gend en Loos v Nederlandse Administratie der Belastingen

Van Gend en Loos
CaseCase 26/62
Decided5 February 1963
CourtEuropean Court of Justice
KeywordsDirect effect of EU law

Van Gend en Loos v Nederlandse Administratie der Belastingen [1963] ECR 1, Case 26/62, is a landmark judgment of the European Court of Justice which established the principle of direct effect: that provisions of European Community (now European Union) law may confer rights on individuals which national courts must enforce, without any need for implementing national legislation. It is generally regarded, with Costa v ENEL, as one of the two foundational decisions of the European legal order.

Facts

Van Gend en Loos, a Dutch transport company, imported a consignment of urea-formaldehyde (a chemical used in adhesives) from West Germany into the Netherlands. Dutch customs charged an import duty of 8 per cent, having reclassified the product into a higher-duty category under a 1959 tariff schedule — an increase from the 3 per cent charged when the Treaty of Rome entered into force. The company challenged the charge before the Dutch Tariefcommissie, arguing that it breached Article 12 of the EEC Treaty, the "standstill" clause prohibiting member states from introducing new customs duties or increasing those already in force. The tribunal made a preliminary reference to the Court of Justice. The Belgian, German and Dutch governments argued that the Treaty created obligations only between states, enforceable by the Commission or other member states, and that individuals could not invoke it before national courts.

Judgment

The Court disagreed. Examining "the spirit, the general scheme and the wording" of the Treaty, it held that "the Community constitutes a new legal order of international law for the benefit of which the states have limited their sovereign rights, albeit within limited fields, and the subjects of which comprise not only Member States but also their nationals." Independently of member-state legislation, Community law could therefore impose obligations on individuals and confer rights on them which become part of their legal heritage. For a treaty provision to have direct effect it must be clear, precise and unconditional, require no further implementing measure, and impose a negative obligation — conditions Article 12 satisfied. The company was thus entitled to rely on it, and the increased duty could not lawfully be applied.

Legacy

Direct effect was subsequently extended: to treaty articles imposing positive obligations (Defrenne on equal pay), to regulations, and, under conditions, to directives (Van Duyn, Ratti), though directives were held incapable of horizontal direct effect against private parties (Marshall, Faccini Dori). Combined with the primacy principle from Costa v ENEL and the Simmenthal duty of national courts to disapply conflicting national law, direct effect turned the preliminary reference procedure into a mechanism by which private litigants became, in the eyes of many commentators, the principal enforcers of European law, and transformed the Court of Justice into something resembling a constitutional court for Europe.
