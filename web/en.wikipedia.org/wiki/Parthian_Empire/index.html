Parthian Empire

[Image: relief of a Parthian horse archer in the act of the famous "Parthian shot", turning in the saddle to fire backwards]

The Parthian Empire (247 BC – AD 224), also known as the Arsacid Empire, was a major Iranian political and cultural power in ancient Iran and Iraq. Founded by Arsaces I, leader of the Parni tribe, who conquered the region of Parthia in Iran's northeast, the empire under Mithridates I (r. 171–132 BC) expanded to rule Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau, becoming Rome's great eastern rival for nearly three centuries.

Rise and expansion

From their steppe homeland the Arsacids absorbed Media, Babylonia and Persis from the declining Seleucid Empire. Under Mithridates II (r. 124–91 BC) Parthia reached from the Euphrates to Central Asia, established relations with Han China — opening the Iranian section of the Silk Road — and first negotiated with Rome. The Parthian army of horse archers and armoured cataphracts destroyed Crassus's invasion at Carrhae in 53 BC, one of Rome's worst defeats, and Parthian armies repeatedly invaded the Roman East, though attempts to conquer Mesopotamia by Trajan and others proved temporary.

Government and culture

The empire was a loose confederation of kingdoms and great noble houses under the King of Kings, with capitals at Ctesiphon, Ecbatana and Hecatompylos. Hellenistic, Persian and local traditions blended in its art and coinage; Zoroastrianism, Greek cults, Judaism, Buddhism and later Christianity coexisted within its borders. The Suren and other great families could make and unmake kings, weakening the monarchy in its later centuries.

Fall

Exhausted by Roman invasions and civil war, the empire fell in AD 224 when Ardashir I, a Persian vassal king, defeated Artabanus IV at Hormozdgan and founded the Sasanian Empire, though Arsacid dynasts continued to rule Armenia for two more centuries.
