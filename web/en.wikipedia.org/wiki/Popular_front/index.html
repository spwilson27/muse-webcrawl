Popular front

A popular front is a broad political coalition of left-wing and centre parties united primarily to resist fascism and the far right. The strategy was formally adopted by the Communist International (Comintern) at its Seventh Congress in 1935, reversing the earlier "social fascism" line that had branded social democrats as the main enemy. Under the leadership of Georgi Dimitrov, communist parties were instructed to ally with socialist, liberal, and other anti-fascist forces in defence of democratic institutions, albeit as a stage toward longer-term revolutionary goals.

France

The most celebrated popular front was the French Front populaire, an alliance of the French Section of the Workers' International (SFIO), the Radical-Socialist Party, and the French Communist Party (PCF). Formed in response to the far-right riot of 6 February 1934, the alliance won the elections of May 1936. The socialist leader Léon Blum became prime minister. A massive strike wave accompanied the victory, and the government concluded the Matignon Agreements with employers, granting workers the right to unionise, collective bargaining, a 40-hour week, and paid holidays. The government also nationalised the armaments industry and reformed the Bank of France. Internal divisions, economic difficulties, Blum's fall in 1937, and the tensions of the approaching war eroded the coalition by 1938.

Spain

In Spain, the Frente Popular, uniting republicans, socialists, communists, and regional parties, won the elections of February 1936. The new government's amnesty for political prisoners and resumption of reform intensified polarisation, and in July 1936 a military uprising led by General Francisco Franco triggered the Spanish Civil War. The Popular Front government led the Republican war effort — with Soviet aid and the International Brigades — until its defeat in 1939.

Elsewhere and legacy

Popular fronts or analogous anti-fascist coalitions arose in Chile, where a Popular Front under Pedro Aguirre Cerda won the 1938 election, and were attempted in various forms in Belgium, the United States, and elsewhere. After the Second World War, communist-led coalitions styling themselves "people's democracies" in Eastern Europe were sometimes described as popular fronts, though they differed fundamentally from the 1930s model. The term has remained a recurring reference point for debates about left unity and anti-fascist alliances.
