Wallace Line

The Wallace Line is a faunal boundary line drawn in 1859 by the British naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace that separates the biogeographic realms of Asia and 'Wallacea', a transitional zone between Asia and Australia. The line runs through the Malay Archipelago, between Borneo and Sulawesi and through the Lombok Strait between Bali and Lombok.

Description

West of the line, species are predominantly of Asian origin; to the east, many species are related to Australasian fauna. Marsupials and placental mammals, for example, are distributed with striking asymmetry: monkeys, tigers, and rhinoceroses occur to the west, while marsupials and birds such as cockatoos dominate to the east. The contrast is remarkable given the short distances involved — Bali and Lombok are only about 35 kilometres apart.

Explanation

The line marks the edge of the Sunda Shelf. During Pleistocene ice ages, sea levels fell by more than 100 metres, joining Borneo, Java, Sumatra, and Bali to mainland Asia, and joining New Guinea to Australia. The deep-water straits along the Wallace Line, however, remained submerged, forming a permanent barrier that few terrestrial animals could cross. Weber's Line and Lydekker's Line mark alternative boundaries within the transitional Wallacea zone, reflecting the graded nature of the faunal change.

Significance

Wallace's observations in the archipelago, published in The Malay Archipelago (1869), helped found the science of biogeography and, alongside his independent formulation of evolution by natural selection, made the line one of the most famous boundaries in natural history. The concept continues to inform studies of island biogeography and speciation.
