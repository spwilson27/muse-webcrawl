Reductionism

Reductionism
Field: Philosophy of science
Forms: Ontological, methodological, theoretical
Opposed by: Holism, emergentism

Reductionism is a family of philosophical positions about the connections between theories, concepts, and phenomena: roughly, that a complex system is nothing more than the sum of its parts and can be fully understood — or explained — in terms of those parts and their interactions.

Forms

Ontological reductionism

The claim that reality itself is composed of a minimal number of basic entities or substances — for example, that biological organisms are nothing but physicochemical systems.

Methodological reductionism

The research strategy of studying a system by taking it apart and examining its components. This strategy has been enormously successful in molecular biology and other sciences, though critics note its limits for organised, multi-level systems.

Theoretical reductionism

The idea, formalised by Ernest Nagel, that one theory can be derived from another — as thermodynamics was (partly) reduced to statistical mechanics, and chemistry, in principle, to quantum mechanics.

Criticism and emergence

Opponents point to emergence: properties of wholes (temperature, life, consciousness) that appear not to be usefully explicable at the level of parts. Philip Anderson's influential essay "More Is Different" (1972) argued that each level of complexity requires its own fundamental concepts, even given complete microphysical knowledge — a position often called weak emergence, contrasted with the strong emergence claimed by holists.
