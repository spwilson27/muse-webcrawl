Free and open-source software

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Free and open-source software (FOSS, or FLOSS) is software that is both free software and open-source software: it is released under licences that grant users the freedom to run, study, modify, and redistribute the program and its source code. The combined term is used to be neutral between the two overlapping but philosophically distinct movements — the free software movement, founded by Richard Stallman and the Free Software Foundation in the 1980s, which emphasises user freedom as an ethical imperative, and the open-source movement, launched in 1998, which emphasises practical benefits such as collaborative development and reliability.

Definitions

The Free Software Foundation's Free Software Definition rests on four freedoms: to run the program for any purpose, to study and change it, to redistribute copies, and to distribute modified versions. The Open Source Initiative's Open Source Definition, derived from the Debian Free Software Guidelines, lists ten criteria concerning free redistribution, source code availability, derived works, and non-discrimination. In practice nearly all licences qualify under both definitions, so most FOSS is simultaneously free and open source, though the two communities sometimes disagree about particular licences and priorities.

Licences

FOSS licences fall broadly into two families. Copyleft licences, such as the GNU General Public License (GPL) and the Mozilla Public License, require that derivative works be distributed under the same licence terms, keeping the code free. Permissive licences, such as the MIT, BSD, and Apache licences, allow derivatives to be released under different, even proprietary, terms, requiring mainly attribution. Other approaches include public-domain-equivalent dedications such as CC0 and the Unlicense.

History and adoption

Collaborative software sharing was normal in the early decades of computing. The modern movement began with Stallman's GNU Project (1983) and the founding of the Free Software Foundation (1985). The release of the Linux kernel under the GPL in 1991–92, combined with GNU tools, produced a complete free operating system. The 1998 coinage of "open source" and the success of projects such as Apache, Mozilla Firefox, and later Android, Git, and the programming ecosystems of Python and JavaScript made FOSS central to the technology industry: the large majority of web servers, smartphones, and supercomputers run free and open-source software.

Business models

Companies build businesses around FOSS through support and services, dual licensing, hosted "software as a service" offerings, open-core models, and donations or foundation sponsorship. Critics within the movement debate whether some models — particularly cloud hosting of copyleft code without reciprocity, addressed by licences such as the AGPL and SSPL — respect the spirit of software freedom.
