The Inn (Latin: Aenus; Romansh: En) is a river in Switzerland, Austria and Germany. It is a right tributary of the Danube and is 518 kilometres (322 mi) long. The highest point of its drainage basin is the summit of Piz Bernina (4,049 metres). The Engadine valley, through which it flows in Switzerland, is the only Swiss valley whose waters end up in the Black Sea (via the Danube).

Course

The Inn rises at Lake Lunghin near the Maloja Pass in the Swiss canton of Graubünden, at an altitude of about 2,484 metres, close to the triple watershed of the Rhine, Po, and Danube basins. It flows northeast through the Engadine, passing St. Moritz and Scuol, and enters Austria near Martina. In Tyrol it flows through Landeck and the provincial capital Innsbruck, whose name means "bridge over the Inn", then past Hall in Tirol, Schwaz, and Kufstein, where it leaves the Alps.

Entering Germany in Bavaria, it flows through Rosenheim and Wasserburg, receives the Salzach near Burghausen, and forms part of the Austrian–German border before joining the Danube at Passau, the "city of three rivers", where the Inn, Danube, and Ilz converge. At the confluence the Inn carries, on average, more water than the Danube itself.

Tributaries

Major tributaries include the Sanna, Ötztaler Ache, Sill, and Ziller in Tyrol, the Mangfall, Alz, and Salzach in Bavaria, and the Pram, Mattig, and Antiesen in Austria. The Salzach, joining near the border, is the largest tributary by discharge.

Hydroelectric power

The Inn and its tributaries are heavily developed for hydroelectric power, taking advantage of the Alpine head and glacial meltwater. A chain of run-of-the-river and storage plants in Tyrol and Bavaria supplies electricity and regulates flow; the Kaunertal and Sellrain-Silz plants are among the largest Austrian hydro schemes. The river is also used for whitewater rafting in the Engadine and Tyrolean gorges.

History

The Inn valley was an important prehistoric and Roman trade route; the Romans knew the river as the Aenus and built roads along it linking Raetia with Noricum. Inn shipping carried salt from Hall and timber downstream for centuries. Severe floods, notably in 2005, prompted extensive flood-protection works in Tyrol and Bavaria.
