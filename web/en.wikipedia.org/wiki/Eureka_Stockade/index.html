Eureka Stockade

The Eureka Stockade was a crude fortification built by rebellious gold miners at Ballarat, Victoria, Australia, in late November and early December 1854, and the site of the armed clash that concluded the Eureka Rebellion on 3 December 1854. The stockade has become one of the most enduring symbols in Australian history, associated with the birth of democracy in Australia.

Construction

Following the licence-burning meeting of 29 November 1854, diggers led by Peter Lalor began enclosing an area of about an acre on the Eureka lead with a barricade of slabs, logs, overturned carts and sharpened stakes. The structure was intended more as a defensive rallying point and symbol of resistance than as a serious fortification. Within it, the rebels drilled under their leaders and flew the Eureka Flag, a blue ensign with the stars of the Southern Cross joined by white lines.

The battle

At dawn on Sunday, 3 December 1854, a combined force of British soldiers of the 12th and 40th regiments and Victorian police, totalling about 276 men under Captain John Thomas, advanced on the stockade. Many diggers had left for the night, expecting no attack on a Sunday, leaving only about 120–150 defenders. The assault overwhelmed the stockade in roughly 15 minutes. At least 22 diggers were killed, along with six members of the government force. Around 120 diggers were arrested; thirteen were later tried for treason and acquitted.

Legacy

Although a military defeat, the stockade achieved most of its political aims within a year: the hated licence was abolished, miners gained the vote, and the goldfields were reformed. The site in Ballarat East is now occupied by the Eureka Stockade memorial and gardens and the Museum of Australian Democracy at Eureka. The original Eureka Flag is displayed at the Eureka Centre in Ballarat. "Eureka" and its flag have since been invoked by trade unions, republicans, and movements across the Australian political spectrum.
