In mathematics, specifically in the study of ordinary differential equations, the Peano existence theorem, Peano theorem or Cauchy–Peano theorem, named after Giuseppe Peano and Augustin-Louis Cauchy, is a fundamental theorem which guarantees the existence of solutions to certain initial value problems.

Statement

Let D be an open subset of R × R with f : D → R a continuous function and let

y′(x) = f(x, y(x)),   y(x0) = y0

be an initial value problem, where (x0, y0) lies in D. Then there exists some ε > 0 such that the problem has a solution defined on the interval [x0 − ε, x0 + ε]. The solution need not be unique: different solutions may branch from the same initial point, as in the equation y′ = 2√|y| with y(0) = 0, which admits both y ≡ 0 and y = x2 (for x ≥ 0).

History and proof

Cauchy stated an existence result under stronger hypotheses (continuity of a derivative); Peano published the first correct existence proof under mere continuity in 1886, with details in 1890. Standard proofs construct solutions as limits of approximate solutions: either by the Euler polygonal method combined with the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem, or by applying the Schauder fixed-point theorem to the equivalent integral equation. Uniqueness requires an additional hypothesis, such as the Lipschitz condition supplied by the Picard–Lindelöf theorem.

Related results

The Carathéodory existence theorem generalises Peano's theorem to equations whose right-hand side is merely measurable in x and continuous in y. In infinite dimensions, continuity alone does not suffice: Dieudonné gave counterexamples showing that Peano's theorem fails in general Banach spaces.
