A Series A round is typically a startup's first significant institutional financing.

A Series A round (also called Series A financing or a Series A investment) is the first significant round of venture capital financing for a startup company, following initial seed funding. The name refers to the class of preferred stock—Series A preferred stock—sold to investors in exchange for their capital. Series A rounds are raised once a company has developed a track record: a working product, early revenue or user growth, and a business model ready to be scaled.

Purpose and process

Proceeds from a Series A are generally used to optimise the product and user base, hire key staff, and fund the company's push to product–market fit and revenue growth. The round is usually led by one venture capital firm (the "lead investor"), which negotiates the term sheet, including the pre-money valuation, liquidation preference, board composition, and protective provisions; other investors join the syndicate on the same terms.

Companies failing to attract a Series A after seed funding sometimes face a "Series A crunch". Subsequent rounds are labelled alphabetically—Series B, C, D—each associated with progressively more mature companies and larger amounts of capital.

Instruments

In the United States, Series A investments are typically structured as purchases of preferred stock with rights senior to common stock held by founders. Outside formal priced rounds, some companies raise "seed extension" or convertible instruments (such as SAFEs and convertible notes) before undertaking a full Series A.
