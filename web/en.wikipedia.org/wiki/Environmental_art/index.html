Environmental art

Environmental art is a range of artistic practices encompassing both historical approaches to nature in art and more recent ecological and politically motivated types of works. It encompasses land art, ecological art, and site-specific installations that engage with natural systems, weather, and environmental issues such as climate change and restoration.

History

The movement emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s, partly in reaction to the commercial gallery system and rising environmental awareness. Robert Smithson's Spiral Jetty (1970) in Utah's Great Salt Lake became its icon. Michael Heizer, Walter De Maria and Nancy Holt created monumental desert works.

Ecological art

From the 1980s, artists such as Agnes Denes (Wheatfield — A Confrontation, 1982), Joseph Beuys (7000 Oaks) and Mel Chin shifted toward remediation, planting, and collaboration with scientists — art intended to heal ecosystems rather than merely occupy them. Andy Goldsworthy works with ephemeral natural materials: leaves, ice, stone.

Themes

Environmental art addresses sustainability, biodiversity loss, water, and human impact on landscapes, often outside museums and changing with seasons and decay — impermanence itself being part of the work.
