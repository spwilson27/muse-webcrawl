The Vega programme (from Russian Venera + Galleya, "Venus" and "Halley") was a pair of Soviet uncrewed space missions launched in December 1984 that combined a flyby and landing at Venus with an interception of Halley's Comet during its 1986 perihelion passage. The two spacecraft, Vega 1 and Vega 2, were built on the Venera design and carried substantial French and international instrumentation.

The missions

Vega 1 and Vega 2 launched on 15 and 21 December 1984 aboard Proton rockets. Each craft consisted of a flyby bus and a descent module. Arriving at Venus in June 1985, each released a lander and a helium balloon that floated in the Venusian atmosphere at about 54 km altitude for some 46 hours, drifting thousands of kilometres while measuring winds, temperature and pressure — the first balloons flown on another planet. The landing capsules transmitted from the surface for roughly an hour, analysing soil composition.

Halley encounter

After the Venus swingby, the flyby buses continued to Halley's Comet. Vega 1 passed within about 8,900 km of the nucleus on 6 March 1986, and Vega 2 within about 8,000 km on 9 March. Together they returned more than 1,500 television images showing the comet's dark, irregular nucleus — about 15 × 8 km — and measured its dust, gas and plasma environment. Coming a week before the European Giotto probe's closer approach, Vega data helped refine Giotto's targeting, earning the missions a place in the informal "Halley Armada."

Legacy

The Vega missions were among the most ambitious and internationally cooperative of the Soviet planetary programme, involving scientists from nine countries. They were the last Soviet missions to Venus, and the balloon aerobot concept they demonstrated remains a reference for proposed future Venus exploration.
