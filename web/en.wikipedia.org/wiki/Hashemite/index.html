The Hashemites (Arabic: الهاشميون, al-Hāshimiyyūn), also known as the House of Hashim, are a royal dynasty and clan claiming descent from Hashim ibn Abd Manaf, the great-grandfather of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, through the tribe of Quraysh. Members of the family served as Sharifs and Emirs of Mecca for centuries and, after the First World War, supplied the monarchs of the Hejaz, Syria, Iraq and Jordan.

History

Sharifs of Mecca

From the tenth century onward, descendants of Hasan ibn Ali — grandson of Muhammad — ruled Mecca as sharifs under the nominal authority of various empires, finally the Ottomans. The last Ottoman-appointed Sharif, Hussein bin Ali, launched the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in 1916 with British support.

Kingdoms after the First World War

Hussein proclaimed himself King of the Hejaz, while his sons took thrones abroad: Faisal became King of Syria in 1920 and, after being expelled by the French, King of Iraq in 1921; Abdullah became Emir (later King) of Transjordan. Hussein lost the Hejaz to Ibn Saud in 1924–25, ending Hashemite rule in Arabia.

Iraq and Jordan

The Iraqi Hashemite monarchy lasted until 1958, when King Faisal II was overthrown and killed in a military coup. The Jordanian branch survives: Abdullah I was assassinated in 1951, his grandson Hussein reigned from 1952 to 1999, and his son Abdullah II is the reigning King of Jordan. The Hashemites also administer, through the Jordanian waqf, the Muslim holy sites of Jerusalem, including the Al-Aqsa Mosque compound.
