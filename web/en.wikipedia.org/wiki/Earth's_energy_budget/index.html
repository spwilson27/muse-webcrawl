Earth's energy budget (or radiation budget) accounts for the balance between the energy Earth receives from the Sun and the energy it radiates back to outer space. Of the roughly 340 watts per square metre of solar radiation arriving at the top of the atmosphere (averaged over the whole globe and over day and night), about 100 W/m² is reflected back to space by clouds, aerosols and bright surfaces such as ice — the planet's albedo — while the remaining ~240 W/m² is absorbed by the atmosphere and surface.

To maintain balance, the Earth system emits long-wave (thermal infrared) radiation to space. Greenhouse gases and clouds absorb much of the infrared emitted by the surface and re-radiate it in all directions, so the surface must warm to a higher temperature — an average of about 15 °C instead of the −18 °C expected without an atmosphere — for the planet as a whole to shed energy at the required rate. This is the natural greenhouse effect.

Components

Within the system, energy is also moved by evaporation and condensation of water (latent heat) and by convection (sensible heat), and transported from tropics to poles by atmospheric and ocean circulation. Satellite instruments such as NASA's CERES measure the radiation fluxes at the top of the atmosphere, while ocean measurements (notably the Argo float array) track where excess heat accumulates.

Energy imbalance and climate change

Human activities, principally the emission of greenhouse gases, have disturbed the budget: Earth currently absorbs more energy than it emits, an imbalance estimated at roughly 0.5–1 W/m² and rising. Over 90% of the excess heat goes into the oceans. Restoring balance requires either reducing the forcing or allowing the planet to warm until outgoing radiation again matches absorbed sunlight — the physical basis of global warming.
