Ancient Greek units of measurement

Ancient Greek units of measurement varied considerably from city-state to city-state and over time, but were fundamentally derived from the parts of the human body: the finger, the palm, the foot, and the cubit. Standard measures were kept in temples and public buildings, and reliefs such as the metrological relief from Salamis display the standards of a community carved in stone.

The stadion, the standard distance for footraces at Olympia, underlies many Greek geographical measurements; Eratosthenes used it in his celebrated calculation of the circumference of the Earth. The Attic foot of about 29.6 cm was used for the Parthenon, while the Olympic foot measured about 32 cm.

Volume and weight

Dry and liquid measures were based on different vessels: the medimnos (about 52 litres) for grain and the metrētēs (about 39 litres) for wine and oil, each divided into smaller units such as the choinix — roughly a day's grain ration — and the kotylē cup. Weights and coinage shared units: the talent (about 26 kg in the Attic system), the mina (one-sixtieth of a talent), the stater, and the drachma, divided into six obols.
