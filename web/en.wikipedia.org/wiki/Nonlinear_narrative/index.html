Nonlinear narrative

Nonlinear narrative, disjointed narrative or disrupted narrative is a narrative technique where events are portrayed out of chronological order or in other ways where temporality does not follow the direct causality pattern of the events featured. Storytellers have always used devices such as flashback and in medias res openings, but some works make nonlinearity a structural principle.

In literature

Early examples include The Odyssey, which begins in the middle of the action, and Laurence Sterne's Tristram Shandy (1759–1767), whose digressions mock linear storytelling. Modernist and postmodernist writers developed the technique extensively: William Faulkner's The Sound and the Fury (1929) presents the same events through disordered perspectives; Joseph Heller's Catch-22 circles its events; Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five has its protagonist "unstuck in time"; and Julio Cortázar's Hopscotch offers alternative reading orders.

In film and television

Cinema developed techniques such as the reverse chronology of Memento (2000) and Irréversible (2002), the fragmented ensemble structure of Quentin Tarantino's Pulp Fiction (1994), and the branching "hyperlink cinema" of Syriana and Babel. Influential precursors include Citizen Kane's nested flashbacks and Akira Kurosawa's Rashomon, which recounts contradictory versions of one event. Interactive media and video games can make nonlinearity literal, allowing the audience's choices to determine the order — or occurrence — of events.
