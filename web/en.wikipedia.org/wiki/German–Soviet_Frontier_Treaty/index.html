The German–Soviet Frontier Treaty, officially the German–Soviet Boundary and Friendship Treaty, was signed by Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union on 28 September 1939, shortly after their joint invasion of Poland. It was the second of the two pacts that are collectively known as the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, supplementing the non-aggression treaty of 23 August 1939.

Terms

The public treaty redrew the boundary between the German and Soviet occupation zones in conquered Poland, roughly along the Bug River (the "Curzon Line" in part), assigning the Lublin region and part of the Warsaw province to Germany in exchange for placing Lithuania in the Soviet sphere. A secret protocol confirmed Lithuania's transfer and adjusted the August spheres of influence; further secret articles pledged both parties to suppress Polish agitation in their zones and to exchange populations of German and Ukrainian/Belarusian minorities.

Context and consequences

Germany had invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and the Red Army followed from the east on 17 September. The treaty formalised the fourth partition of Poland and was accompanied by a joint declaration blaming Britain and France for continuing the war. German–Soviet cooperation continued — in trade, and in the handover of refugees — until Germany repudiated the arrangement by invading the Soviet Union in Operation Barbarossa on 22 June 1941.
