Beyer-Garratt

A Beyer-Garratt locomotive, showing the boiler slung between two engine units

A Beyer-Garratt locomotive is a type of articulated steam locomotive built under the Garratt patent by Beyer, Peacock and Company of Manchester, England, and by licensees worldwide. The design, patented by Herbert William Garratt in 1907, places the boiler on a central cradle frame suspended between two separate engine units, each with its own cylinders, wheels, fuel and water capacity.

Design

Hinging the locomotive into three parts lets a very large boiler and firebox be carried on a machine that can still negotiate sharp curves, because the engine units pivot beneath the boiler cradle. The front unit carries a water tank and the rear unit carries fuel (coal or oil) and additional water. Because the boiler is unobstructed by driving wheels, it can be short, wide and deep, giving excellent steaming capacity. Garratts were built in wheel arrangements from small 0-4-0+0-4-0s up to the massive 4-8-2+2-8-4 "Double Mountain" types used in Africa.

Use

The first Garratt was built in 1909 for the Tasmanian Government Railways. Beyer, Peacock built over a thousand Garratts, and they became mainstays of railways in Africa, Australia, South America and Asia, where steep gradients, light track and tight curves demanded high power at low axle load. Notable classes include the East African Railways 59 class — among the largest steam locomotives ever built for metre gauge — and the South African Railways GMA/GMAM class. Though largely displaced by diesels, Garratts survive in heritage service, notably on the Welsh Highland Railway, which operates several ex-South African examples.
