Pliosaur

Pliosaurs are an extinct group of marine reptiles belonging to the order Plesiosauria, distinguished from the long-necked plesiosaurs (in the strict sense) by their short necks and large, elongated heads armed with powerful jaws and conical teeth. They were among the top predators of Mesozoic seas, flourishing from the Early Jurassic until the middle of the Late Cretaceous, roughly 200 to 85 million years ago. The group includes some of the largest marine predators of all time, such as Kronosaurus, Liopleurodon, and Pliosaurus.

Anatomy

Pliosaurs had a compact, streamlined body, a very short tail, and four large paddle-like limbs — the hind flippers being especially large in many forms — used in a "underwater flight" style of swimming. In contrast to long-necked plesiosaurs, the head was proportionally huge: in the largest species the skull alone could exceed 2 metres in length, and the neck contained as few as about 13 vertebrae compared with more than 70 in elasmosaurs. The jaws bore large, robust, roughly circular-in-cross-section teeth suited to seizing and dismembering large prey, and estimates of bite force for the biggest pliosaurs rival or exceed those of any living animal.

Evolution and classification

Pliosaurs evolved from early plesiosaurians resembling the long-necked forms. Early Jurassic genera such as Rhomaleosaurus and Attenborosaurus show intermediate proportions. The family Pliosauridae proper includes Pliosaurus, Liopleurodon, Simolestes, and Peloneustes. In the Cretaceous, the giant brachaucheniines — including Kronosaurus of Australia and Colombia and Brachauchenius of North America — were the last and among the largest of the lineage. Pliosaurs declined and disappeared by the Turonian stage of the Late Cretaceous, after which their apex-predator role in the oceans was taken over by mosasaurs.

Palaeobiology

Like other plesiosaurs, pliosaurs were air-breathing and gave birth to live young, as demonstrated by a fossil of a pregnant specimen of the related Polycotylus. Stomach contents include fish, belemnites and ammonites, and the bones of other marine reptiles, indicating that the largest pliosaurs were generalist apex predators. Their olfactory anatomy suggests a good sense of smell, possibly used to track prey, and they may have combined ambush and pursuit strategies using their powerful flippers.

Famous specimens

Specimens given popular names include "Predator X", a giant pliosaur from Svalbard later named Pliosaurus funkei, and the "Monster of Aramberri" from Mexico. A near-complete skull from the Jurassic Coast of Dorset, England, attributed to Pliosaurus, was excavated in 2023 and described as one of the most complete pliosaur skulls known.
