Good enough principle

The good enough principle is a rule in software and systems design: a system or product that meets the needs of its users is preferable to a more elaborate system that fails to ship or that users cannot use. In other words, developers should deliver systems that are good for users rather than systems that are perfect. It is closely related to the design philosophy of "worse is better" and the notion of satisficing.

Good enough principle
DomainSoftware engineering, product design
RelatedWorse is better; satisficing; KISS principle; minimum viable product

Explanation

The principle holds that, at some point, additional refinement yields diminishing value compared with the costs of delay and complexity. A "good enough" word processor that most people can master may succeed where a technically superior but unwieldy one fails — a pattern illustrated by the historical success of simple systems such as early web protocols and the IBM PC's open architecture.

Related ideas

  • Worse is better — Richard P. Gabriel's argument that simplicity and adequacy in implementation can beat correctness and completeness.
  • Satisficing — Herbert Simon's term for choosing an adequate option rather than the optimal one.
  • Perfect is the enemy of good — the aphorism, often attributed to Voltaire, warning against letting perfection block completion.
