Nephrectomy

A nephrectomy is the surgical removal of a kidney, performed to treat a number of kidney diseases including kidney cancer. It is also done to remove a normal healthy kidney from a living or deceased donor, which is part of a kidney transplantation procedure.

History

The first successful nephrectomy was performed by the German surgeon Gustav Simon on August 2, 1869, in Heidelberg. Simon experimented beforehand in animal experiments. He proved that one healthy kidney can be sufficient for urine excretion in humans.

Indications

There are various indications for this procedure, which include renal cell carcinoma, a non-functioning kidney (which may cause high blood pressure), and a congenitally small kidney (in which the kidney is swelling, causing it to press on nerves which can cause pain in unrelated areas such as the back). Nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma is rapidly modifying to allow partial removal of the kidney. Nephrectomy is also performed for the purpose of living donor kidney transplantation. A nephroureterectomy is the removal of a kidney, the entire ureter, and a small cuff of the bladder for urothelial cancer of the kidney or ureter.

Procedure

The surgery is performed with the patient under general anesthesia. A kidney can be removed through an open incision or by laparoscopic surgery. For the open procedure, the surgeon makes an incision in the side of the abdomen to reach the kidney. Depending on circumstances, the incision can also be made midline. The ureter and blood vessels are disconnected, and the kidney is then removed. The laparoscopic approach involves several small incisions and the use of a camera and specialized instruments; the kidney is placed in a bag and removed through one of the incisions.

Types

A partial nephrectomy removes only the diseased or injured portion of the kidney while sparing the healthy remainder, and is increasingly preferred for small renal tumours because it preserves renal function. A simple nephrectomy removes the kidney alone for benign disease, while a radical nephrectomy removes the kidney together with the surrounding fascia, adjacent lymph nodes, and sometimes the adrenal gland, typically for cancer.

Risks and complications

Possible complications include infection, bleeding, damage to adjacent organs, and reduced function of the remaining kidney. In the long term, living with a single kidney is generally well tolerated, though patients are monitored for hypertension and declining glomerular filtration rate.

After care

Pain medication is often given following the surgery, and an IV may be administered to help with electrolytes and fluids. Recovery from an open nephrectomy typically takes several weeks, while laparoscopic patients often leave hospital within a few days.
