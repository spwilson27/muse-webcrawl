Succubus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A succubus as depicted in early modern demonological illustrations.

A succubus (plural succubi) is a demon or supernatural entity in folklore, in female form, that appears in dreams to seduce men, usually through sexual activity. According to religious tradition, a succubus needs semen to survive; repeated sexual activity with a succubus will result in a bond being formed between the succubus and the man, and a succubus will drain or harm the man with whom it is having intercourse. In modern representations, a succubus is often depicted as a beautiful seductress or enchantress, rather than as demonic or frightening.

The male counterpart to the succubus is the incubus. Religious traditions hold that repeated encounters with such beings could lead to deterioration of health or mental state, and in the most extreme cases, death.

Etymology

The word is derived from Late Latin succuba, meaning "paramour", from succubare, "to lie beneath" (sub- "under" and cubare "to lie"), used to describe this being since at least 1387. The term was also used for a prostitute in medieval Latin.

In folklore

According to the Zohar and the Alphabet of Ben Sira, Lilith was Adam's first wife, who later became a succubus. She left Adam and refused to return to the Garden of Eden after she mated with the archangel Samael. In Zoharistic Kabbalah, there were four succubi who mated with the archangel Samael: Lilith, Eisheth Zenunium, Naamah, and Agrat bat Mahlat.

In 1589, Peter Binsfeld paired each of the deadly sins with a demon supposedly responsible for it; lust was paired with Asmodeus. Female demons in Babylonian and Jewish mythology, such as the lilin, were believed to visit men at night.

Ability to reproduce

According to the Malleus Maleficarum (1486), succubi collect semen from the men they seduce. Incubi then use that semen to impregnate human females, thus explaining how demons could supposedly sire children despite the traditional belief that they were incapable of reproduction. Children so begotten were called cambions.

In fiction

Succubi appear throughout literature, film, television, and video games, usually as alluring female temptresses or antagonists. Notable appearances include characters in fantasy role-playing games, the television series Charmed and Lost Girl, and numerous works of fantasy fiction.
