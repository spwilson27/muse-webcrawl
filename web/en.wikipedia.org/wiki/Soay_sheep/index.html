The Soay sheep is a primitive breed of domestic sheep descended from a population of feral sheep on the island of Soay in the St Kilda archipelago, about 65 kilometres west of the Outer Hebrides in Scotland. It is one of the Northern European short-tailed sheep breeds and is thought to closely resemble the sheep kept in Britain in the Bronze Age and Iron Age. The name "Soay" comes from the Old Norse for "sheep island".

Characteristics

Soays are small, fine-boned and exceptionally hardy. Ewes weigh only around 25 kilograms and rams about 40 kilograms. The fleece is short and sheds naturally in spring, so the animals need not be shorn and can be "rooed" — plucked by hand. The coat is usually chocolate brown or tan, sometimes with a pale belly patch and rump patch resembling those of the mouflon, and occasionally black or fawn individuals occur. Both sexes are usually horned; the rams' horns are large and curved, while a minority of ewes are polled or have small scurs.

History

Sheep were kept on Soay for centuries, largely unmanaged, while the human population of St Kilda lived mainly on the neighbouring island of Hirta. When the remaining islanders were evacuated in 1930, the sheep were left behind. In 1932, 107 Soays were transferred to Hirta, where they have lived as a fully feral population ever since. Small numbers were also taken to the mainland, where the breed is now kept by hobby farmers and used for conservation grazing; it is classed as "at risk" by the Rare Breeds Survival Trust.

Research population

The feral Soay population on Hirta is one of the most intensively studied wild mammal populations in the world. Since 1985, researchers led by teams from the University of Edinburgh and other institutions have individually marked and followed the sheep, recording survival, reproduction, body weight, parasites and genetics. The long-term study has produced influential findings on population cycles, natural and sexual selection, ageing, and the genetics of coat colour and horn size, including the discovery that the horn gene RXFP2 also affects survival — an example of antagonistic pleiotropy maintaining variation in the population.
