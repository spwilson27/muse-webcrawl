Calypso is a style of Afro-Caribbean music that originated in Trinidad and Tobago in the early to mid-19th century and spread throughout the Caribbean and Venezuela. It is characterised by highly rhythmic, lyrical songs whose words — delivered by a singer called a calypsonian — traditionally comment on social and political events, often with satire, double entendre, and improvised wit.

History

Origins

Calypso developed from the West African kaiso tradition and the songs of enslaved Africans brought to the French-speaking Caribbean colonies. On Trinidad's sugar plantations, singers known as chantwells led work songs and Carnival performances, praising or mocking figures in the community. Singing was long dominated by French Creole, but by the early 20th century English became the principal language of the calypso tent.

The recording era

Calypso was among the earliest Caribbean music to be commercially recorded: the Lovey's String Band recorded in New York in 1912, and vocal recordings by singers such as Sam Manning followed. In the 1930s and 1940s, performers including Roaring Lion, Lord Invader, Attila the Hun, and the Duke of Iron carried calypso to New York, where it became part of the Harlem music scene.

International popularity

In 1956 Harry Belafonte's album Calypso, featuring "Day-O (The Banana Boat Song)", became the first LP to sell over a million copies, sparking a worldwide calypso craze. Lord Invader's "Rum and Coca-Cola", popularised by the Andrews Sisters, produced a landmark copyright case over the song's authorship. In Trinidad itself, stars such as Lord Kitchener and the Mighty Sparrow dominated the Carnival competitions; Sparrow's "Jean and Dinah" (1956) marked a new era of social commentary.

Calypso and Carnival

Calypso is inseparable from the Trinidad Carnival. Each season, calypsonians perform new compositions in calypso tents, competing for titles such as Calypso Monarch. The genre's tradition of picong — ritualised verbal sparring — and its role as a newspaper of the street made it a vital channel of public opinion, sometimes leading colonial and post-colonial governments to censor lyrics.

Legacy and related styles

In the 1970s calypso blended with funk, soul, and Indian rhythms to produce soca, which largely supplanted traditional calypso as Carnival party music, while calypso retained its role as the festival's vehicle for commentary. Related forms include rapso, chutney soca, and extempo, an improvisational competitive form. Calypso influence can also be heard in mento (Jamaica), zouk, and early ska.
