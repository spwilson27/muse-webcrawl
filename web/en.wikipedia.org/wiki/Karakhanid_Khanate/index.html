The Karakhanid Khanate (Persian: قره‌خانیان, Qarakhāniyān; Chinese: 黑汗王朝) — also rendered Kara-Khanid Khanate — was a confederation ruled by a Turkic dynasty in Transoxiana and the western Tarim Basin in Central Asia between 840 and 1212. The Karakhanids were the first Turkic dynasty to embrace Islam, and under them the Turkic peoples of Central Asia began a long process of Islamisation and Turkification of formerly Iranian-speaking regions such as Transoxiana. Name and origins

The dynasty's name derives from the title Qara Khan ("Black Khan" or "Great Khan"), carried by its supreme rulers; the element qara in Old Turkic connotes both "black" and "great/northern". Chinese sources call the realm the "Black Khan kingdom". The ruling clan drew on Karluk, Chigil, and Yagma tribal groups whose homelands lay in Semirechye (the region of the seven rivers) and the Chu and Talas valleys, and who moved into the vacuum left by the collapse of the Uyghur Khaganate in 840. Their self-designation linked them to the epic hero Afrasiyab of Turan.

The khanate's zenith came in the decades after 999, when it controlled the rich Silk Road cities of Samarkand, Bukhara, Khujand, and Kashgar and shared a frontier with Ghaznavid Khorasan along the Oxus. Chronic succession disputes among the many titled khans weakened the confederation, and by the 12th century both halves survived as vassals — of the Seljuks, then of the Kara-Khitai — until first the west (1212) and then the east (by 1218) were absorbed by the Khwarazmian Empire and the Mongols respectively.

Political organisation

The khanate functioned less as a unitary state than as a dynastic confederation: senior and junior khans (Qara Khan, Arslan Khan, Bughra Khan, Ilig, and others in a ranked titulature) held appanages (ulus) across the realm, issuing their own coinage at numerous mints. Persian bureaucratic practice, inherited from the Samanids, ran the cities' taxation and chanceries, and Persian remained a language of administration and high culture alongside Turkic.

Legacy

The Karakhanids set the template for Turkic-Islamic statehood in Central Asia: their era produced the first Turkic Islamic literature (the Kutadgu Bilig and Kashgari's Dīwān), sponsored madrasas and caravanserais on the Silk Road, and left monuments such as the Kalyan minaret in Bukhara and the Uzgend mausolea in the Fergana valley. The name "Karakhanid" survives in historiography for the entire period of their rule and in the archaeological typology of their ceramics and coinage.
