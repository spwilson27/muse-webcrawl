Harveys of Hayle was a family-founded iron foundry and engineering works in Hayle, Cornwall, England, established by John Harvey in 1779. The company became one of the great engineering firms of the Cornish mining industry, famous above all for the enormous beam engines it built to pump water from mines, and for its long rivalry with the neighbouring Cornish Copper Company at Copperhouse.

History

John Harvey, a blacksmith and millwright from Carnhell Green, set up a small foundry and forge at Penpol in Hayle in 1779. The business grew with Cornwall's booming copper and tin mines, supplying castings, boilers and machinery. Under his son Henry Harvey and with engineer Arthur Woolf associated with the firm in its early years, Harveys developed into one of the world's leading makers of Cornish beam engines. Their engines were exported worldwide; a Harveys engine of 1840s vintage with a 144-inch cylinder for the Dutch polder drainage at Cruquius in the Netherlands was among the largest steam engines ever built.

The firm also ran a shipping and banking business and built the harbour works that made Hayle a major port. Feuding with the Cornish Copper Company across the estuary — including disputes over wharf access and coal imports — is remembered in the local saying "Hell is Hayle".

Decline

As Cornish mining declined in the later nineteenth century, so did Harveys' engineering trade. The engineering works closed in 1903, though the family firm continued in builders' merchanting and retail in Cornwall for much of the twentieth century. Surviving Harveys engines are preserved at sites including the Crofton Pumping Station and in the Netherlands at the Cruquius Museum.
