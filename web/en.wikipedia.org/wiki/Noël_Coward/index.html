Sir Noël Peirce Coward (16 December 1899 – 26 March 1973) was an English playwright, composer, director, actor, and singer, known for his wit, flamboyance, and what Time magazine called "a sense of personal style, a combination of cheek and chic, pose and poise". Born into a comfortable middle-class family in Teddington, he was a child actor from the age of eleven and achieved his first major success as a playwright in his twenties.

Career

Coward's breakthrough came with The Vortex (1924), a sensational play about nymphomania and drug addiction among the fashionable classes that he also starred in. By the early 1930s he was among the world's highest-earning writers, with successes including the operetta Bitter Sweet (1929), the epic pageant Cavalcade (1931), the comedy Private Lives (1930) — written for Gertrude Lawrence and himself — and Design for Living (1933). His songs, among them "Mad Dogs and Englishmen" and "I'll See You Again", became standards of the cabaret repertoire.

During the Second World War he entertained troops and worked for British intelligence, while also writing and co-directing (with David Lean) the patriotic film In Which We Serve (1942). His play Blithe Spirit (1941) ran for 1,997 performances in London, a record for a non-musical. With Lean he adapted his one-act play Still Life into the film Brief Encounter (1945), now regarded as a classic of British cinema.

Later years and legacy

After a slump in the 1950s, when critics favoured the new "kitchen sink" realism, Coward's reputation revived in the 1960s with revivals of his plays (dubbed "Dad's Renaissance") and late works such as Suite in Three Keys (1966). He was knighted in 1970. Coward lived much of his later life in Jamaica, where he died in 1973. His plays — noted for their polished dialogue, brittle sophistication, and underlying emotional seriousness — remain staples of the English-speaking stage.
