Tracheal intubation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tracheal intubation, usually simply referred to as intubation, is the placement of a flexible plastic tube into the trachea (windpipe) to maintain an open airway or to serve as a conduit through which to administer certain drugs. It is frequently performed in critically injured, ill, or anaesthetised patients to facilitate ventilation of the lungs, including mechanical ventilation, and to prevent the possibility of asphyxiation or airway obstruction.

The most widely used route is orotracheal, in which an endotracheal tube is passed through the mouth and vocal cords into the trachea. In a nasotracheal procedure, a tube is passed through the nose and vocal cords. Terms for the procedure include endotracheal intubation and tracheal intubation, and among emergency physicians intubation is a component of rapid sequence intubation, or RSI.

Indications

Indications include failure to maintain or protect the airway (for example, in coma or after cardiac arrest), failure of ventilation or oxygenation, an anticipated need for mechanical ventilation in critical illness, and the need to secure the airway during general anaesthesia for surgery. Intubation protects the lungs against aspiration of stomach contents, though it does not eliminate the risk entirely.

Equipment and technique

Intubation is usually performed with the aid of a laryngoscope — either a conventional direct laryngoscope with a Macintosh or Miller blade, or a video laryngoscope — to visualise the vocal cords. The endotracheal tube, typically a cuffed plastic tube with an inflatable balloon at its tip, is advanced between the cords; the cuff is inflated to seal the airway. Correct placement is confirmed principally by waveform capnography (detection of exhaled carbon dioxide), supplemented by auscultation of breath sounds and observation of chest movement. Unrecognised oesophageal intubation is a potentially fatal complication, and capnography is now a mandatory standard in most settings.

Before the procedure, patients are preoxygenated, and drugs — a sedative induction agent and a neuromuscular blocking agent such as succinylcholine or rocuronium — are usually given (rapid sequence induction) to optimise conditions and reduce aspiration risk. A bougie or stylet may assist difficult placements.

Difficult airway and alternatives

Some intubations are difficult because of anatomy, trauma, swelling or restricted neck movement. Predictors are assessed using tools such as the Mallampati score and the mnemonic LEMON. Alternatives and rescue techniques include supraglottic airway devices such as the laryngeal mask airway, bag-valve-mask ventilation, fibreoptic intubation, and, when intubation and oxygenation both fail, emergency front-of-neck access (cricothyrotomy or tracheostomy).

Complications

Complications include trauma to the teeth, lips, tongue and larynx; oesophageal or endobronchial (mainstem) intubation; hypoxia and aspiration during attempts; cardiovascular instability from induction drugs; and, with prolonged intubation, vocal cord injury, tracheal stenosis and ventilator-associated pneumonia. Patients requiring prolonged ventilation may receive a tracheostomy.

History

Early descriptions of tracheotomy date to antiquity, and William Macewen reported oral intubation for anaesthesia in 1878. Intubation was advanced during the First World War by Ivan Magill and Stanley Rowbotham, and the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic — during which medical students hand-ventilated patients through tracheostomies — established modern intensive care ventilation.
