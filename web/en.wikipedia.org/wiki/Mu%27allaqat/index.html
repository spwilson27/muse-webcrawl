Mu'allaqat

TitleAl-Mu'allaqāt (The Suspended Odes)
LanguageClassical Arabic
Period6th century CE (pre-Islamic)
Number of poemsSeven (some lists give ten)
Notable poetsImru' al-Qays, Labid, Zuhayr, Tarafa, Antara, Amr ibn Kulthum, al-Harith ibn Hilliza

The Mu'allaqat (Arabic: المعلقات) is a group of seven long Arabic poems, or qasidas, composed in the pre-Islamic era and regarded as the finest surviving works of early Arabic poetry. According to tradition, the poems were so esteemed that they were written in gold on Coptic linen and suspended on the walls of the Kaaba at Mecca, an account reflected in their name, which means 'The Suspended Ones' or 'The Hanging Poems', though modern scholars treat the literal hanging story with caution.

The poems

The canonical seven poets are Imru' al-Qays, Tarafa, Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, Labid ibn Rabi'a, Amr ibn Kulthum, Antara ibn Shaddad and al-Harith ibn Hilliza. Some recensions extend the list to ten, adding poets such as al-A'sha and an-Nabigha. Each poem follows the conventions of the qasida, typically opening with a halt at the abandoned campsite of the beloved (the nasib), continuing with a journey section describing the poet's mount and the desert, and concluding with boasting, praise or tribal counsel.

Imru' al-Qays's ode, opening 'Stop, let us weep at the memory of a beloved', is the most famous of the set.

Transmission and significance

The odes were transmitted orally by professional reciters before being collected by early Muslim scholars; the recension of Hammad al-Rawiya in the 8th century is central to their survival. Their language became a normative standard for Classical Arabic and a yardstick of poetic excellence.

The Mu'allaqat remain central to Arabic literary studies and have been translated into many languages, influencing later Arabic, Persian and Urdu poetic traditions.
