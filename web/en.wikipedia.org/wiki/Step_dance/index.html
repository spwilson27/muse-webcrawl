Step dance

Step dance is a generic term for dance styles in which the footwork is considered the most important part of the dance, and in which the dancer's shoes strike the floor to create audible percussive rhythms. Body movements and arm movements are either restricted or considered irrelevant. The term covers a family of related forms found across the British Isles, North America and elsewhere.

Forms

  • Irish stepdance — characterised by a stiff upper body, arms held straight at the sides, and rapid, intricate footwork. It is danced in soft shoes (ghillies) and hard shoes with fibreglass taps. Popularised worldwide by shows such as Riverdance (1995).
  • Clog dancing — originating in the industrial north of England, danced in wooden-soled clogs; related forms include Welsh clogging and Lancashire clog dance.
  • Tap dance — an American form that developed from African American and Irish dance traditions, using shoes fitted with metal taps.
  • Canadian step dance — found in Ottawa Valley, Quebec and Maritime traditions, blending French, Scottish and Irish influences.
  • Cape Breton step dance — a Scottish-derived style kept close to the floor, danced to fiddle music.
  • English step dance — including solo hornpipe steps and the stepping in morris dance.

History

Percussive dance traditions developed in working-class communities where dance floors were wooden and music was provided by a single fiddler or melodeon player. Dancing masters in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Ireland taught set steps as prized sequences, often in exchange for food and lodging, and competitions (feiseanna) standardised much of the Irish repertoire. In industrial Lancashire and Durham, clog dancing grew in mill towns, with dancers practising steps in their work clogs. In North America, these European forms met African American rhythms and buck dancing, giving rise to tap and Appalachian flatfooting and clogging.

Competitions and performance

Irish step dancing is governed competitively by bodies such as An Coimisiún Le Rincí Gaelacha, with dancers wearing embroidered costumes and curled wigs. Theatrical productions beginning with Riverdance and Lord of the Dance brought step dance to a global audience in the 1990s.
