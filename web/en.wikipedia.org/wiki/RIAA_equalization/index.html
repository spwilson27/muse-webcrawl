RIAA equalization

RIAA equalization is a specification for the recording and playback of phonograph records, established by the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA). It is a form of pre-emphasis on recording and de-emphasis on playback: a record is cut with low frequencies reduced and high frequencies boosted, and the phono preamplifier applies the inverse (complementary) curve on playback.

Purpose

The purposes of the curve are to permit greater recording times (by decreasing the mean width of the groove), to improve sound quality, and to reduce groove damage that would otherwise arise during playback from large low-frequency excursions. Reducing bass also lowers demands on the cutting stylus and the playback cartridge.

The curve

The RIAA curve is defined by three time constants: 3180 μs (about 50 Hz), 318 μs (about 500 Hz) and 75 μs (about 2122 Hz). Bass is cut by roughly 20 dB at 20 Hz during recording, and treble is boosted by roughly 20 dB at 20 kHz; playback applies the mirror image. A fourth time constant (7950 μs, about 20 Hz) was proposed by the IEC for rumble reduction but was never widely adopted by the RIAA.

History

Before the 1950s each record label used its own equalization curve. The RIAA standard, adopted in 1954 from RCA Victor's "New Orthophonic" curve, unified LP production; by the late 1950s it was effectively universal in the West.
