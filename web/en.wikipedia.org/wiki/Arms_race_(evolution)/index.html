Arms race (evolution)

The rough-skinned newt and its garter snake predator are locked in a coevolutionary arms race

FieldEvolutionary biology
Related conceptsCoevolution; Red Queen hypothesis

In evolutionary biology, an evolutionary arms race is an ongoing struggle between competing sets of co-evolving genes, phenotypic and behavioral traits that develop escalating adaptations and counter-adaptations against each other, resembling the geopolitical concept of an arms race. These struggles are commonly described as examples of positive feedback and are often tied to the Red Queen hypothesis.

The co-evolving gene sets may be in different species, as in the arms race between a predator species and its prey (Vermeij's concept of escalation), or a parasite and its host. Alternatively, the arms race may occur within a single species, as in models of sexual conflict or runaway evolution such as Fisherian runaway.

Examples

A classic case is the rough-skinned newt (Taricha granulosa) and the common garter snake (Thamnophis sirtalis) in western North America: newts produce the potent neurotoxin tetrodotoxin, and snakes have evolved resistance, with populations of exceptionally toxic newts matched by exceptionally resistant snakes. Host–parasite systems show similar escalation, as do plants and herbivores evolving chemical defenses and detoxification mechanisms.

Dynamics

Arms races may be symmetrical, with both parties escalating similar traits, or asymmetrical, dominated by one party. They can produce extreme traits but are costly, and escalation may be limited by trade-offs, by the "life–dinner principle" (the prey runs for its life while the predator runs for its dinner, creating asymmetric selection pressure), or by shifts to entirely new defenses that reset the race.
