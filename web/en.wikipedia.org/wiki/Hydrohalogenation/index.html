Hydrohalogenation

Hydrohalogenation is an electrophilic addition reaction in which a hydrogen halide (HX, where X = Cl, Br, I, or F) adds across the carbon–carbon double bond of an alkene, or the triple bond of an alkyne, to give an alkyl halide. It is one of the classic functional-group transformations of introductory organic chemistry.

Regiochemistry

When the alkene is unsymmetrical, the addition normally follows Markovnikov's rule: the hydrogen attaches to the carbon bearing more hydrogens, and the halogen to the more substituted carbon, because the reaction proceeds through the more stable carbocation intermediate. A notable exception is the addition of HBr in the presence of peroxides or light, which proceeds by a radical mechanism and gives the anti-Markovnikov product — the peroxide effect, unique to HBr because only H–Br bond energetics suit both propagation steps.

Mechanism

In the ionic pathway, the π bond attacks a proton from HX, forming a carbocation, which is then captured by the halide ion. Rearrangements can occur if a hydride or alkyl shift produces a more stable carbocation. With alkynes, two equivalents can add to give geminal dihalides, again following Markovnikov regiochemistry.
