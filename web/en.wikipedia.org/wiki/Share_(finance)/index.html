In financial markets, a share (sometimes called stock, especially in American usage) is a unit of equity ownership in the capital stock of a corporation. Shares are divided into classes and provide the shareholder — the share's owner — with an ownership stake proportional to the number of shares held relative to the total shares outstanding, together with rights to dividends, to vote at general meetings, and to a residual claim on assets in liquidation.

Types of shares

  • Common (ordinary) shares — carry voting rights and variable dividends.
  • Preferred shares — usually pay fixed dividends and have priority over common shares in liquidation, but limited voting rights.
  • Dual-class shares — some companies issue classes with unequal voting power, a structure used by many technology firms (for example, Tencent-era founders' structures and Alphabet's Class A/B/C shares).

Issuance and trading

Companies issue shares to raise capital, initially to private investors and to the public through an initial public offering (IPO), after which shares trade on stock exchanges. The total value of a company's shares outstanding is its market capitalization. Share prices reflect supply and demand driven by expected future earnings, and most shares are held in electronic, dematerialized form through central securities depositories, though paper certificates were once standard. The liquidity of a share — how easily it can be sold — depends on its trading volume and free float.

Shareholder rights and returns

Shareholders elect the board of directors, approve major corporate actions, and may receive dividends at the board's discretion; many growth companies instead reinvest profits or repurchase shares (buybacks), returning value through price appreciation. Limited liability means a shareholder's loss is capped at the amount invested.
