Batwa

The Batwa (also called Twa or, in the forest regions, by the wider term "Pygmies") are an indigenous people of the Great Lakes region of Central Africa, living in Rwanda, Burundi, Uganda and the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. They are regarded as among the earliest inhabitants of the region's forests, where they traditionally lived as hunter-gatherers.

History and way of life

Batwa communities lived for centuries in the montane forests — including the forests now protected as Bwindi Impenetrable, Mgahinga and Nyungwe national parks — hunting small game, gathering honey, fruit and medicinal plants, and trading forest products with neighbouring farming peoples. In Rwanda and Burundi, where Twa form roughly one percent of the population, they historically occupied a low social status within the kingdom hierarchies alongside Hutu and Tutsi.

Displacement and marginalisation

During the twentieth century most Batwa were evicted from their ancestral forests as the land was converted to agriculture and, later, gazetted as national parks and gorilla sanctuaries — generally without compensation or alternative land. In Uganda, the Batwa were removed from Bwindi and Mgahinga in 1991. Landlessness has left most Batwa communities in extreme poverty, with poor access to education and healthcare and high rates of discrimination; many survive as labourers, potters and beggars on the margins of other communities.

Today

Batwa-led and allied organisations campaign for land rights, services and recognition as indigenous peoples under national law and the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. In 2021 Uganda's Constitutional Court ruled in favour of Batwa petitioners who argued their eviction without compensation was unconstitutional, though implementation of remedies has been slow. Cultural tourism — Batwa-led forest walks and performances — provides some income while transmitting forest knowledge.
