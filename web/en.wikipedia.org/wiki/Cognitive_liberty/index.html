Cognitive liberty

Cognitive liberty concerns freedom of thought and mental self-determination.

Cognitive liberty, or the "right to mental self-determination", is the freedom of an individual to control his or her own mental processes, cognition and consciousness. It has been argued to be both an extension of, and the principle underlying, the rights to freedom of thought and freedom of expression.

Concept

Proponents, including the Center for Cognitive Liberty & Ethics founded by neuroethicist Wrye Sententia and Richard Glen Boire in 2000, argue that as neurotechnology, psychoactive drugs and surveillance techniques advance, individuals need explicit protection both from coerced alteration of their mental states and from intrusion into their thoughts. The concept covers two sides: a right to use cognition-enhancing or consciousness-altering technologies and substances, and a right to refuse such interventions.

Legal developments

In 2021 Chile became the first country to enact legislation protecting "neuro-rights", amending its constitution to safeguard brain activity and information derived from it. Similar proposals have been discussed in other jurisdictions and in international human-rights scholarship under the heading of neurorights.

Criticism and debate

Debate centres on where mental privacy begins and ends, whether existing rights already cover the territory, and how cognitive liberty interacts with drug prohibition, workplace drug testing, forensic use of brain-based lie detection, and compulsory psychiatric treatment.
