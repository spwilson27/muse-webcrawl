Anglo-Egyptian treaty of 1936

The Anglo-Egyptian treaty of 1936 was an agreement between the United Kingdom and the Kingdom of Egypt that formally ended the British occupation of Egypt while preserving major British privileges, especially around the Suez Canal. It was signed in London on 26 August 1936 and ratified later that year.

Terms

Britain agreed to withdraw its forces from Egypt except for troops guarding the Suez Canal and its surroundings. Those forces were limited in peacetime and were to remain in the canal zone for twenty years. Egypt gained greater control over its armed forces and foreign affairs, joined the League of Nations, and the capitulatory regime was later abolished by the Montreux Convention of 1937.

Consequences

The treaty strengthened the position of the Wafd government that negotiated it, but Egyptian nationalists continued to object to the canal-zone presence. During the Second World War Britain again used Egypt as a major base. The treaty became increasingly contested after 1945 and was abrogated by Egypt in 1951, a crisis that preceded the Egyptian revolution of 1952 and the Suez Crisis of 1956.
