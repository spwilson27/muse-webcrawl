Hohokam

Hohokam (UK: /həʊˈhəʊkəm/, US: /hoʊˈhoʊkəm/) was a culture in the North American Southwest, centred primarily on the middle Gila River and lower Salt River basins of what is now south-central Arizona. The word, rendered Huhugam in the O'odham language, is often translated as "those who are gone" or "the ancestors". Archaeologists conventionally date the Hohokam sequence from about AD 450 (or earlier) to about 1450.

Irrigation and settlement

The Hohokam are renowned for the largest pre-Columbian irrigation system in the Americas north of Peru: by the Classic period, their canals in the Phoenix Basin stretched for hundreds of kilometres in total, watering tens of thousands of acres of maize, beans, squash, cotton, and agave. Major settlements included Snaketown, excavated by Emil Haury, and the great adobe "great house" at Casa Grande. Many modern Salt River Project canals follow the alignments of Hohokam canals.

Culture

Hohokam settlements featured ballcourts — more than 200 are known — resembling those of Mesoamerica, reflecting trade and cultural contact southward, from which copper bells, macaws, and possibly ballcourt ritual were obtained. Distinctive material culture includes red-on-buff pottery, carved shell jewellery, palettes, and cremation burials. Platform mounds appeared in the Classic period (c. 1150–1450), when settlements consolidated into fewer, larger towns.

Decline and descendants

By about 1450 the Hohokam archaeological pattern had ended, for reasons debated — flood damage to canals, waterlogging and salinisation of fields, internal conflict, and disease have all been proposed. The Akimel O'odham (Pima) and Tohono O'odham (Papago) peoples of southern Arizona are widely regarded as cultural descendants of the Hohokam, and O'odham oral traditions preserve connections to the platform-mound towns and their builders.
