Allium is a large genus of monocotyledonous flowering plants in the amaryllis family, Amaryllidaceae. It includes some of the world's most familiar vegetables and herbs: the onion (A. cepa), garlic (A. sativum), leek (A. ampeloprasum), chives (A. schoenoprasum), shallot and spring onion. The generic name is the classical Latin word for garlic.

Description

Alliums are mostly perennial bulbous plants of the Northern Hemisphere, especially diverse in Central Asia, the Mediterranean and western North America. They characteristically produce sulphur compounds (cysteine sulfoxides) that give the crushed tissues their pungent onion or garlic smell and flavour; the enzyme alliinase converts these into the volatile compounds responsible for eye-watering when onions are cut. The flowers are borne in umbels atop leafless stems, and many species are valued as ornamentals, including the giant globe alliums of gardens.

Taxonomy and uses

With close to a thousand species, Allium is one of the largest plant genera, and its classification into subgenera and sections has been extensively revised using molecular phylogenetics. Cultivated alliums have been eaten since antiquity — garlic appears in Egyptian, Greek and Chinese records — and are used in virtually every cuisine. Some species are toxic to dogs and cats, and a few wild species are gathered as ramps or wild garlic. Because the bulbs store well and the plants resist grazing, alliums have spread and naturalised widely beyond their native ranges.
