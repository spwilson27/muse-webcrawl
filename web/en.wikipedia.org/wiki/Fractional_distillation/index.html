Fractional distillation

Fractional distillation is the separation of a mixture into its component parts, or fractions, by heating it so that components vaporise at their different boiling points and then condense in sequence. It is a special type of distillation and one of the most widely used separation methods in chemistry and industry.

Principle

When a liquid mixture is heated, the vapour is richer in the more volatile (lower-boiling) components. In fractional distillation the vapour passes up a fractionating column packed with material providing a large surface area — glass beads, metal rings, or trays in industrial columns. Vapour repeatedly condenses and re-evaporates as it rises, becoming progressively enriched in the most volatile component; each condensation–vaporisation step acts like a simple distillation. A temperature gradient is maintained along the column, hottest at the bottom.

Industrial applications

The defining application is petroleum refining: crude oil is separated in tall fractionating towers into gases, petrol (gasoline), kerosene, diesel, lubricating oils and bitumen. Air separation plants fractionally distil liquefied air to produce oxygen, nitrogen and argon. The process is also used to purify ethanol and other solvents, and in the production of whisky and other spirits where it shapes flavour.

Laboratory practice

In the laboratory, a Vigreux column or a column packed with glass helices is fitted between the distillation flask and the condenser. Fractions are collected over successive temperature ranges corresponding to the boiling points of the components.
