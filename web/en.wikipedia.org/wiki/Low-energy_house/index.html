Low-energy house

A low-energy house is any type of house designed to use less energy than a conventional house built to the applicable building code. Low-energy designs combine high levels of insulation, airtight construction, energy-efficient windows, controlled ventilation (often with heat recovery) and careful orientation to exploit passive solar gain and shading. The term has no single universal definition, but more rigorous voluntary standards — most famously the German Passive House (Passivhaus) standard — set measurable targets for heating demand and airtightness.

Design principles

The core strategy is to reduce heat losses so drastically that heating needs become small. Walls, roofs and floors are insulated far beyond code minima and detailed to avoid thermal bridges. The building envelope is made airtight, and fresh air is supplied mechanically with a heat exchanger that recovers most of the heat from exhaust air. Large windows are placed on the sunny side (south-facing in the northern hemisphere) to capture winter sun, with overhangs or shutters to prevent summer overheating. Compact building form — a low surface-area-to-volume ratio — further reduces losses.

Standards and history

Experiments with superinsulated houses began during the 1970s energy crises in North America and Scandinavia. In 1988 the concept of the passive house was developed by Bo Adamson and Wolfgang Feist, and the first certified examples were built in Darmstadt, Germany in 1991. The Passive House standard requires, among other criteria, a space-heating demand of no more than 15 kWh per square metre per year and airtightness of 0.6 air changes per hour at 50 pascals. National low-energy labels and building codes in many European countries have since adopted similar performance levels, and EU rules require nearly zero-energy buildings for new construction.

Related concepts

A zero-energy building goes further, generating as much energy on site (usually from rooftop photovoltaics) as it consumes over a year, while a "plus-energy" house generates a surplus. Low-energy design can also be applied in retrofits of existing buildings, where insulation, airtightness and efficient services are added to older stock.
