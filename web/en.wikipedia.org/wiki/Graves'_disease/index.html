Graves' disease, also known as toxic diffuse goitre, is an autoimmune disease that affects the thyroid. It is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism. It frequently results in and is the most common cause of an enlarged thyroid. Signs and symptoms of hyperthyroidism may include irritability, muscle weakness, sleeping problems, a fast heartbeat, poor tolerance of heat, diarrhoea and unintentional weight loss.

The condition is named after the Irish physician Robert James Graves, who described it in 1835; it had earlier been described by Caleb Hillier Parry, and in continental Europe it is often called Basedow's disease after Karl Adolph von Basedow.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms arise from excessive thyroid hormone action and from the autoimmune process itself. In addition to the features of hyperthyroidism, about a third of people develop Graves' ophthalmopathy — inflammation of the tissues around the eyes, producing protrusion of the eyeballs (exophthalmos), lid retraction and double vision. A smaller number develop pretibial myxoedema, a thickening of the skin over the shins, and thyroid acropachy (clubbing of the fingers).

Cause

Graves' disease is caused by autoantibodies (TSH-receptor antibodies, TRAb) that bind to and activate the thyrotropin (TSH) receptor on thyroid follicular cells, mimicking TSH and driving continuous synthesis and release of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Genetic susceptibility (including HLA and CTLA-4 variants) interacts with environmental triggers such as stress, infection, iodine intake, and smoking — the last strongly associated with ophthalmopathy.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture together with blood tests showing a suppressed TSH and raised free T4 and/or T3. Detection of TSH-receptor antibodies can confirm the diagnosis. A thyroid uptake scan typically shows diffuse increased uptake of radioiodine, distinguishing Graves' disease from thyroiditis.

Treatment

Three main treatments are used: antithyroid medication (carbimazole, methimazole, or propylthiouracil), radioactive iodine ablation, and thyroidectomy. Beta blockers such as propranolol control adrenergic symptoms while thyroid hormone levels fall. Radioiodine and surgery usually result in hypothyroidism requiring lifelong levothyroxine. Ophthalmopathy may require corticosteroids, orbital radiotherapy, or surgery in severe cases.

Prognosis and epidemiology

With treatment, the outlook is generally good, although relapse after antithyroid drugs is common. Graves' disease affects about 0.5% of males and 3% of females, and is roughly seven times more common in women. It often begins between the ages of 40 and 60.
