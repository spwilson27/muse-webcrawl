The Fonserannes Locks (French: écluses de Fonserannes, also called the Neuf Écluses, "nine locks") are a flight of staircase locks on the Canal du Midi near Béziers in southern France. Considered one of the great engineering works of the 17th-century canal, the flight raises and lowers boats through a height of about 21.5 metres (71 ft) over a distance of some 300 metres. They are among the most visited sites on the canal, attracting hundreds of thousands of visitors a year.

Description

The flight consists of eight oval-shaped lock chambers in a staircase arrangement, where the gates of one chamber serve as the gates of the next, with nine sets of gates in total (hence the traditional name). The chambers are built with curved, elliptical walls — a design used by the canal's engineer, Pierre-Paul Riquet, to resist the pressure of the surrounding hillside when the chambers are empty. The staircase was completed in 1680, the year before the canal opened to navigation in 1681. Boats take about 45 minutes to an hour to pass through the flight, which operates in one direction at a time on a published schedule in the boating season.

History

Riquet, a tax farmer from Béziers, conceived the Canal du Midi to link the Atlantic with the Mediterranean and drove its construction from 1667 until his death in 1680, months before its inauguration. The Fonserannes staircase, built into the hill of Ensérune west of Béziers, was one of the canal's showpieces, together with the Malpas tunnel and the canal bridge over the Orb at Béziers (an aqueduct added in the 1850s which allowed boats to bypass a difficult river crossing below the locks and slightly altered the flight's working arrangement).

In 1983–84 a remarkable inclined plane — the Fonserannes water slope (pente d'eau) — was built beside the locks on the model of the Montech water slope, intended to speed commercial barges past the flight. It was used only briefly, suffered technical difficulties and a tyre failure, and was abandoned by 2001; commercial traffic on the canal had in any case declined, and the original locks remain in daily use by pleasure craft.

Tourism

The site includes a visitor centre, viewing platforms, and the Maison du Canal du Midi, and is accessible from Béziers by a path along the canal. The Canal du Midi was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996, with the Fonserannes Locks among its best-known structures.
