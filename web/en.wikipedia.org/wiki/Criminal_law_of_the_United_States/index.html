Criminal law of the United States

Most criminal law in the United States is made and enforced by the states.

The criminal law of the United States is the body of law that defines offences against the public and prescribes their punishment. Unlike many countries, the United States has no single national criminal code: criminal law is primarily the province of the fifty states, each with its own statutes and common-law heritage, while the federal government prosecutes offences arising under federal statutes — such as crimes on federal property, interstate and organised crime, drug trafficking, fraud against the United States, and violations of federal regulatory law.

Elements of a crime

Most crimes require both an actus reus (a prohibited act or culpable omission) and a mens rea (a culpable mental state such as purpose, knowledge, recklessness or negligence). The American Law Institute's Model Penal Code (1962) systematised these concepts and influenced the revision of many state codes, though it was adopted in full by no state. Offences are graded, typically as felonies (punishable by more than a year in prison) and misdemeanours, with punishments ranging from fines and probation to imprisonment and, in a minority of jurisdictions, capital punishment.

Constitutional limits

Federal and state criminal law alike are constrained by the U.S. Constitution: the Fourth Amendment limits searches and seizures, the Fifth guarantees grand jury indictment (for federal felonies), protection against double jeopardy and self-incrimination, and due process; the Sixth guarantees a speedy and public trial by jury, confrontation of witnesses and the assistance of counsel; the Eighth prohibits excessive bail and cruel and unusual punishments. Landmark Supreme Court decisions — including Miranda v. Arizona (1966) and Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) — define defendants' rights in practice.
