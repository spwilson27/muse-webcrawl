Lewis Fry Richardson, FRS (11 October 1881 – 30 September 1953) was an English mathematician, physicist, meteorologist, psychologist, and pacifist who pioneered modern mathematical techniques of weather forecasting and applied similar methods to the study of the causes of war. He is remembered for the Richardson number in fluid dynamics, for Richardson extrapolation in numerical analysis, for early work on fractals (the "coastline paradox"), and for his quixotic but prophetic vision of computing the weather by hand with an army of human calculators.

Numerical weather prediction

During the First World War, Richardson served with the Friends' Ambulance Unit in France. In his spare time he attempted the first numerical weather forecast: dividing the atmosphere over Europe into grid cells and computing pressure changes from the equations of motion, performing all calculations by hand over many weeks. His six-hour forecast, published in his 1922 book Weather Prediction by Numerical Process, was wildly wrong — it predicted an impossibly large pressure change — because of unbalanced initial data and too large a time step, flaws understood only later. In the same book he imagined a vast hall in which 64,000 human "computers" would calculate the weather faster than the weather itself could evolve — a scheme that anticipated modern massively parallel weather models, now run on supercomputers using essentially his grid-point method.

Other work

Richardson contributed the dimensionless Richardson number used to predict turbulence, developed Richardson extrapolation for improving the accuracy of finite-difference approximations, and showed that the measured length of a border or coastline grows without bound as the measuring scale shrinks — an observation later central to fractal geometry. A devout Quaker and conscientious objector, he lost his position at the Meteorological Office when it was absorbed into the Air Ministry, and he devoted his later life to the mathematical study of war and peace. His books Arms and Insecurity and Statistics of Deadly Quarrels, published posthumously, founded quantitative peace science, including Richardson's "arms race" equations modelling escalation between rival states.
