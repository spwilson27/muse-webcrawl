Helladic chronology

Helladic chronology is a relative dating system used in archaeology and art history for Bronze Age Greece. It subdivides the Helladic period — the Bronze Age cultures of mainland Greece — into Early, Middle and Late phases, principally on the basis of changes in pottery styles.

The system

The scheme was modelled on the Minoan chronology devised by Sir Arthur Evans for Crete, and was developed for the mainland by Carl Blegen and Alan Wace. The three main periods are:

  • Early Helladic (EH), c. 3200/3100–2050/2000 BC: subdivided into EH I, II and III. EH II is the age of large "corridor houses" such as the House of the Tiles at Lerna; EH III sees cultural disruption and new pottery styles.
  • Middle Helladic (MH), c. 2050/2000–1550 BC: characterised by grey Minyan ware and matt-painted pottery, and by a relatively modest material culture on the mainland; it ends with the rich Shaft Grave burials at Mycenae.
  • Late Helladic (LH), c. 1550–1050 BC: the Mycenaean period, subdivided into LH I, II, IIIA, IIIB and IIIC. LH IIIA–IIIB covers the palatial era of Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos and Thebes; LH IIIC follows the palace destructions of around 1200 BC and shades into the Greek Dark Ages.

Dating

Because the system is relative, absolute dates are established by cross-links with the historically dated cultures of Egypt and the Near East — for example, Egyptian imports in Aegean contexts and Aegean pottery in Egyptian ones — and by radiocarbon dating, which has been important in debates such as the dating of the eruption of Thera, where radiocarbon evidence suggests a date in the late 17th century BC, earlier than the conventional archaeological date.
