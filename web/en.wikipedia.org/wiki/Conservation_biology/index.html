Conservation biology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conservation biology is the scientific study of the conservation of nature and of Earth's biodiversity, with the aim of protecting species, their habitats and ecosystems from excessive rates of extinction and the erosion of biotic interactions. It is an interdisciplinary subject drawing on ecology, genetics, biogeography and the social sciences, formed in the 1970s–80s as scientists recognised an accelerating biodiversity crisis driven by habitat destruction, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution and climate change.

Tools and practice

Conservation biologists assess extinction risk — the assessments compiled in the IUCN Red List are the field's global standard — design protected areas and habitat corridors, manage small populations against inbreeding and random loss (minimum viable population analysis), run captive-breeding and reintroduction programmes (California condors, Arabian oryx, wolves in Yellowstone), and restore degraded ecosystems. Modern practice increasingly involves local and Indigenous communities, payments for ecosystem services, and international frameworks such as the Convention on Biological Diversity and its "30 by 30" target — protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030, agreed at the 2022 Kunming–Montreal conference.

Why it matters

Biodiversity underpins ecosystem services — pollination, water purification, fisheries, carbon storage — worth vast sums, as well as pharmaceuticals and wild genes for crops. Conservation biology also grapples with hard trade-offs: triage among species, conflicts between predators and farmers, and whether to intervene against climate change by moving species ("assisted migration"). The field's founding figures, notably Michael Soulé, framed it explicitly as a "crisis discipline": like emergency medicine, it must act on incomplete knowledge because waiting for certainty can mean extinction.
