The Peloponnese (Greek: Πελοπόννησος) is a large peninsula in southern Greece, separated from the central part of the country by the Gulf of Corinth and joined to it only by the narrow Isthmus of Corinth, cut through by the Corinth Canal since 1893. It covers about 21,550 square kilometres and is famous as the heartland of ancient Greek civilisation south of the isthmus.

History

The region was home to the Mycenaean civilisation — Mycenae, Tiryns, and Pylos are all Peloponnesian — and later to the classical city-states of Sparta, Corinth, and Argos. Olympia, site of the ancient Olympic Games, lies in its west, and Epidaurus with its great theatre in its north-east. The Peloponnese was the prize of the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) between Sparta's Peloponnesian League and Athens. In the medieval period it was known as the Morea; it was a stronghold of Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian, and Ottoman rule in turn, and a principal theatre of the Greek War of Independence in the 1820s.

Geography

The peninsula is mountainous, with the Taygetus range in the south (rising to 2,404 m at Profitis Ilias) and deep gulfs — Messenian, Laconian, and Argolic — cutting into its southern coasts. Modern administration divides most of it among the regions of Peloponnese and Western Greece, with a slice belonging to Attica. The Rio–Antirrio Bridge (2004) now links its north-west to central Greece.
