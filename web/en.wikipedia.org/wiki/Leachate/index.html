Leachate

Leachate is any liquid that, in the course of passing through matter, extracts soluble or suspended solids, or any other component of the material through which it has passed. In environmental science the term most commonly refers to the liquid produced when water percolates through a landfill, dissolving and carrying with it the products of decomposing waste.

Formation and composition

Landfill leachate forms from rainfall entering the waste, from water present in the waste itself, and from the biochemical breakdown of organic matter. Its composition varies strongly with the age of the landfill: young leachate is rich in biodegradable organic matter, ammonia, and volatile fatty acids, while mature leachate is more stable but retains high concentrations of ammonia and refractory organic compounds. Leachate may also contain heavy metals, salts, and xenobiotic organic compounds such as solvents and plasticisers.

Environmental risks

If it escapes, leachate can contaminate groundwater and surface water, depleting oxygen in rivers and poisoning aquatic life. Historic unlined dumps are a persistent source of such pollution, and migration of leachate plumes can threaten drinking-water wells.

Management

Modern sanitary landfills are built with impermeable liners (clay and geomembranes) and drainage layers that collect leachate for treatment. Treatment methods include recirculation through the waste to accelerate stabilisation, biological treatment, membrane processes such as reverse osmosis, and discharge to municipal sewage works. Leachate generation declines after a site is capped, but monitoring is typically required for decades after closure.
