Lucky Luciano

Charles "Lucky" Luciano (born Salvatore Lucania; November 24, 1897 – January 26, 1962) was an Italian-born American mobster who operated mainly in the United States. He is considered the father of modern organized crime in the United States for establishing the Commission and restructuring the Italian-American Mafia into the Five Families of New York after the Castellammarese War of 1930–31.

Early life and rise

Lucania emigrated with his family from Sicily to New York in 1906, settling on the Lower East Side. He rose through street gangs into bootlegging during Prohibition, working with Meyer Lansky, Bugsy Siegel, and Arnold Rothstein's circle. In 1929 he survived a brutal beating and stabbing by rival gunmen — reportedly the origin of his nickname "Lucky."

Reorganization of the Mafia

During the Castellammarese War between Joe Masseria and Salvatore Marano, Luciano plotted the removal of both: Masseria was murdered in April 1931, and Marano the following September. Luciano then reorganized New York crime into Five Families governed by a national Commission, abolishing the old "boss of bosses" title in favour of a corporate-style board.

Conviction, deportation, and death

In 1936 special prosecutor Thomas E. Dewey secured Luciano's conviction on charges of running a prostitution ring; he was sentenced to 30 to 50 years. During the Second World War, U.S. naval intelligence reached an understanding with his associates regarding New York waterfront security ("Operation Underworld"). In 1946 his sentence was commuted on condition of deportation to Italy. He later lived in Cuba until U.S. pressure forced his return to Italy in 1947. Luciano died of a heart attack at Naples airport in 1962.
