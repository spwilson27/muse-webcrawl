Minkowski space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A light cone diagram in Minkowski space: future cone above, past cone below, the present at the apex]
The light cone structure of Minkowski space.

In physics, Minkowski space (or Minkowski spacetime) is the mathematical setting in which Albert Einstein's theory of special relativity is most conveniently formulated: a four-dimensional real vector space with a metric of signature (3,1) in which the interval −c²Δt² + Δx² + Δy² + Δz² is the same for all inertial observers. It was developed by Hermann Minkowski, Einstein's former mathematics teacher, in 1908, from the Lorentz transformations.

Minkowski space is flat — the vacuum solution of general relativity — and serves as the local approximation to curved spacetime everywhere (the equivalence principle) and as the background of Quantum field theory. Its geometry encodes causality: each event's light cone separates its possible causes (past cone) and possible effects (future cone) from the absolutely elsewhere. Mass, energy and momentum combine into four-vectors on Minkowski space, and the requirement of Lorentz invariance on it structures all of relativistic physics.

Reception

Einstein initially dismissed Minkowski's formalism as 'superfluous learnedness', then adopted it completely — general relativity is unthinkable without it. Minkowski's 1908 Cologne lecture ('Space and Time') remains a landmark of mathematical physics; he died suddenly in 1909, aged 44, before seeing its full triumph.
