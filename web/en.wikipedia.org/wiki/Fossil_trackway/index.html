Fossil trackway

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fossil trackway is a set of fossilised footprints in soft sediment left by an animal moving across a surface, preserved when the impressions were filled or covered by sediment and lithified. Trackways are trace fossils (ichnofossils): they record the behaviour of an organism rather than its body, and a single animal may produce many tracks while leaving no skeleton.

Information content

From a trackway, palaeontologists can infer the trackmaker's gait, stride length and speed — using relations such as Alexander's formula linking stride length, hip height and velocity — as well as limb posture, foot anatomy, and whether the animal walked bipedally or quadrupedally. Parallel trackways may indicate herding or group movement, and trackways that continue without tail-drag marks helped establish that dinosaurs held their tails off the ground. Trackways can also reveal behaviour such as turning, limping, swimming ("punting" marks) or predator–prey interaction.

Famous examples

Celebrated trackways include the Laetoli footprints in Tanzania, 3.66-million-year-old hominin tracks attributed to Australopithecus afarensis; the dinosaur trackways of Dinosaur Valley State Park in Texas, where sauropod and theropod tracks run together in the bed of the Paluxy River; the Lark Quarry site in Queensland, Australia, long interpreted as a dinosaur stampede; and the vast track sites at White Sands National Park in New Mexico, where human footprints dating to roughly 21,000–23,000 years ago rank among the earliest evidence of people in the Americas.
