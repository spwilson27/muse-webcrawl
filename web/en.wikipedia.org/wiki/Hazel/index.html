Hazels are plants in the genus Corylus (from the Greek korys, "helmet", referring to the husk covering the nut) of deciduous trees and large shrubs native to the temperate Northern Hemisphere. The genus is placed in the birch family Betulaceae. The fruits of the hazel are known as hazelnuts (also cobnuts or filberts).

Hazels have simple, rounded leaves with double-serrate margins. The flowers are produced very early in spring before the leaves, and are monoecious, with single-sex wind-pollinated catkins: the male catkins are pale yellow and 5–12 cm long, while the female flowers are tiny and largely concealed, with only the bright-red styles visible.

Species

There are 14–18 species, including the common hazel (Corylus avellana) of Europe and western Asia, the filbert (C. maxima), the beaked hazel (C. cornuta) and American hazel (C. americana) of North America, and the Turkish hazel (C. colurna), a larger tree species.

Uses

The common hazel is extensively grown for its nuts, with commercial orchards in Turkey (the world's largest producer), Italy, Spain, Greece, Azerbaijan and the United States (Oregon). Hazelnuts are eaten raw or roasted and are a key ingredient of praline, Nutella-style spreads and many confectionery products. Hazel wood and coppice rods have traditionally been used for wattle fencing, thatching spars, walking sticks and basketry; hazel coppicing is also valuable for wildlife, supporting dormice, nightingales and fritillary butterflies. The corkscrew hazel (C. avellana 'Contorta') is a popular ornamental shrub.

Ecology

The nuts provide food for squirrels, jays, nuthatches and mice, which also disperse the seed. At least 73 species of insect have been recorded feeding on hazel in Britain. Hazel pollen is an early source for bees, but wind-pollinated hazel pollen is also a common spring allergen.

In culture

Hazel features in Celtic mythology as a tree of knowledge — in Irish legend the hazelnuts of wisdom fell into the Well of Segais, imparting knowledge to the salmon that ate them. Hazel rods were the traditional material for water-divining (dowsing). In the Ogham alphabet the letter Coll corresponds to hazel.
