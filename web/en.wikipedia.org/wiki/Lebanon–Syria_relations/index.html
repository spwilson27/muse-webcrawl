Lebanon–Syria relations are the bilateral relations between the Lebanese Republic and the Syrian Arab Republic. The two states share deep historical, cultural and economic ties dating to the Ottoman era and the French Mandate, when both emerged from Greater Syria. For most of the period after Lebanon's independence in 1943, Syria declined to treat Lebanon as a fully separate state — the two countries did not exchange ambassadors until 2008.

History

Syria intervened militarily in the Lebanese Civil War in 1976, initially against the Palestinian–leftist alliance and later as the dominant power in Lebanon under the Arab Deterrent Force mandate. The Taif Agreement (1989) and the 1991 Treaty of Brotherhood, Cooperation and Coordination regularised a relationship in which Damascus exercised extensive influence over Lebanese politics and security, with Syrian troops stationed in Lebanon until 2005.

The assassination of former prime minister Rafic Hariri in February 2005 sparked the Cedar Revolution, mass protests that forced Syria's military withdrawal by April 2005. In 2008, following the Doha Agreement, the two countries agreed to establish full diplomatic relations for the first time, and embassies opened in 2009. Outstanding issues include the demarcation of the border, the Shebaa Farms dispute, Lebanese detainees formerly held in Syria, and water sharing.

Syrian civil war spillover

From 2011 the Syrian civil war profoundly affected Lebanon: over a million Syrian refugees entered the country, Hezbollah intervened militarily in Syria in support of the Assad government, and border towns suffered clashes and shelling. The fall of the Assad regime in December 2024 opened a new phase, with the new Syrian authorities and Lebanon seeking to reset relations, address the border and refugees, and resolve the fate of missing persons.
