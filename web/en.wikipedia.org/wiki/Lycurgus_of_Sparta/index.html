Lycurgus (Greek: Λυκοῦργος, Lykourgos) was the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, credited by the ancient sources with establishing the military-oriented reformation of Spartan society and the constitution set out in the Great Rhetra. The dates of his life and even his historicity are disputed: ancient authors placed him anywhere from the 10th to the 7th century BC, and modern scholars disagree on whether a single historical legislator stood behind the reforms attributed to him.

Biography in the tradition

According to the tradition preserved in Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, Lycurgus was a regent of Sparta for his infant nephew, the king Charilaus. To study laws, he travelled widely — to Crete, Ionia, and in some accounts Egypt — and consulted the oracle at Delphi, which is said to have sanctioned his constitution. Having bound the Spartans by oath to keep his laws until his return, he departed and never came back, in some versions starving himself to death so that the oath would remain in force forever.

Reforms attributed to Lycurgus

The reforms ascribed to Lycurgus include the establishment of the gerousia (a council of elders), the formalising of the apella (assembly of citizens), the redistribution of land into equal lots (kleroi), the introduction of iron money of deliberately low value to discourage wealth accumulation, the common messes (syssitia) at which citizens ate together, and the agoge, the rigorous communal upbringing of Spartan boys. These institutions shaped Sparta's distinctive social system, in which full citizens (the homoioi, or "equals") devoted themselves to military training while agricultural labour was performed by the subject helot population.

Historicity

Ancient writers already disagreed about Lycurgus's date and identity; Herodotus reports two conflicting traditions. Many modern historians regard "Lycurgus" as the personification of a long process of institutional change, though others defend a historical lawgiver of the archaic period. The Spartan constitution continued to be invoked in his name for centuries, and he was venerated at Sparta with a sanctuary and annual sacrifices.
