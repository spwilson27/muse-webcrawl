The Treaty of Lausanne is a peace treaty signed in Lausanne, Switzerland, on 24 July 1923. It officially settled the state of war that had existed between the Ottoman Empire and the Allied powers since the beginning of the First World War, and it superseded the Treaty of Sèvres of 1920, which the Turkish national movement under Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk) had rejected and overturned by victory in the Turkish War of Independence. The treaty recognised the sovereignty and borders of the new Republic of Turkey, proclaimed later that year.

Background

After the Ottoman defeat in 1918 and the Armistice of Mudros, the Allies imposed the Treaty of Sèvres, partitioning Anatolia. The Turkish nationalists repudiated it, and after defeating Greek forces in western Anatolia and confronting the Allies at Chanak, the parties agreed the Armistice of Mudanya (October 1922) and opened negotiations at Lausanne, where Turkey was represented chiefly by İsmet Pasha (İnönü).

Terms

The treaty defined the borders of modern Turkey: Turkey retained Anatolia and eastern Thrace, while Mosul's status was deferred (it was awarded to British-mandated Iraq in 1926), and the province of Hatay joined Turkey only in 1939. The Dodecanese went to Italy and Cyprus to Britain. The Turkish Straits were demilitarised under an international commission (revised by the Montreux Convention of 1936). The capitulations were abolished, Turkey accepted no reparations obligations to the Allies, and provisions protected non-Muslim minorities in Turkey and Muslim minorities in Greece — alongside the separate convention for the Greco-Turkish population exchange of some 1.5 million people. With ratifications exchanged in 1924, the treaty closed the post-Ottoman settlement in the Near East; Lausanne remains the only First World War peace treaty still broadly in force.
