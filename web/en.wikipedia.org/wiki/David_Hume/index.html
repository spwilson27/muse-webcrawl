David Hume (1711–1776) was a Scottish Enlightenment philosopher, historian and essayist, regarded as one of the most important figures in Western philosophy. His empiricism and scepticism — about causation, induction, the self and religion — posed challenges philosophy still answers; Immanuel Kant credited Hume with awakening him from his 'dogmatic slumber'.

Thought

In A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40), Hume argued that all ideas derive from impressions of sense or feeling, and that our belief in cause and effect rests not on reason but on custom: we observe constant conjunction, never necessary connection. His problem of induction asks how inference from observed to unobserved can be justified. He analysed the self as a 'bundle' of perceptions and argued, in a famous passage, that no 'ought' follows from an 'is'. His ethics grounds morality in sympathy and sentiment rather than reason.

Religion and later life

Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (published posthumously, 1779) criticise arguments for God's existence, and his essay 'Of Miracles' argues testimony can never make a miracle probable enough to outweigh uniform experience. Barred from university posts by religious controversy, he found fame with his multi-volume History of England. Affable and sociable — 'le bon David' to Paris friends — he faced terminal illness with famous cheerfulness, documented by Adam Smith and James Boswell.
