The Wu experiment was a particle and nuclear physics experiment conducted in 1956 by the Chinese American physicist Chien-Shiung Wu in collaboration with the Low Temperature Group of the US National Bureau of Standards. The experiment demonstrated that parity — the symmetry between left and right — is violated by the weak interaction, overturning a principle physicists had assumed for three decades.

Background

The conservation of parity had been taken for granted in physics since the 1920s. In 1956, theorists Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang, puzzled by the "tau–theta problem" in kaon decays, reviewed the evidence and found that parity conservation had never been tested in weak interactions. They proposed several experiments, and Wu, an expert on beta decay, took up the most direct one.

Experiment

Wu's team cooled cobalt-60 nuclei to near absolute zero (about 0.003 K) using adiabatic demagnetisation and aligned their spins in a magnetic field. Cobalt-60 decays by beta emission, emitting electrons. If parity were conserved, the electrons should be emitted equally in the direction of the nuclear spin and opposite to it. Instead, the team found a large asymmetry: electrons were emitted preferentially opposite to the spin direction. The asymmetry reversed when the field was reversed, confirming the effect was real. Parity was maximally violated.

Impact

Announced in January 1957, the result shocked the physics community — Wolfgang Pauli had written that he could not believe "the Lord is a weak left-hander". Lee and Yang received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1957 for their theoretical work. Wu's exclusion from that prize is frequently cited as one of the Nobel Committee's notable omissions; she later received the inaugural Wolf Prize in Physics (1978). The discovery established the V−A structure of the weak interaction and became a cornerstone of the Standard Model.
