Napoleonic Wars

The Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815) were a series of conflicts fought between the French Empire under Napoleon and a changing set of European coalitions. They continued, in many respects, the wars provoked by the French Revolution of 1789, and they reshaped the political map, legal systems and military practice of Europe. The wars are usually divided into the War of the Third Coalition (1803–1806), the War of the Fourth Coalition (1806–1807), the War of the Fifth Coalition (1809), the French invasion of Russia (1812), the War of the Sixth Coalition (1813–1814), and the Hundred Days of 1815, ending with Napoleon's defeat at the Battle of Waterloo.

Background

France's revolutionary governments had been at war with much of Europe since 1792. Napoleon seized power in the coup of 18 Brumaire (1799) and, as First Consul and then Emperor (from 1804), consolidated French dominance. The brief Peace of Amiens (1802–1803) collapsed over disputes including Malta, and Britain declared war in May 1803. Napoleon assembled the Army of England at Boulogne for a planned invasion of Britain, but British sea power — confirmed by Nelson's victory at Battle of Trafalgar in October 1805 — made invasion impracticable.

Course of the wars

Third and Fourth Coalitions (1805–1807)

In 1805 Napoleon marched east instead, encircling an Austrian army at Ulm and then defeating the Austrians and Russians at the Battle of Austerlitz (2 December 1805), often regarded as his tactical masterpiece. The Peace of Pressburg dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. Prussia's challenge ended in catastrophic defeat at Jena and Auerstedt (October 1806). After the bloody winter campaign in East Prussia, Napoleon defeated Russia at Friedland (June 1807), and the Treaties of Tilsit made France dominant from the Niemen to the Atlantic, with Russia briefly an ally. Napoleon enforced his Continental System, a blockade intended to ruin British trade by closing European ports.

Spain and the Fifth Coalition (1808–1809)

French intervention in Spain in 1808 — installing Joseph Bonaparte on the Spanish throne — provoked a Spanish uprising and the long guerrilla struggle of the Peninsular War, Napoleon's "Spanish ulcer", in which a British army under Wellington increasingly took the offensive. Austria's renewed challenge in 1809 was defeated at Wagram, though not before Napoleon suffered his first major battlefield reverse at Aspern-Essling.

Russia and the Sixth Coalition (1812–1814)

In June 1812 Napoleon invaded Russia with the Grande Armée, perhaps 600,000 strong — the largest army yet assembled in Europe. The Russians retreated, gave battle at Borodino (7 September 1812), and abandoned a burning Moscow. Napoleon waited in vain for Alexander I to negotiate, then retreated in late October; cold, hunger, disease and Cossack attacks destroyed the army, of which only a fraction recrossed the Niemen. Prussia and Austria joined Russia and Britain in the Sixth Coalition; Napoleon, though still capable of brilliant victories with raw conscripts, was beaten in the gigantic Battle of Leipzig — the "Battle of the Nations" — in October 1813. The allies invaded France, took Paris in March 1814, and Napoleon abdicated and was exiled to Elba.

The Hundred Days (1815)

Napoleon escaped Elba in February 1815 and marched on Paris, regaining power as the allies at Vienna declared him an outlaw. In June he struck first in Belgium, defeating the Prussians at Ligny, but on 18 June 1815 Wellington's Anglo-allied army, supported late in the day by Blücher's Prussians, defeated him at Waterloo. Napoleon abdicated again and was exiled to remote Saint Helena in the South Atlantic, where he died in 1821. The second Treaty of Paris (November 1815) closed the wars.

Legacy

The wars killed several million people and exhausted Europe's treasuries, but they also spread the Code Napoléon and other revolutionary reforms — legal equality, abolition of feudal dues, meritocratic careers — across the continent. The Congress of Vienna, guided by figures such as Klemens von Metternich and Talleyrand, constructed a conservative balance of power that kept a general European peace for decades. Nationalism, both awakened and resisted, became a defining force of the nineteenth century.
