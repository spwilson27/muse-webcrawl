History of tea

The history of tea begins in ancient China, where the leaves of Camellia sinensis were consumed as a medicinal drink, and follows tea's journey through Japan, along the maritime trade routes to Europe, and into the plantations and cultures of the modern world.

Origins in China

Legend credits the emperor Shennong with discovering tea around 2737 BCE. Historically, tea drinking emerged in the southwest — Sichuan and Yunnan — and by the Tang dynasty (618–907) was a national custom. Lu Yu's Classic of Tea (c. 760) codified its preparation: tea was steamed, pressed into cakes, ground and whisked or boiled. Song-dynasty culture refined whisked powdered tea and tea competitions; the Ming dynasty shifted to loose-leaf infusion.

Spread through Asia

Buddhist monks carried tea to Japan, where Eisai promoted it in the 12th century; the Japanese tea ceremony, perfected by Sen no Rikyū in the 16th century, raised preparation to an art. Tea also spread into Korea, Vietnam, Tibet (as butter tea and a currency of bricks) and along the caravan routes to Russia and Persia.

Tea reaches Europe

The Portuguese and then the Dutch East India Company brought tea to Europe in the early 17th century; it became a fashionable, costly drink in England after Catherine of Braganza popularised it at court in the 1660s. Heavy taxation encouraged smuggling until the Commutation Act of 1784 slashed duties. Tea clippers raced from China, and tea's demand — paid for in silver, then balanced by opium exports — led to the Opium Wars with Qing China.

The plantation era and modern tea

Britain broke China's monopoly by establishing plantations in Assam (using the native assamica variety) and Darjeeling, and Robert Fortune famously smuggled plants and expertise from China in 1848–51. Ceylon converted from coffee to tea under planters such as James Taylor. The tea bag, iced tea and instant tea were 20th-century innovations; today tea is the world's most consumed manufactured drink after water, with China and India the largest producers.
