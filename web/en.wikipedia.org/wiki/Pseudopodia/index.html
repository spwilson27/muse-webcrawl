Pseudopodia

Pseudopodia (singular pseudopodium, from Greek ψευδοποδός, "false foot") are temporary arm-like projections of the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells, most famously amoebae, used for locomotion and feeding. A cell that produces pseudopodia is referred to as an amoeboid.

Types

Four main types are distinguished by shape: lobopodia, blunt and tubular, typical of Amoeba; filopodia, thin and thread-like; reticulopodia, forming net-like networks, found in foraminifera; and axopodia, stiff projections stiffened by microtubules, found in radiolaria and heliozoa.

Formation and function

Pseudopodia are driven by the reversible assembly of actin filaments. At the leading edge, actin polymerisation pushes the membrane forward while the cytoplasm flows into the extension; myosin-driven contraction at the rear propels the cell body. Directional movement toward food or away from harm involves chemotaxis. In feeding, pseudopodia surround prey and enclose it in a food vacuole (phagocytosis). Human white blood cells such as macrophages and neutrophils use similar amoeboid motion to engulf pathogens and move through tissues.
