Animal identification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Animal identification refers to the marking of individual animals, or groups of animals, so that they can be recognised and traced. Identification systems support ownership records, disease control, breeding programmes, food safety traceability, and the return of lost pets. Methods range from ancient branding to modern electronic transponders governed by international standards.

Methods

Livestock have traditionally been marked by hot-iron or freeze branding, ear notching, and tattooing. Modern systems rely heavily on plastic or metal ear tags bearing unique numbers, often combined with machine-readable barcodes. Electronic identification uses radio-frequency identification (RFID): a small transponder, either in an ear tag, an injectable microchip, or a ruminal bolus retained in the stomach of ruminants, returns a unique code when scanned. Companion animals are commonly identified with subcutaneous microchips, and birds may carry closed leg rings.

Regulation and traceability

Many jurisdictions mandate official identification for farm animals. The European Union requires double ear tagging of cattle linked to individual "passports" and a central database, a system strengthened after the BSE crisis to allow full traceability of meat from farm to consumer. Australia operates the National Livestock Identification System (NLIS) based on electronic tags. National animal identification programmes aim to trace diseased animals within 48 hours to contain outbreaks of diseases such as foot-and-mouth disease.

Wildlife and research

Scientists identify wild animals with bird rings (bands), flipper tags, PIT tags, and natural markings such as whale fluke patterns or zebra stripes, enabling long-term population studies without recapture in some cases.
