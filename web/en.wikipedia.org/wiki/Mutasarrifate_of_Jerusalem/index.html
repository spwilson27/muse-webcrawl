Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem

The Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem (Ottoman Turkish: Kudüs-i Şerif Mutasarrıflığı; Arabic: متصرفية القدس الشريف), also known as the Sanjak of Jerusalem, was an Ottoman district with special administrative status established in 1872. The district encompassed Jerusalem as well as Hebron, Jaffa, Gaza and Beersheba. It was administered by a governor (mutasarrif) who reported directly to the imperial government in Istanbul rather than to the provincial governor of Syria or Beirut.

History

Jerusalem had been an Ottoman sanjak since the conquest of the Levant by Selim I in 1516–17, normally subordinate to the eyalet (later vilayet) of Damascus. In 1872, recognising the city's religious importance to Muslims, Christians and Jews and the growing involvement of European powers in its affairs, the Sublime Porte detached the sanjak from Syria and made its governor answerable directly to Istanbul. The arrangement reflected both the city's international significance and Ottoman attempts to assert control amid capitulations and foreign consular activity.

The mutasarrifate covered much of southern Palestine. Its population in the late Ottoman period was predominantly Arab Muslim, with significant Christian communities and a growing Jewish population, swelled by immigration from Europe beginning in the 1880s (the First Aliyah). The district saw new development in the late 19th century: neighbourhoods outside Jerusalem's Old City walls, the Jaffa–Jerusalem railway (opened 1892), and expanding citrus exports through Jaffa.

Dissolution

During the First World War the district was a theatre of the Sinai and Palestine campaign. British forces under General Allenby captured Jerusalem in December 1917, ending four centuries of Ottoman rule. The territory was administered by the Occupied Enemy Territory Administration and then incorporated into the British Mandate for Palestine, confirmed by the League of Nations in 1922.
