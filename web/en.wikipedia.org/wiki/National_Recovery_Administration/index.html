The National Recovery Administration (NRA) was a prime agency established by U.S. president Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1933 as the centrepiece of the New Deal programme to combat the Great Depression. Created under the National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA), the NRA brought industry, labour, and government together to set codes of fair competition — covering prices, wages, working hours, and production — with the aim of ending destructive competition, raising purchasing power, and reducing unemployment.

Operation

Under its energetic first administrator, Hugh S. Johnson, the agency negotiated more than 500 codes for industries ranging from steel and automobiles to textiles and burlesque theatre. Businesses that adopted the codes displayed the Blue Eagle symbol in their windows and on their products, accompanied by the slogan "We Do Our Part", and consumers were urged to patronise only Blue Eagle firms. Section 7(a) of the NIRA guaranteed workers the right to organise and bargain collectively, triggering a surge in union membership. The NRA's blanket code, the President's Reemployment Agreement, sought to shorten working hours so that available work could be spread among more employees.

Criticism

The agency quickly drew criticism from all sides: large firms were accused of dominating code-writing and using the codes to fix prices and restrict output; small businesses complained of burdensome paperwork and rules; and economists questioned whether raising prices during a deflationary collapse was sound policy. Johnson's flamboyant management also generated controversy, and he resigned in 1934.

Schechter decision and legacy

On May 27, 1935, in A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States — the "sick chicken case" — a unanimous Supreme Court held the NIRA unconstitutional, ruling that Congress had unlawfully delegated legislative power and that the codes exceeded federal authority over interstate commerce. The NRA was abolished effective January 1, 1936. Several of its labour and fair-competition ideas nonetheless survived in later legislation, notably the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938.
