Tariff

[Image: customs officers inspecting imported goods containers at a port checkpoint]

A tariff is a tax imposed by a government on imported (occasionally exported) goods, usually as a percentage of value (ad valorem) or per unit. Tariffs raise the domestic Price of imports, protecting home producers from foreign Competition, raising revenue and serving as an instrument of trade and industrial policy; their cost is borne largely by domestic consumers and downstream firms.

History and modern use

Tariffs were the young United States' main federal revenue source and a pillar of Mercantilism; Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws (1846) opened the free-trade era Adam Smith and David Ricardo had argued for. The US Smoot–Hawley Tariff (1930) deepened the Great Depression era collapse of trade and spurred the post-1945 GATT/World Trade Organization system of negotiated reductions, which brought rich-country industrial tariffs to low single digits. Economists mostly favour low tariffs on comparative-advantage grounds, while governments retain them for strategic industries, retaliation and bargaining: the US–China tariffs imposed from 2018 on hundreds of billions of dollars of goods, and subsequent rounds of industrial policy worldwide, mark a partial turn from the liberalising era.
