Territorial integrity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Territorial integrity is the principle under international law that prohibits states from the use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of another state, and forbids the acquisition of territory by force. It is enshrined in Article 2(4) of the UN Charter, elaborated in the Friendly Relations Declaration (1970), and restated — with accompanying commitments on inviolability of frontiers and human rights — in the Helsinki Final Act (1975).

Tensions

The principle collides structurally with self-determination: colonial peoples' right to independence was carved out of territorial integrity by practice (decolonization redrew empires, not states), but secession from existing states enjoys no general right in international law (the ICJ's Kosovo advisory opinion of 2010 found declarations of independence not prohibited as such, while avoiding any secession entitlement), leaving "remedial secession" a debated exception. The annexation of Crimea (2014) and the full-scale invasion of Ukraine (2022) made territorial integrity the rallying norm of the largest post-1945 European war: near-universal non-recognition of annexations, UN General Assembly majorities, and sanctions rest on it — while Kashmir, Western Sahara and other frozen files record its uneven application. Uti possidetis, the freezing of administrative borders at independence, serves it in practice.
