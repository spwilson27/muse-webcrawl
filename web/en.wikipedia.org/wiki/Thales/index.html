Thales of Miletus


Thales of Miletus, Greek philosopher and mathematician

Thales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 546 BC) was a Greek mathematician, astronomer, and pre-Socratic philosopher from Miletus in Ionia, Asia Minor. He was one of the Seven Sages of Greece. Many, most notably Aristotle, regarded him as the first philosopher in the Greek tradition, and he is otherwise historically recognized as the first individual known to have entertained and engaged in scientific philosophy. He is often referred to as the Father of Science.

Thales broke with the use of mythology to explain the world and the universe and instead explained natural objects and phenomena by offering naturalistic theories and hypotheses. Almost all of the other pre-Socratic philosophers followed him in explaining nature as deriving from a unity of everything based on the existence of a single ultimate substance, instead of using supernatural explanations.

Life

The primary source for details of Thales' life and career is the doxographer Diogenes Laërtius, in his third-century AD work Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers. While most modern scholars regard Diogenes' information to present an at-best patchy and sometimes entirely fictional account, it does give a picture of Thales as an engaged, theorist, statesman, and engineer.

Thales is said to have predicted the solar eclipse of 585 BC, which occurred during a battle between the Medes and the Lydians, prompting a truce. According to Herodotus, Thales predicted the eclipse years in advance.

Water as the arche

Thales believed that the arche (origin or first principle) of all things was water. Aristotle reported that Thales proposed that the Earth floats on water, and that water is the material cause of all things. The choice may have been influenced by the observation that moisture is essential to life, that water takes the forms of solid, liquid, and gas, and that Miletus sat beside the sea.

Mathematics

Several theorems in geometry are attributed to Thales, including that a circle is bisected by its diameter, that the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, that vertical angles are equal, that a triangle is determined by one side and the two adjacent angles, and that the angle in a semicircle is a right angle — now known as Thales's theorem. He is also credited with measuring the height of the pyramids by their shadows and the distance of ships at sea.

Astronomy

Thales was famous as an astronomer; he composed a work on the solstice and equinox and was the first to explain eclipses of the Sun. According to legend, he fell into a well while star-gazing, prompting a witty remark from a Thracian servant girl that he was so eager to know what went on in the heavens that he could not see what was in front of him.

Legacy

Thales' turn toward naturalistic explanation is widely treated as the beginning of Western philosophy and science. His name survives in Thales's theorem, and the French electrical and aerospace company Thales Group bears his name.
