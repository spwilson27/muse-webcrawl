Stockholm Convention

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants is an international environmental treaty, signed on 22 May 2001 in Stockholm and effective from 17 May 2004, that aims to eliminate or restrict the production and use of persistent organic pollutants (POPs). The convention is administered by the United Nations Environment Programme and has been ratified by more than 180 parties.

Key elements

Parties must prohibit or phase out the production and use of chemicals listed in Annex A, restrict those in Annex B, and reduce releases of unintentionally produced POPs listed in Annex C, such as dioxins and furans. Parties must also manage stockpiles and wastes containing POPs in an environmentally sound manner, and implement national plans for meeting their obligations.

Listed substances

The convention initially targeted twelve chemicals known as the "dirty dozen", including aldrin, chlordane, DDT, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, mirex, toxaphene, PCBs, hexachlorobenzene, and the unintentionally produced dioxins and furans. A Persistent Organic Pollutants Review Committee evaluates proposals for additional chemicals; since 2009 many more substances — including lindane, PFOS, endosulfan, and various brominated flame retardants — have been added, and DDT remains listed with an exemption for disease-vector control.

Related agreements

The Stockholm Convention works alongside the Basel Convention on hazardous wastes and the Rotterdam Convention on prior informed consent for hazardous chemicals; the three secretariats cooperate under a joint "BRS" arrangement.
