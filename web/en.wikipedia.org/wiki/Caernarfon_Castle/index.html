Caernarfon Castle

Caernarfon Castle (Welsh: Castell Caernarfon; Welsh pronunciation: [kastɛɬ kai̯rˈnarvɔn]) is a medieval fortress in Gwynedd, north-west Wales. The castle was first built in the late 11th century as a motte-and-bailey castle over a pre-existing motte, and the present stone castle was built by King Edward I of England beginning in 1283, following his conquest of the Principality of Wales. Its banded polygonal towers are thought to evoke the walls of Constantinople and the imperial power of Rome.

History

Edward I began the castle and its walled town in 1283 after the death of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd. The castle was damaged during the revolt of Madog ap Llywelyn in 1294–95 and repaired; building continued until about 1330, though the interior was never finished. It withstood sieges during the Glyndŵr rising and the English Civil War, after which it was slighted only in part and fell into neglect. The state repaired it in the later 19th century. In 1911 the castle hosted the investiture of the future Edward VIII as Prince of Wales, and in 1969 that of Charles, Prince of Wales (later Charles III).

Architecture

Caernarfon is noted for its polygonal towers and colour-banded masonry, consciously different from Edward's other Welsh castles such as Conwy and Harlech. The Eagle Tower, with its three turrets, is the grandest; the castle forms part of the "Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd" World Heritage Site, administered by Cadw.
