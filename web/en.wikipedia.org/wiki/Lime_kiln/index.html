Lime kiln

Lime kiln
FunctionCalcination of limestone to produce quicklime
ReactionCaCO3 + heat → CaO + CO2
Operating temperatureAbout 900 °C and above

A lime kiln is a kiln used for the calcination of limestone (calcium carbonate) to produce the form of lime called quicklime (calcium oxide). The chemical equation for this reaction is:

CaCO3 + heat → CaO + CO2

This reaction can take place at over 840 °C, but typically occurs at around 900–1000 °C (the temperature at which the partial pressure of CO2 is 1 atmosphere), because this is easily reached in a traditional kiln. Excessively high temperatures are to be avoided because they produce unreactive, "dead-burnt" lime.

Early kilns

Lime burning is ancient: burnt lime was used in plaster and mortar in the Near East by 7000 BC. The earliest kilns were simple clamp or flare kilns — heaps of limestone and fuel covered with turf, burned for several days and then dismantled. Permanent stone kilns became common where limestone and fuel were plentiful, particularly in the 18th and 19th centuries as demand rose for agricultural lime, mortar, whitewash and sanitation.

Permanent draw kilns

The classic rural lime kiln is a stone shaft built against a hillside or into a bank, loaded from the top with alternating layers of limestone and fuel (wood, coal or coke) and drawn from an arched opening at the bottom. In continuous "draw kilns" the burnt lime was raked out at the base while fresh charge was added at the top. Such kilns survive as industrial monuments in many limestone districts.

Modern kilns

Modern lime is produced in rotary kilns and in vertical shaft kilns, including regenerative parallel-flow designs (such as the Maerz kiln) that recover heat from exhaust gases to preheat the stone, markedly improving fuel efficiency. Lime manufacture is a significant source of industrial CO2 emissions, both from fuel and from the calcination reaction itself.

Uses of lime

Quicklime and its hydrated form, slaked lime, are used in cement, steelmaking (as a flux), water treatment, flue-gas desulfurisation, paper production, agriculture (to raise soil pH), and historically in burial, tanning and disinfection.
