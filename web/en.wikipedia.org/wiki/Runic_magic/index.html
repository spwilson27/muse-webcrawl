Runic magic

Runic magic refers to the use, historical and modern, of runes — the letters of the runic alphabets used by Germanic peoples — for magical or divinatory purposes. Historical evidence for runes being used magically in the pre-Christian and early medieval periods exists but is debated; much of what is popularly believed about runic magic derives from later Icelandic grimoires and from 20th-century occult revivals.

Historical evidence

The Roman historian Tacitus describes Germanic peoples casting lots with marked slips of wood, which some scholars have connected to runes. Inscriptions on objects such as the Lindholm amulet, bracteates and weapons sometimes contain apparently formulaic words — alu, laukaR, auja — interpreted as protective or empowering charms, and the Björketorp and Stentoften stones carry explicit curses against anyone who breaks the monument. The Poetic Edda, especially Sigrdrífumál and Hávamál, presents Odin as winning the runes through self-sacrifice and lists "victory runes", "ale runes" and "birth runes" among their uses.

Icelandic grimoires

In post-medieval Iceland, magical staves (galdrastafir) were recorded in manuscripts such as the Galdrabók (c. 1600), including famous designs like the Ægishjálmur (helm of awe) and Vegvísir (wayfinder). These symbols blend runic letters with other elements and belong more to early modern folk magic than to the Viking Age.

Modern use

The 20th century saw runes taken up by Germanic occultists such as Guido von List, whose "Armanen runes" influenced esoteric and, later, some extremist movements, and by modern Heathen and neopagan practitioners, who use runes in divination — casting lots and interpreting drawn runes — drawing on Ralph Blum's popular The Book of Runes (1982) and similar works. Scholars note that rune-row "meanings" used in divination are largely modern constructions based on the medieval rune poems.
