Cement kiln

Cement kilns are used for the pyroprocessing stage of manufacture of Portland and other types of hydraulic cement, in which calcium carbonate reacts with silica-bearing minerals to form a mixture of calcium silicates known as clinker. The industry produces billions of tonnes of cement annually, and its kilns are among the largest pieces of industrial equipment in existence.

Design

The modern cement kiln is a rotary kiln: a huge steel cylinder, up to about 6 metres in diameter and 200 metres long, lined with refractory brick and inclined slightly from horizontal, rotating slowly on supporting rollers. Raw meal enters the cool upper end; a burner firing pulverised coal, gas, or alternative fuels at the lower end drives a counter-current stream of hot gas, so the feed passes through drying, calcining, and finally clinkering zones where temperatures reach about 1,450 °C.

Chemistry

In the calcining zone, calcium carbonate decomposes to calcium oxide and carbon dioxide. In the burning zone the oxides combine to form the clinker minerals alite, belite, aluminate, and ferrite. The hot clinker is discharged into a cooler and later ground with gypsum to make cement.

Environmental issues

Cement manufacture is a major source of industrial carbon dioxide emissions — from both fuel combustion and the calcination reaction itself — as well as dust, nitrogen oxides, and trace heavy metals. Modern plants use preheater and precalciner towers, waste-heat recovery, and alternative fuels including waste materials.
