Battle of Königgrätz

The Battle of Königgrätz (also known as the Battle of Sadowa, from the village of Sadová) was fought on 3 July 1866 near the fortress of Königgrätz (modern Hradec Králové) in Bohemia. It was the decisive battle of the Austro-Prussian War: the Prussian armies under King Wilhelm I and Chief of Staff Helmuth von Moltke crushed the Austrian North Army under Ludwig von Benedek, settling the question of supremacy in Germany in Prussia's favour.

Background

In the Seven Weeks' War, Moltke invaded Bohemia with three armies advancing on separate lines, intending to concentrate on the battlefield. Benedek took up a position on the heights between the Bistritz stream and the Elbe, anchored on the village of Chlum, hoping to destroy the Prussian First and Elbe armies before the Crown Prince's Second Army arrived from the northeast. His deployment, however, left the decisive Chlum heights inadequately held, and the Austrian corps commanders handled their reserves poorly.

The battle

On the misty morning of 3 July the Prussian First Army attacked across the Bistritz and became bogged down in savage fighting in the woods of Sadowa (the Swiepwald) against superior Austrian artillery, which inflicted heavy losses. The battle hung in the balance until early afternoon, when the Crown Prince's Second Army, after a forced march, fell on the exposed Austrian right flank and stormed the heights of Chlum. Austrian counterattacks to retake Chlum were shot to pieces by the rapid fire of the Prussian Dreyse needle gun, whose breech-loading allowed Prussian infantry to fire prone several times faster than the Austrians' muzzle-loaders. Benedek extricated what he could across the Elbe bridges; Austrian cavalry and artillery fought desperate rearguard actions to cover the rout, at enormous cost.

Aftermath

Königgrätz ended Austrian influence in Germany. The Peace of Prague (23 August 1866) dissolved the German Confederation; Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt and created the North German Confederation, the direct precursor of the German Empire of 1871. The battle demonstrated to European armies the impact of breech-loading rifles, railways and the telegraph-directed staff system, and it reshaped the continent's balance of power.
