Slippery slope

A slippery slope argument asserts that a relatively small first step will inevitably lead to a chain of related events culminating in some significant — usually negative — outcome, and that the first step should therefore be rejected. The metaphor suggests that once set in motion, movement down the slope cannot be stopped. Whether such an argument is sound or a logical fallacy depends on whether a plausible mechanism connects the steps: with evidence of a causal or precedential chain, it can be a legitimate form of reasoning; asserted without justification, it is the fallacious slippery slope fallacy.

Types

Scholars such as Eugene Volokh classify slippery slope mechanisms into several kinds: precedent slopes, where a decision in one case logically or legally compels similar decisions in later, broader cases (common in law); causal slopes, where the first step changes incentives, costs, or attitudes so that the next step becomes more likely; and attitude-altering slopes, where accepting a practice shifts public opinion about its more extreme forms. Related concepts include the camel's nose, the thin end of the wedge, and the domino theory in foreign policy.

Use and criticism

Slippery slope arguments are common in debates over regulation, civil liberties, bioethics, and technology. Critics respond that intermediate stopping points often exist, that each step can be judged on its own merits, and that the predicted slide frequently fails to occur; defenders note that institutional momentum and precedent are real forces, so the burden is to assess the strength of the proposed mechanism rather than dismiss the form of argument outright.
