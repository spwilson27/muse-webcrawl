Fractional factorial design

In statistics, fractional factorial designs are experimental designs consisting of a carefully chosen subset (fraction) of the experimental runs of a full factorial design. The subset is chosen so as to exploit the sparsity-of-effects principle to expose information about the most important features of the problem studied, while using a fraction of the effort of a full factorial design in terms of experimental runs and resources.

History

Fractional designs were introduced by D. J. Finney in the 1940s, building on the factorial methods developed by R. A. Fisher. They became central tools in industrial experimentation and quality improvement.

Notation and resolution

A fractional factorial design is commonly written as 2k−p, where k is the number of factors and 1/2p is the fraction of the full design used. Designs are classified by resolution: Resolution III designs confound main effects with two-factor interactions; Resolution IV designs keep main effects clear of two-factor interactions; Resolution V designs keep two-factor interactions clear of each other.

Aliasing and confounding

Because only a fraction of runs is performed, some effects are aliased — estimated together and indistinguishable within the design. The defining relation and alias structure determine what can be learned, and follow-up experiments such as fold-over designs can break selected aliases.

Applications

Fractional factorials are widely used in screening experiments in manufacturing, agriculture, chemical engineering and product development, where many candidate factors must be assessed economically.
