Alternative manga is a term for Japanese comics created outside the conventions and commercial structures of the mainstream manga industry. Where mainstream manga is shaped by the editorial systems of the big weekly and monthly magazines and their demographic categories (shōnen, shōjo, seinen, josei), alternative manga encompasses work that is more personal, experimental, literary, or underground in form and subject matter — including gekiga, avant-garde comics, autobiographical and diary manga, and much dōjinshi-adjacent independent publishing.

History

The roots of alternative manga lie in the post-war kashihon (rental book) market, whose adult readership supported darker and more realistic stories than children's magazines allowed. In the late 1950s Yoshihiro Tatsumi and others coined the term gekiga ("dramatic pictures") for this serious, cinematic style. The magazine Garo, founded in 1964 by Katsuichi Nagai to publish Shirato Sanpei's Kamui, became the central platform of the alternative scene, publishing artists such as Yoshiharu Tsuge, whose dreamlike, confessional stories redefined what manga could do, as well as avant-garde and underground creators through the 1960s and 1970s. Later magazines such as COM, AX, and Ikki carried the tradition forward, and from the 1990s onward publishers and translators abroad (notably Drawn & Quarterly and Fantagraphics) introduced gekiga and alternative manga to Western readers.

Characteristics

Alternative manga frequently features unconventional visual styles — scratchy, minimalist, or deliberately crude drawing; decompressed or abstract layouts; and themes drawn from everyday life, eroticism, politics, history, and the artist's own experience. Critics sometimes group the more literary end of the field under "manga as literature", exemplified by creators like Tsuge, Tatsumi, Tadao Tsuge, Shigeru Mizuki's historical works, and contemporary artists such as Taiyo Matsumoto in his more experimental work.
