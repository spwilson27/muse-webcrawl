Speculation

In finance, speculation is the purchase of an asset (a commodity, good, or real estate) with the hope that it will become more valuable in the near future. In a broader financial sense, speculation can also involve trading in stocks, bonds, currencies, futures, collectibles, and other instruments, where the speculator seeks to profit from fluctuations in market value rather than from the asset's use or income. Speculation differs from investment in the degree of risk assumed and the time horizon: speculators typically accept a substantial risk of loss in exchange for the possibility of large short-term gains.

Speculators versus investors and hedgers

Economists distinguish speculation from both investment and hedging. An investor typically buys an asset for its expected income and long-term appreciation, while a hedger transacts to reduce risk—for example, a farmer selling futures to lock in a price. The speculator, by contrast, deliberately assumes market risk in anticipation of price movements. In practice the roles overlap, and Keynes famously compared professional speculation to predicting the outcome of a beauty contest based on what others will think others will think.

Economic role

Defenders argue that speculators provide liquidity, improve price discovery, and bear risks that hedgers wish to shed. Critics contend that speculation can destabilise markets, inflate asset bubbles, and raise prices of essential commodities. John Maynard Keynes and later economists such as Hyman Minsky examined how speculative finance can amplify booms and busts, while Benjamin Graham warned that in the short run the market behaves like a "voting machine" driven by speculation rather than fundamentals.

Historical episodes

Famous speculative manias include the Dutch tulip mania of 1636–37, the Mississippi and South Sea bubbles of 1720, the railway manias of the 1840s, the Florida land boom and stock bubble preceding the Wall Street Crash of 1929, the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, and the housing and credit bubble preceding the 2008 financial crisis. Such episodes are staple subjects of behavioural finance, which studies cognitive biases—overconfidence, herding, and extrapolation—that fuel speculative excess.

Regulation

Governments regulate speculation through margin requirements, position limits, short-selling rules, and taxes on transactions. Speculation in food and energy commodities has been especially controversial; limits on commodity speculation were strengthened in the United States under the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010.

Other meanings

Outside finance, "speculation" refers to conjecture or theorising without firm evidence, as in philosophical speculation or speculative fiction.
