The Last Laugh (German: Der letzte Mann, "The Last Man") is a 1924 German silent film directed by F. W. Murnau and starring Emil Jannings. It is one of the most famous works of Weimar cinema and of the Kammerspielfilm ("chamber drama") movement.

Plot

An ageing doorman at a luxurious Berlin hotel takes immense pride in his splendid uniform and the respect it earns him in his tenement neighbourhood. Judged too old for the strenuous post, he is demoted to washroom attendant and stripped of the uniform; when he steals it back to preserve appearances at his daughter's wedding, the deception is exposed and he is left broken and humiliated. A famous epilogue — openly acknowledged by an intertitle as an improbable concession to the audience — has a millionaire die in the washroom and leave the old man his fortune, restoring him in triumph.

Technique

The film is celebrated for Karl Freund's "unchained camera" (entfesselte Kamera), which glides, soars, and drifts to convey the doorman's pride, drunkenness, and despair. It tells its story almost entirely without intertitles, using a single title card before the epilogue. Its subjective camera work and visual storytelling were hugely influential on later filmmakers, including Alfred Hitchcock, who observed Murnau at work in Berlin.
