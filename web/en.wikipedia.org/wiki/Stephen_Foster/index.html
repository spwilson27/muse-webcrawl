Stephen Collins Foster (4 July 1826 – 13 January 1864), known as the "father of American music", was an American composer and songwriter who wrote more than 200 songs in the minstrel and parlour traditions, including "Oh! Susanna", "Hard Times Come Again No More", "Camptown Races", "Old Folks at Home" ("Swanee River"), "My Old Kentucky Home", "Jeanie with the Light Brown Hair" and "Beautiful Dreamer". Many of his songs remain internationally famous; three became official state songs of Florida, Kentucky and (formerly) others.

Life

Foster was born in Lawrenceville, now part of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, the youngest of ten children of William Barclay Foster and Eliza Tomlinson. Largely self-taught, he published his first song at eighteen. In 1846 he moved to Cincinnati, working as a bookkeeper for his brother's steamboat company while his early songs — "Oh! Susanna" (1848) became a Gold Rush anthem — spread through minstrel troupes, often uncredited. In 1849 he signed a publishing contract with Firth, Pond & Co. and returned to Pittsburgh to become one of America's first professional songwriters, writing extensively for E. P. Christy's minstrel troupe.

Career and decline

Foster's output spanned minstrel songs in dialect, sentimental parlour ballads, hymns and Civil War songs. Although his melodies earned publishers fortunes, weak early copyright protection and flat sales meant he received modest income; his marriage to Jane McDowell broke down, and in 1860 he moved alone to New York, where his work declined in popularity. He died in January 1864 in the charity ward of Bellevue Hospital after a fall or fever, with 38 cents in his pocket and a scrap of paper reading "Dear friends and gentle hearts".

Legacy and debate

Foster is memorialised in the Stephen Foster Memorial at the University of Pittsburgh and in state parks in Florida and Kentucky. His minstrel songs have long been debated: some celebrate their melodic craft and note that songs like "My Old Kentucky Home" express sympathy for enslaved people, while lyrics and dialect passages are now widely regarded as racist caricature, and official versions have been revised or replaced.
