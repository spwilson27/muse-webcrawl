Iron–sulfur cluster

Iron–sulfur clusters are molecular ensembles of iron and sulfide ions found in a wide range of metalloproteins. They are among the most ancient and versatile cofactors in biology, mediating electron transfer, catalysis, and the sensing of iron and oxygen.

Types

The commonest cluster types, coordinated in proteins usually by cysteine thiolate ligands, are:

  • [1Fe–0S] — a single iron atom, as in rubredoxin;
  • [2Fe–2S] — two iron atoms bridged by two sulfides, found in plant-type and Rieske ferredoxins;
  • [3Fe–4S] — an incomplete cubane;
  • [4Fe–4S] — a cube of alternating iron and sulfur atoms, the classic cluster of bacterial ferredoxins and high-potential iron proteins.

Functions

Iron–sulfur proteins form the electron-transfer chains of respiration and photosynthesis: complexes I, II and III of the mitochondrial respiratory chain contain multiple clusters relaying electrons one at a time. Clusters also perform catalysis in enzymes such as aconitase and nitrogenase (whose iron–molybdenum cofactor is an elaborate Fe–S assembly), act as sensors of O2, NO and iron availability (e.g. the FNR regulator and iron regulatory proteins), and participate in radical chemistry in the radical SAM enzyme superfamily.

Biosynthesis

Because free iron and sulfide are toxic, cells assemble clusters on dedicated scaffolding machinery — the ISC system in bacteria and mitochondria, the SUF system in plastids and many bacteria, and a cytosolic assembly pathway in eukaryotes. Mutations in human cluster-assembly genes cause diseases such as Friedreich's ataxia and sideroblastic anemias. The ease with which Fe–S clusters form spontaneously under anaerobic conditions has inspired the "iron–sulfur world" hypothesis for the origin of life.
