Gorgias

Gorgias of Leontini (c. 483 – c. 375 BC) was a pre-Socratic philosopher, rhetorician and one of the leading Sophists of ancient Greece. A pupil in the circle of Empedocles and a celebrated ambassador of his native Leontini in Sicily to Athens in 427 BC, Gorgias dazzled Greece with his ornate, rhythmical prose and his paradoxical showpieces — the Encomium of Helen, the Defence of Palamedes, and the treatise On Non-Existence (or On Nature), which argued that nothing exists; if anything existed it could not be known; and if known it could not be communicated. Plato named a dialogue after him, the Gorgias, in which Socrates debates his teaching that rhetoric is the master art of persuasion.

Works

In the Encomium of Helen Gorgias exonerates Helen of Troy on four grounds — fate, force, persuasion, or love — arguing in each case that she cannot be blamed, and in the process calls speech (logos) "a powerful lord" that can stop fear, remove grief and produce joy. On Non-Existence, preserved in paraphrase by Sextus Empiricus and in the pseudo-Aristotelian On Melissus, Xenophanes, and Gorgias, is variously read as satire of Eleatic metaphysics, as a serious exercise in scepticism, or as a demonstration of rhetoric's power to argue any thesis.

Style and influence

Gorgias pioneered a florid "Gorgianic" style of balanced clauses, antithesis, rhyme-like endings and poetic vocabulary, which influenced Greek prose and, through his pupil Isocrates, the rhetorical tradition. Plato's Gorgias stages his confrontation with Socrates over whether rhetoric is a genuine art or a knack of flattery, and whether it is better to suffer wrong than to do it.
