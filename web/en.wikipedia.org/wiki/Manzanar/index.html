Manzanar is best known as the site of one of ten American concentration camps where more than 120,000 Japanese Americans were incarcerated during World War II following Executive Order 9066. Located in California's Owens Valley at the foot of the Sierra Nevada, between the towns of Lone Pine and Independence, Manzanar held 10,046 people at its peak — over 90 percent of them from Los Angeles — in 36 blocks of tarpaper-covered barracks ringed by barbed wire and guard towers.

History

The first internees arrived in March 1942 to finish building the camp themselves. Conditions were harsh: extreme temperatures, dust storms, communal latrines, and little privacy. Despite this, residents built schools, gardens, a hospital, and a newspaper (the Manzanar Free Press), and photographer Toyo Miyatake famously smuggled in a camera lens to document camp life; Ansel Adams photographed the camp in 1943. Tensions culminated in the December 1942 "Manzanar Riot," in which military police killed two internees. In 1943, a controversial "loyalty questionnaire" split families, and men from Manzanar served in the celebrated 442nd Regimental Combat Team. The camp closed on November 21, 1945.

Preservation

An annual pilgrimage, begun in 1969, helped drive the redress movement that produced the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 and a formal apology. Manzanar was designated a National Historic Site in 1992 and is administered by the National Park Service; its visitor centre (the former high-school auditorium) opened in 2004, and reconstructed barracks, a mess hall, and the cemetery monument — inscribed "Soul Consoling Tower" (Ireitō) — anchor the site.
