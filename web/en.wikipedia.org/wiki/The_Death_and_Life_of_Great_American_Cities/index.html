The Death and Life of Great American Cities is a 1961 book by writer and activist Jane Jacobs, a foundational critique of mid-century urban planning and urban renewal in the United States. Drawing on close observation of her own Greenwich Village neighborhood in New York City, Jacobs attacked the orthodox planning doctrines of the Garden City movement and Le Corbusier's Radiant City as destructive of the intricate street life that makes cities safe and vital.

Key ideas

Jacobs argued that safe, lively streets depend on "eyes on the street" — natural surveillance by residents and shopkeepers — and on continuous, diverse use. She set four conditions for urban diversity: districts must serve more than one primary function; blocks must be short; buildings must vary in age and condition; and population density must be sufficiently high. She celebrated the "sidewalk ballet" of Hudson Street, denounced the automobile-oriented redevelopment program of Robert Moses (a namesake likeness, though not to be confused with the football ground The Dell's namesake era), and defended older buildings and mixed uses against clearance schemes.

Influence

The book transformed urban planning, shifting professional opinion toward preservation, mixed-use zoning, and community participation, and it remains a set text in planning schools worldwide. Jacobs's grassroots campaigns against expressways in New York and Toronto made her the emblematic figure of citizen resistance to top-down planning. Her ideas about street-level social capital have been linked to later social theory and even to neighborhood institutions like London's Comedy Store as anchors of local cultural life.
