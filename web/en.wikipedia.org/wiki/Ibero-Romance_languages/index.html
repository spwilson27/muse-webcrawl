The Ibero-Romance languages are a group of Romance languages that developed in the Iberian Peninsula from Vulgar Latin after the fall of the Western Roman Empire. They include Spanish (Castilian) and Portuguese, two of the world's most widely spoken languages, as well as Galician, Asturleonese, Aragonese and the extinct Mozarabic. Catalan is sometimes grouped with them as part of an Occitano-Romance branch, though its classification is debated.

History

Latin arrived in Hispania with Roman conquest beginning in 218 BC. After the Germanic invasions and then the Muslim conquest of 711, the Christian kingdoms of the north preserved and developed distinct Romance varieties: Galician-Portuguese in the west, Asturleonese, Castilian in the centre, Navarro-Aragonese and Catalan in the east, while Mozarabic was spoken under Muslim rule in the south. The Reconquista spread Castilian southward, and the unification of Spain elevated it to national language; Portuguese developed independently in the County of Portugal.

Spread

Through Spanish and Portuguese colonisation, Ibero-Romance languages spread to the Americas, Africa and Asia, giving them roughly 600 million native speakers combined. Characteristic features include the development of Latin initial F- to h- (later silent) in Castilian (e.g., facer → hacer), diphthongisation of Latin short Ĕ and Ŏ, and retention of a five-vowel system.
