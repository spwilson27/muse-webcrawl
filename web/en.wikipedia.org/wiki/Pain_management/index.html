Pain management is a branch of medicine that applies science and health-care practice to the relief of pain. It encompasses the treatment of acute pain (such as after surgery or injury), chronic pain (such as persistent back pain or neuropathic pain), cancer pain, and pain at the end of life. Modern pain medicine is typically interdisciplinary, combining pharmacological treatment with physical, psychological, and interventional approaches.

Assessment

Because pain is subjective, assessment relies on patient report, commonly a 0–10 numerical rating scale or a visual analogue scale, together with the history of the pain's site, character, timing, and effects on sleep, mood, and function. Clinicians distinguish nociceptive pain (from actual or threatened tissue damage), neuropathic pain (from damage or disease of the nervous system), and nociplastic pain (arising from altered nociception without clear tissue or nerve damage).

Drug treatment

The World Health Organization's analgesic ladder, originally published for cancer pain, sets out stepwise treatment: non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol/acetaminophen and NSAIDs), then weak opioids, then strong opioids such as morphine, with adjuvant drugs at every step. Adjuvants include gabapentinoids and tricyclic antidepressants or SNRIs for neuropathic pain, muscle relaxants, and topical agents such as lidocaine and capsaicin. Concerns about dependence and the opioid epidemic in many countries have driven guidelines favouring non-opioid and non-drug measures first for chronic non-cancer pain, and careful, time-limited opioid prescribing when they are used.

Interventional and non-drug methods

Interventional techniques include epidural steroid injections, nerve blocks, radiofrequency ablation, joint injections, and — for selected patients — spinal cord stimulation and intrathecal drug delivery. Non-drug treatments have strong evidence for many chronic pain conditions: structured exercise and physical therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, mindfulness-based programmes, and multidisciplinary pain rehabilitation. Anaesthetic techniques such as regional blocks and epidurals are central to postoperative pain control.

Palliative and special settings

In palliative care, pain relief is a core goal, with opioids titrated to comfort. Pain management in children, in older people, and in patients unable to communicate requires adapted assessment tools such as observational behaviour scales.
