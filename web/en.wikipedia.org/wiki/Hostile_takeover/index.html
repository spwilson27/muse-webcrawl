Hostile takeover

In business, a hostile takeover is the acquisition of one company (the target) by another (the acquirer) that is accomplished by going directly to the target's shareholders or by fighting to replace its management, against the wishes of the target's board of directors. A takeover is "hostile" precisely because the board resists; an acquisition agreed with the board is a friendly takeover or merger.

Methods

An acquirer typically proceeds by a tender offer — a public offer to buy shares directly from shareholders at a premium to the market price, conditional on obtaining a controlling stake — or by a proxy fight, persuading shareholders to vote out the incumbent board and elect directors who will accept the offer. A bidder may first accumulate a "toehold" stake, in the United States filing disclosure under Schedule 13D once it crosses 5% ownership. Notable hostile bids include Kraft's acquisition of Cadbury (2010) and the 1988 bidding war for RJR Nabisco won by Kohlberg Kravis Roberts, then the largest leveraged buyout in history.

Defenses

Target boards deploy a repertoire of "shark repellents":

  • Poison pill (shareholder rights plan): dilutes the bidder's stake if it crosses a threshold.
  • Staggered board: only a fraction of directors stand for election each year, delaying control.
  • Golden parachutes: lucrative severance for executives if the company is taken over.
  • White knight: seeking a friendly alternative acquirer; Crown jewel defense: selling prized assets.
  • Pac-Man defense: the target counter-bids for the acquirer.

In Delaware, directors' use of defenses is reviewed under the Unocal standard of reasonableness and proportionality to the threat.
