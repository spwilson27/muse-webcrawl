Sima Qian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Sima Qian in scholar's dress, from a later dynasty album]

Sima Qian (c. 145 – c. 86 BC) was a Chinese historian of the Han dynasty, author of the Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian) — the monumental history of China from the legendary Yellow Emperor to his own time, and the model for all subsequent Chinese dynastic histories. Inheriting the project and the office of Grand Historian (taishi) from his father Sima Tan, he chose castration over suicide after offending Emperor Wu by defending a disgraced general, enduring the disgrace in order to finish the work — a decision he explained in the famous Letter to Ren An. The Shiji's five-part structure (basic annals, tables, treatises, hereditary houses, biographies) and its vivid, judgement-laden narrative set the standard for East Asian historiography.

Life and punishment

Sima Qian travelled widely in his youth collecting traditions at historical sites, served at court, and in 99 BC spoke in defence of the general Li Ling, who had surrendered to the Xiongnu. Emperor Wu read the defence as an attack on his brother-in-law's mediocre generalship and condemned Sima Qian for deceiving the throne; lacking money to commute the sentence, he accepted castration (98 BC) rather than the honourable suicide expected of an official — 'a man has only one death', he wrote, but his history was unfinished. He completed the Shiji around 91 BC, in 130 chapters and some 526,500 characters, and hid a copy 'in a famous mountain' for posterity.

The Shiji's achievement

The work ranges from sage-kings to Sima Qian's contemporaries — assassins, merchants, jesters and generals rendered in dramatic scenes and invented-yet-plausible speeches. His biographies of Xiang Yu and the Qin emperors, his economic treatise, and his willingness to criticise the reigning dynasty (between the lines) made the Shiji literature as much as record; every later dynasty's official history imitated its form. Sima Qian is honoured in China as the father of Chinese historiography, the counterpart of Herodotus — whom he resembles in scale and surpasses in documentary rigour.
