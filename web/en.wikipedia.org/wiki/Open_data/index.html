Open data

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Open data is data that are openly accessible, exploitable, editable, and shared by anyone for any purpose, even commercial ones, subject at most to requirements that preserve provenance and openness. It is data that can be freely used, reused, and redistributed by anyone with no restrictions beyond, at most, attribution and share-alike. The movement is similar to other "open" movements such as open source, open hardware, open content, and open access.

Overview

The Open Definition, maintained by the Open Knowledge Foundation, states that "knowledge is open if anyone is free to access, use, modify, and share it — subject, at most, to measures that preserve provenance and openness." Open data is typically required to be machine-readable, available in bulk, and provided under an open licence (such as CC0, CC BY, or the Open Database License). Tim Berners-Lee proposed a five-star deployment scheme grading datasets from merely accessible under an open licence (one star) to fully linked open data (five stars).

Open government data

Governments are among the largest producers of open data. National portals such as data.gov (United States, launched 2009) and data.gov.uk (United Kingdom) publish datasets on spending, transport, crime, health, and the environment, motivated by transparency, economic value, and public-service improvement. International frameworks include the Open Data Charter (2015) and the OECD's open government data reviews. In the European Union, the PSI Directive (recast as the Open Data Directive, 2019) requires public-sector information to be reusable by default.

Open science data

In research, open data initiatives promote sharing of scientific datasets for verification and reuse, guided by the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Long-standing examples predate the term itself, including the Human Genome Project's Bermuda Principles requiring rapid public release of sequence data.

Issues

Debates around open data concern privacy and re-identification risks, data quality, sustainability of publishing, the capacity gap between data producers and users, and the concern that openness can disproportionately benefit well-resourced actors.
