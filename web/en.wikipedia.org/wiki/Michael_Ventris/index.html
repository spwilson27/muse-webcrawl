Michael George Francis Ventris (12 July 1922 – 6 September 1956) was an English architect, classicist, and philologist who deciphered Linear B, the syllabic script used for writing Mycenaean Greek, the earliest attested form of the Greek language. Working with grids of sign frequencies and drawing on the statistical groundwork of Alice Kober, Ventris announced in 1952 that Linear B recorded Greek; his findings were developed with John Chadwick in Documents in Mycenaean Greek (1956).

Early life

Ventris was born in Wheathampstead, Hertfordshire, into a military family and grew up partly in Switzerland, becoming fluent in several languages. In 1936, as a schoolboy, he heard Sir Arthur Evans lecture on the Minoan scripts from Knossos and became fascinated by the undeciphered Linear B. He published his first article on the subject at eighteen, trained and practised as an architect, and served in the RAF as a navigator during the Second World War, while continuing his decipherment work as an amateur.

Decipherment

Ventris circulated a series of informal "Work Notes" to other scholars, building a syllabic grid relating Linear B signs to probable sound values. Kober had demonstrated that the language was inflected; Ventris's breakthrough came from guessing that certain sign sequences represented Cretan place names such as Knossos and Amnisos. On 1 July 1952 he announced on BBC radio that the language of the tablets was Greek — centuries older than previously thought. Skepticism faded after a tablet excavated at Pylos, interpreted with the new values, matched a depicted tripod.

Death and legacy

Ventris was appointed OBE in 1955. He died in a car accident near Hatfield in September 1956, aged 34, weeks before the publication of Documents in Mycenaean Greek. His decipherment is considered one of the great intellectual achievements of the twentieth century, transforming the study of the Aegean Bronze Age.
