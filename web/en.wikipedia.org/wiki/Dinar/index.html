Dinar

The dinar is the principal currency unit in several countries in the Middle East and North Africa and was formerly used in several European countries. The name derives from the Roman denarius, a silver coin of ancient Rome. The modern dinar was modelled on the gold dinar of the early Islamic caliphates: the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan introduced a standardised gold dinar in about 696 AD, weighing one mithqal (about 4.25 grams), which circulated throughout the Muslim world and influenced medieval European coinage.

Modern currencies

Countries currently using a currency called the dinar include Algeria (Algerian dinar), Bahrain (Bahraini dinar), Iraq (Iraqi dinar), Jordan (Jordanian dinar), Kuwait (Kuwaiti dinar — for many years the world's highest-valued currency unit), Libya (Libyan dinar), North Macedonia (Macedonian denar, a cognate name), Serbia (Serbian dinar) and Tunisia (Tunisian dinar). The Bahraini, Jordanian and Kuwaiti dinars are subdivided into 1,000 fils; the Algerian, Libyan, Serbian and Tunisian issues use 100 subunits (centimes, dirhams, para and millimes respectively).

Historical dinars

The dinar was the currency of Yugoslavia from 1918 to 2003, in successive issues plagued in their final years by hyperinflation. It was also used in Croatia (1994–2023, replaced by the kuna and later the euro), Bosnia and Herzegovina (1992–1998), Sudan (1992–2007), and in the Spanish protectorate and earlier Andalusian usage as the dinar and maravedí systems evolved. In Iran, the dinar survives as a subdivision equal to one hundredth of a rial.
