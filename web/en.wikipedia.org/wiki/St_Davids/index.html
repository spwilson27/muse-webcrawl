St Davids

St Davids Cathedral, Pembrokeshire.

St Davids (Welsh: Tyddewi, "David's house") is a city and community and a Restored county borough in Pembrokeshire, Wales, on the River Alun at the westernmost point of Wales, within the Pembrokeshire Coast National Park. It is the smallest city in the United Kingdom by population, with about 1,600 residents recorded at the 2021 census. The city is the resting place of Saint David, the patron saint of Wales, and has been a site of pilgrimage since the Early Middle Ages; St Davids Cathedral is the seat of the Bishop of St Davids in the Church in Wales.

History

St David (c. 500 – 589) founded a monastery on the site in the 6th century, at a place then known in Latin as Meneva or Menevia. The monastery was plundered several times by Norse raiders between the 8th and 11th centuries, and several bishops were killed. In 1123 Pope Calixtus II is said to have decreed that two pilgrimages to St Davids were equivalent to one to Rome. The present cathedral was begun in 1181; the shrine of St David, damaged at the Reformation, was restored in 2012.

City status was lost in 1886 when the Victorian reforms removed automatic city status from small boroughs, but it was formally restored in 1994 by Queen Elizabeth II in recognition of the town's historical importance.

Geography and tourism

The city stands just inland from St Davids Head and Whitesands Bay, within striking distance of Ramsey Island, a Royal Society for the Protection of Birds reserve noted for its choughs and grey seals. The Pembrokeshire Coast Path passes through the area. Tourism, together with agriculture, is the mainstay of the local economy, and the city has notable galleries, including Oriel y Parc, operated with Amgueddfa Cymru – Museum Wales.

Landmarks

Landmarks include the cathedral, the ruined 14th-century Bishop's Palace beside it, the medieval cross in Cross Square, the 13th-century Tower Gate, and St Non's Chapel and Well on the coast, traditionally the birthplace of St David.
