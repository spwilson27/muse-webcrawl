Baltic paganism is the collective term for the pre-Christian religions of the Baltic peoples — the Lithuanians, Latvians, and the now-extinct Old Prussians, Curonians, Semigallians and related tribes. It was a polytheistic, nature-centred belief system within the wider family of Indo-European religions. The Balts were among the last peoples in Europe to be Christianised: the Lithuanian lands accepted Christianity only in 1387 (with Samogitia following in 1413), and pagan practices persisted in rural areas for centuries afterwards.

Deities

The supreme god was Dievas (Latvian Dievs), the bright sky god. Other major deities included Perkūnas (Latvian Pērkons), the thunder god and one of the most widely venerated; Velnias, associated with the dead and the underworld; Žemyna, goddess of the earth; Saulė, the sun goddess; Mėnulis, the moon; and Laima, goddess of fate and childbirth. Latvian tradition preserves hundreds of short mythological songs, the dainas, which are a principal source for the mythology.

Practices

Worship centred on sacred groves (alka), hilltops, rivers and oak trees, where offerings were made and sacred fires maintained. Animal sacrifices, including horses, are reported by crusader-era chroniclers such as Peter of Dusburg. Priests included the krivis (chief priest) and soothsayers called vaidilos. The great temple at Romowe in Prussia, described in chronicles, housed images of the gods Percunatel, Patollo and Potrimpo.

Christianisation

The Prussian tribes were forcibly converted during the 13th-century Prussian Crusade by the Teutonic Order. Lithuania's grand duke Jogaila accepted baptism in 1386 as a condition of his marriage to Jadwiga of Poland, and mass baptisms followed, although resistance and survivals were strongest in Samogitia. Folk customs, seasonal festivals such as Joninės (St John's Eve) and harvest rites preserved many pre-Christian elements.

Modern revival

In the 20th century the faith was reconstructed as organised neopagan movements: Romuva in Lithuania (rooted in the work of Jonas Basanavičius and Vydūnas, and formally re-established in the late 20th century) and Dievturība in Latvia, founded by Ernests Brastiņš in the 1920s. Romuva has sought official recognition in Lithuania as a traditional religion.
