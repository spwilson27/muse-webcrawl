Sengoku period

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Sengoku-era castle keep rising over stone ramparts]

The Sengoku period ('Warring States period', c. 1467–1600, conventionally 1467–1615) was an era of near-constant civil war in Japan, when the Ashikaga shogunate's authority collapsed and rival daimyo — regional warlords — fought for supremacy amid social upheaval summed up as gekokujō, 'the low overthrow the high'. Beginning with the Ōnin War (1467–1477), which devastated Kyoto, the period saw the rise of castle towns, massed ashigaru infantry, and from 1543 European firearms. It ended through the successive campaigns of the three great unifiers — Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu.

Warlords and warfare

Sengoku daimyo such as Takeda Shingen, Uesugi Kenshin, Date Masamune and Mōri Motonari built compact territorial states, codified house laws, surveyed land and fostered trade. Warfare changed scale: battles involved tens of thousands, castles multiplied across the landscape, and Portuguese-introduced arquebuses decided engagements such as Nagashino (1575), where Oda's volley fire destroyed the Takeda cavalry (in the famous, partly embellished account).

Unification

Oda Nobunaga, rising from a minor Owari house, took Kyoto in 1568, destroyed the warrior monks of Enryaku-ji, and was on the verge of supremacy when assassinated by his general Akechi Mitsuhide in 1582 (the Honnō-ji Incident). His lieutenant Toyotomi Hideyoshi — a peasant's son — completed the conquest by 1590, disarmed the peasantry, and launched two disastrous invasions of Korea (1592–1598). After his death, Tokugawa Ieyasu's victory at Sekigahara (1600) opened the final settlement: the Tokugawa shogunate (1603).
