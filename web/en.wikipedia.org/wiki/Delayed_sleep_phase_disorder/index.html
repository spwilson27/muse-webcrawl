Delayed sleep phase disorder

Delayed sleep phase disorder (DSPD), more commonly known as delayed sleep–wake phase disorder, is a disorder of the circadian rhythm in which a person's sleep schedule is delayed relative to the socially conventional or desired clock times. Affected people typically cannot fall asleep until the early hours of the morning — often between 2 a.m. and 6 a.m. — and correspondingly wake late in the morning or afternoon. When allowed to follow their own schedule, their sleep is usually of normal quality and duration; the disorder arises from the mismatch with societal schedules.

Signs and symptoms

People with DSPD report chronic difficulty initiating sleep at a desired earlier time and extreme difficulty waking for school or work, often with severe sleep inertia in the morning. Attempts to force an earlier schedule commonly result in chronic sleep deprivation, daytime sleepiness, and impaired academic or occupational performance. The condition is frequently misdiagnosed as insomnia or attributed to laziness or poor discipline. Depression and other psychiatric symptoms are common comorbidities, though it is debated whether they are causes or consequences of the disorder.

Causes and mechanisms

DSPD is thought to involve a circadian period longer than 24 hours or an altered phase relationship between the internal circadian clock and the sleep–wake cycle. Studies have found associations with polymorphisms in circadian clock genes such as PER3 and CRY1. The disorder often emerges in adolescence, when a normal developmental phase delay coincides with early school start times, and persists into adulthood in a subset of cases. Prevalence in adults is estimated at roughly 0.1–0.2%, with higher rates reported among adolescents.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical history, at least one to two weeks of sleep diaries or actigraphy showing a stable delay of the major sleep episode, and exclusion of other sleep disorders. Polysomnography may be used to rule out conditions such as sleep apnoea. Diagnostic criteria appear in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3) under circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders.

Management

Treatment aims to shift the circadian phase earlier. Timed bright light therapy in the morning and avoidance of light in the evening are mainstays; low-dose melatonin administered in the evening (several hours before habitual sleep onset) can advance the phase. Chronotherapy — progressively delaying the schedule around the clock until it realigns — was historically used but carries a risk of inducing non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder. Strict sleep hygiene and, where possible, occupational accommodation of a later schedule are also recommended. Conventional hypnotics generally do not correct the underlying phase delay.
