Caslon

Caslon is a family of serif typefaces created by the English engraver and typefounder William Caslon I (1692–1766) in London. First released in the 1720s and 1730s, Caslon became one of the most widely used typefaces of the eighteenth century and is often summed up in the printers' maxim: "When in doubt, use Caslon."

History

William Caslon set up his foundry in London after working as an engraver of gun locks and bookbinding tools. His specimen sheet of 1734 displayed roman, italic and other types cut in a Dutch-influenced old-style manner, with bracketed serifs, moderate contrast and a sturdy colour on the page that suited long texts.

Caslon types dominated English printing for decades and were exported to the American colonies. The first printed versions of the United States Declaration of Independence were set in Caslon, and Benjamin Franklin was a noted admirer and user of the face.

Characteristics

Caslon is recognised by its relatively short ascenders and descenders, its bracketed serifs, and italic letterforms with a calligraphic, swashed quality. The capitals are somewhat narrower than the lowercase, giving text a warm, slightly condensed rhythm.

Revivals

The face has been revived many times, including versions by the H. W. Caslon company, Monotype, Linotype, and digital revivals such as Adobe Caslon (1990, by Carol Twombly) and ITC Founder's Caslon. It remains a staple for book typography, editorial design and formal documents. See also type founding and serif typefaces.
