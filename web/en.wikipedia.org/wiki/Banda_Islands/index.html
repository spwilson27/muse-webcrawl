Banda Islands


Banda Neira and the volcano Gunung Api.

The Banda Islands (Indonesian: Kepulauan Banda) are a volcanic group of ten small islands in the Banda Sea, about 140 kilometres southeast of Seram Island, in the Maluku province of Indonesia. Until the nineteenth century they were the world's only source of the spices nutmeg and mace, and were therefore among the most valuable — and most violently contested — places in early modern trade. The main islands include Banda Neira, Banda Besar (Lontar), and Gunung Api; the total land area is about 172 square kilometres and the population roughly 20,000.

Early trade

Bandanese society was organised in villages led by chiefs (orang kaya), and its nutmeg was traded for centuries through Javanese, Malay, Chinese, and Arab merchants. Portuguese navigators reached the islands in 1512, a year after taking Malacca, and attempted to buy nutmeg directly and build a fort, without success.

Dutch conquest

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) sought a monopoly. After years of conflict, in 1621 the governor-general Jan Pieterszoon Coen launched a brutal conquest: most of the Bandanese population of about 15,000 was killed, starved, enslaved, or driven off — an act now widely described as genocide. The islands were repopulated with enslaved labourers from elsewhere in Asia, and nutmeg production was reorganised into plantation lots (perken) worked by slaves and granted to Dutch perkeniers. Trees found growing outside Banda were ordered destroyed to protect the monopoly, and nuts were limed to prevent germination elsewhere.

The English East India Company contested the small island of Run, and by the Treaty of Breda (1667) the English formally abandoned their claim to Run in exchange for Dutch recognition of English possession of New Netherland in North America — including Manhattan, an exchange famously remembered as "Run for Manhattan".

Later history

The nutmeg monopoly was eventually broken in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries when plants were smuggled to and cultivated in Mauritius, Grenada, and elsewhere. Banda's importance faded; the islands were used in the Dutch colonial era as a place of exile for Indonesian nationalists, including Mohammad Hatta and Sutan Sjahrir in the 1930s. Today the islands are known for nutmeg production, historic forts such as Fort Belgica on Banda Neira, colonial architecture, diving tourism, and the rich marine life of the surrounding reefs.
