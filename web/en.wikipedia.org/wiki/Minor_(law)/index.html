Minor (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Minors have a distinct legal status, with restricted capacity but special protections.

In law, a minor is a person under a certain age — usually the age of majority — which legally demarcates childhood and adolescence from adulthood. A minor lacks full legal capacity: in most jurisdictions minors cannot vote, enter binding contracts on the same terms as adults, consent to certain medical treatment independently, or purchase alcohol or tobacco, but they also benefit from special protections in criminal law, employment, and civil proceedings.

Age of majority

The age of majority is 18 in most countries, following the near-universal adoption of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, which defines a child as a person under 18. Some jurisdictions set the age at 19, 20, or 21 for particular purposes, and different thresholds may apply within one legal system for different rights and responsibilities — for example, separate ages for driving, voting, drinking alcohol, criminal responsibility, and consent to sexual activity.

Legal consequences

Contracts entered into by minors are generally voidable at the minor's option, except for necessaries. Minors typically act through parents or legal guardians, who hold rights and duties of custody and care. In criminal law, minors are usually dealt with by separate juvenile justice systems emphasising rehabilitation, and in many jurisdictions young children below a minimum age (doli incapax) cannot be held criminally responsible at all.

A minor may be emancipated — released from parental control and granted adult legal status before the age of majority — by court order, marriage, or military service in some jurisdictions. Conversely, legal systems may extend protections beyond majority for particular purposes, such as compulsory education or care leavers' support.
