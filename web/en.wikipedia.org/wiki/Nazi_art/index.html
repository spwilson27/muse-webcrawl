Art of Nazi Germany

The art of Nazi Germany was the state-sanctioned visual culture of the Third Reich (1933–1945). The regime of Adolf Hitler sought to use art as propaganda for National Socialist ideology, promoting a conservative, heroic style rooted in classical and romantic models while suppressing modernist art, which it condemned as "degenerate".

Ideology and style

Approved art celebrated the supposed virtues of the German Volk: healthy Aryan bodies, martial heroism, peasant life rooted in the soil (Blut und Boden, "blood and soil"), motherhood, and obedience to the Führer. Sculpture favoured monumental, muscular nudes, epitomised by Arno Breker and Josef Thorak; painting favoured heroic realism and romanticised landscapes and genre scenes. Adolf Ziegler became president of the Reich Chamber of Visual Arts and a favoured painter of the regime.

The Degenerate Art campaign

Modernist works — expressionist, abstract, Dadaist, and works by Jewish artists — were purged from German museums; more than 16,000 works were confiscated. In 1937 the regime staged twin exhibitions in Munich: the Great German Art Exhibition, displaying approved art in the new Haus der Deutschen Kunst, and the Degenerate Art Exhibition (Entartete Kunst), which mocked modernism by hanging some 650 works chaotically with derisive captions. The "degenerate" show drew far larger crowds. Many confiscated works were sold abroad for foreign currency or destroyed.

Architecture

Architecture expressed the regime's ambitions most grandly in stripped neoclassicism — the Nuremberg rally grounds, Berlin's Olympiastadion, and Albert Speer's unbuilt plans for a monumental "Germania" capital. After 1945, Nazi art was largely removed from public view, though it remains studied as a case of art under totalitarianism.
