Xerophthalmia

Xerophthalmia
SpecialtyOphthalmology, nutrition
CauseVitamin A deficiency
SymptomsNight blindness, dry conjunctiva and cornea, Bitot's spots, corneal ulceration
PreventionVitamin A supplementation, dietary improvement

Xerophthalmia is a medical condition in which the eye fails to produce tears, caused by vitamin A deficiency, in which the conjunctiva and cornea become abnormally dry and thickened. It is a progressive eye disease which, if untreated, can lead to irreversible blindness, and it remains a leading preventable cause of blindness in children in developing countries.

Signs and progression

The World Health Organization classification grades the condition from night blindness (XN) — often the earliest sign, since vitamin A is required for the photopigment rhodopsin — through conjunctival xerosis (X1A) and Bitot's spots (X1B, foamy white patches on the conjunctiva), to corneal xerosis (X2), corneal ulceration and keratomalacia (X3), in which the cornea softens and may perforate. Corneal scarring (XS) is the blinding end stage. The dry-eye syndrome caused by lacrimal-gland dysfunction (as in Sjögren syndrome) is a different condition, though the name is similar.

Causes and treatment

Xerophthalmia results from dietary vitamin A deficiency, often compounded by measles and other infections that deplete vitamin A stores and increase demand. Treatment is urgent high-dose vitamin A supplementation according to WHO schedules, along with treatment of infection and underlying malnutrition; corneal involvement is an emergency because blindness can follow within days. Prevention relies on periodic vitamin A supplementation of young children, food fortification, and promotion of vitamin A–rich foods.
