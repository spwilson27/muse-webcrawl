The Case–Shiller indices are repeat-sales house price indices for the United States, developed by economists Karl E. Case and Robert J. Shiller (with Allan Weiss) and now published monthly as the S&P CoreLogic Case–Shiller Home Price Indices. They are the most widely cited measures of U.S. residential property prices.

Methodology

The indices use the repeat-sales method: when a single-family home is sold twice, the change in its price is used as a measure of price change for its market, with pairs of sales aggregated and weighted (value-weighted in the main indices) to dampen the influence of very frequently traded homes. This avoids the distortion of simple average or median prices caused by changes in the mix of homes sold. Indices are calculated as three-month moving averages and published with a two-month lag.

Indices

The headline products are the U.S. National Home Price Index, covering all nine U.S. census divisions, and the 10-City and 20-City Composite indices (the latter including New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, Dallas, and other major metros), plus tiered and condominium sub-indices for some cities.

Uses and significance

Case and Shiller's long historical series (Shiller's data extend back to 1890) underpin research on housing bubbles; Shiller famously used the index to argue that U.S. house prices in the 2000s were unsustainable before the subprime mortgage crisis. Futures and options on the 10-City index trade on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, allowing investors to hedge or speculate on house prices.
