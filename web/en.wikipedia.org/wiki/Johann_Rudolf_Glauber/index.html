Johann Rudolf Glauber

Johann Rudolf Glauber
Born: 1604, Karlstadt, Germany
Died: 16 March 1670, Amsterdam, Netherlands
Known for: Glauber's salt (sodium sulfate); early chemical industry

Johann Rudolf Glauber (1604 – 16 March 1670) was a German-Dutch alchemist and chemist. Regarded by many historians of chemistry as one of the first chemical engineers, he developed methods for producing several important chemicals on a commercial scale and is remembered for the discovery of sodium sulfate, known as Glauber's salt.

Life

Glauber was born in Karlstadt in Franconia and received little formal education, learning through apprenticeship and practice. He lived and worked in several places, including Vienna, Salzburg, Giessen, Wertheim, and finally Amsterdam, where he built laboratories and a successful business manufacturing pharmaceutical and industrial chemicals. His work was repeatedly disrupted by war, including the Thirty Years' War, and he complained often of competitors and imitators.

Contributions

Glauber described improved methods for preparing hydrochloric acid, nitric acid, and other mineral acids, and studied the reactions of metals and salts. In 1625 he discovered sodium sulfate, which he regarded as a near-universal medicine — sal mirabile, "miraculous salt" — and it is still known as Glauber's salt. He is also credited with early observations of chemical gardens (silicate gardens), improvements to furnaces and distillation, the preparation of various organic substances such as benzene-containing coal-tar fractions, and an early recognition that plants require mineral nutrients, anticipating aspects of agricultural chemistry.

His major written work, Furni Novi Philosophici (1648–1650), described his furnaces and chemical operations, alongside treatises on mineral waters, Germany's prosperity, and other subjects. Glauber's combination of experimental practice, commercial production, and publication marks a transition between alchemy and modern industrial chemistry.
