Redundancy (engineering)


Duplication of critical components increases reliability.

Redundancy in engineering is the intentional duplication of critical components or functions of a system with the goal of increasing reliability, usually in the form of a backup or fail-safe, or to improve actual system performance. It is a central strategy in reliability engineering and fault-tolerant design.

Forms of redundancy

  • Hardware redundancy — additional physical components, such as redundant power supplies, engines, or flight computers.
  • Information redundancy — extra bits, such as error-correcting codes, that allow corrupted data to be detected and repaired.
  • Time redundancy — repeating an operation and checking that the results agree.
  • Software redundancy — independently written implementations, such as N-version programming.

Active and standby redundancy

In active redundancy, all duplicated units operate simultaneously and share the load, so a failure is masked without interruption—for example, the multiple engines of an airliner. In standby redundancy, a backup unit remains idle or in reserve until a failure is detected, at which point switching logic brings it into service.

Voting systems

Triple modular redundancy (TMR) uses three identical systems performing the same task, with a voter that takes the majority result. Airliners and spacecraft commonly use redundant flight computers that cross-check one another.

Drawbacks

Redundancy adds cost, weight, complexity, and maintenance burden, and can itself introduce failure modes—for example, switch-over failures or common-cause failures that disable all copies at once. Designers must weigh these against the reliability gained.
