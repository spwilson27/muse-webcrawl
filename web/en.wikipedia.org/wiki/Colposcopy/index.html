A colposcope

Colposcopy is a medical diagnostic procedure to visually examine the cervix as well as the vagina and vulva using a surgical microscope known as a colposcope. The main goal of colposcopy is to prevent cervical cancer by detecting and treating precancerous lesions early.

Indications

Most women undergo colposcopy following an abnormal Pap smear (cervical cytology) result, a positive test for high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), or an abnormal-looking cervix noted during a pelvic examination. It may also be used to evaluate other symptoms of the lower genital tract.

Procedure

During the procedure the patient lies in the lithotomy position and a speculum is inserted. The colposcope, which remains outside the body, provides magnified illumination of the cervix. Dilute acetic acid (vinegar) is applied to make abnormal cells appear white (acetowhite), and Lugol's iodine may be used to highlight areas that do not take up iodine. Suspicious areas are sampled with a small biopsy for histological examination, and endocervical curettage may be performed.

Findings and follow-up

Findings are graded by the appearance of vessels, margins, and colour change, and may be scored with systems such as the Reid colposcopic index or the Swede score. Depending on results, management ranges from surveillance with repeat cytology to excisional treatment such as loop electrosurgical excision (LEEP).

History

The technique was developed by the German physician Hans Hinselmann in the 1920s, working with Eduard Wirths; the first colposcopes were built in Hamburg in 1925.
