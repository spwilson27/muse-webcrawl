Tennessee Williams

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Tennessee Williams at his typewriter in the 1950s, cigarette in hand, pages piled beside him]

Thomas Lanier Williams III (26 March 1911 – 25 February 1983), known by his pen name Tennessee Williams, was an American playwright and screenwriter, the poet laureate of the American South's decay. With Eugene O'Neill and Arthur Miller he defines postwar American drama: lyrical, memory-haunted plays in which fragile dreamers — above all his Southern women — break against a brutal, changing world.

His masterpieces are The Glass Menagerie (1944), A Streetcar Named Desire (1947, Pulitzer Prize), Cat on a Hot Tin Roof (1955, Pulitzer), Sweet Bird of Youth (1959) and The Night of the Iguana (1961).

Life and materials

Born in Columbus, Mississippi, and raised in St Louis, Williams drew lifelong material from his family: the domineering mother Edwina, the travelling-salesman father, and his beloved sister Rose, whose schizophrenia and 1943 lobotomy — performed without his knowledge while he was away — haunted his work and life. After years of menial jobs and rejection, The Glass Menagerie, drawn from the family, made him famous at 33.

Style and later years

Williams called his method 'plastic theatre': memory narrators, screens, music and symbol woven into realism. His great decade ran from Menagerie to Iguana; the 1960s–70s brought commercial failures, addiction and depression even as the earlier plays became permanent repertory. He died in a New York hotel room in 1983, by the coroner's account choking on a bottle cap — a grim accident long disputed by his brother.
