Santa Ana winds

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Santa Ana winds blow dust and wildfire smoke from the deserts out over the Pacific.

The Santa Ana winds, also sometimes called the devil winds, are strong, extremely dry downslope winds that originate inland and affect coastal Southern California and northern Baja California. They occur chiefly in autumn and early winter, when high pressure over the Great Basin drives air southwestward through the passes and canyons of the Transverse and Peninsular Ranges toward the coast.

Meteorology

As the air descends from the high desert plateaus to sea level it is compressed and warms adiabatically, dropping its relative humidity — often below 10–15 percent — and accelerating through constricted passes such as Cajon Pass and the Santa Ana Canyon, from which the winds take their name. Gusts commonly reach 40–60 mph (65–95 km/h) and can exceed hurricane force in favoured locations. The winds bring exceptionally clear skies, high temperatures even in mid-winter, and dramatic visibility.

Effects

Santa Ana conditions create extreme wildfire danger: vegetation is desiccated, fires ignite readily and are driven faster than suppression can contain, and many of California's most destructive wildfires — including the Witch Creek fire (2007), the Thomas Fire (2017) and the wind-driven urban fires of January 2025 in Los Angeles County — burned during Santa Ana events. The winds also damage trees, roofs, power lines and high-profile vehicles, churn dust and pollen, and rough the coastal sea, sometimes producing notable surf breaks offshore.

In Southern California culture the Santa Anas have a long literary reputation for unease: Raymond Chandler wrote of "those hot dry Santa Anas that come down through the mountain passes and curl your hair and make your nerves jump", and Joan Didion famously described the oppressive mood of the "Santa Ana" season in her essay of that name.

Etymology

The name is generally traced to Santa Ana Canyon in Orange County, though folk etymologies have also connected it to a supposed Spanish Satanás ("Satan") — hence "devil winds" — a derivation most linguists consider incorrect.
