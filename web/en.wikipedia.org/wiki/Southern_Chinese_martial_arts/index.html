Southern Chinese martial arts

The Southern Chinese martial arts are the kung fu styles originating south of the Yangtze River, principally in Guangdong and Fujian provinces. They are often contrasted with the Northern styles: southern systems tend to emphasise strong, low stances, powerful hand and arm techniques, and close-range fighting, a character summed up in the saying "fists in the south, legs in the north".

Characteristics

Because southern practitioners historically fought in crowded towns, on boats and in rice paddies, their arts favour a stable base, short steps and rapid hand combinations over the long, acrobatic kicks of the north. Deep stances such as the horse stance build leg strength, and many techniques are delivered from the waist with vocal exhalations (hei gung) to focus power.

Major styles

  • Hung Ga — a hard, powerful style associated with the folk hero Wong Fei-hung.
  • Choy Li Fut — founded in the 19th century, combining northern footwork with southern hand techniques.
  • Wing Chun — a close-range, efficiency-focused style popularised by Ip Man and Bruce Lee.
  • White Crane and Southern Praying Mantis — Fujianese and Hakka styles respectively, noted for short power and sticky hands.
  • Five Ancestors Fist (Ngo Cho Kun) — a Fujianese composite of five older systems.

History

Legend traces many southern styles to fugitive monks of the Southern Shaolin Temple in Fujian, said to have been destroyed by Qing forces. Whatever the literal truth, southern arts were shaped by Hakka migration, secret societies and the martial culture of the Pearl River Delta, and spread worldwide through the Cantonese diaspora.
