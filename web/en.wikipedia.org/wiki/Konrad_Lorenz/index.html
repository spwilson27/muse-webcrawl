Konrad Lorenz

Lorenz with the greylag goslings that made imprinting famous

Konrad Zacharias Lorenz (7 November 1903 – 27 February 1989) was an Austrian zoologist and one of the founders of ethology, the scientific study of animal behaviour. He is best known for his work on imprinting — the rapid, irreversible learning by which young birds follow the first moving object they see — and for sharing the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Nikolaas Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch "for their discoveries concerning organization and elicitation of individual and social behaviour patterns".

Work

At his family estate at Altenberg and later at research stations in Germany, Lorenz kept colonies of free-ranging animals — jackdaws, greylag geese, ducks — and observed them naturalistically, arguing against purely laboratory-based behaviourism that instinctive behaviour consists of fixed action patterns released by specific stimuli. His observations of goslings that imprinted on his boots became the canonical image of the critical period in development. His popular books, including King Solomon's Ring (1949) and On Aggression (1963), brought ethology to a wide readership; the latter controversially extended animal aggression to human affairs, and his late work Civilized Man's Eight Deadly Sins (1973) turned to environmental and social criticism.

Nazi period and legacy

Lorenz joined the Nazi Party in 1938 and held a chair at Königsberg; writings from this period applied his biological ideas to racial ideology, a record examined critically after the war and especially after his Nobel award. He was captured while serving as a Wehrmacht medic and spent years as a Soviet prisoner of war before returning to Austria in 1948, where he founded an institute at Altenberg and later directed the Max Planck Institute for Behavioural Physiology. Post-war scholarship continues to separate his foundational contributions to ethology — instinct, imprinting, the comparative method — from his political compromises.
