Nicotine is an alkaloid produced in the roots of plants in the nightshade family (Solanaceae), principally tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum and Nicotiana rustica), where it serves as a potent insecticide. It is a stimulant and a highly addictive substance; in tobacco products it is the chief chemical responsible for dependence. Nicotine is named after the tobacco genus Nicotiana, itself named for Jean Nicot, the French ambassador who sent tobacco seeds from Portugal to Paris in the 16th century.

Chemical structure of nicotine, a pyridine alkaloid.

Pharmacology

Nicotine acts as an agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the brain and autonomic nervous system. In the brain it triggers release of dopamine in the reward pathways, along with norepinephrine, serotonin, and other neurotransmitters, producing stimulant effects at low doses and reinforcing repeated use. The body metabolises nicotine mainly in the liver (via CYP2A6) to cotinine, which is used as a biomarker of tobacco exposure. Its addictive liability is comparable to that of other major drugs of dependence, particularly when delivered rapidly to the brain by cigarette smoke.

Uses

Beyond recreational use in cigarettes, cigars, smokeless tobacco, and e-cigarettes, nicotine is used medically in nicotine replacement therapy — patches, gum, lozenges, sprays, and inhalers — to help people stop smoking, delivering nicotine without the carcinogens and carbon monoxide of smoke. Historically, nicotine was widely used as an agricultural insecticide, a use largely discontinued because of its toxicity to mammals and pollinators.

Health effects

Nicotine itself is not classified as a carcinogen, and most of the disease burden of smoking — lung cancer, COPD, and cardiovascular disease — is caused by tar, combustion products, and other constituents of tobacco smoke. Nevertheless, nicotine poses risks: it raises heart rate and blood pressure, is harmful to adolescent brain development, and exposure during pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes. Acute nicotine poisoning (from concentrated liquids or green tobacco handling) causes nausea, vomiting, and in severe cases seizures and respiratory failure.

History

Nicotine was isolated from tobacco leaves in 1828 by Wilhelm Heinrich Posselt and Karl Ludwig Reimann. Its empirical formula was described by Louis Melsens in 1843, its structure determined by Adolf Pinner in 1893, and it was first synthesised in 1904 by Amé Pictet and A. Rotschy.
