English Electric Lightning

The English Electric Lightning is a British fighter aircraft that served as an interceptor during the 1960s, the 1970s and into the late 1980s. It is the only Mach 2 fighter that was entirely designed and manufactured in the United Kingdom. The Lightning was designed, developed, and manufactured by English Electric. The company was later absorbed into the newly formed British Aircraft Corporation in 1960, and therefore the aircraft is sometimes known as the BAC Lightning.

Development

The Lightning grew out of the experimental P.1 research aircraft built to specification ER.103. Its most distinctive feature was the vertically staggered arrangement of its two Rolls-Royce Avon turbojet engines, which minimised frontal area and drag while providing a very high thrust-to-weight ratio. The P.1A first flew on 4 August 1954, and the definitive P.1B prototype exceeded Mach 2 in November 1957. The production Lightning F.1 entered RAF service with No. 74 Squadron in 1960.

Design

The Lightning combined a highly swept, notched delta-like wing of 60 degrees sweep with a low-mounted tailplane. Its stacked engine configuration gave exceptional climb performance: the Lightning could climb to 36,000 ft (11,000 m) in under three minutes, and its rate of climb was frequently compared to that of a manned rocket. Armament consisted of two 30 mm ADEN cannon and two Firestreak or Red Top air-to-air missiles, later supplemented by overwing fuel tanks and a ventral weapons pack. The type suffered from short range and limited development potential, issues addressed in part by the enlarged ventral tank of the F.6 variant.

Operational history

The Lightning formed the backbone of RAF air defence of the United Kingdom throughout the Cold War, performing Quick Reaction Alert interceptions of Soviet reconnaissance aircraft over the North Sea. Export versions served with the Royal Saudi Air Force, which briefly operated them in the ground-attack role over Yemen, and with the Kuwait Air Force. The last RAF Lightnings were withdrawn in April 1988, ending the type's front-line career. Several aircraft survive in museums, and a small number were flown by civilian operators in South Africa until an accident grounded the fleet.

Variants

Production marks included the F.1 and F.1A, the improved F.2 and F.2A, the radar- and missile-updated F.3, and the long-range F.6. Two-seat trainer variants were designated T.4 and T.5, while export models for Saudi Arabia and Kuwait were the F.52, F.53 and T.54/55/56 series.
