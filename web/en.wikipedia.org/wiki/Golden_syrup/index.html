Golden syrup

Golden syrup, or light treacle, is a thick, amber-coloured form of inverted sugar syrup made by refining sugar cane or sugar beet juice into sugar, or by treating a sugar solution with acid. It has a rich, buttery flavour and is used in baking and desserts, notably in treacle tart, flapjacks, steamed puddings and Anzac biscuits.

History

Golden syrup was developed in the 1880s by the Scottish businessman Abram Lyle, whose firm refined sugar in Plaistow, London. Lyle found a marketable use for a by-product of the refining process and sold it cheaply in wooden casks as a sweetener for the poor. In 1885 it was first marketed in the now-famous green and gold tins bearing the image of a lion and the motto from the Book of Judges, "Out of the strong came forth sweetness" — a reference to Samson and the lion. The design, among the world's oldest unchanged brand packaging, was recognised by Guinness World Records.

The firm of Abram Lyle & Sons later merged with its rival Henry Tate & Sons to form Tate & Lyle in 1921.

Production and properties

Golden syrup consists of roughly equal parts glucose and fructose produced by inverting sucrose, together with some unconverted sucrose. It is about 7–8 on a scale where water is 0 in viscosity terms comparable to honey, does not crystallise readily, and retains moisture in baked goods. A home-made substitute can be prepared by boiling sugar, water and a small amount of lemon juice or citric acid until amber.

Use

Golden syrup is a pantry staple in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa. It is poured over porridge and pancakes, used in gingerbread and treacle tart, and forms the binding syrup of oat flapjacks. In Australia and New Zealand it is a key ingredient of Anzac biscuits, associated with soldiers of the First World War.
