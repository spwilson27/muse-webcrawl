The Cassegrain reflector is a combination of a primary concave mirror and a secondary convex mirror, often used in optical telescopes and radio antennas. The design is attributed to Laurent Cassegrain, a French priest and teacher, who published it in 1672, shortly after the rival Gregorian design of James Gregory and Isaac Newton's reflecting telescope.

Design

In the classical Cassegrain, a paraboloidal primary mirror collects light and reflects it toward a hyperboloidal convex secondary mirror placed before the primary's focus. The secondary reflects the converging beam back through a central hole in the primary mirror, forming an image behind the primary at the Cassegrain focus. This folded path produces a long effective focal length in a short tube, and places heavy instruments conveniently at the rear of the telescope.

Variants

Variants include the Ritchey–Chrétien design, in which both mirrors are hyperboloidal to eliminate coma — used in most modern professional telescopes, including the Hubble Space Telescope — and catadioptric derivatives such as the Schmidt–Cassegrain and Maksutov–Cassegrain, which add a corrector lens and are extremely popular with amateur astronomers. A Nasmyth focus uses a third, flat mirror to divert the beam sideways. Cassegrain geometry is also used in radio dishes and satellite communications antennas.

Advantages

Advantages include compactness, an accessible focal plane, and aberration correction comparable to or better than a classical Newtonian of equal focal ratio, at the cost of a larger central obstruction and more difficult mirror fabrication.
