Golden age of Jewish culture in Spain

The golden age of Jewish culture in Spain refers to a period during Muslim rule in Iberia (roughly the 9th to 12th centuries) when Jewish religious, secular, and philosophical culture flourished to an extraordinary degree. Jews served as courtiers, physicians, diplomats, and administrators, and produced poetry, philosophy, and science that shaped Jewish life for centuries.

Background

After the Muslim conquest of Visigothic Spain in 711, conditions for Jews improved markedly compared with the persecutions of the Visigothic kings. Under the Umayyad Caliphate of Córdoba, the courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut (c. 915–970) — physician and unofficial vizier to Abd ar-Rahman III — patronised scholars and is often taken as the inaugurating figure of the golden age.

Achievements

Jewish poets writing in Hebrew adapted Arabic metres and genres, producing the great age of medieval Hebrew poetry: Samuel ibn Naghrillah (Shmuel ha-Nagid), Solomon ibn Gabirol, Moses ibn Ezra, and Judah Halevi, whose Kuzari and poems to Zion remain classics. Ibn Gabirol's Neoplatonic Fountain of Life influenced Christian scholasticism. Grammarians such as Jonah ibn Janah systematised Hebrew linguistics, and scholars translated and transmitted Arabic science and philosophy.

Decline

The golden age ended with the Almohad invasions from 1147, which brought forced conversions and persecution; the family of Maimonides fled Córdoba, and Jewish cultural leadership shifted to Christian Spain and Provence. The era has remained a symbol of Jewish cultural achievement in a tolerant society, though modern historians debate how far the convivencia was harmonious in everyday practice.
