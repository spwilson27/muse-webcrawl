The Song of Roland

The Song of Roland (French: La Chanson de Roland) is an 11th-century epic poem (chanson de geste) based on Roland and the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, during the reign of Charlemagne. It is the oldest surviving major work of French literature and the earliest and most celebrated of the chansons de geste. The poem exists in several manuscript versions; the oldest, the Oxford manuscript, contains about 4,000 lines.

Synopsis

Charlemagne's army has fought the Muslim Saracens in Spain for seven years. The Saracen king Marsile offers terms; Roland's stepfather Ganelon, sent as envoy, betrays the Franks out of hatred for Roland, who had nominated him for the dangerous mission. The Saracens ambush the rearguard at Roncevaux, commanded by Roland and his companion Oliver. Roland refuses to blow his horn, the olifant, to summon help until too late; the rearguard is annihilated, Roland dies facing the enemy, and Charlemagne avenges him, defeats Marsile's allies, and has Ganelon executed for treason.

Historical basis

The historical Roland, prefect of the Breton March, was killed in 778 when Basques ambushed Charlemagne's rearguard in the Pyrenees — the poem transforms the attackers into Saracens and magnifies the episode into a cosmic struggle between Christianity and Islam reflective of the Crusading era.
