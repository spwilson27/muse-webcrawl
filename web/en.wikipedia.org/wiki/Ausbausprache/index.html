In sociolinguistics, an Ausbausprache (German: "language by development") is a variety that counts as a separate language chiefly because it has been deliberately elaborated and standardised — through grammars, dictionaries, literature, and official use — rather than because it is structurally very different from neighbouring varieties. The concept belongs to the Abstand–Ausbau framework developed by the German linguist Heinz Kloss, first in 1952 and more fully in 1967.

The framework

Kloss distinguished two independent criteria for languagehood. An Abstandsprache ("language by distance") is distinct by virtue of its intrinsic linguistic distance from all others — Basque, for example. An Ausbausprache, by contrast, may be mutually intelligible with its neighbours but has been "built out" (ausgebaut) into an autonomous standard. Many European standard languages are Ausbau languages relative to one another: Norwegian, Danish, and Swedish; or Czech and Slovak; or the standards that emerged from the South Slavic dialect continuum, such as Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian, and Montenegrin.

Examples and dynamics

Hindi and Urdu are classic Ausbau languages built on the same Hindustani colloquial base but elaborated with different scripts (Devanagari vs. Perso-Arabic) and different high-register vocabularies (Sanskrit vs. Persian-Arabic). Conversely, varieties can lose Ausbau status: Scots and Low German were once fully developed standards but were overshadowed by English and High German. Ausbau processes continue today in corpus planning, script reforms, and the codification of new national standards.
