Breslov

Breslov (also Bratslav) is a Hasidic movement founded by Rebbe Nachman of Breslov (1772–1810), a great-grandson of the Baal Shem Tov, founder of Hasidic Judaism. The movement takes its name from the Ukrainian town of Bratslav (Breslov), where Nachman spent the last years of his life, and his grave in nearby Uman has become a major pilgrimage site. Uniquely among Hasidic dynasties, Breslov has had no living rebbe since Nachman's death in 1810; followers study his teachings, recorded chiefly by his disciple Nathan of Breslov (Noson Shternhartz).

Teachings

Rebbe Nachman's teachings, collected in Likutey Moharan, stress joyful worship, personal prayer in one's own words (hitbodedut, ideally practised daily, traditionally in nature), simplicity, and perseverance against despair. His famous maxim holds that "it is a great mitzvah to be always joyful", and his saying "Do not despair" (often rendered "Gevalt, yidden, zayt nit meyaesh") has become the movement's signature. His tales (Sipurei Ma'asiot), including "The Seven Beggars" and "The Lost Princess", encode Kabbalistic ideas in folk-story form.

History

After Nachman's death, his disciple Nathan of Breslov systematized, published, and spread the teachings, effectively leading the small, often-persecuted group known derisively as the "dead Hasidim" for having no living rebbe. Communities persisted in Ukraine and Poland and were largely destroyed in the Holocaust. In the later 20th century Breslov grew markedly in Israel and the diaspora, drawing both hereditary Hasidim and many baalei teshuva (newly religious Jews).

Uman pilgrimage

Rebbe Nachman asked his followers to gather with him for Rosh Hashanah; the annual pilgrimage (the Rosh Hashanah kibbutz) to his grave in Uman draws tens of thousands of mostly male pilgrims each year, continuing even under Soviet religious repression and, remarkably, through the disruptions of the 2020s.
