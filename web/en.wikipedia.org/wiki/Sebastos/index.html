Sebastos

Byzantine courtiers of sebastos rank

Sebastos (Greek: σεβαστός, "venerable one", plural σεβαστοί, sebastoi) was an honorific title used in the ancient Greek world to translate the Latin imperial title Augustus, and later a senior court title in the Byzantine Empire. Its feminine form was sebastē (σεβαστή).

History

The word derives from the verb sebazomai, "to revere". From the 1st century BC onward, Greek speakers used Sebastos as the standard rendering of Augustus, the title borne by Roman emperors, and the term appears throughout the Greek East for the emperor and members of the imperial family.

In the late 11th century, under Emperor Alexios I Komnenos, sebastos was transformed into the basis of a new hierarchy of court dignities reserved largely for the imperial family and its closest relatives. Alexios created a family-based aristocracy by combining sebastos with other titles to form grades such as sebastokrator (created for his brother Isaac), panhypersebastos, sebastohypertatos, protosebastos and megalepiphanestatos sebastos. Close relatives of the emperor through marriage, the gambroi, were typically granted sebastos rank.

Later use

As the Komnenian system expanded, the title proliferated and gradually declined in prestige; by the Palaiologan period sebastos had become a relatively modest court rank, also granted to provincial officials and even found in the administration of districts (where a sebastos might head a fiscal or military subdivision). The related title sebastokrator remained one of the empire's highest dignities, second only to despot and emperor in later centuries.

Legacy

The name Sebastiani and place names such as Sebastopol (Sevastopol, "city of the sebastos/emperor") preserve the term. The Byzantine use of sebastos influenced neighbouring states, including Serbia, where sevast became an administrative title, and the Crusader states of the Levant.
