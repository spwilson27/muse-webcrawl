Waqf

A waqf (Arabic: وَقْف; plural awqāf), also known as a hubous or mortmain property, is an inalienable charitable endowment under Islamic law. It typically involves donating a building, plot of land, or other assets for Muslim religious or charitable purposes with no intention of reclaiming the assets. The donated assets are held in perpetual trust and administered by a trustee (mutawalli).

Many historic mosques, madrasas and fountains were maintained by waqf endowments.

Terminology and origins

The term waqf literally means "confinement and prohibition" — the property is detained, and its usufruct is devoted to the beneficiaries designated by the founder. Classical jurists traced the institution to the Prophet Muhammad, who advised Umar ibn al-Khattab to retain the corpus of land he acquired at Khaybar and give its produce in charity. One of the earliest documented waqfs was a mosque and its surrounding lands in Medina.

Legal characteristics

Under most schools of Islamic law, once property is declared waqf it is irrevocable, perpetual and inalienable: it cannot be sold, inherited or gifted. The founder (waqif) stipulates the purpose in a deed (waqfiyya). Disputes over interpretation historically fell to the qadi. A family waqf (waqf ahli) could support the founder's descendants, with the remainder passing to charity when the line died out, while a charitable waqf (waqf khayri) served the public from the start.

Social role

Waqfs funded much of the public infrastructure of pre-modern Muslim societies: mosques, madrasas, hospitals, caravanserais, public kitchens (imarets), fountains, bridges and libraries. The complex of Süleymaniye in Istanbul and the endowments of Mamluk Cairo are famous examples. Because they were legally autonomous, awqaf also sheltered wealth from rulers and taxation, which sometimes drew criticism.

Modern administration

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries many states — the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, and later republics — created ministries of awqaf that centralised administration, and colonial regimes in India and North Africa regulated or confiscated endowments. Today waqf law remains significant in countries from Turkey and Malaysia to India, where the Waqf Act governs hundreds of thousands of properties, and new cash waqfs are promoted as instruments of Islamic social finance.
