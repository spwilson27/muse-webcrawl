Indian python (Python molurus)

The Indian python (Python molurus) is a large nonvenomous python species native to tropical and subtropical regions of the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia. Typically 3 metres (about 10 feet) or more in length, it is lighter in color than its close relative the Burmese python, with irregular dark blotches outlined in black along its back and sides.

Habitat and behaviour

Indian pythons inhabit grasslands, marshes, rocky foothills, woodlands, and river valleys, usually near a permanent water source. They are excellent swimmers and climbers, though largely terrestrial as adults. Like all pythons, they kill prey — mammals, birds, and reptiles — by constriction. Females lay clutches of up to about 100 eggs and coil around them, using muscular contractions to regulate incubation temperature. The species is mainly nocturnal and generally sluggish, relying on camouflage.

Conservation

The Indian python is listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List, owing to hunting for its skin and meat, capture for the pet trade, and habitat loss. It is protected under Indian law and listed in CITES Appendix I, which prohibits commercial international trade. It is the state reptile of several Indian states and is familiar in Indian culture, including as the species of Kaa in Kipling's The Jungle Book.
