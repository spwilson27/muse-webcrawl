Machiavellianism (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In personality psychology, Machiavellianism is a personality trait centred on interpersonal manipulation, a cynical disregard for morality, and a focus on self-interest and deception. It is named after the Italian diplomat and philosopher Niccolò Machiavelli, whose political writings — particularly The Prince (1532) — were interpreted as advocating cunning and duplicity in statecraft. Along with narcissism and subclinical psychopathy, Machiavellianism forms the "Dark Triad" of personality traits.

Assessment and research

The trait is usually measured with the MACH-IV inventory, developed by Richard Christie and Florence Geis in the 1970s, which scores agreement with statements expressing cynical views of human nature and endorsement of manipulative tactics. People scoring high ("high Machs") tend to be more adept at lying, less affectively empathic, and more oriented toward strategic, long-term manipulation than psychopathy's impulsive style. Research has linked high Machiavellianism to workplace counterproductive behaviour, though also sometimes to success in negotiation and sales contexts. Twin studies suggest moderate heritability, with substantial environmental influence.
