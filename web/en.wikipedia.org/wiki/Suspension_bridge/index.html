A suspension bridge is a type of Bridge in which the deck is hung below suspension cables on vertical suspenders. The main cables, draped between two (or more) towers in a curve approximating a parabola, carry the load in tension to anchorages at either end, while the towers carry the load in compression to the foundations. Because the structure works almost entirely in tension and compression, suspension bridges can span far greater distances than any other bridge type.

History

Early suspension bridges were rope and chain footbridges in Asia and South America. The first iron-chain suspension bridges appeared in the early 19th century, including Thomas Telford's Menai Suspension Bridge (1826). John A. Roebling pioneered the use of spun wire cables, culminating in the Brooklyn Bridge (1883). The modern era of very long spans began with the George Washington Bridge (1931) and the Golden Gate Bridge (1937).

Structure

The principal elements are the main cables, towers (pylons), suspenders (hangers), the stiffening deck or truss, and the anchorages. Aerodynamic stability of the deck became a central design concern after the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940, which twisted itself apart in wind and led to the routine use of wind-tunnel testing and streamlined box-girder decks.

Longest spans

The longest suspension bridge main span in the world is that of the 1915 Çanakkale Bridge in Turkey (2,023 m, opened 2022), followed by the Akashi Kaikyō Bridge in Japan (1,991 m, 1998). Suspension bridges remain the standard solution for spans above roughly 1,000 metres.
