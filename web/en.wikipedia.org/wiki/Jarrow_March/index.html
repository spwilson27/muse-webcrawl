Jarrow March

Date5–31 October 1936
RouteJarrow, County Durham, to London (about 280 miles)
Participants200 selected marchers
OrganiserJarrow Borough Council; MP Ellen Wilkinson prominent
CauseMass unemployment after closure of Palmer's shipyard

The Jarrow March, also known as the Jarrow Crusade, was an organised protest by unemployed men from the town of Jarrow in north-east England, who marched to London in October 1936 to petition Parliament for work. It is the best known of the hunger marches of the interwar years and has become a symbol of the social cost of the Great Depression in Britain.

Background

Jarrow's economy depended on Palmer's shipyard and steelworks. After the yard's closure in 1933 — influenced by rationalisation in the shipbuilding industry — unemployment in the town reached around 70 percent, and conditions were described in Ellen Wilkinson's book The Town That Was Murdered (1939). Wilkinson, the town's Labour MP, and the borough council organised a disciplined, non-political march deliberately distinct from the communist-led National Unemployed Workers' Movement marches.

The march

Two hundred men, medically screened and chosen from many volunteers, set out on 5 October 1936 carrying a petition bearing some 11,000 signatures, an oak chest in which to present it, and a banner reading "Jarrow Crusade". They marched about 280 miles in stages through Ripon, Harrogate, Leeds, Sheffield, Nottingham, Leicester and other towns, receiving food and lodging of variable generosity, and holding public meetings en route. The marchers arrived in London on 31 October.

Outcome and legacy

The petition was presented to the House of Commons, but Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin declined to meet the delegation and no immediate relief followed; the marchers returned home by train with money raised by supporters. Improvement came only later, partly through rearmament and a new steelworks and ship-repair activity on the eve of war. The march's importance is now chiefly symbolic: it is commemorated in Jarrow by memorials and a permanent display, and is frequently invoked in British political debate about deindustrialisation and regional inequality. In 2011 and 2016, commemorative marches retraced the route.
