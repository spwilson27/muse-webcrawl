Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy

Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) is a fatal complication of epilepsy. It is defined as the sudden, unexpected death of a person with epilepsy, with or without evidence that a seizure occurred, in which post-mortem examination reveals no other cause of death. SUDEP is the leading cause of epilepsy-related mortality and accounts for a substantial fraction of deaths in people with chronic, drug-resistant epilepsy.

Epidemiology

The incidence is roughly 1 per 1,000 person-years among adults with epilepsy overall, rising to as high as 5–9 per 1,000 person-years in people with refractory epilepsy and frequent convulsive seizures. The single strongest risk factor is the occurrence of generalised tonic–clonic seizures, especially nocturnal ones and those occurring when the person is unsupervised. Poor adherence to anti-seizure medication and frequent changes of medication also increase risk.

Mechanisms

The precise mechanism is not fully established. Monitored cases captured in epilepsy units — the MORTEMUS study of 2013 — showed a consistent sequence: a generalised tonic–clonic seizure, followed by postictal generalised EEG suppression, central apnoea (cessation of breathing), and terminal asystole minutes later. Proposed contributors include seizure-driven respiratory arrest, cardiac arrhythmia, autonomic dysfunction and impaired arousal, with serotoninergic and adenosinergic pathways implicated in animal models.

Prevention

No proven preventive treatment exists. Management focuses on maximising seizure control — effective medication, epilepsy surgery where appropriate — and on supervision and nocturnal monitoring for those at highest risk. Clinical guidelines recommend that clinicians discuss SUDEP with patients and families so that risk can be weighed in treatment decisions.
