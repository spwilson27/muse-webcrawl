Transformative works

Transformative works
ExamplesFan fiction, fan art, fan vids, remixes, parody
Legal doctrineFair use (US) — transformativeness
Key organisationOrganization for Transformative Works (OTW)

A transformative work is a creative work that takes existing material — such as the characters, settings or text of a copyrighted work — and transforms it, adding new expression, meaning or message. The term is used both in fan culture, where it covers fan fiction, fan art, fan videos, podcasts and similar productions, and in copyright law, where "transformativeness" is central to the US fair use doctrine.

In copyright law

The concept gained prominence through Judge Pierre Leval's 1990 article "Toward a Fair Use Standard" and the US Supreme Court's decision in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994), which held that a parody's transformative character weighed in favour of fair use even when commercial. Transformativeness has since become a key, though contested, factor in fair use analysis; the Supreme Court revisited its scope in Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023), emphasising that a new meaning alone does not settle the first fair-use factor when the secondary work shares the original's commercial purpose.

Fan culture

Fan communities have produced transformative works for over a century, from Sherlock Holmes pastiches to Star Trek fanzines and today's online archives. The non-profit Organization for Transformative Works (founded 2007) advocates for the legitimacy of fan works and runs the archive Archive of Our Own (AO3), which won the Hugo Award for Best Related Work in 2019.
