Reforestation is the natural or intentional restocking of forests and woodlands that have been depleted, usually through deforestation, logging, fire, or other disturbance. It differs from afforestation, the establishment of forest on land that was not forested in the recent past. Reforestation may proceed by natural regeneration from seed sources and stumps, by assisted regeneration, or by deliberate planting of seedlings — in commercial forestry typically after clear-cutting, and in ecological restoration with diverse native species.

Benefits

Growing forests absorb carbon dioxide and store carbon in biomass and soils, making reforestation a prominent climate-mitigation strategy; restored forests also rebuild habitat and biodiversity, stabilise soils against erosion, regulate water flow and quality, and can supply timber, fuelwood, and non-timber products to local communities. Large-scale pledges include the Bonn Challenge, launched in 2011, which aimed to restore 350 million hectares of degraded land by 2030, and China's Grain for Green programme and shelterbelt ("Great Green Wall") plantings, among the largest state reforestation efforts in history.

Methods and challenges

Outcomes vary widely with method. Single-species plantations grow fast and are easy to manage but support far less biodiversity than natural forest and can be vulnerable to pests, fire, and drought; ecologists increasingly favour mixed native plantings and protection of natural regrowth, which is often cheaper and more resilient. Reforestation can fail where the causes of loss — grazing pressure, insecure land tenure, competing agricultural demand — are unaddressed, and planting trees in naturally grassy ecosystems such as savannas can harm rather than help biodiversity. Reforestation is also criticised when used to justify continued emissions or to offset fossil-fuel use without permanent protection of the restored forest.
