Cul-de-sac

A cul-de-sac (from French, literally "bottom of the bag") is a street or road with only one inlet and outlet, terminating in a turnaround area that lets vehicles reverse direction. Synonyms include dead end (common in North America, though "dead end" can also denote any street without an exit, even without a bulb), no through road, and court in some naming conventions. The classic post-war suburban cul-de-sac ends in a circular bulb on which houses front.

History and planning

Dead-end streets existed in ancient settlements and medieval towns, but the cul-de-sac as a deliberate planning device came to prominence with the garden city movement and early automobile suburbs. It was heavily promoted in the United States after the Federal Housing Administration's 1936 guidelines favored curvilinear streets and cul-de-sacs, and it became a defining feature of post-war subdivisions, valued for low through-traffic, quiet, and perceived safety for children.

Criticism and revival

From the late twentieth century, New Urbanist planners criticized networks dominated by cul-de-sacs for poor connectivity: they lengthen walking and cycling trips, concentrate traffic on arterials, hinder emergency access and transit, and are associated with car dependence. Empirical studies link high cul-de-sac density to lower walking rates and higher vehicle miles traveled. Modified designs — cul-de-sacs with pedestrian and bicycle cut-throughs ("fused grid" layouts) — attempt to retain their traffic-calming benefits while restoring permeability for non-motorized travel.
