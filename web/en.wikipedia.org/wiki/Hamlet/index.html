The Tragedy of Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, usually shortened to Hamlet, is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare sometime between 1599 and 1601. It is Shakespeare's longest play, with about 30,000 words in some editions, and is considered among the most powerful and influential works of world literature. Set in Denmark, the play depicts Prince Hamlet's attempts to take revenge on his uncle Claudius, who has murdered Hamlet's father in order to seize the throne and marry Hamlet's mother Gertrude.

Plot

The ghost of the old King appears to Hamlet and demands revenge. Feigning madness, Hamlet stages a play re-enacting the murder to test Claudius's guilt — "the play's the thing wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king". He berates his mother and, mistaking a hidden listener for Claudius, kills the counsellor Polonius, whose daughter Ophelia, Hamlet's beloved, goes mad and drowns. Claudius plots Hamlet's death in a rigged fencing match with Polonius's son Laertes, using a poisoned sword and a poisoned cup. In the final scene the cup kills Gertrude, Laertes and Hamlet wound each other with the poisoned blade, and Hamlet at last kills Claudius before dying himself, leaving the Norwegian prince Fortinbras to take the throne.

Famous lines and themes

The play contains some of the most quoted lines in English: "To be, or not to be, that is the question", "To thine own self be true", "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark", "The rest is silence", and Yorick's skull — "Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio". Its themes of revenge, madness (real and feigned), mortality, corruption and procrastination have generated four centuries of criticism, from Goethe and Coleridge to Freudian readings of Hamlet's delay.

Sources and texts

Shakespeare drew on the legend of Amleth in Saxo Grammaticus and on an earlier lost play, the so-called Ur-Hamlet. Three early texts survive — the "bad quarto" of 1603, the second quarto of 1604–05, and the First Folio version of 1623 — differing in length and wording, so modern editions are composites. Hamlet has been played by the greatest actors of every era since Richard Burbage, and the play has inspired countless adaptations, from Akira Kurosawa's The Bad Sleep Well to The Lion King's distant echo of its plot.
