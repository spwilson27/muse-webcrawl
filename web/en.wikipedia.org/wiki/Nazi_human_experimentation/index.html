Nazi human experimentation

Nazi human experimentation was a series of medical experiments on large numbers of prisoners, including children, by Nazi Germany in its concentration camps during the Second World War and the Holocaust. Prisoners — Jews, Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, and others — were coerced into experiments that typically resulted in death, trauma, disfigurement, or permanent disability. The programme is regarded as among the most notorious examples of medical atrocity and torture in history.

Types of experiments

At Dachau, Luftwaffe and SS doctors conducted high-altitude experiments in low-pressure chambers and freezing experiments that placed prisoners in ice water or naked in the cold to test rewarming methods for downed pilots; many subjects died. At Ravensbrück, sulfonamide experiments inflicted wounds deliberately infected with bacteria, glass, and wood to test the drug's efficacy, and bone, muscle, and nerve regeneration and transplantation experiments were performed, mainly on Polish women prisoners known as the "Rabbits". At Auschwitz, Josef Mengele performed experiments on twins and people with dwarfism and other conditions; Carl Clauberg and Horst Schumann pursued mass sterilisation methods by injection, irradiation, and surgery. Other programmes included malaria, typhus, poison, incendiary-bomb, seawater-potability, and epidemic jaundice experiments at camps including Sachsenhausen, Buchenwald, and Natzweiler.

Aftermath

After the war, 23 leading physicians and officials were tried in the Doctors' Trial at Nuremberg (1946–47); seven were hanged, including Karl Brandt, Hitler's personal physician. The proceedings produced the Nuremberg Code (1947), the foundational statement of research ethics requiring voluntary informed consent. The scientific value of the experiments' data — most notoriously the Dachau hypothermia data — has been debated for decades; ethicists generally hold the data to be methodologically unreliable and morally tainted.
