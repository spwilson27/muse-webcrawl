The Demak Sultanate was a Javanese Muslim state located on Java's north coast in Indonesia, at the site of the present-day city of Demak. A port fief to the Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit kingdom thought to have been founded in the last quarter of the 15th century, it was influenced by Islam brought by Arab and Gujarati traders. The sultanate played an important role in the establishment of Islam in Indonesia and became the dominant power on the north coast of Java — the Pasisir — in the early 16th century, before fragmenting in succession disputes after the death of its third ruler.

Formation

According to tradition, Demak was founded by Raden Patah, a son of a Majapahit king, who established a mosque and a settlement at a swampy port known as Bintoro. The Great Mosque of Demak, built in the late 15th century with the help of the Wali Sanga (the nine saints credited with spreading Islam in Java), remains one of the oldest mosques in Indonesia and a symbol of the sultanate.

Expansion

Under Raden Patah and his successors, notably the military leader Fatahillah and Sultan Trenggana, Demak expanded along Java's north coast, subordinating ports such as Jepara, Tuban and Gresik, and campaigning against the remaining Hindu polities and against Portuguese interests in the region. Demak's fleets attacked Portuguese Malacca in 1512–1513, though without success. Demak influence also reached Banjarmasin and other parts of the archipelago's trade network.

Decline

Trenggana's death in 1546 triggered a violent succession struggle. Power eventually passed to Jaka Tingkir, who moved the court inland to Pajang, effectively ending Demak as the seat of power; the sultanate is generally considered to have ended by 1568, with Pajang and later Mataram inheriting its role in Javanese history.
