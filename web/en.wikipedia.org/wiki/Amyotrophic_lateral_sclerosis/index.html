Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as motor neurone disease (MND) in some countries and Lou Gehrig's disease in the United States, is a neurodegenerative disease that results in the progressive loss of motor neurons controlling voluntary muscles. As the neurons die, patients gradually lose the ability to walk, speak, swallow, and eventually breathe, while sensation and usually cognition remain intact.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms typically include muscle weakness in a hand, arm, foot, or leg, muscle cramps and twitching (fasciculations), and stiffness (spasticity). About a quarter of patients present with bulbar symptoms — slurred speech and difficulty swallowing. The disease spreads to involve most voluntary muscles. Death usually results from respiratory failure. Roughly half of those affected develop mild cognitive or behavioural changes, and 10–15% develop frontotemporal dementia. The disease does not affect the senses, bladder and bowel control, or (usually) eye movements.

Causes and genetics

The cause is unknown in roughly 90% of cases, which are termed sporadic. About 10% are familial. More than 20 genes have been associated with ALS; the most common causes of familial ALS are a hexanucleotide repeat expansion in the C9orf72 gene and mutations in SOD1 (superoxide dismutase 1), the first ALS gene identified, in 1993. Proposed mechanisms include glutamate excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, protein aggregation (notably of TDP-43, found in the neurons of nearly all patients), and neuroinflammation.

Diagnosis

There is no single test for ALS. Diagnosis is clinical, based on progressive upper and lower motor neuron signs, and is supported by electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies. The El Escorial and Awaji criteria are used to grade diagnostic certainty. Other conditions — such as cervical spondylotic myelopathy, multifocal motor neuropathy, and Kennedy's disease — must be excluded.

Management

No treatment cures or reverses ALS. The drug riluzole modestly prolongs survival, by about two to three months on average. Edaravone may slow functional decline in some patients, and the antisense therapy tofersen is approved for SOD1-related ALS. Multidisciplinary care — including non-invasive ventilation, nutritional support (sometimes via gastrostomy), speech and physical therapy — improves quality of life and survival.

Epidemiology and history

ALS affects roughly 2 per 100,000 people per year, with peak onset between ages 55 and 75; men are affected slightly more often than women. The French neurologist Jean-Martin Charcot characterised the disease in the 1870s. It is named in the US for the baseball player Lou Gehrig, who was diagnosed in 1939. The physicist Stephen Hawking, diagnosed at 21, lived with ALS for more than 50 years — an unusually slow progression. In 2014 the "Ice Bucket Challenge" viral campaign raised over $100 million for ALS research.
