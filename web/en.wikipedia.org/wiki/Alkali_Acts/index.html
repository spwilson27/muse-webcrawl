The Alkali Acts were a series of British statutes, beginning with the Alkali Act 1863, regulating air pollution from the chemical industry. They are regarded as among the first modern pieces of environmental legislation and created the world's first national pollution inspectorate, the Alkali Inspectorate.

Background

The Leblanc process for making soda ash — essential for soap, glass, and textiles — released vast quantities of hydrogen chloride gas, which devastated vegetation, corroded buildings, and harmed health around the alkali works of Tyneside, Glasgow, and south Lancashire. Landowners brought successful private actions, including the landmark case St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping (1865), and a House of Lords select committee investigated the nuisance in 1862.

The 1863 Act and the Inspectorate

The Alkali Act 1863 required Leblanc works to condense at least 95 per cent of their hydrogen chloride emissions. It appointed a small professional inspectorate — headed by the chemist Robert Angus Smith, often called the father of acid rain research — to monitor compliance. Follow-on legislation, notably the Alkali, &c. Works Regulation Acts of 1881 and 1906, extended control to other gases and industries and introduced the standard of the "best practicable means" of preventing emissions.

Legacy

The Inspectorate's cooperative, technical approach became a template for British environmental regulation. Its functions eventually passed to successor bodies, and the Alkali Acts were consolidated and finally replaced by the Environmental Protection Act 1990.
