Wave interference

In physics, interference is a phenomenon in which two coherent waves are combined by adding their intensities or displacements with due consideration for their phase difference. The resulting wave may have greater intensity (constructive interference) or lower amplitude (destructive interference) if the two waves are in phase or out of phase, respectively. Interference effects can be observed with all types of waves, for example light, radio, acoustic and surface water waves.

Mechanism

Interference follows from the superposition principle: the displacement at any point is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves. Where crest meets crest the waves reinforce; where crest meets trough they cancel. For a stable pattern the sources must be coherent — maintaining a constant phase relationship — and usually of the same frequency.

Light interference

Thomas Young's double-slit experiment (c. 1801) demonstrated interference of light and established its wave nature, producing alternating bright and dark fringes whose spacing depends on wavelength, slit separation and screen distance. Thin films such as soap bubbles and oil slicks show iridescent colours because light reflected from the two surfaces interferes, some wavelengths cancelling and others reinforcing. Interferometers exploit the effect for exquisitely precise measurements, from Michelson and Morley's ether experiment to LIGO's detection of gravitational waves.

Applications

Interference underlies holography, anti-reflective coatings, radio antenna arrays and noise-cancelling headphones, which generate an inverted copy of ambient sound to cancel it destructively. In quantum mechanics, interference of probability amplitudes — seen when single electrons build up a double-slit pattern — lies at the heart of wave–particle duality.
