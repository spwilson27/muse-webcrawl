Combinatorial game theory

Combinatorial game theory (CGT) is a branch of mathematics and theoretical computer science that studies sequential games with perfect information — typically two-player games in which players take turns changing the position according to defined rules, with no chance elements or hidden information. Despite the name, it differs from the game theory of economics, which focuses on strategic interactions and games of imperfect information. CGT grew out of the analysis of impartial games such as Nim, via the Sprague–Grundy theorem, and was greatly expanded by John Horton Conway, Elwyn Berlekamp, and Richard Guy, whose book Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays (1982) systematized the subject.

Core concepts

A combinatorial game is described recursively by the sets of positions its two players (conventionally Left and Right) can move to, written G = {GL | GR}. Games can be added together (the disjunctive sum), negated, and compared, giving the class of games an algebraic structure. Their outcome under perfect play falls into four classes: first-player win, second-player win, Left win regardless of who starts, or Right win regardless of who starts.

Impartial games, where both players have the same moves, reduce via the Sprague–Grundy theorem to equivalent Nim heaps, with the nim-value (or Grundy number) computed by the "mex" rule. Partizan games, where the players' moves differ, give rise to Conway's surreal numbers: numbers themselves can be represented as games, and quantities distinctive to CGT — such as temperature, values that are "fuzzy" with zero, and infinitesimal games like up, down, and star — measure the incentive to move.

Games studied

CGT has been applied to analyze games including Nim, Treblecross, Hackenbush, Domineering, Snort, Dawson's Kayles, and the endgames of Go, where its methods of thermography and cooling are used to evaluate local fights. The theory connects to complexity theory as well: determining the winner of generalized positions in many such games is PSPACE-complete.
