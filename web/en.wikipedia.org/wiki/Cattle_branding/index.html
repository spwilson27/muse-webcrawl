Livestock branding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (redirected from Cattle branding)

[Hero image placeholder: a branding iron being applied on a ranch]

Livestock branding is a technique for marking livestock so as to identify the owner. Originally, it referred to burning a mark into the hide of large animals with a hot branding iron; the term now also encompasses ear marking, tattooing, and freeze branding. Branding has been practised since ancient times — tomb paintings of the Egyptians show cattle being branded around 2700 BC — and proved especially important on the open ranges of the Americas and Australia, where herds of many owners grazed together.

Fire branding

In traditional hot-iron branding, an iron bearing the owner's registered mark is heated until red or grey hot and pressed to the animal's hide, producing a permanent scar in the shape of the brand. Brands use a distinctive vocabulary of symbols and positions ("bar", "circle", "lazy", "rocking") that allows them to be read aloud and registered with state or provincial brand boards — a legal record central to prosecuting cattle rustling.

Freeze branding

Freeze branding uses irons chilled in liquid nitrogen or a dry-ice and alcohol mixture. Applied briefly, the cold destroys the pigment-producing cells of the hair follicles, so the hair regrows white; a longer application kills the follicle entirely, leaving a bald mark. Freeze brands are considered less painful and are readily visible on dark-coated animals.

Modern alternatives

Ear tags, electronic RFID implants, and DNA profiling have supplemented or replaced branding in many jurisdictions, and some countries restrict hot-iron branding on animal-welfare grounds. Nevertheless, registered brands remain legally significant in much of the American West.
