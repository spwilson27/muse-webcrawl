Speakers' Corner

Speakers' Corner is an area in the north-eastern corner of Hyde Park in London, near Marble Arch, where open-air public speaking, debate and discussion are allowed. Speakers stand on ladders, crates or small platforms — popularly, on a soapbox — and address whoever gathers, with heckling an accepted part of the tradition. Although Hyde Park is the best known, the term "speakers' corner" now describes similar designated free-speech areas in other cities and countries.

History

The site lies close to the former Tyburn gallows, where the condemned were traditionally allowed final speeches. Mass demonstrations in Hyde Park in 1855 and 1866, including the Reform League's protests, pressed for the right to assemble and speak; the Parks Regulation Act 1872 formalised the right of public speech in the park. Historic speakers have included Karl Marx, Vladimir Lenin, George Orwell, William Morris, and campaigners for women's suffrage and Irish independence.

Tradition

Anyone may speak on any subject, subject to the law on matters such as incitement and obscenity; police intervene only sparingly. Religious preachers (Christian, Muslim and others), political campaigners, conspiracy theorists and eccentrics draw crowds, and the exchanges between speaker and hecklers are considered the essence of the institution. Speakers' Corners have since been established in other British towns and abroad, including in Singapore, where the Hong Lim Park corner requires registration.

In popular culture

The corner symbolises British traditions of free expression and tolerance of dissent, and is a frequent tourist attraction on Sunday mornings. The image of standing on a soapbox to preach to passers-by derives from such informal public speaking.
