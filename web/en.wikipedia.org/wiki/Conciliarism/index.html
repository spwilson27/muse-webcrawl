Conciliarism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Conciliarism was a reform movement within the Catholic Church in the 14th, 15th, and 16th centuries which held that supreme authority in the Church resided with an ecumenical council, apart from, or even against, the pope. It arose in response to the Western Schism (1378–1417), when rival claimants to the papal throne divided Latin Christendom.

Background

Conciliar theory had roots in medieval canon law, in the writings of thinkers such as Marsilius of Padua (whose Defensor pacis of 1324 located authority in the whole body of believers) and William of Ockham, and in the practice of corporations and universities, whose members could correct or depose a head who betrayed their trust. The schism made these ideas urgent: when two, and later three, men claimed to be pope, a general council appeared to be the only body able to judge between them.

The conciliar epoch

The Council of Pisa (1409) attempted to end the schism by deposing both popes but only added a third claimant. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) succeeded: it secured the resignation or deposition of the rival popes and elected Martin V. Its decree Haec sancta (1415) declared that a general council, legitimately assembled, has its power directly from Christ and that everyone, including the pope, must obey it in matters of faith, the ending of schism, and reform. The decree Frequens (1417) required that councils be held regularly.

The Council of Basel (1431–1449) pressed conciliar claims further, quarrelling with Pope Eugene IV over reform and reunion with the Greek Church; its rump eventually elected an antipope, Felix V, discrediting the movement, and the council dissolved in 1449.

Decline and legacy

Pope Pius II's bull Execrabilis (1460) condemned appeals from a pope to a future council, and the restored papal monarchy of the Renaissance largely suppressed conciliarism as a practical programme. Yet conciliar ideas survived in Gallicanism in France and in early modern constitutional thought; historians have traced lines of influence from conciliar theorists such as Nicholas of Cusa and Jean Gerson to later theories of representative government and constitutionalism. Debates over the relative authority of pope and council resurfaced at the First Vatican Council (1870), which defined papal infallibility, and again in the collegiality discussed at the Second Vatican Council.
