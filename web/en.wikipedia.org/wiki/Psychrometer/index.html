A psychrometer is an instrument used in meteorology and in industrial and scientific applications to measure the humidity of air. It consists of two thermometers, one with a dry bulb and one with a bulb kept moist with distilled water on a wick. The moistened thermometer is known as the wet-bulb thermometer, and the other as the dry-bulb thermometer.

Evaporation from the wet bulb lowers its temperature, so the wet-bulb thermometer usually shows a lower reading than the dry-bulb thermometer. When the air temperature is below the freezing point of water, the wet bulb is covered by a thin coating of ice, which may be more accurate than a wet bulb operated under the same conditions. The temperature difference between the two bulbs, called the wet-bulb depression, is used with psychrometric tables or equations to compute the relative humidity and the dew point of the air.

Principle of operation

The rate of evaporation from the wet bulb depends on how close the air is to saturation. In saturated air no evaporation occurs and the two thermometers read the same temperature. In drier air evaporation is faster, the wet bulb is cooled further, and the depression is larger. The quantitative relationship between the depression, the air temperature, and the vapour pressure is described by the psychrometric equation, which involves the psychrometric constant (about 0.66 °C−1 at sea-level pressure for a ventilated psychrometer).

Types

Sling psychrometer

In a sling psychrometer the two thermometers are mounted on a frame with a handle and chain by which they can be whirled through the air. Ventilation ensures that the wet bulb reaches its equilibrium temperature quickly. The operator whirls the instrument for about a minute, reads the wet bulb, and repeats the process until successive readings agree.

Aspirated psychrometer

The Assmann aspirated psychrometer draws air over the bulbs with a spring-driven or electric fan, shielding the thermometers from radiation with polished metal tubes. It gives more reproducible results and was long the standard instrument for humidity measurement at meteorological stations.

Modern instruments

Electronic hygrometers have largely replaced psychrometers for routine work, but the wet-and-dry-bulb psychrometer remains a reference instrument and is still used in greenhouses, industrial drying, and air-conditioning practice.
