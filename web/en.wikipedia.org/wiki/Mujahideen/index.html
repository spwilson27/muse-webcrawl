Mujahideen (Arabic: المجاهدين, mujāhidīn) is the plural of mujahid, one who engages in jihad — in its broadest sense, striving in the way of God — and in modern usage usually denotes Muslim guerrilla fighters. The term gained worldwide currency during the Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989).

Afghanistan

After the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, a loose alliance of Afghan resistance parties — based largely in Peshawar, Pakistan, and commonly grouped as the "Peshawar Seven" — fought a decade-long insurgency. They received extensive covert funding and arms from the United States (notably through Operation Cyclone, run with Pakistan's ISI), Saudi Arabia and others; foreign volunteers, later known as "Afghan Arabs," also joined. The mujahideen's guerrilla war in Afghanistan's mountains raised the cost of occupation until the Soviet withdrawal in February 1989.

Victory did not bring unity: the mujahideen parties took Kabul in 1992 but fell into a destructive civil war among commanders such as Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, Ahmad Shah Massoud and Burhanuddin Rabbani. The chaos of this period enabled the rise of the Taliban, who captured Kabul in 1996; former mujahideen factions then formed the core of the Northern Alliance that, with US support, overthrew the Taliban in 2001.

Other uses

The label has been applied to Muslim fighters in many conflicts, including Bosnian foreign volunteers (El Mudžahid) in the Bosnian War, Kashmiri militant groups, and movements such as the Moro fighters of the southern Philippines in earlier centuries. Organisations have also adopted the word in their names, from Iran's People's Mujahedin (MEK) to various South Asian groups, with meanings that vary widely by context.
