Paolo Caliari (1528 – 19 April 1588), known as Paolo Veronese, was an Italian Renaissance painter based in Venice, famed for extremely large history paintings of religion and mythology, including The Wedding at Cana (1563) and The Feast in the House of Levi (1573). With Titian and Tintoretto he forms the great trio dominating Venetian painting of the late Renaissance.

Life and career

Born in Verona — hence his nickname "Veronese" — he was the son of a stonecutter and trained in the workshop of Antonio Badile. After early work in Verona and Mantua, he settled in Venice in the early 1550s. Commissions for the Doge's Palace, the church of San Sebastiano (which he covered with frescoes over many years), and the library of San Marco established his reputation. Around 1560 he decorated the Villa Barbaro at Maser, built by Andrea Palladio, with illusionistic frescoes that remain among his most admired works.

Major works

The Wedding at Cana, painted for the refectory of San Giorgio Maggiore and now in the Louvre, is an enormous banquet scene crowded with contemporary portraits set in classical architecture. The Feast in the House of Levi, intended as a Last Supper for a Dominican refectory, drew the attention of the Inquisition in 1573 over its inclusion of comic and secular figures; Veronese defended artistic licence but retitled the picture rather than repaint it, and the transcript of his testimony survives as a famous document of Renaissance art theory.

His late masterpiece, the ceiling cycle of the Doge's Palace including The Apotheosis of Venice, celebrates the Venetian state in luminous, silvery colour. Veronese died in Venice in 1588 and was buried in San Sebastiano.

Style and influence

Veronese is celebrated for his luminous palette, mastery of perspective and architectural setting, and pageant-like compositions that present sacred subjects as contemporary Venetian spectacle. His work influenced later decorative painting across Europe, from Rubens to Tiepolo and the French Rococo.
