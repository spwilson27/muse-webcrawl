Positive law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Positive law (Latin: ius positivum) is the law explicitly established by human authority — enacted by legislatures, decreed by sovereigns, or recognised through official practice — as opposed to natural law, which is held to derive from nature, reason or divine order. The category is medieval (Aquinas's human law as determinations of natural law) but became programmatic with legal positivism: Austin defined positive law as commands of the sovereign, and later positivists (Hart, Kelsen, Raz) refined the social-sources account — law is what the system's own validity criteria certify, whatever its moral quality.

Significance

The distinction organises perennial conflicts: whether unjust statutes bind in conscience (the civil-disobedience literature), whether judges may refuse to apply wicked law (Radbruch versus Hart in the post-Nazi informer cases), and how international lawyers treat norms that are morally compelling but not yet positive law (emerging customary norms, jus cogens peremptory norms occupy the borderline). In ordinary legal usage the term is descriptive, not polemical: the "positive law" of a jurisdiction is simply the law in force.
