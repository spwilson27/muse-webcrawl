Borophaginae
Extinct subfamily of canids
Range: Oligocene to Pliocene, North America
Common name: bone-crushing dogs

The Borophaginae are an extinct subfamily of canids (dogs) that lived in North America from the Oligocene to the Pliocene, roughly 36 to 2.5 million years ago. Often called "bone-crushing dogs", many of the later, larger members evolved powerful jaws and robust premolars adapted for crushing bone, occupying an ecological niche similar to that of modern hyenas.

Evolution

Borophagines arose from early hesperocyonine canids and diversified into dozens of genera. Early forms were small and fox-like; over time some lineages became large hypercarnivores. The last and best-known genus is Borophagus, whose species persisted into the early Pleistocene before the subfamily disappeared, possibly under competition from other carnivorans and changing climates.

Feeding ecology

The enlarged premolars and short, deep skulls of derived borophagines produced very high bite forces. Isotopic and wear studies suggest they consumed entire carcasses including bone, much like spotted hyenas, though some smaller genera were more omnivorous or insectivorous.

Classification

The subfamily includes genera such as Phlaocyon, Cynarctus, Aelurodon, Epicyon (among the largest canids ever), and Borophagus. Epicyon haydeni could exceed the size of a large wolf.
