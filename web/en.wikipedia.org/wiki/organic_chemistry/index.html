Organic chemistry

FieldChemistry
Central elementCarbon
Key topicsHydrocarbons, functional groups, synthesis, mechanisms

Organic chemistry is a subdiscipline within chemistry involving the scientific study of the structure, properties, and reactions of organic compounds and organic materials, that is, matter in its various forms that contain carbon atoms.

History

The field was once defined as the chemistry of compounds from living organisms, under the theory of vitalism. Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate undermined that doctrine. Structural theory developed in the 19th century through Kekulé's work on benzene and the tetrahedral carbon of van 't Hoff and Le Bel.

Characterization

Organic compounds range from simple gases such as methane to huge macromolecules like proteins and DNA. Carbon's ability to form stable chains (catenation) and bonds to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, halogens, and other elements underlies this diversity.

Reactions and synthesis

Organic reactions are classified by mechanism — substitution, addition, elimination, rearrangement — and organic synthesis builds target molecules from simpler precursors, a discipline recognized by numerous Nobel Prizes, including those for Grignard reagents, the Diels–Alder reaction, and palladium-catalyzed cross-couplings.

Applications

Organic chemistry underpins pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, polymers, dyes, food chemistry, and biochemistry.
