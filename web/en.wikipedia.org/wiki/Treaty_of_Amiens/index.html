Treaty of Amiens

Signed: 25 March 1802

Location: Amiens, France

Parties: United Kingdom; French Republic, Spain and the Batavian Republic

Context: French Revolutionary Wars

The Treaty of Amiens, signed on 25 March 1802, temporarily ended hostilities between France and the United Kingdom during the French Revolutionary Wars. Together with the Treaty of Lunéville (1801), it brought about the only general peace in Europe between 1793 and 1814. Signing for Britain was Lord Cornwallis; for France, Joseph Bonaparte.

Terms

Britain agreed to return most of the French, Spanish and Dutch colonies it had captured during the war, retaining only Trinidad (from Spain) and Ceylon (from the Dutch). France undertook to withdraw its troops from Naples and the Papal States. Malta was to be restored to the Order of St John and its neutrality guaranteed — the clause whose non-execution became the central dispute of the peace. Egypt was restored to the Ottoman Empire, the Ionian Islands' independence was acknowledged, and the House of Orange was to be compensated for its losses in the Netherlands.

Breakdown

The peace lasted barely a year. Britain, alarmed by Napoleon's continued interventions in Italy, Switzerland and Germany and his colonial ambitions, refused to evacuate Malta; France protested and Napoleon publicly berated the British ambassador, Lord Whitworth. Britain declared war on 18 May 1803, beginning the Napoleonic Wars. The brief peace had, however, allowed a famous interlude of cross-Channel travel, with British tourists flocking to Paris.
