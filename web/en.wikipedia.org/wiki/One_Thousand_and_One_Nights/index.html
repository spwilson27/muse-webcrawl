One Thousand and One Nights (Arabic: أَلْفُ لَيْلَةٍ وَلَيْلَةٌ, Alf layla wa-layla) is a collection of Middle Eastern folk tales compiled in Arabic during the Islamic Golden Age. It is often known in English as the Arabian Nights, from the first English-language edition (c. 1706–1721), which rendered the title as The Arabian Nights' Entertainments.

Frame story

The collection is held together by a frame tale: the Persian king Shahryar, betrayed by his first wife, resolves to marry a new bride each day and execute her the next morning. The vizier's daughter, Scheherazade, volunteers to marry him and saves herself by telling him a story each night, breaking off at a cliffhanger at dawn so that the king spares her to hear the ending. After a thousand and one nights, the king abandons his cruel resolve.

Origins and composition

The tales derive from Persian, Arabic, Indian, and Egyptian folklore and literature, layered over centuries. The Persian prototype, the Hazār Afsān ("A Thousand Tales"), is lost but described by medieval bibliographers. The collection has no single author or definitive text; the earliest substantial Arabic manuscript is the Syrian recension used by Antoine Galland.

Famous stories

Some of the best-known tales — including Aladdin's Wonderful Lamp, Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves, and the voyages of Sinbad the Sailor — do not appear in the oldest Arabic manuscripts and were added by Galland from stories told to him, in part by the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab. These "orphan tales" have since become inseparable from the collection in the popular imagination.

Influence

The Nights has had an enormous influence on world literature and culture, shaping Western perceptions of the Middle East and inspiring writers from Chaucer and Shakespeare to Borges and Rushdie, as well as countless stage, film, and pantomime adaptations.
