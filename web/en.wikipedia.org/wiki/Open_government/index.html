Open government

Open government: transparency, participation and accountability in public institutions.

Open government is a doctrine and political movement holding that citizens have the right to access the documents and proceedings of their government, and more broadly that government should be transparent, participatory and collaborative. In its contemporary form it is closely associated with freedom-of-information laws, open data portals and digital civic technology.

Components

  • Transparency — proactive publication of budgets, contracts, laws and decision records; freedom of information (FOI) legislation, beginning with Sweden's 1766 Freedom of the Press Act
  • Participation — mechanisms for public comment, petitioning and co-creation of policy
  • Accountability — open data used by journalists, watchdogs and courts to scrutinise power

Modern movement

In 2009 the United States issued an Open Government Directive and launched Data.gov; in 2011 the multilateral Open Government Partnership (OGP) was founded by eight governments, growing to dozens of member countries whose national action plans commit them to reforms reviewed by independent reporters. Civil-society organisations such as the Sunlight Foundation and mySociety built tools (FOI request sites, spending visualisations, parliamentary monitors) that operationalise openness.

Criticism

Critics note that disclosure alone does not guarantee accountability: data can be published in unusable forms, transparency can be a substitute for substantive reform, and security, privacy and commercial confidentiality place genuine limits on openness. Scholars also distinguish open government (about state institutions) from open data (a technical property of datasets).
