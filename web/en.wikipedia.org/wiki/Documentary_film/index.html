Documentary film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A documentary film crew shooting an interview in natural light in a subject's home]
A documentary film crew shooting an interview in natural light in a subject's home

A documentary film is a non-fictional motion picture intended to document reality, primarily for instruction, education or maintaining a historical record. The term — coined by John Grierson reviewing Moana (1926), and defined by him as 'the creative treatment of actuality' — covers forms from the observational cinema of Cinéma vérité to essay films, nature documentaries and the archival compilations of the streaming age.

History

Early actuality films gave way to Robert J. Flaherty's Nanook of the North (1922), a staged-yet-truthful portrait of Inuit life that set documentary's founding ethical dilemma. The 1930s brought state-sponsored classics — Grierson's British movement (Night Mail, 1936), Leni Riefenstahl's Nazi propaganda Triumph of the Will (1935), whose artistry remains inseparable from its poison — and wartime reportage. Post-war television made documentary a mass form.

Styles and movements

The 1960s' lightweight cameras enabled Direct Cinema (D. A. Pennebaker, the Maysles brothers) and Jean Rouch's cinéma vérité; expository 'voice of God' narration (nature films, the BBC's landmark series with David Attenborough) coexisted with participatory, reflexive and performative modes (Bill Nichols's taxonomy). Claude Lanzmann's Shoah (1985) — nine hours of testimony without archive footage — stands as the form's moral summit.

Contemporary documentary

Festival and streaming economics made documentary a prestige genre: Oscar winners from Bowling for Columbine (2002) to Free Solo (2018), investigative series and celebrity profiles flood platforms, while deepfakes and true-crime ethics strain the audience's trust in 'actuality'.
