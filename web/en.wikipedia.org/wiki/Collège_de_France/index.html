The Collège de France is a higher education and research establishment in Paris, France, located near the Sorbonne in the Latin Quarter. Founded in 1530 by King Francis I as the Collège Royal, it was created to teach subjects — Hebrew, Greek and mathematics — that the University of Paris then neglected. It grants no degrees; instead, its professors hold chairs and deliver annual courses of lectures that are free and open to the public, presenting their latest research.

Organisation

The Collège has around fifty chairs spanning the natural sciences, humanities and social sciences. Chairs are created, renamed or left vacant by vote of the assembly of professors as fields evolve, which allows the institution to respond to new disciplines. Professors are elected by their peers and are expected to teach "knowledge in the making" rather than established doctrine. Lectures are also recorded and published online.

Notable professors

Distinguished holders of chairs have included the philosopher Henri Bergson, the physicist Pierre-Gilles de Gennes, the historian of ideas Michel Foucault, the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu and the anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss. Nobel laureates have frequently taught there, and the institution remains one of the most prestigious in French intellectual life.
