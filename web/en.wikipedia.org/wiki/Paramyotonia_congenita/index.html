Paramyotonia congenita (also called von Eulenburg disease) is a rare hereditary disorder of skeletal muscle ion channels characterised by myotonia — delayed relaxation of muscle after contraction — that is characteristically triggered by cold exposure and, paradoxically, worsens with repeated exercise rather than improving. It is one of the non-dystrophic myotonias, a group distinct from myotonic dystrophy.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are usually present from infancy or early childhood and persist throughout life without progression. Cooling of the face, eyelids, tongue and hands provokes stiffness; a classic sign is inability to open the eyelids quickly after screwing them shut in cold weather. Continued activity in the cold aggravates the stiffness ("paramyotonia") and may be followed by flaccid weakness lasting minutes to hours. Some patients also experience episodes of weakness resembling those of hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis, and the two conditions are now regarded as closely related allelic disorders.

Genetics and pathophysiology

The disorder is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and is caused by missense mutations in SCN4A on chromosome 17q23, which encodes Nav1.4, the voltage-gated sodium channel of skeletal muscle. The mutations impair fast inactivation of the channel, so muscle fibres fire repetitively (myotonia) or become inexcitable (weakness), with cold further slowing channel kinetics. Albert von Eulenburg described the condition in 1886 in a family whose members were famed for their stiffness in winter.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical pattern, electromyography (showing myotonic discharges that change with cooling and exercise testing, including the short exercise test) and genetic testing for SCN4A variants. There is no cure; management emphasises avoiding cold exposure and treating troublesome attacks. Sodium-channel-blocking drugs such as mexiletine, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide, can reduce myotonia and weakness. Potassium intake may aggravate symptoms and anaesthesia requires precautions because of the shared physiology with related channelopathies.
