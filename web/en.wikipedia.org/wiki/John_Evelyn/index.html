John Evelyn (31 October 1620 – 27 February 1706) was an English writer, landowner and gardener. He is chiefly remembered for the diary he kept from 1640 to 1706, a valuable record of the art, culture and politics of seventeenth-century England, published posthumously in 1818 alongside the diary of his contemporary Samuel Pepys.

Life

Evelyn was born at Wotton House in Surrey into a family prospering from gunpowder manufacture. Educated at Oxford and the Middle Temple, he travelled in France and Italy during the Civil War years, then returned to England and settled at Sayes Court, Deptford, which he made famous for its gardens. A royalist, he nevertheless served the Commonwealth and Restoration governments in various administrative posts and was a founding fellow of the Royal Society (1660).

Works

Evelyn's Sylva, or A Discourse of Forest-Trees (1664), commissioned by the Royal Society amid concern over timber supplies for the navy, urged landowners to plant trees and went through many editions. His other works include Fumifugium (1661), a pioneering tract on London's air pollution proposing the relocation of noxious trades and the planting of green belts; Sculptura (1662), on engraving; and Kalendarium Hortense, a gardener's almanac.

The Diary

Evelyn's diary spans over sixty years and covers the execution of Charles I, the Great Plague, the Great Fire of London — for the rebuilding of which he submitted a plan — and the Glorious Revolution. Less intimate and more formal than Pepys's, it remains a primary source for the period.
