The Great Peace of Montreal (French: La Grande paix de Montréal) was a peace treaty between New France and 39 First Nations of North America signed on 4 August 1701 in Montreal. It ended the Beaver Wars — the long series of conflicts between the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and the French and their Indigenous allies — and established a durable settlement that held, in broad outline, until the British conquest of New France in 1760.

Background and negotiation

Decades of war, disease, and the near-destruction of Huronia had exhausted all parties. Years of preliminary diplomacy, involving the Onondaga and other go-betweens and Huron-Wendat chief Kondiaronk (whose death during the final council, and state funeral in Montreal, became part of the treaty's legend), culminated in the summer of 1701, when some 1,300 Indigenous delegates gathered at Montreal. Governor Louis-Hector de Callière presided; the terms were ratified through speeches, wampum exchange, and the sharing of the calumet, and the written document was signed with pictographic clan marks by the delegates of the nations.

Terms and legacy

The treaty provided for the return of prisoners, free passage and trade, common hunting grounds in the Great Lakes country (the "dish with one spoon"), and Haudenosaunee neutrality in future wars between France and England — a diplomatic posture of great consequence in the French and Indian War era. It is regarded as a landmark of Indigenous diplomacy: negotiations were conducted substantially within Indigenous diplomatic forms, and the peace enabled France's alliance system and the fur trade of the pays d'en haut for two generations. The tercentenary in 2001 was commemorated in Montreal, and the original treaty document is held by Library and Archives Canada.
