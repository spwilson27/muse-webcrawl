Diaphragm (anatomy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diaphragm (anatomy)
Type: Skeletal muscle
Function: Principal muscle of respiration
Innervation: Phrenic nerve (C3–C5)

The thoracic diaphragm is a sheet of skeletal muscle that separates the thoracic cavity (heart and lungs) from the abdominal cavity. Dome-shaped at rest, it is the principal muscle of breathing in mammals.

When the diaphragm contracts, it flattens, increasing the volume of the chest and drawing air into the lungs; when it relaxes, elastic recoil expels air. It is pierced by openings for the oesophagus, the aorta and the inferior vena cava, and is supplied by the phrenic nerves — injury to which can paralyse half the diaphragm. Its rhythmic contractions, when thrown into spasm by irritation, produce hiccups.

Beyond breathing, the diaphragm assists in raising abdominal pressure for urination, defecation, vomiting and childbirth, and its crura anchor it to the lumbar spine. Singers, wind players and freedivers train it deliberately; in most of us it works unnoticed some twenty thousand times a day, in concert with the homeostatic control of blood gases.
