Air taxi

An air taxi is a small commercial aircraft that makes short flights on demand. Air taxis operate on an unscheduled, on-demand basis, carrying small numbers of passengers between airports — frequently small general-aviation airfields that lack scheduled airline service — at times chosen by the customer. In the United States, air taxi operations are conducted under Part 135 of the Federal Aviation Regulations, which also covers commuter and on-demand charter operations.

Air taxis use small aircraft to link airports without scheduled service.

Operations

Air taxi services range from piston singles and twins such as the Cessna 402 and Piper Navajo to turboprops like the Pilatus PC-12 and King Air, and small jets. Because many thousands of small airfields exist in countries such as the United States, air taxis can bring passengers far closer to their final destination than scheduled airlines using large hub airports. The model is used heavily in remote regions — the Canadian and Alaskan bush, the Scottish Highlands and islands, and the outback of Australia — where it may be an essential service rather than a luxury.

Per-seat and very light jet concepts

The early 2000s saw attempts to build scheduled, per-seat air taxi networks around very light jets (VLJs) such as the Eclipse 500, most prominently the Florida-based operator DayJet, which launched in 2007 but ceased operations in 2008 during the financial crisis. Per-seat charter and membership services, in which customers book individual seats on small aircraft flown between secondary airports, have periodically revived the concept.

Electric and urban air mobility

Since the late 2010s the term "air taxi" has increasingly been applied to proposed electric vertical take-off and landing (eVTOL) aircraft intended for urban air mobility — short, piloted or autonomous flights within and between cities, launched from vertiports. Companies including Joby Aviation, Archer Aviation, Lilium, and Vertical Aerospace have developed multi-rotor and lift-plus-cruise designs, and regulators in the United States, Europe, and Asia have been developing certification frameworks for them.
