The CFM International LEAP ("Leading Edge Aviation Propulsion") is a high-bypass turbofan engine produced by CFM International, a 50–50 joint venture between American GE Aerospace and French Safran Aircraft Engines. It is the successor to the CFM56, the best-selling engine family in commercial aviation history, and competes with the Pratt & Whitney PW1000G in the market for narrow-body airliners.

Development

CFM launched the LEAP programme in 2008. The first engine ran in 2013, and the LEAP-1A entered service on the Airbus A320neo in August 2016 with Pegasus Airlines. The LEAP-1B powers all variants of the Boeing 737 MAX, entering service in 2017, while the LEAP-1C powers the Chinese Comac C919, which entered commercial service in 2023.

Design

The LEAP incorporates technologies aimed at reducing fuel burn by around 15% compared with earlier CFM56 models: an 18-blade woven carbon-fibre composite fan, a high pressure ratio core, a twin-annular pre-swirl (TAPS II) combustor, and ceramic matrix composite (CMC) components in the hot section. It was also among the first production engines to use additively manufactured (3D-printed) fuel nozzles.

Variants

  • LEAP-1A — Airbus A320neo family, thrust roughly 24,500–35,000 lbf depending on sub-variant.
  • LEAP-1B — Boeing 737 MAX, with a smaller fan (69.4 in) to fit the 737's ground clearance, thrust roughly 23,000–29,300 lbf.
  • LEAP-1C — Comac C919, thrust about 30,000 lbf.

Durability issues

In service, the LEAP fleet has faced durability challenges, particularly in hot and dusty environments, where erosion and degradation of hot-section coatings have shortened time on wing compared with the CFM56. CFM has introduced durability improvement packages, including a redesigned high-pressure turbine blade introduced in the mid-2020s.
