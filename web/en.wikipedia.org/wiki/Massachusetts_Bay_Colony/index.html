Massachusetts Bay Colony

The Massachusetts Bay Colony (1628–1691) was an English settlement on the east coast of North America around Massachusetts Bay, centred on the towns of Salem and Boston. Established by Puritan settlers under a charter granted to the Massachusetts Bay Company in 1629, it grew into the most populous and economically powerful of the New England colonies and played a central role in the early history of the United States.

History

Founding and the Great Migration

The Massachusetts Bay Company received a royal charter in 1629. Unlike earlier ventures, the company's charter was transferred to New England itself, giving the Puritan colonists an unusual degree of self-government. In 1630 a fleet led by John Winthrop carried about 1,000 settlers to the bay in what became known as the Great Migration; over the following decade some 20,000 English Puritans emigrated to New England. Winthrop famously described the colony as a "city upon a hill" in his lay sermon A Model of Christian Charity.

Government and religion

The colony was governed by a governor, deputy governor, assistants and a General Court of freemen. Church membership was effectively required for full political participation, and the colony enforced strict religious conformity, banishing dissenters such as Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson, whose departures led to the founding of Rhode Island. Harvard College was founded in 1636 to train ministers.

Conflicts and expansion

The colony fought the Pequot War (1636–1638) and King Philip's War (1675–1676), the latter devastating to both colonists and Native peoples. Massachusetts also absorbed neighbouring settlements, and its merchants built a thriving trade in fish, timber and shipping.

Loss of charter and province

King Charles II revoked the colony's charter in 1684. After the short-lived Dominion of New England (1686–1689), a new charter in 1691 created the Province of Massachusetts Bay, with a royally appointed governor and religious toleration for Protestants, ending the old Puritan commonwealth.

Legacy

The colony's traditions of town meetings, congregational churches and public education (the 1647 "Old Deluder Satan" law required towns to establish schools) left a lasting imprint on New England and American political culture.
