The Army of Flanders (Spanish: Ejército de Flandes) was a multinational field army raised by the Spanish Monarchy to defend its territories in the Low Countries and to fight the rebellious northern provinces of the Netherlands. Created in 1567 when the Duke of Alba marched some 10,000 Spanish veterans from Italy along the Spanish Road, it fought throughout the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) and the later Franco-Spanish conflicts, and for much of its existence was regarded as one of the finest — and most feared — armies in Europe.

Composition

Despite its name and Spanish high command, the army was genuinely multinational. Its elite infantry core was the Spanish tercios, but Walloons, Germans, Italians, Burgundians, English, Irish and Scots also served in large "nations" (naciones). Cavalry included Spanish, Italian and Walloon horse; artillery and engineering were often Italian-led. Pay arrived irregularly down the Spanish Road, and arrears provoked a string of mutinies — famously the sack of Antwerp in 1576, the "Spanish Fury".

Operations

Under commanders including the Duke of Alba, Alexander Farnese (who retook much of the southern Netherlands in the 1580s), Archduke Albert, the Cardinal-Infante Ferdinand and Ambrogio Spinola, the army besieged and stormed dozens of fortified towns in the war of sieges that characterised the Dutch Revolt. Its victories included Gembloux (1578) and the capture of Breda (1625), immortalised by Velázquez; its great defeats included Nieuwpoort (1600), Rocroi (1643) — which shattered the myth of tercio invincibility — and the Dunes (1658).

Decline and disbandment

After the Peace of Münster (1648) recognised Dutch independence, the army shrank and fought mainly against France in the Spanish Netherlands. Chronic underfunding eroded it through the later 17th century. During the War of the Spanish Succession the Army of Flanders passed out of existence as the Spanish Netherlands changed hands, and it was formally wound up by 1706.

Legacy

Historians such as Geoffrey Parker have used the Army of Flanders as a case study in the logistics, finance and discipline of early modern warfare, drawing on its exceptionally rich surviving administrative records.
