In complex analysis, the Casorati–Weierstrass theorem, named after Felice Casorati and Karl Weierstrass, describes the remarkable behaviour of holomorphic functions near essential singularities.

Statement

If a function f is holomorphic on a punctured neighbourhood of a point z0, and z0 is an essential singularity of f, then the image of that punctured neighbourhood under f is dense in the complex plane ℂ. In other words, in every neighbourhood of an essential singularity, the function takes values arbitrarily close to every complex number.

Context and proof idea

The theorem follows from the classification of isolated singularities: at a removable singularity the function stays bounded and extends holomorphically; at a pole it tends to infinity. The essential case is the negation of both, and the proof proceeds by contradiction — if the image omitted a neighbourhood of some value, constructing a suitable auxiliary function would produce either a removable singularity or a pole.

Picard's theorem

The result is substantially deepened by the great Picard theorem, which states that near an essential singularity a holomorphic function actually takes every complex value infinitely often, with the possible exception of one value. Francesco Severi is sometimes also credited, giving the alternative name Casorati–Weierstrass–Severi theorem.
