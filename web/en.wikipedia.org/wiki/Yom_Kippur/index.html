Yom Kippur (יוֹם כִּפּוּר, "Day of Atonement") is the holiest day in Judaism. Falling on the 10th day of Tishrei, it concludes the Ten Days of Repentance that begin with Rosh Hashanah, and is devoted to atonement and repentance through a roughly 25-hour fast and intensive synagogue prayer. Even many Jews who rarely attend synagogue observe Yom Kippur.

Biblical origins

The Torah (Leviticus 16) prescribes the day's Temple ritual: the High Priest entered the Holy of Holies — the only day of the year anyone did so — offered sacrifices, and sent a scapegoat bearing the people's sins into the wilderness to Azazel. Leviticus 23 ordains self-affliction and complete rest on the day.

Observance

Traditional observance includes five prohibitions: eating and drinking, washing, applying lotions or oils, wearing leather shoes, and marital relations. Many wear white, symbolising purity. The liturgy begins before sunset with the haunting Kol Nidre declaration, annulling vows, and continues through five prayer services — Maariv, Shacharit, Musaf (recalling the Temple service and the martyrology), Mincha (including the Book of Jonah) and Ne'ila, the "closing of the gates", ending at nightfall with a single long shofar blast and the first meal after the fast. Confession (Vidui) is repeated communally throughout; tradition holds that Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, while wrongs against other people must first be put right with them.

Significance

The day is understood as the culmination of the annual judgment: on Rosh Hashanah the decree is written, on Yom Kippur it is sealed. In Israel the day is a legal holiday on which traffic virtually ceases and streets fill with pedestrians and cyclists. The surprise attack that began the Yom Kippur War of 1973 took its name from the date.
