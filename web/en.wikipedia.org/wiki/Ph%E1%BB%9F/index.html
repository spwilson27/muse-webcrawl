Phở is a Vietnamese soup dish consisting of broth, flat rice noodles (bánh phở), herbs and meat — usually beef (phở bò) or chicken (phở gà). Widely considered Vietnam's national dish, it is eaten at any time of day, often for breakfast, from street stalls and restaurants alike.

History

Phở developed in northern Vietnam in the early 20th century, around Nam Định and Hanoi, in a context of growing beef consumption under French colonial rule and the influence of Chinese noodle soups. The broth is simmered for hours with charred onion and ginger and spices such as star anise, cloves, cinnamon and fish sauce, yielding a clear, aromatic stock. After 1954 the dish spread to the south, where cooks added bean sprouts, Thai basil, lime, hoisin and chili, producing the sweeter, garnish-rich southern style. Vietnamese communities abroad after 1975 made phở a global food.

Preparation and serving

A bowl is assembled by pouring boiling broth over blanched rice noodles and thinly sliced meat — rare beef slices cook in the broth itself; other cuts include brisket, flank, tendon and meatballs. It is served with herbs, onion, scallions and condiments to taste.
