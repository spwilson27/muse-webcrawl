Fire extinguisher

Fire extinguisher Types: water, foam, dry powder, carbon dioxide, wet chemical
Fire classes: A (solids), B (liquids), C (gases), D (metals), E/electrical, F/K (cooking oils)
Invented (modern form): 1818, George William Manby

A fire extinguisher is a handheld active fire protection device, usually filled with a dry or wet chemical, used to extinguish or control small fires, often in emergencies. It is not intended for use on an out-of-control fire, such as one which has reached the ceiling, endangers the user, or otherwise requires professional firefighting.

Types

Water extinguishers suit ordinary combustibles (Class A) but are dangerous on electrical or oil fires. Foam forms a blanket over flammable liquids. Dry powder extinguishers interrupt the chemical reaction of the fire and are versatile, though messy. Carbon dioxide displaces oxygen and leaves no residue, making it suitable for electrical equipment. Wet chemical extinguishers are designed for cooking-oil fires in commercial kitchens.

Operation

Use is commonly taught with the acronym PASS: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, and Sweep from side to side. Extinguishers require regular inspection, pressure checks, and periodic servicing under fire safety regulations.

History

Early devices date to the eighteenth century; Captain George William Manby invented a portable pressurised extinguisher in 1818. The soda-acid extinguisher followed in the late nineteenth century, and later developments added stored-pressure designs, foams, and halon replacements.
