Tantō

Type: Dagger
Place of origin: Japan
Period: Heian period – present

A tantō (短刀, "short blade") is a Japanese short blade traditionally carried by the samurai class of feudal Japan. The tantō dates to the Heian period, when it was mainly used as a weapon, and was thereafter produced in many different schools and styles. It is a companion blade to the longer sword (katana or tachi) and the wakizashi, and together with those swords forms the traditional set of samurai blades.

Description

The tantō is a single- or double-edged dagger with a blade length generally between 15 and 30 cm (about 6 to 12 inches). It was designed primarily as a stabbing instrument, although its sharp edge could also be used for slashing. Tantō are generally flat-sided without a ridged section (hira-zukuri), though other cross sections exist.

As with other Japanese blades, the tantō was forged from tamahagane steel, often with a visible hamon (temper line) created by differential hardening. Mountings (koshirae) could be highly decorative; the aikuchi style, in which the guard sits flush with the scabbard mouth so that no visible tsuba separates them, was especially popular.

History

Production of tantō peaked in the Muromachi period, when many were produced as votive offerings and gifts as well as weapons. Famous swordsmiths such as Awataguchi Yoshimitsu, Shintōgo Kunimitsu, and Masamune are renowned for their tantō. During the peace of the Edo period, tantō continued to be made, often as works of art; the blade was also associated with the practice of seppuku, in which a disembowelling cut was made with a short blade, often a tantō or wakizashi.

Cultural role

Tantō were carried by samurai and also by women of the samurai class, who sometimes carried a smaller version called a kaiken in their obi for self-defence. Today, antique tantō are collected as art objects, and modern practitioners of martial arts such as aikido and ninjutsu train with wooden or blunted practice versions.
