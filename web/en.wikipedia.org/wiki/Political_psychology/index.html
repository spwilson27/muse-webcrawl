Political psychology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Political psychology is an interdisciplinary academic field, dedicated to understanding politics, politicians and political behavior from a psychological perspective. The relationship between politics and psychology is considered bidirectional, with psychology being used as a lens for understanding politics and politics being used as a lens for understanding psychology. The field draws on social psychology, personality psychology, cognitive psychology, political science and sociology.

History

Precursors include Gustave Le Bon's crowd psychology (1895), Graham Wallas's Human Nature in Politics (1908) and Sigmund Freud's writings on group behaviour. Harold Lasswell's Psychopathology and Politics (1930) is often treated as the founding text of the modern discipline, applying psychoanalytic ideas to political leaders. After the Second World War, research on authoritarianism — Theodor W. Adorno and colleagues' The Authoritarian Personality (1950) — and on voting behaviour at Columbia and Michigan universities established the field's empirical core. The International Society of Political Psychology (ISPP) was founded in 1978, and its journal Political Psychology began publication in 1979.

Main topics

  • Political attitudes and ideology — how beliefs are structured, constrained and connected to personality traits such as openness to experience and conscientiousness.
  • Voting and participation — party identification, motivated reasoning, heuristics and the effects of campaigns and media.
  • Leadership and elite behaviour — psychobiography, operational code analysis, and decision-making in crises and small groups (including groupthink).
  • Intergroup conflict — prejudice, social identity theory, authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, realistic conflict and the psychology of genocide and terrorism.
  • Political cognition and emotion — framing, priming, hot cognition, and the roles of anxiety, anger and enthusiasm in politics.
  • Mass beliefs — public opinion, belief in conspiracy theories, misinformation and its correction.

Methods

Political psychologists use surveys and survey experiments, laboratory and field experiments, personality assessment at a distance, content analysis, and increasingly computational and neuroscientific methods ("neuropolitics"). Debates within the field concern the relative weight of elite cues versus stable predispositions, the symmetry or asymmetry of bias across the political spectrum (e.g. motivated reasoning among liberals and conservatives alike), and the generalisability of findings beyond Western, educated samples.
