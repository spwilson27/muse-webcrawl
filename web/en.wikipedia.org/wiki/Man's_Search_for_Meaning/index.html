Man's Search for Meaning is a 1946 book by Viktor Frankl chronicling his experiences as a prisoner in Nazi concentration camps during World War II, and describing his psychotherapeutic method, logotherapy, which involves identifying a purpose in life to feel positive about and immersively imagining that outcome.

Contents

The first part recounts Frankl's experiences in Theresienstadt, Auschwitz and a subcamp of Dachau, and his observations on the psychology of prisoners: apathy, the "delusion of reprieve", and the inner freedom that remained even in extreme suffering. The second part, "Logotherapy in a Nutshell", sets out his theory that the primary human motivation is the search for meaning — the "will to meaning" — in contrast to Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power.

Key ideas

Frankl argued that meaning can be found through creative work, through love and experience, and — where suffering is unavoidable — through the attitude one takes toward it. His famous dictum, adapted from Nietzsche, holds that "he who has a why to live can bear almost any how".

Reception

The book, first published in German in 1946 and in English in 1959, has sold well over ten million copies and been translated into dozens of languages. A 1991 survey for the Library of Congress named it one of the ten most influential books in the United States.
