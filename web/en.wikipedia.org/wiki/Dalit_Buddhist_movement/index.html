Dalit Buddhist movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Dalit Buddhist movement (also called Navayana Buddhism) is a revivalist and socio-political movement among Dalits — the communities formerly stigmatised as "untouchable" within the Hindu caste order — in India. It was founded by B. R. Ambedkar, the jurist, social reformer and principal architect of the Indian Constitution, who converted to Buddhism on 14 October 1956 at Deekshabhoomi in Nagpur, Maharashtra, together with an estimated 380,000 followers.

Origins

Ambedkar concluded after decades of campaigning that caste hierarchy was inseparable from Hindu scripture and that Dalits must leave Hinduism to attain dignity and equality. At Nagpur he administered to his followers the Twenty-two Vows, renouncing Hindu deities and rituals and pledging adherence to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha. His posthumously published work The Buddha and His Dhamma (1957) presented a socially engaged reinterpretation of Buddhism, emphasising liberty, equality and fraternity and de-emphasising karma and rebirth in their traditional forms — the "Navayana" or new vehicle.

Development

The movement is concentrated in Maharashtra, where the Mahar community formed its original base, and has spread to Uttar Pradesh and other states. Conversion ceremonies continue to be held, often on Ashoka Vijaya Dashami. Navayana Buddhists form the large majority of India's Buddhist population, which numbered about 8.4 million in the 2011 census. See also Dalit, Caste system in India, and Navayana.
