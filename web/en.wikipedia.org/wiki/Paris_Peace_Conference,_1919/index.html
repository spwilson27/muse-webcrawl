Paris Peace Conference, 1919

Dates18 January 1919 – 21 January 1920
LocationParis and Versailles, France
ParticipantsAllied and Associated Powers (32 states); Central Powers excluded until treaties presented
Key figuresWoodrow Wilson, David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau, Vittorio Emanuele Orlando ("Big Four")
OutcomesTreaty of Versailles and the other Paris treaties; League of Nations

The Paris Peace Conference was a set of formal and informal meetings in 1919 and 1920, following the end of the First World War, at which the victorious Allies set the peace terms for the defeated Central Powers. Dominated by the leaders of Britain, France, the United States, and Italy — the "Big Four" — the conference produced five treaties, redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, and established the League of Nations.

The "Big Four": Orlando, Lloyd George, Clemenceau, and Wilson at Versailles.

Organisation

The conference formally opened on 18 January 1919. Real decision-making quickly concentrated in the Council of Ten (heads of government and foreign ministers of the five principal powers), then in the Council of Four: Georges Clemenceau of France, David Lloyd George of Britain, Woodrow Wilson of the United States, and Vittorio Emanuele Orlando of Italy. More than 50 commissions worked on territorial, economic, and technical questions. The defeated powers were not permitted to negotiate; they were summoned to receive terms.

The treaties

  • Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) with Germany: war guilt clause (Article 231), reparations, territorial losses (Alsace-Lorraine to France, Polish Corridor, colonies), disarmament, and the Rhineland occupation.
  • Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919) with Austria; Treaty of Trianon (1920) with Hungary — dissolving Austria-Hungary.
  • Treaty of Neuilly (1919) with Bulgaria.
  • Treaty of Sèvres (1920) with the Ottoman Empire, later superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923).

Key issues and legacy

Wilson's Fourteen Points, especially national self-determination and the League of Nations, collided with French security demands, British imperial interests, and the secret treaties between the Allies. New states emerged or were reconstituted in central and eastern Europe, and Allied mandates divided Ottoman and German colonies. The conference left many grievances unresolved: the United States Senate refused to ratify the Versailles Treaty, Germany resented the "diktat," and Italy felt cheated of promised territory. Historians continue to debate the conference's role in the instability that led to the Second World War; the economist John Maynard Keynes, a Treasury delegate, famously criticised the reparations settlement in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919).
