Building information modeling (BIM) is a process involving the generation and management of digital representations of the physical and functional characteristics of places — buildings and other built assets. BIM models are shared knowledge resources that support decision-making across an asset's whole life cycle, from early design through construction to operation and demolition.

A BIM model is not merely 3D geometry: its objects carry data — materials, dimensions, costs, manufacturers, maintenance information. Extensions of the model are sometimes described as 4D (adding construction sequencing/time), 5D (cost), 6D (sustainability), and 7D (facility management).

History

Concepts of building modeling date to the 1970s, notably Charles Eastman's "Building Description System" at Carnegie Mellon. The term "building information model" appeared in print in the early 1990s, and the acronym BIM was popularized in 2002 by a white paper associated with Autodesk and consultant Jerry Laiserin. Software such as ArchiCAD (with roots in the 1980s), Revit (released 2000), and Tekla made object-based modeling practical, replacing flat CAD drawings with parametric, data-rich models.

Practice and standards

BIM workflows emphasize collaboration: architects, structural and services engineers, and contractors federate their discipline models into a common data environment, running clash detection to resolve interferences before construction. Levels of model development (LOD 100–500) specify how detailed and reliable model elements are at each stage.

The ISO 19650 series, developed from the British PAS 1192 standards, provides an international framework for managing information over the life cycle of a built asset; the UK government mandated "Level 2 BIM" on centrally procured public projects from 2016. Interoperability relies on open formats such as IFC (Industry Foundation Classes, ISO 16739) and COBie for handover data. Governments in many countries now mandate or encourage BIM for public infrastructure, and the approach extends into digital twins — live, data-fed counterparts of operating assets.

Benefits and criticism

Reported benefits include fewer design errors and rework, better cost and schedule estimates, improved energy analysis, and richer handover information for operators. Criticisms include software and training costs, interoperability problems between vendor platforms, ambiguous legal ownership of model data, and the risk that the model is mistaken for a complete representation of construction reality.
