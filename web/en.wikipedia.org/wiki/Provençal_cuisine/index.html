Provençal cuisine is the cooking tradition of Provence in southeastern France, shaped by the region's Mediterranean climate and by centuries of exchange with Italy and the wider Mediterranean world. Where the classic cuisines of northern France are built on butter and cream, Provence cooks with olive oil, and its pantry is defined by garlic, tomatoes, olives, anchovies, aromatic herbs—thyme, rosemary, bay, fennel and the lavender-tinged blend sold as herbes de Provence—and the fish and shellfish of the Mediterranean coast. Historically a frugal peasant cuisine that made much of vegetables, pulses and preserved fish, it was codified in the early 20th century by writers such as Jean-Baptiste Reboul, whose La Cuisinière provençale (1897, repeatedly expanded) collected hundreds of regional recipes, and it later became central to the international image of the healthy "Mediterranean diet".

Characteristic dishes

  • Bouillabaisse — Marseille's great fish stew, originally made by fishermen from the unsold rock fish of the catch, simmered with saffron, fennel and orange peel and served with rouille (a fiery garlic-and-chilli mayonnaise) and croûtons; a charter drawn up by local chefs in 1980 sought to fix its authentic composition.
  • Ratatouille — a stew of aubergines (eggplant), courgettes (zucchini), peppers, tomatoes, onions and garlic in olive oil, from the Nice area; the related bohémienne and confit byaldi are variations.
  • Aïoli — a pungent garlic mayonnaise, and by extension le grand aïoli, a festive platter of salt cod, boiled vegetables, eggs and snails served with it.
  • Tapenade — a paste of black olives, capers (Provençal tapéno, whence the name), anchovies and olive oil, spread on toast or used as a stuffing.
  • Salade niçoise — Nice's composed salad of tomatoes, green beans, olives, anchovies or tuna and hard-boiled eggs; purists dispute the presence of cooked vegetables.
  • Soupe au pistou — a summer vegetable and bean soup enriched at the table with pistou, a basil-garlic-olive oil paste close kin to Ligurian pesto.
  • Daube provençale — beef (or wild boar) braised slowly in red wine with olives, orange zest and herbs, traditionally in an earthenware daubière.
  • Pissaladière — a Nice flatbread topped with slow-cooked onions, anchovies and olives, related to Italian pizza and named for pissalat, an anchovy condiment.
  • Brandade de morue — an emulsion of salt cod, olive oil, milk and garlic, associated with Nîmes.
  • Socca — a thin chickpea-flour pancake baked in a wood oven, the street food of Nice (called farinata across the Italian border).

Ingredients and products

Olives and olive oil anchor the cuisine: the region produces appellation oils (notably from Les Baux-de-Provence and Nyons, whose black olives are also eaten cured) and the tiny black Niçoise olive essential to local salads. Garlic appears everywhere, including in aillade sauces; anchovies are preserved in salt and oil and melted into sauces and the bag-na cauda-like anchoïade. Coastal fisheries supply red mullet, sea bream, monkfish, John Dory and the scorpion fish indispensable to bouillabaisse, while the hills give lamb (the prized agneau de Sisteron), goat cheeses such as the leaf-wrapped Banon, and truffles from the Luberon and Tricastin in winter. Rice from the Camargue, chickpeas, and market vegetables—artichokes, fennel, chard (used in the sweet-savoury tourte de blettes)—complete the pantry. Desserts include the treize desserts of Christmas (thirteen sweets symbolising Christ and the apostles), calissons of Aix (almond wafers iced on host paper), nougat of Montélimar, and candied fruits of Apt.

Wines

Provence is one of the world's great rosé regions: the Côtes de Provence, Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence and Bandol appellations produce pale, dry rosés, alongside Bandol's long-lived Mourvèdre-based reds and the whites of Cassis. The sweet muscat of Beaumes-de-Venise and the reds of the southern Rhône fringe (Châteauneuf-du-Pape lies just west of historic Provence) are also closely associated with the regional table.

Influence

Provençal cooking spread internationally in the 20th century through writers such as Elizabeth David, whose French Provincial Cooking (1960) introduced British and American readers to it, and through the "Mediterranean diet" research that followed Ancel Keys's studies, in which the olive-oil-based cuisines of the northern Mediterranean shore were linked to cardiovascular health. Its emphasis on vegetables, legumes, fish and olive oil continues to make it a reference point for modern seasonal cooking.
