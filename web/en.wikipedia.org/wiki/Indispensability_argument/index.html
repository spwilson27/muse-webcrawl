Indispensability argument

The indispensability argument is an argument in the philosophy of mathematics for the existence of abstract mathematical objects such as numbers and sets. It is associated principally with Willard Van Orman Quine and Hilary Putnam, and is therefore often called the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument.

Mathematics is woven into our best physical theories.

The argument

In outline, the argument runs as follows. Our best scientific theories make essential reference to mathematical entities — quantification over numbers, functions and sets is woven into physics and cannot be eliminated without serious loss. If we are ontologically committed to the entities our best theories quantify over — including electrons and other unobservables accepted on the strength of those theories — then consistency demands that we be equally committed to the mathematical entities, since both are equally indispensable to the theories we accept.

Background

The argument rests on two Quinean doctrines: confirmational holism, the thesis that scientific theories are confirmed as a whole rather than sentence by sentence, so that the mathematics embedded in a confirmed theory shares in its confirmation; and naturalism, the rejection of any "first philosophy" external to science from which scientific ontology might be criticised. Putnam added the thought that realism about mathematics is the only philosophy of mathematics that does not make the success of science a miracle.

Criticisms

Critics such as Hartry Field have attempted to show that mathematics is in fact dispensable, by reformulating physical theories (notably Newtonian gravitation) without quantifying over mathematical objects. Others, including Penelope Maddy and Elliott Sober, have challenged the holism premise, arguing that confirmation does not flow to every part of a theory equally. Fictionalists such as Field and Mark Balaguer maintain that mathematical statements can be useful without being literally true.
