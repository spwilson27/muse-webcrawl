English contract law

English contract law is the body of law that regulates legally binding agreements in England and Wales. With its genesis in the lex mercatoria and the Industrial Revolution, it forms a cornerstone of the English common law tradition, and its principles have influenced contract law across the Commonwealth and beyond. A contract is traditionally understood as an agreement giving rise to obligations which are enforced or recognised by law.

Formation

A valid contract generally requires an offer, acceptance, consideration, and an intention to create legal relations, together with certainty of terms. Classic authorities include Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893], which upheld a unilateral offer to the world at large, and Entores v Miles Far East on the moment of acceptance. Consideration — something of value exchanged by each party — distinguishes enforceable bargains from gratuitous promises, subject to exceptions such as deeds and promissory estoppel (Central London Property Trust v High Trees House).

Content and fairness

Terms may be express or implied by fact, law, custom, or statute. Incorporation of exclusion clauses is controlled by the rules on notice and, in modern law, by the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 and the Consumer Rights Act 2015, which police fairness, particularly in consumer contracts. Doctrines of misrepresentation, mistake, duress and undue influence may render a contract voidable, while illegality and frustration can discharge it entirely.

Remedies

The primary remedy for breach is damages, designed to put the claimant in the position as if the contract had been performed, subject to rules of remoteness from Hadley v Baxendale (1854). Equitable remedies such as specific performance and injunction are discretionary and exceptional. Parties may also agree liquidated damages, enforceable where they protect a legitimate interest and are not extravagant (Cavendish Square Holding v Makdessi).
