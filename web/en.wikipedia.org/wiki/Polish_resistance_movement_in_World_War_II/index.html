Polish resistance movement in World War II

Polish resistance
Active1939–1945 (armed struggle to 1947+ by "cursed soldiers")
Main formationHome Army (Armia Krajowa), up to ~400,000 members
Political authorityPolish government-in-exile; Polish Underground State
Major operationsOperation Tempest, Warsaw Uprising, intelligence for Allies

The Polish resistance movement in World War II, centred on the Home Army (Armia Krajowa) and the Polish Underground State, was the largest resistance movement in German-occupied Europe. Poland never signed an armistice and formed no collaborationist government; instead its resistance operated under the legal Polish government-in-exile, fighting Germany from September 1939 to the end of the war.

Home Army soldiers in Warsaw during the 1944 uprising.

Activities

The resistance conducted sabotage and diversion on a massive scale, damaging German rail transport heading to the Eastern Front, and ran one of the Allies' most productive intelligence networks — reporting on the Holocaust (Witold Pilecki's Auschwitz reports, Jan Karski's missions), delivering Enigma-machine groundwork from pre-war Polish cryptologists, and recovering V-2 rocket components in Operation Most III. Underground courts tried collaborators, and secret education reached over a million pupils.

Warsaw Uprising and aftermath

On 1 August 1944, as part of Operation Tempest, the Home Army rose in Warsaw; after 63 days of fighting the city was destroyed and some 150,000–200,000 civilians killed. In the Soviet-occupied east, NKVD units arrested Home Army members; after the war, many former resisters were persecuted by the communist authorities, and some fought on as the "cursed soldiers" into the early 1950s.
