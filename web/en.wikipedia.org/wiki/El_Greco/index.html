El Greco

El Greco
Born: Doménikos Theotokópoulos, 1 October 1541, Heraklion, Crete
Died: 7 April 1614, Toledo, Spain
Movement: Mannerism; Spanish Renaissance

Doménikos Theotokópoulos (1541 – 7 April 1614), most widely known as El Greco ("The Greek"), was a Greek painter, sculptor and architect of the Spanish Renaissance. His dramatic, expressionistic style — marked by elongated figures and fantastical, phosphorescent colouring — was formed by his training in post-Byzantine Cretan painting and his years in Venice and Rome before he settled in Toledo, Spain, in 1577.

Career

Trained as an icon painter in Crete, then a Venetian possession, El Greco moved to Venice around 1567, where he absorbed the art of Titian and Tintoretto, and then to Rome, where he studied Michelangelo and the Mannerists. Failing to win major commissions in Italy, he moved to Spain hoping for work at the court of Philip II. The king disliked his Martyrdom of St Maurice, but in Toledo El Greco found lasting patronage from the Church and local nobility.

Major works

His masterpieces include The Burial of the Count of Orgaz (1586–88), a vast canvas uniting earthly and heavenly registers; View of Toledo (c. 1599–1600), among the earliest pure landscapes in Spanish art; The Opening of the Fifth Seal; Laocoön, his only mythological subject; and penetrating portraits of Spanish churchmen and gentlemen.

Legacy

Long dismissed as eccentric, El Greco was rediscovered in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and came to be seen as a forerunner of Expressionism and modern art, influencing artists from Delacroix and Manet to Cézanne, Picasso, and the Blue Rider group.
