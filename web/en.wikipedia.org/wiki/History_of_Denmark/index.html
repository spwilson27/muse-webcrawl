History of Denmark

The history of Denmark as a unified kingdom begins in the 8th century, though written sources and archaeological evidence document Danish society far earlier. Control of Denmark and the surrounding region — prized for its command of the sea lanes between the North Sea and the Baltic — has been the central theme of its history.

Viking Age and Christianisation

From about 793 to 1066, Danes participated in Viking raids and settlement across Europe, ruling England briefly under Sweyn Forkbeard and Cnut the Great, whose North Sea Empire (1016–1035) united Denmark, England and Norway. King Harald Bluetooth unified Denmark and introduced Christianity around 965, commemorated on the Jelling runestones.

Medieval kingdom and the Kalmar Union

Under Valdemar I and his successors Denmark became a major Baltic power, conquering parts of northern Germany and Estonia. In 1397 Queen Margaret I united Denmark, Norway and Sweden in the Kalmar Union, which lasted until Sweden's final secession under Gustav Vasa in 1523. The Reformation was introduced in 1536, making Lutheranism the state religion.

Early modern decline

Denmark's rivalry with Sweden dominated the following centuries; the Treaty of Roskilde (1658) ceded Scania and other provinces to Sweden, permanently reducing Denmark's territory. Absolute monarchy was established in 1660. During the Napoleonic Wars Britain attacked Copenhagen in 1801 and 1807, and in 1814 Denmark lost Norway to Sweden.

Modern Denmark

The constitution of 1849 made Denmark a constitutional monarchy. Defeat by Prussia and Austria in 1864 cost Denmark Schleswig and Holstein; northern Schleswig returned after a 1920 plebiscite. Denmark remained neutral in the First World War and was occupied by Germany from 1940 to 1945 despite declaring neutrality in the Second. Post-war Denmark joined NATO (1949) and the European Economic Community (1973), developed a comprehensive welfare state, and retained Greenland and the Faroe Islands as autonomous territories within the realm.
