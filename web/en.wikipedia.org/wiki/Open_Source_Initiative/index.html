The Open Source Initiative (OSI) is an American non-profit organisation that promotes and protects open source software. It is the steward of the Open Source Definition, the set of criteria a software licence must meet to be certified — and its software marketed — as open source, and it maintains the official review process and list of approved licences.

History

The OSI was founded in February 1998 by Bruce Perens and Eric S. Raymond, following Netscape's announcement that it would release the source code of its browser (which became Mozilla). The founders launched the term "open source" as a pragmatic, business-friendly reframing of free software, hoping to make the concept more acceptable to corporations. Perens drafted the Open Source Definition, adapting the Debian Free Software Guidelines he had authored. Perens left in 1999, and leadership passed through presidents including Raymond, Russ Nelson, Michael Tiemann and Stefano Maffulli.

Licence approval

Licences are submitted to the OSI's public mailing list for discussion and board decision; well over a hundred have been approved, though the OSI distinguishes widely used licences (such as Apache-2.0, MIT, BSD and the GPL family) from proliferating or superseded ones. Licences that have been refused approval, or withdrawn — such as the Server Side Public License and the Cryptographic Autonomy License controversies — often attract public debate about the limits of the definition.

Other work and disputes

The OSI has opposed "open washing" (marketing proprietary products as open), taken positions on public policy, and in 2024 published the Open Source AI Definition (OSAID 1.0), a contested attempt to define what counts as an open-source AI system, especially regarding training data. (The OSI is a software-licensing body and is unrelated to the Open Archives Initiative, which works on scholarly-repository interoperability.)
