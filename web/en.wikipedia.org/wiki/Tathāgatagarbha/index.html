Tathāgatagarbha

Tathāgatagarbha (Sanskrit: तथागतगर्भ; Chinese: 如來藏, rúláizàng; Japanese: nyoraizō; Tibetan: de bzhin gshegs pa'i snying po) is a Mahāyāna Buddhist doctrine according to which all sentient beings possess, hidden within them, the "womb", "embryo" or "matrix" of the Tathāgata (the Buddha). It is closely identified with the concept of Buddha-nature (buddhadhātu) and became one of the most influential ideas in East Asian Buddhism.

Scriptural sources

The doctrine is set out in a family of sutras composed in India roughly between the 3rd and 5th centuries CE, including the Tathāgatagarbha Sūtra — which illustrates the hidden Buddha with nine vivid similes, such as a Buddha statue wrapped in rags, honey surrounded by bees, and a treasure buried beneath a poor man's house — the Śrīmālādevī Siṃhanāda Sūtra, the Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, the Aṅgulimālīya Sūtra and the Ratnagotravibhāga, the principal Indian treatise on the subject.

Interpretations

These texts teach that the Buddha-nature is permanent, blissful, pure and — provocatively, in language recalling the Hindu ātman — a true "self", though Buddhist commentators generally explain this as an antidote to attachment to the false self, or as a skillful means. Interpretations diverge: some traditions, especially in China (Tiantai, Huayan, Chan) and Tibet (notably the Jonang school with its "other-emptiness", gzhan stong, view), treat Buddha-nature as a positive ultimate reality present in beings; others read it as equivalent to emptiness and the potential for awakening, not an underlying substance. The Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra explicitly warns that the tathāgatagarbha is taught to free beings from fear of selflessness and is itself empty of self.

Influence

The doctrine underwrote the East Asian teaching that enlightenment is the uncovering of what is already present ("original enlightenment", hongaku, in Japanese Tendai), shaped Chan/Zen, and remains debated by modern scholars — including the "Critical Buddhism" movement in Japan, which charged that Buddha-nature thought betrays the doctrine of no-self.
