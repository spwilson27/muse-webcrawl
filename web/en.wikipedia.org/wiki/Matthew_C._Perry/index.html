Matthew Calbraith Perry (1794–1858) was a commodore of the United States Navy who commanded the expedition that ended Japan's more than two centuries of self-imposed isolation (sakoku) and opened the country to trade with the West. His arrival in Edo Bay in 1853 with a squadron of steam warships — the kurofune, or "black ships" — is one of the pivotal events of modern Japanese history, setting in motion the fall of the Tokugawa shogunate and the Meiji Restoration.

Early career

Perry joined the navy as a midshipman in 1809 and served in the War of 1812. He became a leading advocate of naval reform, steam propulsion and professional education (he is often called the "father of the steam navy"), helped establish the naval station and colony that grew into Liberia's relations with the US, commanded the Africa Squadron suppressing the slave trade, and led American naval forces in the Mexican–American War, capturing Tabasco and directing operations on the Gulf coast.

The Japan expedition

President Millard Fillmore charged Perry with obtaining a treaty to protect shipwrecked American sailors, open ports for provisioning and coaling, and begin trade with Japan — vital as a way-station for American whalers and China traders. Perry sailed in November 1852 with four ships. On 8 July 1853 he anchored at Uraga in Edo (Tokyo) Bay, refused demands to leave for Nagasaki, delivered Fillmore's letter with great ceremony, and promised to return the following year for the shogunate's answer. He returned in February 1854 with nine ships. After weeks of negotiation, the Convention of Kanagawa was signed on 31 March 1854, opening the ports of Shimoda and Hakodate to American ships and providing for an American consul — Townsend Harris, who arrived in 1856 and negotiated a full commercial treaty in 1858.

Legacy

Perry's three-volume Narrative of the expedition became a bestseller. In Japan his landing is commemorated at Kurihama, where a monument and museum stand; the shock of the black ships remains a byword for forced opening to the outside world. Perry died in New York in 1858, his reputation in America overshadowed somewhat by that of his elder brother, Oliver Hazard Perry, hero of Lake Erie.
