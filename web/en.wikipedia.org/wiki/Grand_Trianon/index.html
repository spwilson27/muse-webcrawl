Grand Trianon

The Grand Trianon is a palace within the grounds of the Palace of Versailles in France, built in 1687–88 by Jules Hardouin-Mansart for Louis XIV as a private retreat from court ceremonial, where the king could entertain his family and, later, Madame de Maintenon. A single-storey building of pink Languedoc marble and porphyry around a courtyard, with a peristyle linking the wings, it stands amid gardens designed by André Le Nôtre near the Grand Canal. It remains an official residence of the French Republic, used for state guests.

History

The Grand Trianon replaced Louis XIV's earlier Trianon de porcelaine (1670), a pavilion clad in blue-and-white faience, on the site of the village of Trianon. Louis XV later added the Petit Trianon nearby and kept a menagerie and botanical interests at the estate; Napoleon refurnished the Grand Trianon and stayed there, as did Louis-Philippe, who made it a residence for his family. In the Fifth Republic, Charles de Gaulle restored it as a residence for visiting heads of state — its most famous diplomatic moment being the signing of the Treaty of Trianon (1920, in the Galerie des Cotelle), by which Hungary lost two-thirds of its territory.

Architecture and gardens

The palace consists of the main corps de logis with the celebrated peristyle — an open colonnade between courtyard and garden — and the Trianon-sous-Bois wing added for the king's family. Interiors, repeatedly redecorated, retain notable Empire furnishings from Napoleon's refurbishment. The gardens combine formal parterres, bosquets and a small theatre of greenery, laid out to Le Nôtre's taste for intimate variety within the vast Versailles park.
