In algebraic geometry, the Chow groups of an algebraic variety are abelian groups built from its algebraic cycles — formal sums of subvarieties — modulo a relation called rational equivalence. Named for Wei-Liang Chow, they are a fundamental tool for studying subvarieties up to deformation, playing the role for algebraic cycles that homology and cohomology groups play in topology. The standard reference for the modern theory is William Fulton's Intersection Theory (1984).

Definition

For a variety X, the group of k-dimensional cycles Zk(X) is the free abelian group on the k-dimensional subvarieties of X. Two cycles are rationally equivalent if their difference can be expressed through cycles arising as divisors of rational functions on (k+1)-dimensional subvarieties. The Chow group CHk(X) is the quotient of Zk(X) by the subgroup of cycles rationally equivalent to zero. Equivalently one may grade by codimension, writing CHp(X).

Examples and special cases

  • CHdim X(X) is generated by the whole variety, and the degree-zero part CH0(X) for a curve is closely related to its Jacobian.
  • For codimension one, CH1(X) agrees with the Picard group of line bundles modulo isomorphism (for smooth varieties).
  • For projective space, the Chow groups are simple: each CHk of projective n-space is infinite cyclic, generated by a linear subspace.
  • The group CH0 of zero-cycles, and its degree map to the integers, is a subtle birational invariant.

Intersection product and Chow ring

For a smooth variety, Chow's moving lemma ensures that cycles can be moved within their rational equivalence classes until they intersect properly, allowing an intersection product to be defined. This makes the direct sum of codimension-graded Chow groups into a commutative ring, the Chow ring, an algebraic analogue of the cohomology ring. The Chern classes of vector bundles live naturally in the Chow ring, and the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem relates the Chow ring to algebraic K-theory.

Relations to other theories

There are cycle class maps from Chow groups to homology theories such as étale cohomology and singular cohomology, and the conjectural theory of motives seeks to explain these maps uniformly. For smooth projective varieties over the complex numbers, Griffiths's work on intermediate Jacobians and the Abel–Jacobi map refined the understanding of cycles homologically but not rationally trivial. The Bloch–Beilinson conjectures predict deep structure for Chow groups of varieties over number fields.
