Legba is a lwa (spirit) in Haitian Vodou and related African diasporic religions, originating in the Vodun of the Fon people of present-day Benin. He is the guardian of the crossroads and of gates and doors, the first spirit saluted in every Vodou ceremony, and the indispensable intermediary who opens the way of communication between human beings and the other lwa.

Character and worship

In Haiti, Legba — commonly Papa Legba — is pictured as an elderly man, bent and walking with crutches or a cane, dressed in rags and a straw hat, often accompanied by a dog and carrying a small sack. His humble, aged appearance conceals his great power: nothing can be accomplished in ritual without his consent, for he holds the keys to the barrier between the worlds. Ceremonies begin with invocations and offerings to Legba, asking him to "open the gate" (ouvri baryè) so that the other spirits may be reached.

Offerings to Legba include coffee, rum sprinkled on the ground, grilled food, peanuts, candies, and coins left at crossroads or at the foot of the poteau-mitan, the central post of the temple. His ritual day is Monday, and his colours are often given as red and black or white.

Origins and diaspora

Legba descends from the Vodun divinity of the same name among the Fon of Dahomey, where he serves as a trickster-messenger of the gods, comparable in some respects to the Yoruba orisha Eshu-Elegba. Carried across the Atlantic by enslaved Africans, he retained his role as opener of the way in Haitian Vodou, in Louisiana Voodoo, and in the Afro-Cuban and Brazilian traditions.

Through the Catholic environment of colonial Saint-Domingue, Legba became syncretized with Saint Peter, keeper of the keys of heaven — the correspondence rests on Peter's keys — and with the ragged, crutch-bearing Saint Lazarus, whose images match Legba's own appearance.

In popular culture

Legba is the most widely known of the lwa outside Vodou practice, appearing frequently in literature, blues lyrics about the crossroads, television, and film, often in the simplified role of a devil-like figure at the crossroads — a depiction practitioners criticize as a distortion of his benevolent, gate-keeping function.
