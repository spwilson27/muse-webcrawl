Legendary saga

Legendary sagas were preserved in Icelandic vellum manuscripts

A legendary saga (Old Norse: fornaldarsaga, "saga of ancient times") is a category of Old Norse saga set in the distant Germanic past, before the settlement of Iceland, typically in Scandinavia and the wider world of Norse myth and hero legend. Unlike the more realistic Sagas of Icelanders, legendary sagas freely feature dragons, dwarfs, magic weapons, berserks, and heroes of superhuman stature, drawing on older oral tradition and Eddic poetry.

Corpus

About thirty legendary sagas survive, conventionally grouped by setting. Heroic sagas treat the great figures of Germanic legend: Völsunga saga retells the story of Sigurd the dragon-slayer and the cursed gold of the Niflungs, closely related to the Middle High German Nibelungenlied; Hervarar saga ok Heiðreks compiles poems about the magic sword Tyrfing and the battle of the Goths and Huns. Viking sagas such as Örvar-Odds saga and Ragnars saga loðbrókar — the tale of Ragnar Lodbrok — follow adventurers across the Baltic and beyond, while "adventure sagas" like Göngu-Hrólfs saga incorporate romance and folktale motifs from across Europe.

Style and transmission

Many legendary sagas embed older poems, such as the riddle contest in Hervarar saga and the Waking of Angantyr, preserving some of the oldest strata of Norse verse. Most were written down in Iceland in the 13th and 14th centuries, though their material may be centuries older. Long dismissed as mere entertainment compared with the family sagas, they are now studied as vital sources for Germanic mythology, heroic legend, and medieval Icelandic ideas about the pagan past.

Influence

The legendary sagas profoundly influenced modern fantasy literature. J. R. R. Tolkien drew names, motifs, and even a dwarf-list from this tradition for The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings, and the sagas' terse prose and haunted, doom-laden atmosphere shaped the genre's portrayal of the Northern past.
