Copenhagen interpretation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Copenhagen interpretation
ProponentsNiels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Max Born
EraFormulated 1920s, Copenhagen
Key ideasComplementarity, wave function collapse, Born rule
[Image: Photograph of the Niels Bohr Institute in Copenhagen, a classical building facade]
The Niels Bohr Institute, Copenhagen, where the interpretation was developed.

The Copenhagen interpretation is a collection of views about the meaning of quantum mechanics, principally attributed to Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg, developed in Copenhagen in the 1920s — notably around Bohr's complementarity, Heisenberg's Uncertainty principle and Max Born's statistical interpretation of the Wave function. It was the dominant view of quantum mechanics for most of the twentieth century and remains the most commonly taught.

In its standard form: a quantum system is described completely by a wave function evolving deterministically; measurement outcomes are irreducibly probabilistic, with probabilities given by the Born rule |ψ|²; and upon measurement the wave function 'collapses' to the observed outcome. Bohr's complementarity holds that phenomena such as wave and particle behaviour are complementary aspects that require mutually exclusive experimental arrangements.

Features and disputes

The interpretation draws a line ('Heisenberg cut') between the quantum system and the classical measuring apparatus, leaving the exact status of that division — and of 'collapse' — imprecise, which critics from Albert Einstein and Erwin Schrödinger (the cat paradox, 1935) onward attacked as evidence of incompleteness. Historians note that 'the' Copenhagen interpretation was never a single codified doctrine: Bohr's and Heisenberg's views differed in emphasis, and later writers bundled them together.

Rivals and legacy

Alternatives include the Many-worlds interpretation (no collapse; all outcomes occur), pilot-wave/de Broglie–Bohm theory (definite trajectories guided by the wave), spontaneous-collapse models (GRW), and QBism (wave function as an agent's beliefs). Surveys of physicists show Copenhagen-style views still the most common working philosophy, though no interpretation commands a majority consensus on all points.
