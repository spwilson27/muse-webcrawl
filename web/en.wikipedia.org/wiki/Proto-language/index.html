In historical linguistics, a proto-language is the reconstructed common ancestor of a group of languages forming a language family. Proto-languages are typically unattested—no direct written record of them survives—and are reconstructed by applying the comparative method to their descendants: by identifying systematic correspondences of sound and meaning among related languages, linguists work backwards to the forms from which the attested forms can most economically be derived. Reconstructed words and sounds are conventionally marked with an asterisk to show that they are hypothetical; compare, for example, Proto-Indo-European *ph₂tḗr "father", ancestral to Latin pater, Greek patḗr, Sanskrit pitā́ and English father.

Reconstruction

The comparative method begins with lists of probable cognates—words in different languages descended from the same ancestral word. Regular sound correspondences among the cognates (such as the correspondences codified in Grimm's law between Germanic and the other Indo-European languages) allow the linguist to posit the proto-sounds from which each set developed, and then to reconstruct vocabulary, morphology and, to a lesser degree, syntax. Internal reconstruction, which exploits irregularities within a single language (such as alternations left behind by earlier sound changes), can supplement the comparative evidence. The result is a system that models the ancestor language as spoken at roughly the moment before it began to diverge into the known daughters; reconstructed forms are best understood as formulas encoding the correspondences, not as exact transcriptions of prehistoric speech.

Properties and limitations

Real proto-languages were ordinary languages, with dialects, registers and variation, but reconstruction tends to yield a uniform idealisation, because variation that left no trace in any daughter cannot be recovered. Reconstructions also become progressively less complete and less certain the older the proto-language: beyond roughly 6,000–10,000 years of divergence, the surviving cognate vocabulary is generally too sparse and eroded for the comparative method to demonstrate relationship, which is why proposed "macro-family" ancestors such as Proto-Nostratic or Proto-World remain controversial and unproven. Where a proto-language is attested, as Latin is for the Romance languages, comparison with the reconstruction (Proto-Romance) both validates the method and exposes its limits: the method recovers the phonology of Vulgar Latin reasonably well but misses features that all daughters lost.

Well-known proto-languages

  • Proto-Indo-European (c. 4500–2500 BC, probably on the Pontic–Caspian steppe): ancestor of the Indo-European family, the most thoroughly reconstructed proto-language, with a large recovered vocabulary illuminating the society of its speakers.
  • Proto-Germanic (c. 500 BC): ancestor of English, German, Dutch and the Scandinavian languages.
  • Proto-Slavic (up to c. AD 900): ancestor of Russian, Polish, Czech, Bulgarian and the other Slavic languages, close in time to the earliest Slavic texts.
  • Proto-Bantu: ancestor of the several hundred Bantu languages of sub-Saharan Africa, central to debates about the Bantu expansion.
  • Proto-Austronesian and Proto-Oceanic: ancestors of the languages of Madagascar, Southeast Asia and the Pacific.
  • Proto-Semitic, Proto-Turkic, Proto-Uralic (ancestor of Finnish and Hungarian), Proto-Sino-Tibetan and Proto-Quechuan, among many others.

Urheimat and archaeology

The reconstructed vocabulary of a proto-language can indicate where and when it was spoken: if words for particular trees, animals, technologies (such as wheeled vehicles or domesticated horses) can be securely reconstructed, the homeland (Urheimat) must lie where those things existed at the right date. For Proto-Indo-European, words for wheels, wool and the horse, and the absence of common words for the sea or the olive, contributed to the steppe hypothesis locating the homeland north of the Black and Caspian Seas in the 4th millennium BC—an inference since given striking support by ancient-DNA studies of steppe migrations. Such reasoning must be used cautiously, since words change meaning and can be borrowed.
