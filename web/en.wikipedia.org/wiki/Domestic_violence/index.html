Domestic violence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Domestic violence is violence or other abuse that occurs in a domestic setting, such as in a marriage or cohabitation, by one person against another in an intimate relationship — and, in broader definitions, against children, parents or elders in the household. It encompasses physical, sexual, psychological and economic abuse and coercive control (a pattern of intimidation, isolation and regulation of a partner's life, criminalised as such in England and Wales in 2015, and in several other jurisdictions since).

Historically tolerated as a husband's prerogative (the “rule of thumb” story is a folk etymology), domestic violence was reframed by the feminist movement of the 1970s as a public crime: refuges (the first in Chiswick, London, 1971), mandatory-arrest experiments (the Minneapolis study, 1981, and its contested replications), protection orders, specialised courts and coordinated community response followed. Global prevalence estimates (WHO) suggest roughly one in three women worldwide have experienced physical or sexual intimate-partner or non-partner violence — a figure cited with methodological caveats — and the Istanbul Convention (2011) set the European standard for prevention, protection and prosecution. Legal systems now grapple with evidence without victim testimony, the intersection with custody disputes, and technology-facilitated abuse.
