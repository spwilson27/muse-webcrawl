Truth

Allegorical representation of Truth

Truth is the property of being in accord with fact or reality. In everyday language, truth is typically ascribed to statements, beliefs and propositions that aim to describe reality and that succeed in matching it. Truth is a central topic in philosophy, logic, mathematics, religion and law, and its nature has been debated since antiquity.

Theories of truth

Several major theories attempt to explain what makes something true:

  • Correspondence theory — a belief or statement is true if it corresponds to the facts or the way the world is; this view traces to Aristotle's dictum that to say of what is that it is, is true.
  • Coherence theory — truth consists in coherence with a specified set of beliefs or propositions.
  • Pragmatic theory — truth is associated with what works in practice or proves useful in the long run of inquiry, associated with Peirce, James and Dewey.
  • Deflationary theories — the predicate "is true" merely serves a logical or expressive function, and does not attribute any substantial property.

Truth in logic and mathematics

In formal logic, truth values (typically true and false) are assigned to statements, and the semantics of languages is studied through notions such as satisfaction and validity. Tarski's semantic theory of truth and his undefinability theorem showed that a sufficiently rich language cannot consistently define its own truth predicate, a result related to the liar paradox ("This sentence is false").

Related concepts

Truth is closely connected to knowledge (often defined as justified true belief), belief, certainty, honesty and veracity. Its opposite is falsehood, and a deliberate falsehood is a lie.
