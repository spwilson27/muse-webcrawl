The British Mark I was the world's first tank to enter combat. Developed in 1915–16 by the Landship Committee in response to the stalemate of trench warfare on the Western Front, it first saw action at the Battle of the Somme on 15 September 1916, near Flers-Courcelette, becoming the ancestor of all subsequent British heavy tanks of the First World War.

Design

The Mark I had a distinctive rhomboidal hull with tracks running around the entire profile, allowing it to cross wide trenches. Armour was 6–12 mm thick, proof against rifle fire but not artillery. Steering required four of the eight-man crew working in coordination, and conditions inside — heat, fumes and noise — were notoriously severe.

Two armament variants were built. The Male carried two 6-pounder (57 mm) naval guns in sponsons plus machine guns; the Female carried machine guns only, intended to defend against infantry while the Males attacked strongpoints. A wheeled steering tail was fitted at the rear but proved of little use and was dropped in later marks.

Combat use

Of the 150 Mark Is built, 49 were deployed for the first attack at Flers; only a handful reached their objectives, but their psychological effect on both sides was significant. Mark Is also fought at Arras in 1917. The design was rapidly improved through the Mark II–V series.

Survivors

A single Mark I survives, at The Tank Museum, Bovington — the world's oldest surviving combat tank, displayed in its 1916 configuration with the grenade screen and steering tail.
