Fasti

In ancient Rome, the fasti (Latin, plural of fastus) were chronological records and lists: most importantly the calendar of religious festivals and days on which public business could lawfully be conducted (dies fasti), and the official lists of magistrates, triumphs and priesthoods. The word also names Ovid's poem on the Roman calendar.

Fasti as calendars

Roman calendars known as fasti listed the days of the year, marking them as fasti (permitted for legal business), nefasti (forbidden) or comitiales (assembly days), and noting festivals and anniversaries. Originally secret priestly knowledge, the calendar was famously published by Gnaeus Flavius in 304 BC. The best-preserved painted example is the Fasti Praenestini, set up by the grammarian Verrius Flaccus at Praeneste in the Augustan age.

Fasti as lists

The Fasti Capitolini, inscribed in the Augustan period and now in the Capitoline Museums, list Rome's consuls from the early Republic and record triumphs (Fasti Triumphales). Such lists underpin the conventional chronology of the Roman Republic, though their early portions are partly reconstructed.

Ovid's Fasti

The poet Ovid composed a six-book elegiac poem, the Fasti, treating the festivals of January through June and explaining their myths and origins; the poem was left unfinished at his exile in 8 AD and is a major source for Roman religion.
