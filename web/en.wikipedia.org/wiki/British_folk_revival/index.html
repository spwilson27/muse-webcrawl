The British folk revival refers to two movements that promoted traditional folk music, song and dance in Britain: the first revival from about 1890 to 1920, when figures such as Cecil Sharp collected folk songs and dances from a vanishing rural tradition; and the second revival from 1945 into the 1970s, which brought folk music to urban folk clubs, the recording industry and, through folk rock, the pop charts.

First revival (1890–1920)

Collectors worried that industrialisation and urbanisation were destroying an oral tradition of song and dance. Sharp collected some 4,600 tunes, published folk songs for schools, and founded the English Folk Dance Society (1911). Composers like Ralph Vaughan Williams and Gustav Holst drew directly on folk melodies. The movement largely recorded and revived English material, though collectors worked elsewhere in the British Isles.

Second revival (1945–1970s)

After the Second World War a politicised folk scene grew, led by Ewan MacColl and A. L. Lloyd, who linked traditional song to working-class and left-wing culture, and spread through the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament and skiffle. The BBC's Radio Ballads (1957–64) combined documentary voice and new songs written in traditional style. Hundreds of folk clubs flourished, and singers such as Shirley Collins, Anne Briggs, Martin Carthy and the Watersons defined a stripped-back, source-singer style. In 1969 Fairport Convention's Liege & Lief fused folk with rock, founding British folk rock alongside Steeleye Span and Pentangle. The revival fed a continuing tradition of folk festivals and sessions today.
