Synecdoche

Synecdoche (pronounced /sɪˈnɛkdəki/) is a figure of speech in which a term for a part of something is used to refer to the whole, or a term for the whole is used to refer to a part. It is closely related to metonymy, and many rhetoricians treat synecdoche as a special case of it. The word derives from the Greek synekdoche, "simultaneous understanding". Examples in English include "all hands on deck" (hands for sailors), "wheels" for a car, "the Crown" for the monarchy considered institutionally, and "Australia won the match" (a country for its team).

In synecdoche, a part stands for the whole, or vice versa.

Types

Traditional rhetoric distinguishes several patterns: pars pro toto (part for the whole), as in "fifty head of cattle" or "sails" for ships; totum pro parte (whole for the part), as in "the police" for particular officers; genus for species and species for genus; and material for the thing made, as in "steel" for a sword or "silver" for cutlery. Kenneth Burke, in A Grammar of Motives (1945), placed synecdoche among his "four master tropes" alongside metaphor, metonymy and irony, treating it as the trope of representation — the part standing for the whole mirrors how a representative stands for a constituency.

Synecdoche and metonymy

Because both tropes substitute one term for another on the basis of association or contiguity, the boundary between synecdoche and metonymy is disputed. Some theorists define synecdoche narrowly by the part–whole relation and metonymy by other relations of association; others absorb synecdoche into metonymy entirely. Roman Jakobson's influential account of the metaphoric and metonymic poles of language groups synecdoche with metonymy on the axis of combination and contiguity.

Use in literature and film

Synecdoche is ubiquitous in poetry and narrative, where concrete parts evoke wholes vividly. The film scholar's phrase "New York synecdoche" aside, the trope lends its name to Charlie Kaufman's 2008 film Synecdoche, New York, whose protagonist builds a life-size replica of the city — a part–whole conceit at the heart of the work.
