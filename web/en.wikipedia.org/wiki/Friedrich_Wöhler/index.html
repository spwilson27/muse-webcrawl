Friedrich Wöhler

Friedrich Wöhler
Born: 31 July 1800, Frankfurt
Field: Chemistry
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

Friedrich Wöhler (1800–1882) was a German chemist best known for synthesising urea in 1828 from ammonium cyanate — an 'inorganic' starting material — thereby striking the first blow against the doctrine of vitalism, which held that organic compounds required a living 'vital force'.

Wöhler also isolated aluminium (1827) and beryllium and yttrium, co-discovered (with Liebig) the benzoyl radical — the first clear example of a compound radical — and produced calcium carbide and acetylene. His Göttingen laboratory trained generations of chemists.

[Image: Portrait of Wöhler and crystals of urea]

The urea synthesis

Heating ammonium cyanate, Wöhler obtained crystals identical to urea from urine, and wrote to Berzelius that he could make urea 'without a kidney'. Historians note vitalism died slowly rather than in one blow, but 1828 remains the symbolic turning point.

Partnership with Liebig

Wöhler and Justus von Liebig, friends after a youthful rivalry over fulminates, collaborated by post for decades; their benzoin and oil-of-bitter-almonds work (1832) founded radical theory.
