Imperial Roman army

The Imperial Roman army was the terrestrial armed force deployed by the Roman Empire from about 30 BC to 476 AD, the period generally known as the Principate and later Empire. Under Augustus, the standing army was reorganised into a professional long-service force of legions of Roman citizens supplemented by auxilia regiments of non-citizen provincials. At its height under Septimius Severus it numbered perhaps 450,000 men: some 33 legions of about 5,500 men each plus a comparable number of auxiliaries.

Organisation

The legion (legio), commanded by a senatorial legate, was built from ten cohorts; the first cohort was double-strength. The smallest unit was the contubernium of eight men who shared a tent and mess kit; ten contubernia formed a century under a centurion, the professional backbone of the army. Auxiliary units—cohorts of infantry, alae of cavalry, and mixed cohortes equitatae—were raised from the provinces and, on honourable discharge, their soldiers received Roman citizenship, recorded on bronze military diplomas.

The high command lay with the emperor, who held imperium over the armed provinces through his legates. The Praetorian Guard, stationed in Rome, formed the emperor's household troops and often made and unmade emperors.

Pay, service and retirement

Legionaries enlisted for 25 years (later 26), auxiliaries for 25. Pay under Augustus was 225 denarii a year for a legionary, raised in stages by later emperors, part withheld for food and equipment. Veterans received a discharge bonus in cash or land, funded from AD 6 by the aerarium militare (military treasury), financed by inheritance and sales taxes.

Equipment and tactics

The stereotypical legionary of the early Empire wore mail (lorica hamata) or the articulated plate armour known as lorica segmentata, a curved rectangular shield (scutum), helmet (galea), short sword (gladius), dagger (pugio), and heavy javelins (pila). In battle, legions relied on disciplined volleys of pila followed by close-order sword combat, supported by auxiliary archers, slingers and cavalry. The army's engineering was equally famous: marching camps were fortified nightly, and the army built the roads, bridges and frontier works—including Hadrian's Wall—that knit the empire together.

Late reforms

In the third and fourth centuries the army changed substantially: Diocletian and Constantine I multiplied smaller legions and divided forces into frontier troops (limitanei) and mobile field armies (comitatenses), with cavalry gaining prominence. The western army dissolved with the western empire in the fifth century, while the eastern army evolved into the Byzantine military system.
