Kübler-Ross model

The Kübler-Ross model, commonly known as the five stages of grief, is a model of the emotional responses of terminally ill patients facing death, introduced by Swiss-American psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in her 1969 book On Death and Dying. The five stages are denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance (often abbreviated DABDA).

The five stages

  1. Denial – the individual refuses to believe the diagnosis, a temporary defence that buffers the initial shock.
  2. Anger – frustration and resentment emerge ("Why me?"), directed at oneself, others or the situation.
  3. Bargaining – an attempt to negotiate, often with a higher power, for more time or a different outcome.
  4. Depression – a preparatory grief as the reality of loss sinks in; Kübler-Ross distinguished reactive and preparatory depression.
  5. Acceptance – a calm coming to terms; not happiness, but a readiness for what comes.

Kübler-Ross herself emphasised that the stages are not a linear sequence: patients may skip stages, experience several at once, or revisit earlier ones. The model was later extended (with David Kessler) to grief in general in On Grief and Grieving (2005), and Kessler proposed a sixth stage, "finding meaning," in 2019.

Criticism

Empirical studies have found limited support for a fixed stage sequence; critics such as George Bonanno note that many people show resilience without passing through all the stages, and that the model is descriptive of Kübler-Ross's clinical observations rather than a universal law. Nevertheless, the model transformed the care of the dying, helped launch the hospice movement, and remains enormously influential in popular culture and grief counselling.
