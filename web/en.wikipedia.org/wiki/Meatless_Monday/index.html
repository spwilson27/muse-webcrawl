Meatless Monday
Meatless Monday encourages one meat-free day per week
TypePublic health / environmental campaign
Founded2003 (revival); Sid Lerner with the Johns Hopkins Center for a Livable Future

Meatless Monday is an international campaign that encourages people to not eat meat on Mondays for their own health and the health of the planet. It is part of the wider Monday Campaigns initiative, based on the idea that Monday is the day people are most open to making healthy lifestyle changes.

History

The phrase originated in the United States during the First World War, when the U.S. Food Administration under Herbert Hoover urged families to observe "meatless Mondays" and "wheatless Wednesdays" to conserve food for the war effort and for shipment to Europe. The practice was revived during the Second World War, and again in rationing that followed it in some countries.

The modern campaign was relaunched in 2003 by advertising executive Sid Lerner in association with the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health's Center for a Livable Future. It spread to schools, hospitals, city councils, restaurants and worksites; in 2009 Ghent, Belgium, became the first city to officially adopt a weekly vegetarian day (on Thursdays), and Paul McCartney and his daughters launched a parallel British campaign, Meat Free Monday, the same year. Public school systems including Los Angeles and New York City have adopted meatless Monday menus.

Rationale

Supporters cite health evidence associating high consumption of red and processed meat with heart disease, type 2 diabetes and some cancers, and environmental evidence that livestock production accounts for a substantial share of greenhouse-gas emissions, land use and water consumption. Critics in the livestock industry argue the campaign understates meat's nutritional value and the economic role of farming.

Around the world

The campaign operates in more than 40 countries, often under local names — Segunda Sem Carne in Brazil, Lunes Sin Carne in Spanish-speaking countries, and Vegemåndag variants in Scandinavia — sometimes observed on a different weekday, as in Ghent's Thursday.
