Set point theory

Set point theory, as it applies to human body weight, proposes that each individual has a biologically determined, preferred range of body weight (or body fat) that physiological mechanisms act to defend. When weight falls below the set point, compensatory responses — increased appetite, reduced energy expenditure, and hormonal changes — push weight back up; when weight rises above it, the reverse occurs. The theory is used to explain why long-term weight loss is difficult for many people and why weight tends to be regained after dieting.

History

The concept grew out of animal experiments on hypothalamic control of eating in the mid-20th century, including Gordon Kennedy's lipostatic hypothesis (1953), which proposed that the brain monitors fat stores and adjusts intake accordingly. The discovery of the hormone leptin in 1994, secreted by adipose tissue and signalling fat levels to the hypothalamus, provided a concrete feedback mechanism consistent with set-point ideas. William Bennett and Joel Gurin popularised the term "set point" for a general audience in the early 1980s.

Mechanisms

Proposed mechanisms include changes in resting metabolic rate and non-exercise activity thermogenesis, shifts in appetite-regulating hormones such as leptin and ghrelin, and changes in hunger and satiety signalling. Studies of weight-reduced individuals, notably by Rudolph Leibel, Michael Rosenbaum and colleagues, found that after a 10 percent weight loss, total energy expenditure falls by more than would be predicted from the change in body size alone, and these adaptations persist for years.

Set point versus settling point

Critics argue that a fixed biological set point is not necessary: the rival settling point model holds that body weight stabilises where energy intake and expenditure happen to balance given genes, environment, and behaviour, without active defence of a predetermined value. Others propose dual-intervention models in which weight is actively defended at both an upper and a lower threshold, with a relatively unregulated zone between. The set point itself may also drift upward with age, chronic overconsumption, or environmental change, and may be reset by bariatric surgery.
