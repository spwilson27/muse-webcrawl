Hélio Oiticica (Portuguese: [ˈɛʎu ɔjtʃiˈsikɐ]; 26 July 1937 – 22 March 1980) was a Brazilian visual artist, sculptor, painter, performance artist, and theorist, best known for his participation in the Neo-Concrete Movement, for his innovative use of colour, and for later influencing the Tropicália movement. He is regarded as one of the most important Brazilian artists of the 20th century.

Career

Oiticica studied under Ivan Serpa in Rio de Janeiro and joined the Grupo Frente in the 1950s. In 1959 he became a founding member of the Neo-Concrete Group alongside Lygia Clark and Lygia Pape, signing the Neo-Concrete Manifesto written by Ferreira Gullar. His early work moved from geometric painting toward three-dimensional suspended constructions, the Núcleos (Nuclei) and Penetráveis (Penetrables), which viewers walk into.

In 1964 he began the Parangolés — wearable capes, banners, and tents of painted fabric and plastic, designed to be worn and danced in, often with members of the Mangueira samba school. His 1967 installation Tropicália, with sand, tropical plants, live parrots, and a television, gave its name to the Tropicália cultural movement. His 1969 exhibition at the Whitechapel Gallery in London, known as the "Whitechapel Experience", presented the environment Eden. He lived in New York during the 1970s before returning to Rio de Janeiro, where he died of a stroke in 1980, aged 42.

Legacy

Much of Oiticica's archive and oeuvre was stored at his family home in Rio; a 2009 fire destroyed a large part of the collection. Major retrospectives have been held at Tate Modern (2007) and the Whitney Museum of American Art (2017, To Organize Delirium). His slogan "Seja marginal, seja herói" ("Be marginal, be a hero") became an emblem of Brazilian counterculture.
