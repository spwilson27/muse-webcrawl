De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres) is the seminal work on heliocentrism by the Renaissance astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543). Published in Nuremberg in 1543, the year of its author's death, it set out a model of the universe with the Sun — rather than the Earth — near its centre, and is conventionally regarded as the starting point of the Scientific Revolution.

Contents

The six books present the spherical Earth rotating daily on its axis and revolving annually around the Sun; the order and periods of the planets; the Moon's motion; and a mathematical treatment of planetary longitude and latitude retaining epicycles to handle the planets' irregular motions. Copernicus had circulated the ideas earlier in the brief Commentariolus (c. 1510–1514) and delayed publication for decades; the young mathematician Georg Joachim Rheticus persuaded him to publish and oversaw early printing before the task passed to Andreas Osiander.

Reception

Osiander inserted an unauthorised preface presenting heliocentrism as a mere computational hypothesis — a reading most astronomers took for decades, even as they adopted Copernican techniques such as Erasmus Reinhold's Prutenic Tables (1551). The book was placed on the Catholic Index in 1616 "until corrected", amid the Galileo affair. Kepler, Galileo and Newton transformed its hypothesis into physical astronomy. Owen Gingerich's census found that surviving first editions were heavily annotated, showing the work was closely read. See also Heliocentrism and Copernican Revolution.
