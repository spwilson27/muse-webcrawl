Local currency

A local currency is a complementary currency that is accepted only within a limited geographical locality, usually a town, city or region. Local currencies are intended to circulate alongside the national currency, encouraging spending within the local economy, supporting independent businesses, and strengthening community ties. They are not legal tender and participation is voluntary.

Rationale

Advocates argue that money spent with local independent businesses recirculates locally (the "local multiplier effect"), whereas money spent at chain stores or online quickly leaves the area. A currency that can only be spent locally is designed to keep value circulating in the community. Some schemes also use demurrage — a fee on holding the currency, as pioneered by Silvio Gesell — to discourage hoarding and speed circulation.

History

Local and emergency currencies have appeared repeatedly in economic crises: stamp scrip circulated in the United States and Europe during the Great Depression, and the Austrian town of Wörgl's demurrage currency experiment of 1932–33 is frequently cited by proponents. Modern local currencies grew out of the LETS (local exchange trading systems) movement of the 1980s, in which members trade services using a mutual-credit unit rather than printed notes.

Examples

  • Ithaca Hours (New York, USA) — launched 1991, denominated in hours of labour.
  • Chiemgauer (Bavaria, Germany) — launched 2003, a demurrage currency with a large network of accepting businesses.
  • Bristol Pound (UK) — launched 2012, the largest UK local currency, backed 1:1 by sterling deposits; closed in 2020.
  • Lewes Pound, Totnes Pound and Stroud Pound (UK) — Transition Town currencies.
  • BerkShares (Massachusetts, USA) — launched 2006 in the Berkshire region.
  • Sardex (Sardinia, Italy) — a business-to-business mutual credit circuit launched in 2009.

Criticism and challenges

Critics note that local currencies typically remain small in scale, face administrative costs, and may offer little measurable economic benefit; acceptance can decline over time, and tax and regulatory treatment varies. Others question whether restricting spending geographically is efficient, though supporters emphasise social and environmental goals as much as economic impact.
