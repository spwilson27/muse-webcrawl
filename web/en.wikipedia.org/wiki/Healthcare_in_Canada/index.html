Healthcare in Canada

Healthcare in Canada is delivered through provincial and territorial publicly funded systems, informally called Medicare. Spending: about 12% of GDP. Life expectancy: about 82 years.

Healthcare in Canada is delivered through the provincial and territorial systems of publicly funded health care, informally referred to as Medicare. The system is guided by the provisions of the Canada Health Act of 1984, which sets five criteria — public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability, and accessibility — that provinces must meet to receive full federal health transfers.

Structure

Each province and territory administers its own insurance plan covering medically necessary hospital and physician services with no point-of-care charges. Most providers are private — doctors are typically self-employed and bill the provincial plan — while hospitals are usually publicly funded non-profit institutions. Services outside the Canada Health Act core, such as prescription drugs outside hospital, dental care, and vision care, are covered unevenly by a mix of provincial programmes and private insurance; a national pharmacare framework began with legislation in 2024 covering diabetes medication and contraception.

History

Saskatchewan pioneered public hospital insurance in 1947 and full medical care insurance in 1962 under Premier Tommy Douglas, provoking a doctors' strike but setting the national pattern. Federal cost-sharing followed with the Hospital Insurance and Diagnostic Services Act (1957) and the Medical Care Act (1966). The system's strengths are universal coverage and low administrative overhead; persistent criticisms include long waiting times for elective procedures and shortages of primary care physicians.
