Phrase (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Two phrases in a Classical melody, marked with slurs.

In music theory, a phrase is a unit of musical form that has a complete musical sense of its own, built from figures, motifs, and cells, and combining to form melodies, periods, and larger sections. A phrase is analogous to a clause or sentence in language: it typically ends with a cadence that provides a sense of punctuation, ranging from a momentary pause to full closure.

Phrases in the Classical style most commonly span four bars. Two phrases often combine into a period: an antecedent phrase ending with a weak (half) cadence, answered by a consequent phrase ending with a strong (authentic) cadence. In notation, a phrase mark is a curved slur written above or below the notes.

Structure and analysis

Theorists from Heinrich Christoph Koch in the eighteenth century to modern scholars such as William Caplin have treated the phrase as a central unit of musical syntax. A phrase normally presents a musical idea (often setting out a tonic-prolonging melody), and may contain extensions, elisions, or overlaps in which the end of one phrase coincides with the start of the next. The term also extends to performance: "phrasing" is the expressive shaping of a line through dynamics, articulation, and timing, much of it guided by the phrase structure of the written music.
