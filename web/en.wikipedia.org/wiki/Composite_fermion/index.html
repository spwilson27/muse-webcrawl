Composite fermion

A composite fermion is the topological bound state of an electron (or hole) and an even number of quanta of magnetic flux, called vortices. Composite fermions were introduced as a conceptual framework for understanding the fractional quantum Hall effect, particularly by Jainendra K. Jain in 1989. The theory explains the fractional quantum Hall states at filling factors ν = p/(2p+1) as integer quantum Hall states of composite fermions moving in a reduced effective magnetic field.

Formation

When electrons in a two-dimensional system are subjected to a strong perpendicular magnetic field, each electron may bind to an even number (most commonly two) of flux quanta. The resulting quasiparticle, the composite fermion, experiences an effective magnetic field B* = B − 2φ0ρ, where φ0 is the flux quantum and ρ is the electron density. At certain filling factors the effective field vanishes entirely, and the composite fermions form a Fermi sea, giving rise to a compressible metallic-like state at half filling (ν = 1/2).

Evidence

Experimental evidence for composite fermions includes geometric resonances in surface acoustic wave propagation and magnetic focusing experiments, which reveal a Fermi wavevector consistent with composite fermions orbiting in the reduced effective field. Shubnikov–de Haas oscillations of composite fermions have also been observed near half filling.
