Maritime archaeology

Illustration: An archaeologist surveying a shipwreck (placeholder image)

Maritime archaeology is the study of past human interaction with the sea, lakes and rivers through material remains, including ships and boats, harbour installations, submerged landscapes, and the cargoes and possessions lost with vessels. It is a branch of archaeology closely related to nautical archaeology (the specialised study of ship construction) and underwater archaeology, which describes the techniques used where sites lie below water.

Scope and methods

Maritime archaeologists study shipwrecks, submerged settlements drowned by post-glacial sea-level rise (such as the Mesolithic site of Bouldnor Cliff in the Solent), votive deposits in lakes and bogs, fish traps, ballast piles, and the remains of ports and quays. Work typically combines remote sensing — side-scan sonar, magnetometry and multibeam bathymetry — with diver survey, photogrammetry, excavation by airlift, and conservation of waterlogged materials, which require lengthy treatment (for instance with polyethylene glycol) once raised. Practitioners stress that excavation is a last resort and that sites are best preserved in situ, a principle enshrined in the UNESCO Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage (2001).

Notable projects

Landmark investigations include George Bass's excavation of the Bronze Age Uluburun shipwreck off Turkey (1980s), the raising and conservation of the Tudor warship Mary Rose (sunk 1545, raised 1982) and the Swedish flagship Vasa (sunk 1628, raised 1961), the excavation of the Kyrenia ship in Cyprus, and the discovery of the Titanic wreck in 1985. The field has also been shaped by legal and ethical battles over treasure salvage, which often conflicts with archaeological standards.

History of the discipline

Although isolated recoveries occurred earlier — including sponge divers' finds off Antikythera in 1900, which yielded the Antikythera mechanism — scientific maritime archaeology began in earnest in the 1950s and 1960s, with the advent of scuba equipment and Bass's pioneering excavation of a Byzantine wreck at Yassı Ada, Turkey. Professional bodies such as the Nautical Archaeology Society (founded in Britain) and academic programmes at institutions including Texas A&M University professionalised the discipline.
