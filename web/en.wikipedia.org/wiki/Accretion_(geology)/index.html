Accretion (geology)

Accretion, in geology, is a process by which material is added to a tectonic plate or a landmass. This material may be sediment, volcanic arcs, seamounts, or other igneous formations. Accretion is a fundamental mechanism by which continents grow over geological time: material carried on a subducting oceanic plate that is too buoyant to be subducted is instead scraped off and welded onto the edge of an overriding continental plate.

Accretionary wedges

Where an oceanic plate subducts beneath another plate, sediments blanketing the ocean floor may be scraped off the descending slab and piled up in a wedge-shaped mass called an accretionary wedge or accretionary prism. These wedges, found at convergent margins such as the Barbados Ridge and the Nankai Trough, grow by frontal accretion (material added at the toe) and underplating (material added at the base).

Accreted terranes

Larger fragments — island arcs, oceanic plateaus, seamount chains, and slivers of continental crust — that are added to a continent during collision are called accreted terranes. Because these fragments originate elsewhere and carry their own geological history, they are also termed exotic terranes. The western margin of North America is largely composed of terranes accreted during the Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras, stitched to the craton along suture zones and bounded by major faults. The Wrangellia terrane, extending from Alaska through British Columbia, is a well-known example.

Continental growth

Through accretion at convergent plate boundaries, continental crust has grown progressively since the Archean. Geologists debate how much of continental volume was produced early in Earth history versus added steadily by accretion and magmatism at subduction zones.
