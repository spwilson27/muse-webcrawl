Napa Valley AVA is an American Viticultural Area located in Napa County, California. It is considered one of the premier wine regions in the world. Established in 1981, it was the first AVA designated in California and the second in the United States. The valley floor and surrounding hillsides of the Napa River watershed form the appellation, which contains sixteen nested sub-appellations.

Geography and climate

The valley runs roughly 30 miles northwest from San Pablo Bay to Mount St. Helena, flanked by the Mayacamas Mountains to the west and the Vaca Range to the east. Cool marine air and fog from the bay moderate the south, producing a range of mesoclimates suited to many grape varieties; Cabernet Sauvignon dominates the warmer mid-valley floor, while Chardonnay and Pinot Noir thrive in the cooler Los Carneros district at the southern end. Soils are remarkably diverse, of marine, volcanic and alluvial origin.

History

Commercial viticulture began in the 19th century with pioneers such as Charles Krug (1861) and the Inglenook and Beaulieu estates. Prohibition devastated the industry, which revived slowly after 1933. The region gained international fame after the 1976 Judgment of Paris tasting, in which Napa Chardonnay and Cabernet Sauvignon outscored French wines in a blind tasting. Since then the valley has become synonymous with premium, often high-priced wines and wine tourism.

Regulation

Wines labelled "Napa Valley" must be made from at least 85% grapes grown within the AVA. The name is protected internationally; Napa vintners also secured restrictions on the use of "Napa" in brand names for wines not sourced from the region.
