Stefan–Boltzmann law

The Stefan–Boltzmann law, also known as Stefan's law, describes the power radiated from a black body in terms of its temperature. Specifically, the total energy radiated per unit surface area of a black body across all wavelengths per unit time is directly proportional to the fourth power of the black body's thermodynamic temperature T:

j* = σT4

History

The law was deduced by Jožef Stefan in 1879 from experimental measurements and derived theoretically by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1884 using thermodynamics. The constant of proportionality σ, called the Stefan–Boltzmann constant, equals approximately 5.67 × 10−8 W·m−2·K−4.

Applications

The law is used to estimate the temperatures of stars from their luminosity and radius, to model the Earth's energy balance and effective temperature, and in infrared thermography. For real objects that are not perfect black bodies, the emissivity ε (between 0 and 1) multiplies the radiated power: j* = εσT4.
