Sébastien Le Prestre, Marquis of Vauban

Born15 May 1633, Saint-Léger-de-Foucheret, France
Died30 March 1707 (aged 73), Paris, France
OccupationMilitary engineer
RankMarshal of France (1703)

Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban, Seigneur de Vauban and later Marquis de Vauban (15 May 1633 – 30 March 1707), commonly referred to as Vauban, was a French military engineer who worked under Louis XIV. He is generally considered the greatest engineer of his time and one of the most important figures in western military history. His principles of fortification were widely used for nearly a hundred years, while aspects of his offensive tactics remained in use until the early 20th century.

He participated in 48 sieges, built or improved around 160 strongholds, and designed the system of fortresses — the pré carré — that defended France's frontiers. Twelve groups of his fortified sites were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2008 as the Fortifications of Vauban.

Career

Born into minor Burgundian nobility, Vauban joined the army of the Prince of Condé during the Fronde, was captured by royal forces and persuaded to change sides, and rose through his gifts for siegecraft. From 1667 he directed a series of celebrated sieges — Lille, Tournai, Maastricht (1673, where he systematised the use of parallel trenches), Valenciennes, Ghent and Luxembourg — and in 1678 became commissary general of fortifications, succeeding the Chevalier de Clerville.

Siegecraft and fortification

Vauban's methodical approach to attacking fortresses used successive parallels — trenches dug parallel to the defensive works and connected by zig-zag saps — combined with ricochet fire to dismount guns, reducing both the time and the casualties of a siege; he boasted of taking places "without losing men". In defence he developed bastioned systems of increasing sophistication, from modified existing works to the elaborate geometric designs of Neuf-Brisach, built from 1698 as the exemplar of his "third system".

Later life and writings

Made a Marshal of France in 1703, Vauban also wrote on economics and reform, notably the Projet d'une dîme royale (1707), which proposed replacing France's inequitable taxes with a single proportional levy; the book was suppressed and Vauban died in disgrace shortly afterwards. His heart is preserved at Les Invalides in Paris alongside Napoleon's tomb.
