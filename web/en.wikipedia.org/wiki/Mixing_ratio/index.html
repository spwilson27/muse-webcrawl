Mixing ratio

In chemistry and atmospheric science, the mixing ratio is the abundance of one component of a mixture relative to another, usually expressed as a ratio of masses or of amounts (moles). In meteorology, the (mass) mixing ratio of water vapour, w, is defined as the mass of water vapour per unit mass of dry air:

w = mvapour / mdry air

It is typically only a few grams per kilogram in the atmosphere, rising to around 20 g/kg in warm humid tropical air. Because dry air mass does not change as an air parcel rises or sinks, the mixing ratio is conserved during adiabatic processes that do not involve condensation, making it a useful tracer in atmospheric thermodynamics and a standard variable on thermodynamic diagrams such as the tephigram and skew-T log-P diagram.

Related quantities

The saturation mixing ratio is the value at which air becomes saturated at a given temperature and pressure; the ratio of actual to saturation mixing ratio approximates relative humidity. Mixing ratio is distinguished from specific humidity, which is the mass of vapour per unit mass of moist (total) air; for atmospheric values the two are numerically close. In general chemistry, mixing ratios are also expressed as mole fractions, volume mixing ratios (e.g. parts per million by volume, ppmv, used for trace gases such as CO₂ and methane) and mass fractions.
