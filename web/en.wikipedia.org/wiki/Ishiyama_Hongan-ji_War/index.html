The Ishiyama Hongan-ji War (Japanese: 石山合戦, Ishiyama Kassen) was a ten-year campaign (1570–1580) fought between the warlord Oda Nobunaga and the forces of the Jōdo Shinshū Buddhist sect, centred on the sect's fortified cathedral-temple, the Ishiyama Hongan-ji, at the mouth of the Yodo River in present-day Osaka. It was the longest siege of Japan's Sengoku period and a central episode in Nobunaga's unification of the country, marking the destruction of the last great independent religious-military power of medieval Japan.

Background

The Hongan-ji, head temple of the Jōdo Shinshū sect, had moved to Ishiyama in 1496 and grown into a fortified city of perhaps 10,000–20,000 inhabitants, ringed by moats, earthworks and satellite forts (the Nagashima and other Ikkō-ikki strongholds). Its adherents — the Ikkō-ikki leagues of peasants, townspeople and warrior-monks — had already overthrown feudal lords in Kaga Province and opposed the daimyo as a matter of religious as well as political principle. Nobunaga, whose base in central Japan was encircled by hostile leagues, demanded the fortress's surrender in 1570; Abbot Kennyo refused and called the faithful to arms.

The war

Fighting began in 1570 and widened into a network of campaigns: Nobunaga destroyed the Ikkō stronghold of Nagashima in the sieges of 1571–1574 with great loss of life, crushed the Echizen ikki in 1575, and progressively isolated Ishiyama by land. The temple was resupplied by sea through Osaka Bay by the Mōri clan's navy, defeating Nobunaga's fleet at the first Battle of Kizugawaguchi (1576); Nobunaga replied with huge armoured warships (the Atakebune) that blockaded the bay at the second battle in 1578. His general Toyotomi Hideyoshi's western campaigns further tightened the noose. Starved of supplies and abandoned by allies, Kennyo appealed to the imperial court, and in 1580 Emperor Ōgimachi mediated a settlement: Kennyo and the defenders evacuated the fortress, which was then burned — whether by accident or design is disputed — ending a decade of war.

Aftermath

The fall of Ishiyama Hongan-ji eliminated organised Buddhist military power as a political force in Japan: the sect survived as a religious body (splitting into the Nishi and Higashi Hongan-ji in 1602 under Tokugawa auspices) but never again fielded armies. Osaka Castle was built by Hideyoshi on the site from 1583, and the city that grew around it inherited Hongan-ji's commercial role. The war's naval blockades, mass sieges and the systematic reduction of fortified leagues became the template for the final unification campaigns of Nobunaga and Hideyoshi.
