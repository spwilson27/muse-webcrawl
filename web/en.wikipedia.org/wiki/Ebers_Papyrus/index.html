The Ebers Papyrus, also known as Papyrus Ebers, is an Egyptian medical papyrus of herbal knowledge dating to about 1550 BC, in the reign of Amenhotep I. One of the two oldest preserved medical documents in the world (with the Edwin Smith Papyrus, c. 1600 BC), it is also the longest: a scroll some 20 metres (about 65 feet) long containing roughly 700 remedies for ailments from crocodile bites to mental illness. It is named after the German Egyptologist Georg Ebers, who purchased it in Luxor in the winter of 1873–74.

History

According to tradition the scroll was found between the legs of a mummy in the Theban necropolis (Assassif district). It was bought by Edwin Smith in 1862 and later acquired by Georg Ebers, in whose collection at Leipzig it remains. Ebers published a facsimile edition with a hieroglyphic-Latin glossary in 1875; a full English translation by Bendix Ebbell appeared in 1937, and Cyril Bryan's popular translation, The Papyrus Ebers, in 1930. The colophon dates the compilation to the reign of Amenhotep I (c. 1514–1493 BC), though some material is far older, copied from sources the Egyptians themselves attributed to the First Dynasty era.

Contents

The papyrus is written in hieratic and organised as a formulary: brief case headings followed by prescriptions combining drugs (from plants, animals and minerals), procedures, and magical incantations. Topics include:

  • Diseases of the stomach, intestines and internal organs; intestinal parasites such as worms.
  • Heart and vessels — the Egyptians understood the pulse and the central role of the heart ("the heart speaks out of the vessels of every limb").
  • Eye diseases, skin diseases including tumours, burns and wounds.
  • Gynaecology, pregnancy tests, contraception (including a recipe using acacia gum, a spermicide).
  • Dentistry, bone-setting, and surgery of abscesses and swellings.
  • Mental disorders — descriptions interpreted as depression and dementia, treated with incantations.
  • Household remedies, cosmetics, and treatments against vermin and odours.

Medical significance

The Ebers Papyrus shows ancient Egyptian medicine operating simultaneously on empirical and magical principles: pharmacology with real active substances (honey as an antiseptic dressing, pomegranate for tapeworm, castor oil as a purgative) alongside spells addressed to gods such as Sekhmet and Isis. The Egyptian concept of the metu (channels) carrying air, water and blood — and putrefactive wekhedu matter as a cause of disease — influenced later Greek humoral thinking. Along with the Edwin Smith, Kahun, Berlin and London medical papyri, the Ebers is a foundational source for the history of medicine.
