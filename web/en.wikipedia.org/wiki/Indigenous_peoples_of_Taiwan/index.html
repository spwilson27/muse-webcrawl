Indigenous peoples of Taiwan

The Indigenous peoples of Taiwan (Chinese: 原住民族; pinyin: Yuánzhùmínzú), also called Taiwanese Indigenous peoples or Austronesian Taiwanese, are the Austronesian peoples who have inhabited the island of Taiwan for at least 6,500 years, long before the arrival of Han Chinese settlers in the 17th century. They number roughly 580,000 people, about 2.5% of Taiwan's population, and the government officially recognises 16 peoples.

Peoples and languages

The recognised peoples include the Amis (the largest group), Atayal, Paiwan, Bunun, Puyuma, Rukai, Tsou, Saisiyat, Tao (Yami) of Orchid Island, Thao, Kavalan, Truku, Sakizaya, Seediq, Hla'alua and Kanakanavu. Their languages belong to the Formosan branch of the Austronesian family; linguistic evidence suggests Taiwan is the likely homeland from which Austronesian peoples spread across the Pacific and to Madagascar — the "Out of Taiwan" model. Plains Indigenous peoples (Pingpu), such as the Siraya and Taivoan, were largely assimilated into Han society during Qing rule and have campaigned for recognition, which the Constitutional Court supported in a 2022 ruling requiring legal changes.

History

Indigenous communities traded with and resisted successive regimes: the Dutch and Spanish colonial outposts of the 17th century, the Qing dynasty, and the Japanese colonial government (1895–1945), against which the Seediq rose in the 1930 Wushe Incident. After 1945 the Kuomintang government imposed Mandarin and assimilationist policies, but democratisation brought an Indigenous rights movement: the 1994 constitutional reforms adopted the term yuanzhumin ("original inhabitants"), the Council of Indigenous Peoples was established in 1996, the Indigenous Peoples Basic Law passed in 2005, and in 2016 President Tsai Ing-wen delivered a formal state apology to Indigenous peoples, followed by a transitional justice commission.

Culture

Harvest festivals, millet and rice ceremonies, elaborate wood carving (Paiwan and Rukai), and polyphonic singing (Bunun pasibutbut) are hallmarks of Indigenous cultures today, alongside debates over hunting rights, land restitution and language revitalisation.
