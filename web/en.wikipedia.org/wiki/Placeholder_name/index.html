Placeholder name

Placeholder names are names used in place of a real person's name when the actual name is unknown, irrelevant, or must be withheld. They appear in legal documents, medical records, examples, templates, and everyday speech in every language, and each culture has its own traditional set.

English-language examples

The most famous English placeholder names are John Doe and Jane Doe, used in Anglo-American law for unidentified parties — a male or female whose true identity is unknown or must be protected. Unidentified corpses and hospital patients receive the same names. Related legal fictions include Richard Roe, and the pair John Stiles and Richard Miles used in the old English ejectment actions from which the Doe tradition derives. Other common English placeholders include John Q. Public and Joe Bloggs (British) or Joe Blow (American) for the average person, Uncle Tom Cobley and all for a long list of names, and Tom, Dick and Harry for arbitrary individuals.

Other languages and contexts

Many languages have equivalents: German uses Max Mustermann (with Erika Mustermann on specimen documents), French uses Jean Dupont or Paul Durand, Spanish uses Fulano (often with Mengano and Zutano), and Dutch uses Jan Jansen. In computing, placeholder words include foo, bar, and baz for variables, and lorem ipsum for text. In mathematics and logic, variables and metasyntactic names such as "Alice and Bob" (in cryptography) serve the same purpose of standing in for arbitrary members of a class.

Uses

Placeholder names protect privacy in court filings and journalism, identify unknown persons in official systems, demonstrate the use of forms and products, and provide generic protagonists in jokes, examples, and teaching. Because the same few names recur, they have acquired cultural lives of their own in film, music, and literature.
