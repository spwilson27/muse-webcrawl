Kirchhoff's circuit laws

Kirchhoff's circuit laws are two equalities that deal with the current and potential difference (voltage) in the lumped element model of electrical circuits. They were first described in 1845 by the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff. Widely used in electrical engineering, they are the current law (KCL) and the voltage law (KVL), and are consequences of charge conservation and energy conservation respectively.

Kirchhoff's current law

At any node (junction) in an electrical circuit, the sum of currents flowing into the node equals the sum of currents flowing out of it; equivalently, the algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero:

Σ Ik = 0

This law is based on the conservation of electric charge: since charge cannot accumulate at a node in a steady circuit, whatever flows in must flow out.

Kirchhoff's voltage law

The directed sum of the electrical potential differences (voltages) around any closed loop in a circuit is zero:

Σ Vk = 0

This follows from the conservation of energy and the fact that the electric field in a lumped circuit is (to good approximation) conservative: the net work done carrying a charge around a closed loop is nil.

Limitations

Both laws assume the lumped-element model, in which circuit elements are small relative to signal wavelengths and magnetic flux through loops is confined within inductors. KVL can fail in the presence of a changing magnetic field linking the loop (as in a transformer or in circuits enclosing time-varying flux), where the electric field is non-conservative; KCL fails at frequencies where parasitic capacitance lets charge effectively accumulate on conductors.

Applications

Together with Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's laws underpin nodal and mesh analysis, the standard systematic methods for computing all branch currents and voltages in linear circuits, and are built into circuit simulators such as SPICE.
