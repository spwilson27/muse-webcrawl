John of Jandun (French: Jean de Jandun; c. 1285–1328) was a French philosopher, theologian and political writer, a leading figure of Latin Averroism at the University of Paris and a close associate of Marsilius of Padua.

Life

Jandun took his name from his birthplace in the Ardennes. He taught in the faculty of arts at Paris, where his commentaries on Aristotle — heavily influenced by the Andalusian commentator Averroes — made him both famous and suspect. Around 1315–16 he left the university amid controversy over his teachings on the soul.

Defensor pacis and exile

Jandun was long thought to have co-authored with Marsilius the Defensor pacis (1324), the radical treatise that attacked papal claims to temporal power and assigned the church's coercive authority to the civil ruler; modern scholarship attributes the work mainly to Marsilius, with Jandun's contribution debated. Pope John XXII condemned both men in 1327, and they fled to the court of the Holy Roman Emperor Louis IV of Bavaria at Nuremberg and Munich. Jandun accompanied Louis on his Italian expedition and died at Montalto in 1328.

Philosophy

Jandun defended the Averroist doctrine of the unity of the intellect — that all humans share a single possible intellect — while formally deferring to faith on the individual immortality of the soul. His questions on Aristotle's De anima, Physics and Metaphysics circulated widely and influenced later scholastic and Renaissance natural philosophy.
