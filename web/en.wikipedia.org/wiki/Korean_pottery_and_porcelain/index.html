Korean pottery and porcelain have a continuous history stretching back to Neolithic comb-pattern pottery of around 8000 BC. Korean ceramics are celebrated for the luminous jade-green celadon of the Goryeo dynasty, the vigorous folk character of buncheong ware, and the austere white porcelain of the Joseon dynasty.

Early history

The earliest Korean pottery, Jeulmun (comb-pattern) ware, was made by Neolithic hunter-fisher-gatherers. Mumun pottery, plainer and associated with Bronze Age agriculture, followed. During the Three Kingdoms period, Silla and Gaya produced high-fired grey stoneware, including distinctive pedestal vessels and sculptural tomb wares.

Goryeo celadon

From the 10th century, Korean potters — influenced by Chinese Yue and Yaozhou wares but developing distinctive forms — produced celadon (cheongja) of exceptional quality. Goryeo celadon is prized for its kingfisher-blue-green glaze and for the sanggam inlay technique, in which designs carved into the body are filled with white or black slip before glazing, producing crane, cloud and floral motifs unique to Korea. Kilns clustered around Gangjin and Buan on the southwest coast.

Buncheong and Joseon porcelain

In the 15th–16th centuries, buncheong ware — grey-green stoneware decorated with white slip by stamping, brushing, inlay or iron painting — bridged the transition from celadon to porcelain. Under the Confucian Joseon dynasty, white porcelain (baekja) became dominant, valued for its purity. Forms such as the full, slightly asymmetrical moon jar (dal hangari) epitomise Joseon aesthetics. Blue-and-white porcelain, painted in cobalt blue, was reserved largely for the court because of the cost of imported cobalt.

Japanese invasions and legacy

During the Imjin War (1592–1598), many Korean potters were taken to Japan, where they founded kiln traditions including Satsuma ware and the porcelain industry of Arita — an episode remembered in both countries. Korean ceramic traditions survived and were revived in the 20th century; master potters are designated holders of Important Intangible Cultural Properties, and Korean celadon and moon jars command major museum collections worldwide.
