Choquet theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Choquet theory is an area of functional analysis and convex analysis created by French mathematician Gustave Choquet, concerning the representation of points in a compact convex set as "averages" of the extreme points of the set. It generalizes classical results such as the Krein–Milman theorem and Carathéodory's theorem.

Main results

For a compact convex subset K of a locally convex topological vector space, Choquet's theorem states that given any point x in K, there exists a probability measure μ supported on the set of extreme points of K such that x is the "center of mass" (barycenter) of μ; that is, f(x) equals the integral of f with respect to μ for every continuous linear functional f. When K is metrizable, the measure can be supported on the extreme points themselves.

Choquet simplex and uniqueness

When the representing measure is unique, K is called a Choquet simplex. Finite-dimensional simplices and the Poulsen simplex are key examples. The Choquet–Bishop–de Leeuw theorem extends the representation to non-metrizable sets using maximal measures.

Applications

Choquet theory has applications in potential theory, ergodic theory, operator algebras — where states of a C*-algebra form a compact convex set — and in the theory of capacities (Choquet integrals), which are used in decision theory and fuzzy measure theory.
