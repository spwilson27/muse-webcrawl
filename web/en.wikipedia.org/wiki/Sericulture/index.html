Sericulture

Sericulture, or silk farming, is the cultivation of silkworms to produce silk. Although there are several commercial species of silkworms, Bombyx mori (the caterpillar of the domestic silk moth) is the most widely used and intensively studied silkworm. Silk was first produced in China as early as the Neolithic period and remained a closely guarded secret for millennia.

History

According to Chinese tradition, silk production began around 2700 BCE, with legend attributing its discovery to the empress Leizu (Xi Lingshi), who is said to have observed silkworms and unwound their cocoons. Archaeological evidence of silk fabrics in China dates to the Yangshao culture of the Neolithic. Sericulture spread along the Silk Road to Korea, Japan, India, and eventually the Byzantine Empire and Europe, where centres such as Lyon in France became famous for silk weaving.

Production process

The production of silk involves several stages:

  1. Cultivation of mulberry trees, whose leaves are the sole food of Bombyx mori.
  2. Rearing of silkworms: eggs are incubated and the larvae fed chopped mulberry leaves through several instar stages.
  3. Cocoon formation: mature larvae spin cocoons of a single silk filament up to about a kilometre long.
  4. Reeling: cocoons are softened in hot water and the filaments unwound and combined into raw silk thread.
  5. Weaving and dyeing of the thread into fabric.

To preserve the filament intact, the pupa inside the cocoon is usually killed by heat before the moth can emerge and break the thread, a practice that has drawn criticism from animal welfare advocates; "peace silk" or Ahimsa silk allows the moth to emerge.

Economic importance

China and India are the world's largest producers of raw silk, together accounting for the great majority of global output. Sericulture provides employment to millions of rural households and involves some 30 person-days of work per kilogram of raw silk, making it an important cottage industry.
