Synoptic Gospels

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Synoptic Gospels are the first three Gospels of the New Testament — Matthew, Mark and Luke. They are called synoptic (from Greek σύν, "together", and ὄψις, "view") because they include many of the same stories, often in a similar sequence and in similar or sometimes identical wording. They stand in contrast to the Gospel of John, whose content and theological outlook differ greatly.

The close agreements among the three texts in content, arrangement and specific language have led scholars to study their literary relationship, a problem known as the synoptic problem.

Structure and content

The Synoptics share a broad narrative framework: the ministry of John the Baptist, the baptism and temptation of Jesus, a ministry centred on Galilee with parables and miracles, the journey to Jerusalem, the Last Supper, the Passion and the empty tomb. Much of Mark's material appears in both Matthew and Luke; passages shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark are attributed by the dominant hypothesis to a lost sayings source, "Q".

The synoptic problem

The two-source hypothesis — Markan priority plus Q — has been the most widely accepted solution since the nineteenth century. Alternatives include the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first, Luke using Matthew, no Q), the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first), and the two-document and Matthean posteriority hypotheses. The problem remains a central topic in New Testament scholarship because it bears on how the earliest written portraits of Jesus were composed and edited.

Composition

Most scholars date Mark to around AD 70, and Matthew and Luke to AD 80–90, though estimates vary. The Gospels were written in Koine Greek by anonymous authors; the traditional attributions to the apostles Matthew and John Mark and to Luke the physician appear in the manuscripts' titles and in early church tradition.

Distinctive emphases

Though parallel, each Synoptic Gospel has its own portrait: Mark is the shortest and most urgent, emphasising Jesus's suffering and the "messianic secret"; Matthew presents Jesus as a new Moses and teacher, with five great discourses; Luke stresses compassion for the poor and marginalised, the role of the Holy Spirit and prayer, and a salvation history extending into the Acts of the Apostles.
