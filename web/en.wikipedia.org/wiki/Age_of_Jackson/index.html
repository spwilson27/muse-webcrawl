Age of Jackson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Andrew Jackson, central figure of the era (image placeholder)

The Age of Jackson, also known as the Jacksonian era or the era of Jacksonian democracy, is a periodization in United States history corresponding roughly to the presidency of Andrew Jackson (1829–1837) and the influence of his political movement, extending in broader usage from about 1824 into the 1840s. It was characterized by the expansion of suffrage to nearly all white men, vigorous party organization, and the assertion of the common man against entrenched elites.

Key themes

  • Expanded suffrage — states removed property requirements for voting, so that by the 1830s nearly universal white male suffrage prevailed.
  • The spoils system — rotation in office and appointment of loyal partisans to government posts.
  • Party politics — the Democratic Party coalesced around Jackson while opponents formed the Whig Party, creating the Second Party System, with newspapers, conventions, and mass campaigns.
  • Indian removal — the Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to the forced relocation of southeastern tribes, including the Cherokee on the Trail of Tears.
  • The Bank War — Jackson's veto of the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States (1832) and removal of federal deposits, which contributed to the Panic of 1837.
  • Nullification — confrontation with South Carolina over the tariff, resolved by compromise in 1833.

Historiography

Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr.'s The Age of Jackson (1945) framed the era as a democratic movement of farmers and workers against business interests, winning the Pulitzer Prize. Later historians emphasized the era's deep contradictions: the expansion of democracy for white men coincided with the dispossession of Native Americans, the hardening of slavery, and market revolution that benefited speculators. Modern scholarship treats Jacksonian democracy as simultaneously a democratizing and an exclusionary, white-supremacist movement.

Legacy

The era established the modern American political campaign, the expectation that the presidency speaks for the people, and durable tensions between popular democracy, executive power, and minority rights.
