Okunoin

Okunoin (奥之院)
LocationMount Kōya, Wakayama Prefecture, Japan
DenominationShingon Buddhism
Associated figureKūkai (Kōbō Daishi)
Notable featureLargest cemetery in Japan, with over 200,000 tombs

Okunoin (奥之院, "inner sanctuary") is the mausoleum of Kūkai (posthumously Kōbō Daishi), the founder of Shingon Buddhism, located on Mount Kōya in Wakayama Prefecture, Japan. It is surrounded by the largest cemetery in Japan and is one of the most sacred sites in Japanese Buddhism, forming part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing "Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range".

The mausoleum

According to Shingon belief, Kūkai did not die in 835 but entered nyūjō, an eternal meditative state, awaiting the coming of Maitreya, the future Buddha. His mausoleum at Okunoin is therefore treated as a living presence: monks still bring him meals twice daily in a ritual that has continued for more than a thousand years. Photography and talking are restricted in the innermost area before the mausoleum, and visitors are expected to bow and behave reverently.

The cemetery

The approach to the mausoleum runs for about two kilometres through a forest of towering cedar trees, some of them many centuries old, lined with more than 200,000 granite tombs, stupas and memorials. Over the centuries, major figures of Japanese history — including feudal lords such as Oda Nobunaga, Takeda Shingen and Uesugi Kenshin, as well as merchants and modern corporations — have erected graves and cenotaphs here in the hope of resting close to Kōbō Daishi. Moss-covered five-ringed stupas (gorintō) are everywhere along the path.

Notable features include the Ichinohashi bridge at the entrance, where visitors bow toward the mausoleum before entering; the hall of lanterns (Tōrō-dō), containing thousands of donated lanterns, some said to have burned for centuries; and memorials erected by companies, including a monument shaped like a rocket and memorials to pest-control and coffee companies.

Pilgrimage

Okunoin is the finishing point of the Shikoku Pilgrimage tradition in a spiritual sense — pilgrims traditionally visit Mount Kōya after completing the circuit of 88 temples associated with Kūkai — and is itself a major destination for pilgrims and visitors staying in temple lodgings (shukubō) on Mount Kōya.
