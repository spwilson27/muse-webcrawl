The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a condition in which the body makes or releases too much antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin), causing the kidneys to retain water and the blood sodium concentration to fall (hyponatraemia). It is one of the commonest causes of hyponatraemia in hospitalised patients. The condition was described in 1957 by William Schwartz and Frederic Bartter, whose names it also bears.

Causes

ADH may be produced ectopically by tumours — classically small cell lung cancer — or released in excess by the posterior pituitary in response to central nervous system disorders (stroke, haemorrhage, infection, trauma), pulmonary disease (pneumonia, tuberculosis), and a wide range of drugs, including SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide and MDMA. Postoperative states and nausea are also common triggers.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic criteria (after Bartter and Schwartz) include: low serum osmolality (<275 mOsm/kg) with hyponatraemia; inappropriately concentrated urine (osmolality >100 mOsm/kg) despite the dilute blood; clinical euvolemia (no signs of fluid overload or depletion); urinary sodium usually >30 mmol/L on a normal salt and water intake; and normal renal, adrenal and thyroid function. Causes such as diuretic use must be excluded.

Treatment

The mainstays are treating the underlying cause and restricting free water intake. Chronic asymptomatic cases are managed conservatively; severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, reduced consciousness) is a medical emergency treated with hypertonic saline, with correction rates carefully limited to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. Options in selected patients include oral salt with loop diuretics, urea, demeclocycline, and vasopressin receptor antagonists ("vaptans" such as tolvaptan).
