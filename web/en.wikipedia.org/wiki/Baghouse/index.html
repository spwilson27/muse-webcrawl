Baghouse

A baghouse, also known as a baghouse filter, bag filter, or fabric filter, is an air pollution control device and dust collector that removes particulates or gas released from commercial processes out of the air. Power plants, steel mills, pharmaceutical producers, food manufacturers, chemical producers and other industrial companies often use baghouses to control emission of air pollutants. Baghouses came into widespread use in the late 1970s after the invention of high-temperature fabrics capable of surviving the conditions of hot gas streams.

Operation

Unlike electrostatic precipitators, in which performance can vary significantly depending on process and electrical conditions, a properly functioning baghouse typically has a particulate collection efficiency of 99% or better, even for very small particles. Dust-laden gas enters the baghouse and passes through fabric bags that act as filters. The bags may be of woven or felted cotton, synthetic, or glass-fibre material suitable for the operating temperature. Particulate builds up on the fabric as a dust cake, which itself becomes the principal filtering medium.

Cleaning methods

Baghouses are divided by the method used to clean the bags:

  • Mechanical shaker — bags are shaken by a motor-driven mechanism, typically when filtration is stopped.
  • Reverse air — a gentle flow of clean air in the opposite direction collapses and flexes the bags to dislodge the cake.
  • Pulse jet — a short burst of compressed air is directed into each bag, rapidly inflating it and knocking off the dust cake; cleaning can occur while the unit remains online.

Collected dust falls into a hopper below and is removed by conveyor or rotary valve for disposal or recovery.

Design considerations

Key parameters include the air-to-cloth ratio (the volume of gas filtered per unit area of fabric), pressure drop across the filter, gas temperature, and the chemistry of the stream. Common bag materials include polyester, polypropylene, fibreglass, aramid, and PTFE membranes, selected for temperature and chemical resistance. Baghouses cannot operate in moist environments where condensation would blind the bags, and some dusts pose fire or explosion hazards requiring special precautions.

Applications

Baghouses are used in cement plants, asphalt plants, grain handling, woodworking, mining, smelting, and coal-fired boilers, among many other industries, wherever particulate emissions must be reduced to comply with clean-air regulations.
