The Palatine Hill (Latin: Collis Palatium or Mons Palatinus; Italian: Palatino) is the most central of the seven hills of Rome and one of the most ancient parts of the city. According to Roman mythology it is the place where Romulus founded Rome, and it became the site of the imperial palaces from Augustus onward — so closely identified with imperial residence that the word "palace" itself derives from Palatium.

Mythology and early history

Roman tradition held that the twins Romulus and Remus were raised by a she-wolf in the Lupercal cave at the foot of the Palatine, and that Romulus chose the hill for his new city in 753 BC. Archaeology has found Iron Age huts on the hill dating to around the 9th–8th centuries BC, lending the legend a degree of plausibility. In the Republic, the Palatine was a fashionable residential district — Cicero, Crassus, and Mark Antony all had houses there — and it was home to temples including that of Magna Mater (Cybele), dedicated in 191 BC.

Imperial palaces

Augustus, who was born on the Palatine, made his comparatively modest home there and built the Temple of Apollo Palatinus beside it. Tiberius and Caligula extended the residence toward the Forum. The vast permanent palace complex was built by Domitian: the Domus Flavia, the ceremonial wing, and the Domus Augustana, the residential wing, designed by the architect Rabirius, together with a stadium-shaped garden. Later emperors through the Severans enlarged the complex, extending platforms toward the Circus Maximus and the Aventine. The palace remained the principal imperial residence until the later Empire, and parts were reused into the early Middle Ages.

Later history and archaeology

In the Middle Ages the hill was occupied by churches, monasteries, and the fortress-palace of the Frangipane family. In the 16th century the Farnese family created the Farnese Gardens on the north of the hill, among the earliest botanical gardens in Europe. Systematic excavations since the 19th century — notably under Rodolfo Lanciani, Giacomo Boni, and Alfonso Bartoli — have exposed the palace ruins, the House of Augustus with its painted rooms, and the House of Livia. The Palatine forms part of the archaeological park with the Colosseum and Roman Forum and of Rome's UNESCO World Heritage listing.
