Child abduction

Child abduction is the unauthorised removal or retention of a minor from the custody of the child's parents or legal guardians. It takes several distinct forms — abduction by a parent or family member (by far the most common), abduction by acquaintances, and the rare "stereotypical kidnapping" by a stranger — and is addressed by criminal law and, for cross-border cases, by the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction.

Types

Parental abduction

Most child abductions are committed by a parent, usually in the context of custody disputes. When a child is taken across an international border, the 1980 Hague Abduction Convention — adopted by more than 100 countries — provides a civil procedure for the child's prompt return to their habitual residence, subject to narrow exceptions such as grave risk of harm.

Abduction by acquaintances and strangers

Abductions by acquaintances often involve adolescents. Stranger abductions of young children are statistically rare but receive intense attention; in the United States, studies by the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children estimate roughly 100–115 "stereotypical kidnappings" occur per year, defined as abductions by a stranger or slight acquaintance involving detention overnight, transport, ransom or intent to keep or harm the child.

Response systems

Alert systems such as the U.S. AMBER Alert (created in 1996 after the murder of Amber Hagerman) rapidly broadcast information about serious abductions. Missing-children organisations, rapid police response, and public awareness campaigns are considered the most effective safeguards. Prevention education has shifted from "stranger danger" towards teaching children about deceptive lures and empowering them to seek help.
