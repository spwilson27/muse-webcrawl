Flavonoid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Flavonoids (from Latin flavus, "yellow") are a class of polyphenolic secondary metabolites found in plants. More than 5,000 naturally occurring flavonoids have been identified. They share a common 15-carbon skeleton (C6–C3–C6) consisting of two phenyl rings joined by a heterocyclic pyran ring, and they include the pigments responsible for many of the yellow, red, blue, and purple colors of flowers, fruits, and leaves.

Classification

Major subclasses include flavonols (e.g., quercetin, kaempferol), flavones (apigenin, luteolin), flavanones (hesperidin in citrus), flavan-3-ols (catechin and epicatechin, abundant in tea and cocoa, and their polymers the proanthocyanidins), anthocyanins (the red-blue pigments of berries and autumn leaves), and isoflavonoids (genistein and daidzein in soy, which act as phytoestrogens).

Functions in plants

In plants, flavonoids screen ultraviolet light, attract pollinators and seed dispersers through color, deter herbivores and pathogens, modulate auxin transport, and serve in signaling — notably in the nodulation of legume roots by nitrogen-fixing rhizobia.

Diet and health

Flavonoids are consumed in tea, wine, cocoa, apples, onions, berries, and citrus fruit. They show antioxidant activity in vitro, but their bioavailability in humans is low and they are extensively metabolized, so the extent of any direct antioxidant effect in the body is debated. Epidemiology associates flavonoid-rich diets with modestly reduced cardiovascular risk, but health authorities such as the EFSA have rejected most specific health claims for flavonoid supplements for lack of established cause and effect.
