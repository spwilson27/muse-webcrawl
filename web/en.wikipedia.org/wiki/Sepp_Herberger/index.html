Josef "Sepp" Herberger (28 March 1897 – 28 April 1977) was a German football player and manager. He is best remembered as the coach of the West German national team that won the 1954 FIFA World Cup, defeating the heavily favoured Hungarian "Golden Team" in the final—a match remembered in Germany as the Miracle of Bern.

Playing career

Herberger played as a forward for clubs including Waldhof Mannheim, VfR Mannheim, and Tennis Borussia Berlin. He earned three caps for the German national team in the early 1920s but achieved far greater fame as a coach than as a player.

Coaching career

Herberger studied at the German Sport University in Cologne under Otto Nerz, whom he succeeded as national team coach (Reichstrainer) in 1936. He led Germany at the 1936 Berlin Olympics and the 1938 World Cup, tournaments that ended in disappointment. After the Second World War, he became the first coach of the West German national team in 1950.

His greatest triumph came at the 1954 World Cup in Switzerland. After losing 8–3 to Hungary in the group stage (a match in which Herberger rested key players and experimented with tactics), West Germany met the Hungarians again in the final. Trailing 2–0 within eight minutes, the Germans rallied to win 3–2, completing one of the greatest upsets in World Cup history. Herberger's meticulous preparation, squad rotation, and motivational skill were widely credited.

Herberger remained in charge through the 1958 World Cup (fourth place) and the 1962 World Cup, retiring in 1964 after 28 years connected with the national team. He was succeeded by his assistant, Helmut Schön.

Famous sayings

Herberger is remembered in Germany for pithy footballing aphorisms, among them:

  • "The ball is round, the game lasts ninety minutes."
  • "A match is won in the mind." (often paraphrased)
  • "After the game is before the game."
  • "The next opponent is always the hardest."

Legacy

The Sepp Herberger Award, given by the German Football Association (DFB) for outstanding work in youth football and social engagement, is named after him. He is regarded as a founding figure of modern German football coaching.
