Animal navigation

Animal navigation is the ability of many animals to find their way accurately without maps or instruments. Birds such as the Arctic tern undertake annual round-trip migrations of tens of thousands of kilometres, sea turtles cross whole oceans to return to their natal beaches, and salmon return from the sea to the streams where they hatched. Research into the cues and mechanisms involved spans behaviour, physiology, and neurobiology.

Cues

Animals use a wide range of cues: the position of the sun and a circadian clock to compensate for its movement; the pattern of polarised light; the stars, whose rotation centre reveals the pole; landmarks; odours; ocean currents; and infrasound. Many species also possess a magnetic sense. Migratory birds can detect the inclination of the Earth's magnetic field, and the leading hypothesis involves a light-dependent chemical compass based on cryptochrome proteins in the retina.

Map and compass

Navigation is often modelled as a two-step process: a "map" step, in which the animal determines its position relative to a goal, and a "compass" step, in which it sets and maintains a bearing. Young birds on first migration appear to follow inherited clock-and-compass programmes of direction and duration, whereas experienced migrants can perform true navigation, correcting for experimental displacement.

Examples

Homing pigeons combine magnetic, olfactory, visual, and acoustic cues. Spiny lobsters have been shown to possess a magnetic map capable of fixing their position in the ocean. Honeybees communicate navigational information in the waggle dance and use optic flow to gauge distance, while desert ants of the genus Cataglyphis integrate their path using a celestial compass and a stride-counting odometer.
