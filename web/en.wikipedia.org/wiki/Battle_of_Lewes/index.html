The Battle of Lewes was fought on 14 May 1264, near Lewes in Sussex, between the forces of Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester, and the royal army of Henry III. It was the high point of the Second Barons' War: de Montfort defeated an army roughly twice the size of his own and captured both the king and his son, the Lord Edward, making himself the effective ruler of England.

Background

Henry III's repudiation of the Provisions of Oxford and the annulment of reform by the Mise of Amiens drove the barons to arms. De Montfort marched from London to confront the king at Lewes, where Henry occupied the town and its priory and castle, having rejected de Montfort's final peace overtures.

Battle

De Montfort approached by night over the South Downs and attacked at dawn. His right wing, including London militia, was routed by a charge led by Edward, who pursued too far from the field. Meanwhile de Montfort's main body, with a reserve he personally led from high ground, broke the royalist centre under Richard of Cornwall — who was captured after taking refuge in a windmill — and forced Henry back into Lewes Priory. When Edward returned to find the battle lost, he surrendered on terms the following day.

Aftermath

The resulting Mise of Lewes established a council of nine and a triumvirate governing in the king's name, with Edward held as hostage. De Montfort summoned the parliament of 1265, notable for including representatives of the boroughs. His rule lasted until Edward's escape and de Montfort's death at Evesham in August 1265.
