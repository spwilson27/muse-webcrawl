Subconscious

The subconscious is the part of the mind that is not currently in conscious awareness. The word, from the Latin sub ("below") and conscire ("to be aware"), was popularized in the 19th century and is often used loosely in everyday speech to refer to mental processes that occur outside conscious attention — habits, memories, automatic skills, and suppressed feelings that influence behavior without being experienced directly.

Use in psychology

Psychologists generally prefer the more precise term unconscious, because "subconscious" lacks a rigorous technical definition and does not appear as a formal construct in most scientific models of the mind. Sigmund Freud, who developed the topographical model of the psyche, used the related concept of the preconscious for material that is not currently conscious but can be readily brought into awareness, reserving unconscious for repressed material that cannot. Freud himself discouraged the term "subconscious" as ambiguous.

Pierre Janet had earlier used subconscious in his clinical work on hysteria and dissociation in the late 19th century, and the term gained wide currency through the New Thought movement and 20th-century self-help literature, where "the subconscious mind" is often portrayed as a powerful reservoir that can be programmed through affirmation and visualization.

Modern views

Contemporary cognitive psychology studies many phenomena once lumped under the subconscious — implicit memory, priming, automaticity, and nonconscious perception — under the framework of the cognitive unconscious. These processes are understood as ordinary neural computation rather than as the work of a separate sub-personal agent, though the popular usage of "subconscious" persists in everyday language.
