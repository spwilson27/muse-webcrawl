Taxco de Alarcón (usually called Taxco) is a city in the Mexican state of Guerrero, in the Sierra Madre del Sur roughly 170 km southwest of Mexico City. Built on steep hillsides in a former silver-mining district, it is famed for its well-preserved colonial architecture, its baroque church of Santa Prisca, and above all for its silversmithing — Taxco is widely regarded as the silver capital of Mexico.

History

The area was inhabited by the Tlahuica people before the Spanish conquest; the name derives from the Nahuatl Tlachco, "place of the ball game". Silver was mined nearby from the 1520s, making the district one of New Spain's earliest mining centres. Its greatest colonial benefactor was the French-born miner José de la Borda, who funded the magnificent pink-stone church of Santa Prisca (1751–1758), one of the finest examples of Mexican Churrigueresque baroque. Mining declined in the 19th and early 20th centuries as the richest veins were exhausted.

The silver revival

In 1929 the American architect and designer William Spratling settled in Taxco and founded the Taller de las Delicias workshop, training local artisans and reviving Taxco silverwork with designs inspired by pre-Hispanic art. Spratling's success spawned hundreds of workshops and made Taxco jewellery famous internationally; he is honoured as the "Father of Mexican Silver". The city hosts an annual National Silver Fair in late November and early December.

Sights

Taxco's cobbled streets, whitewashed houses with red tile roofs, and wrought-iron balconies climb around the zócalo (Plaza Borda) and Santa Prisca. Other landmarks include the Casa Borda cultural centre, the William Spratling Museum, the ex-Convent of San Bernardino, and a cable car (teleférico) to the Monte Taxco viewpoint. Holy Week processions in Taxco are among Mexico's most elaborate. The federal government has designated the city a Pueblo Mágico and its historic centre a national monument zone.
