Salem witch trials

The Salem witch trials were a series of hearings and prosecutions of people accused of witchcraft in colonial Massachusetts between February 1692 and May 1693. More than 200 people were accused. Thirty people were found guilty, nineteen of whom were executed by hanging (fourteen women and five men). Another man, Giles Corey, died under torture after he refused to enter a plea, and at least five people died in jail.

Background

The trials took place amid the stresses of King William's War on the frontier, factional disputes within Salem Village (now Danvers) over its minister, Samuel Parris, and a long Puritan tradition of belief in witchcraft as a real and capital crime. In early 1692, Parris's daughter Betty and niece Abigail Williams began having fits that local physicians could not explain, and under questioning the girls accused three women: Tituba, an enslaved woman in the Parris household; Sarah Good; and Sarah Osborne. Tituba's detailed confession — she claimed the Devil had appeared to her — seemed to validate the accusations and ignited a widening hunt.

Proceedings

Governor William Phips created a special Court of Oyer and Terminer in May 1692. The court admitted "spectral evidence" — testimony that the accused's spirit or specter had tormented the afflicted — which ministers such as Increase Mather soon argued was unreliable, since the Devil might assume the shape of an innocent person. The first to be executed was Bridget Bishop, hanged on 10 June 1692; hangings followed through September. In October, Phips dissolved the special court after accusations touched prominent families, and its successor court, which excluded spectral evidence, acquitted or reprieved nearly all remaining defendants. Phips pardoned the last of the condemned in May 1693.

Aftermath and legacy

Within a few years, participants publicly repented: jurors signed a statement of regret, Judge Samuel Sewall made a public apology in 1697, and in 1702 the trials were declared unlawful. The colony later reversed the convictions and paid restitution to victims' families, though some names were cleared only in the 20th and 21st centuries. The episode became the archetypal American example of mass hysteria and scapegoating; the phrase "witch hunt" entered political vocabulary, and Arthur Miller's 1953 play The Crucible recast the trials as an allegory of McCarthyism. Salem today commemorates the victims with memorials, while its witch-themed tourism trades on the notoriety of 1692.
