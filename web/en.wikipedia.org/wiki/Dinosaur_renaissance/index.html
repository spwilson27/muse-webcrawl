Dinosaur renaissance

The dinosaur renaissance is the name given to a period of renewed scientific and public interest in dinosaurs beginning in the late 1960s, which overturned the older view of dinosaurs as sluggish, swamp-bound, cold-blooded reptiles destined for extinction. It led to the modern picture of dinosaurs as active, often warm-blooded animals, closely related to — and in the case of birds, directly continuous with — living animals.

The shift is usually dated from John Ostrom's 1969 description of the agile predator Deinonychus and his subsequent work arguing that birds descended from small theropod dinosaurs. Robert Bakker popularized the new view in articles and in his 1986 book The Dinosaur Heresies, championing dinosaur endothermy, while artists such as Gregory S. Paul produced dynamic new restorations.

Consequences of the renaissance included a surge in dinosaur research and museum attendance, the feathered-dinosaur discoveries from Liaoning, China in the 1990s, the cladistic reclassification of birds as living dinosaurs, and the immense popular success of Jurassic Park (1993), whose fast, intelligent dinosaurs embodied the new consensus for a mass audience.
