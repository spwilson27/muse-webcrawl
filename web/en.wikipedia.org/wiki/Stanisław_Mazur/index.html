Stanisław Mazur

Stanisław Mieczysław Mazur (1 January 1905, Lwów – 5 November 1981, Warsaw) was a Polish mathematician, a leading member of the Lwów School of Mathematics, known for fundamental contributions to functional analysis, geometry, and approximation theory.

Life and career

Mazur studied mathematics at Jan Kazimierz University in Lwów, where he was a student of Stefan Banach; he received his doctorate in 1932 under Banach's supervision. He worked at Lwów University and the Lwów Polytechnic, frequenting the Scottish Café, where members of the school — including Banach, Hugo Steinhaus, Stanisław Ulam, and Juliusz Schauder — recorded open problems in the famous Scottish Book. After the Second World War he moved to Warsaw, where he became a professor at the University of Warsaw and a member of the Polish Academy of Sciences. He also served as a deputy in the Polish parliament (the Sejm) in the postwar years.

Mathematical work

Mazur is remembered for several results that bear his name or remain standard tools. With Banach he is associated with the Banach–Mazur theorem (every separable Banach space embeds isometrically into C([0,1])) and the Banach–Mazur game in topology. Mazur's theorem in functional analysis states that if a sequence in a normed space converges weakly, then its convex combinations converge in norm to the same limit — a cornerstone of convex analysis in infinite dimensions. He made fundamental advances on the basis problem and computability of functions; his work on "computable analysis" anticipated later developments. With Ulam, he proved in 1932 that any surjective isometry between real normed spaces is affine (the Mazur–Ulam theorem).

A celebrated episode in the Scottish Book: in 1936 Mazur offered a live goose as the prize for solving the problem of whether every separable Banach space has a Schauder basis. In 1972 Per Enflo solved the problem (in the negative) and collected the goose from Mazur in a ceremony broadcast across Poland.
