Wheellock

A wheellock, wheel-lock or wheellock mechanism is a friction-wheel ignition mechanism used to fire a firearm. Developed around 1500, it was the first self-igniting mechanism for firearms, succeeding the matchlock and preceding the flintlock. It works by spinning a spring-loaded steel wheel against a piece of iron pyrite to generate sparks that ignite the priming charge.

Operation

Before firing, the shooter winds the wheel's mainspring with a separate key (spanner). A piece of pyrite is clamped in the jaws of the "dog" (cock) and lowered onto the wheel. Pulling the trigger releases the wheel, which spins against the pyrite, showering sparks into the priming pan and firing the charge — an action strikingly similar to a modern cigarette lighter.

History

A drawing of a wheellock appears in Leonardo da Vinci's Codex Atlanticus (c. 1500), and the earliest surviving wheellock firearms are German and north Italian, from the first decades of the 16th century. Because the mechanism was complex and expensive, wheellocks were chiefly used in cavalry pistols, sporting guns and prestige weapons rather than mass infantry muskets, where the cheaper matchlock persisted. The wheellock allowed, for the first time, a loaded firearm to be carried concealed and fired instantly with one hand, prompting early firearms legislation; Emperor Maximilian I banned their manufacture in parts of the Holy Roman Empire in 1517.

Decline

From the later 16th century the simpler snaphance and then the true flintlock offered the same convenience at lower cost, and by about 1650 the wheellock had largely disappeared from military service, surviving only on high-grade sporting arms.
