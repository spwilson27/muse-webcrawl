Sanskrit grammar

Sanskrit grammar is the set of rules governing the structure of Sanskrit, the classical language of India. Its study is traditionally attributed to the grammarian Pāṇini, whose Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters"), composed around the 4th century BCE, remains one of the most sophisticated grammatical descriptions of any language produced in the ancient world.

Phonology

Sanskrit has a rich consonant system organized by place of articulation: velars, palatals, retroflexes, dentals, and labials, each with voiced, voiceless, aspirated, and nasal counterparts. Vowels distinguish short and long quantities (a/ā, i/ī, u/ū), and the language also has the vocalic liquids ṛ and ḷ and the diphthongs e, ai, o, au. External sandhi rules govern how sounds change at word boundaries.

Nouns and cases

Nouns are declined for three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), three numbers (singular, dual, plural), and eight cases: nominative, accusative, instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative, and vocative. Adjectives agree with their nouns in gender, number, and case.

Verbs

Verbs are conjugated for three persons, three numbers, and an elaborate system of tenses and moods organized around present, imperfect, perfect, aorist, and future stems. Sanskrit verbs belong to ten conjugational classes in the present system. The language also makes extensive use of participles, absolutives (gerunds), and an infinitive.

Word formation

Sanskrit is famous for its productive compounding: words can be joined into long compounds (samāsa) of several types, including dvandva (copulative), tatpuruṣa (determinative), bahuvrīhi (possessive), and avyayībhāva (adverbial) compounds.
