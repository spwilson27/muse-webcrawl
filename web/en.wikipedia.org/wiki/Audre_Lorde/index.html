Audre Lorde

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Audre Lorde (born Audrey Geraldine Lorde; February 18, 1934 – November 17, 1992) was an American writer, feminist, professor, philosopher, intersectional activist, poet and civil rights activist. She was a self-described "black, lesbian, feminist, socialist, mother, warrior, poet" who dedicated her life and talents to confronting different forms of injustice, as she believed there could be "no hierarchy of oppressions" among "those who share the goals of liberation and a workable future for our children."

As a poet, she is best known for technical mastery and emotional expression, as well as her poems that express anger and outrage at civil and social injustices she observed throughout her life. She received national and international recognition for her works as a celebrated poet and activist. As an author and essayist, her prose addressed themes of civil rights, black studies, feminism, lesbianism, illness, disability, the exploration of black female identity and the intersections of race, gender, class and sexuality.

Early life

Lorde was born Audrey Geraldine Lorde in Harlem, New York City, to Caribbean immigrants Byron and Linda Gertrude Belmar Lorde, who settled in Harlem. Her mother was of mixed ancestry but could "pass" for white, a source of friction in the family, while her father was a shade darker. She grew up in Manhattan and attended Hunter College High School, a school for intellectually gifted girls, where she first began writing poetry. After struggling with early education and nearsightedness, she published her first poem in Seventeen magazine while still in high school.

Career

Lorde worked as a librarian, earning a master's degree in library science from Columbia University in 1961, and held posts at the Mount Vernon Public Library and as head librarian at Town School in New York City. In the late 1960s she began teaching, serving as poet-in-residence at Tougaloo College in Mississippi, and she later taught at John Jay College, Lehman College and Hunter College. Her first volume of poems, The First Cities (1968), was followed by Cables to Rage (1970) and From a Land Where Other People Live (1973), which was nominated for a National Book Award.

Her most influential prose works include The Cancer Journals (1980), drawn from her diaries of living with breast cancer, which won the American Library Association Gay Caucus Book Award; Zami: A New Spelling of My Name (1982), a "biomythography" of her childhood and coming of age; and Sister Outsider (1984), a collection of essays including the celebrated "The Master's Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master's House" and "The Transformation of Silence into Language and Action."

Later life and legacy

Lorde was diagnosed with breast cancer in 1978 and later with liver cancer; she died on November 17, 1992, in St. Croix, U.S. Virgin Islands, where she had lived for her final years. She served as New York State's poet laureate from 1991 until her death. Her work has remained foundational to intersectional feminism and Black feminist thought, and her call to transform silence into speech continues to be widely quoted in activist movements.
