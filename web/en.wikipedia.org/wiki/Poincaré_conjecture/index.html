In mathematics, the Poincaré conjecture is a theorem about the characterisation of the three-dimensional sphere (3-sphere). It states that every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. Informally: if a closed three-dimensional space has the property that every loop in it can be continuously shrunk to a point, then the space is just a three-dimensional sphere.

History

Henri Poincaré posed the question in 1904, near the end of a paper founding algebraic topology — having, embarrassingly, first claimed the result for a homology-based version and then discovered his own counterexample, the Poincaré homology sphere. The conjecture resisted proof for nearly a century and became one of the most famous open problems in mathematics, named by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000 as one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a one-million-dollar prize. Higher-dimensional analogues were settled earlier: Stephen Smale proved the generalised conjecture in dimensions five and above in the 1960s, and Michael Freedman solved the four-dimensional case in 1982.

Perelman's proof

The proof came through Richard Hamilton's programme using Ricci flow, an equation that deforms a manifold's metric in the manner of heat diffusion. Between November 2002 and July 2003 the Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman posted three preprints on the arXiv sketching a complete proof, including the broader geometrisation conjecture of William Thurston, of which Poincaré's conjecture is a special case. After several years of verification by independent teams, the mathematical community confirmed the proof. Perelman was awarded the Fields Medal in 2006 and the Millennium Prize in 2010 — and declined both, withdrawing from mathematics and public life.
