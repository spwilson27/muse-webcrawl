Thermal cooking

Thermal cooking, also called retained-heat cooking or, after its classic apparatus, haybox cooking, is a method of cooking in which food is brought to a boil (or to cooking temperature) and then placed in a heavily insulated container, where it continues to cook slowly in its own retained heat without any further input of fuel or electricity. A modern thermal cooker consists of an inner cooking pot, usually of stainless steel, that nests inside an outer vacuum-insulated vessel similar in principle to a thermos flask. The method saves energy, keeps the kitchen cool, and allows food to be prepared hours in advance and held hot.

History

The technique is ancient — versions involving pots buried in hot earth or wrapped in cloth are found in many cultures — but it was widely popularised in Europe in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as the haybox (German Heukiste): a wooden box packed with hay, straw, feathers or newspaper into which the boiled pot was snugly fitted. Hayboxes were promoted in both World Wars as fuel-saving devices, and appeared in wartime cookery advice such as Britain's Ministry of Food leaflets. From the 1990s, vacuum-insulated stainless-steel thermal cookers manufactured in Japan and Taiwan revived the method for the modern kitchen and for camping and emergency use.

Method and safety

Food is typically boiled on the stove for 10–30 minutes, depending on the dish, then the lidded pot is sealed into the insulated outer container for one to eight hours. Soups, stews, curries, rice, porridge and braised meats suit the method well because their cooking is a matter of time at temperature rather than of technique. Because the food slowly cools, it must remain above about 60 °C (140 °F) until eaten to inhibit bacterial growth; quality thermal cookers keep food above this temperature for six hours or more. Reheating to a boil before serving is recommended for food held for long periods.

Advantages

Fuel savings of 70–80% compared with conventional simmering are commonly claimed, since only the initial heating uses energy. The cooker needs no power during the long cooking phase (making it useful in off-grid and disaster-relief settings), cannot boil over or burn, and frees the cook's attention. Limitations include the need to plan ahead, poor results with foods that need browning or crisping, and the food-safety constraint above.
