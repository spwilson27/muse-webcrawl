Mercury Prize

The Mercury Prize is awarded annually for the best British or Irish album.

The Mercury Prize is an annual music prize awarded for the best album released by a musical act from the United Kingdom or Ireland. It was established by the British Phonographic Industry and the British Association of Record Dealers in 1992 as an alternative to the BRIT Awards, and takes its name from a former sponsor, the telecoms company Mercury Communications.

Format

A panel of musicians, critics, broadcasters and industry figures selects a shortlist of twelve "Albums of the Year", announced in the summer, from which a single winner is chosen at an awards ceremony in the autumn. The winner receives a cash prize (in recent years £25,000) along with the trophy. Any genre is eligible, and the shortlist traditionally mixes mainstream acts with jazz, folk, classical-crossover and experimental records.

History and notable winners

Primal Scream's Screamadelica won the inaugural prize in 1992. Subsequent winners include Suede, Portishead (Dummy), Pulp (Different Class), Gomez, Badly Drawn Boy, Dizzee Rascal (Boy in da Corner), Franz Ferdinand, Arctic Monkeys (Whatever People Say I Am, That's What I'm Not), Elbow, The xx, Alt-J, James Blake, Young Fathers, Skepta (Konnichiwa), Sampha, Wolf Alice, Dave (Psychodrama), Arlo Parks, Little Simz and Ezra Collective. PJ Harvey is the only artist to have won twice (2001 and 2011).

Reputation

The prize has a reputation for unpredictability and for champions outside the commercial mainstream; winners often see a sharp sales boost, though folklore about a "Mercury curse" on subsequent careers persists. Critics periodically question the judging process and the tokenism of some shortlist slots, while defenders credit the prize with broadening the audience for adventurous British and Irish music.
