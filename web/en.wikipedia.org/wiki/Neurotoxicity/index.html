Neurotoxicity

Neurotoxicity is a form of toxicity in which a biological, chemical, or physical agent produces an adverse effect on the structure or function of the central and/or peripheral nervous system. It occurs when exposure to a substance — specifically, a neurotoxin or neurotoxicant — alters the normal activity of the nervous system in such a way as to cause damage to nervous tissue. This can eventually disrupt or even kill neurons, the cells that transmit and process signals in the brain and other parts of the nervous system.

Agents

Neurotoxic agents include heavy metals such as lead, mercury, manganese, and aluminium; organic solvents; pesticides, especially organophosphates; gases such as carbon monoxide; many chemotherapy drugs; and substances of abuse including ethanol and methamphetamine. Natural neurotoxins produced by organisms — tetrodotoxin from pufferfish, botulinum toxin from Clostridium botulinum, and numerous snake and spider venoms — are potent examples evolved to disable the nervous systems of prey or predators.

Mechanisms and effects

Mechanisms include blockade or overactivation of ion channels and neurotransmitter receptors, inhibition of enzymes such as acetylcholinesterase, oxidative stress, excitotoxicity mediated by glutamate, and interference with axonal transport. Effects depend on the agent, dose, and duration of exposure, and range from acute symptoms — headache, dizziness, seizures, paralysis — to chronic outcomes such as peripheral neuropathy, cognitive impairment, movement disorders resembling Parkinson's disease (as in MPTP poisoning), and developmental disorders after prenatal lead or alcohol exposure.

Assessment and treatment

Evaluation combines exposure history, neurological examination, neuropsychological testing, nerve conduction studies, and imaging. Treatment is primarily removal from exposure and supportive care, supplemented in specific poisonings by antidotes such as chelation therapy for heavy metals, atropine and pralidoxime for organophosphates, and antivenoms for envenomation. Unlike neurodegenerative diseases, which arise from intrinsic progressive processes, neurotoxicity by definition has an external cause.
