Lebanon–Syria border

The Lebanon–Syria border is the international border between the Lebanese Republic and the Syrian Arab Republic, running for about 394 kilometres (245 miles) from the Mediterranean coast in the north to the tripoint with Israel in the south. It is one of the few borders in the Levant that has never been fully officially demarcated, a legacy of the Sykes–Picot Agreement and the French Mandate period.

Geography

The border begins at the mouth of the Nahr al-Kabir river on the Mediterranean, east of the Lebanese city of Tripoli, and runs generally southeast through the Akkar plain. It then follows the watershed of the Anti-Lebanon Mountains, whose peaks form much of the frontier, before descending toward the Beqaa Valley. In the south it reaches the area of Mount Hermon (Jabal al-Sheikh) and the disputed Shebaa Farms near the Israeli-occupied Golan Heights.

History

The frontier was drawn in 1920 when France, holding the League of Nations mandate for Syria and Lebanon, proclaimed the State of Greater Lebanon. Because the line was based on administrative rather than surveyed boundaries, numerous villages and farms found themselves split between the two states. After both countries gained independence in the 1940s, demarcation was repeatedly postponed.

Border issues

The porous border has been central to smuggling, arms transfers, and cross-border movement during the Lebanese Civil War and the Syrian civil war that began in 2011. Lebanon hosts over a million Syrian refugees, many of whom entered across informal crossings. Official crossings include Masnaa (on the Beirut–Damascus highway), Abboudieh, and Arida.
