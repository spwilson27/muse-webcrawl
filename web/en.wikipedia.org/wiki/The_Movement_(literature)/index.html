The Movement (literature)

Period1950s
CountryEngland
Key figuresPhilip Larkin, Kingsley Amis, Donald Davie, Thom Gunn, Elizabeth Jennings, John Wain

The Movement was a term coined in 1954 by J. D. Scott, literary editor of The Spectator, to describe a group of English poets of the 1950s, including Philip Larkin, Kingsley Amis, Donald Davie, D. J. Enright, John Wain, Elizabeth Jennings, Thom Gunn, and Robert Conquest. The term was also sometimes applied to associated novelists and critics.

Characteristics

Movement writers reacted against the neo-romanticism and obscurity of the 1940s poets such as Dylan Thomas. They favored clarity, traditional verse forms, restraint, wit, and a tone of detached, often ironic observation of ordinary postwar life. Larkin's dictum about poetry as "the lyrical advancement of a narrative idea" captured the group's preference for intelligibility. Many of the poets were university academics, and their work reflected the lower-middle-class, scholarship-boy background of a generation shaped by wartime and postwar Britain.

Anthologies

The group was defined by two anthologies: D. J. Enright's Poets of the 1950s (1955) and Robert Conquest's influential New Lines (1956), whose introduction attacked the "diffuse and sentimental" poetry of the 1940s. New Lines featured Larkin, Amis, Davie, Enright, Jennings, Wain, Conquest, Gunn, and John Holloway.

Legacy

Most of the writers disliked the label, and by the 1960s the group had dispersed as Larkin's reputation grew on its own terms and Amis became a major novelist. Critics have debated whether the Movement was a coherent school or a journalistic invention, but its ideals of clarity and craft strongly influenced British poetry for decades.
