Maritime history

Maritime history is the study of human interaction with and activity at sea. It covers a broad thematic spectrum in scholarly and popular history, including shipping, navigation, seafaring, ports, fishing, whaling, maritime law, naval history, shipbuilding, and the role of oceans in trade, warfare, migration and colonisation. Maritime historians study developments from the first voyaging craft of prehistory to the container ships of the present day.

Prehistoric and ancient seafaring

Humans took to the water tens of thousands of years ago: the colonisation of Australia (c. 50,000 years ago) required sea crossings, and dugout canoes are among the oldest known boats. In the ancient world, Egyptian, Phoenician and Greek mariners developed trade networks across the Mediterranean; the Phoenicians were famed as sailors and are credited with circumnavigating Africa for Pharaoh Necho II around 600 BC. Roman sea power secured the Mediterranean as mare nostrum, while in Asia monsoon-driven trade linked India, Southeast Asia and China, and Austronesian navigators settled the Pacific as far as Hawaii, Easter Island and New Zealand using sophisticated non-instrument navigation.

Medieval period

Viking longships reached Iceland, Greenland and North America around AD 1000. In northern Europe, cogs and the Hanseatic League dominated Baltic trade, while Venetian and Genoese galleys fought over Mediterranean commerce. In Asia, the treasure fleets of Zheng He (1405–1433) projected Chinese maritime power into the Indian Ocean. Advances included the compass, sternpost rudder, lateen sails and portolan charts.

Age of Discovery and sail

From the late 15th century, Portuguese and Spanish voyages — Diaz around the Cape of Good Hope (1488), Columbus to the Americas (1492), da Gama to India (1498), and Magellan's expedition completing the first circumnavigation (1519–1522) — inaugurated an era of European oceanic empires and global trade, including the Manila galleon route and, tragically, the transatlantic slave trade. The Dutch, English and French East India Companies organised trade on an unprecedented scale. Naval warfare between sailing fleets, privateering, and piracy flourished; maritime law and insurance (e.g. Lloyd's of London) developed in parallel.

Steam, steel and globalisation

The 19th century brought steamships, iron and steel hulls, the telegraph and canals at Suez (1869) and Panama (1914), which revolutionised routes and schedules. Mass emigration by sea, the clipper era, industrial fishing and whaling, and imperial navies marked the period. In the 20th century, dreadnoughts, aircraft carriers, submarines and amphibious warfare dominated two world wars; after 1945, containerisation (from 1956) slashed cargo costs and underpinned late-century globalisation, while supertankers, cruise ships and offshore industries reshaped maritime activity. Today's merchant fleet carries the great majority of world trade by volume.
