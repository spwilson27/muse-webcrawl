Tilting train

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tilting train is a train that has a mechanism enabling increased speed on regular rail tracks. As a train (or other vehicle) rounds a curve at speed, objects inside the train experience centrifugal force. This can cause packages to slide about or passengers to feel uncomfortable, and limits the safe speed on curves. Tilting designs lean the carbodies into the curve, partly compensating for the lateral acceleration and allowing the train to traverse curves faster without exceeding passenger-comfort limits or endangering the load.

Technology

Two broad approaches exist:

  • Passive tilting (pendular suspension) suspends the carbody above the centre of roll so that it swings outward-and-inward naturally under centrifugal force, as in Spain's Talgo Pendular trains. Passive systems tilt by only a few degrees (around 3.5°).
  • Active tilting uses sensors — typically accelerometers or gyroscopes in a leading bogie — and hydraulic or electric actuators to tilt the body by up to about 8–9 degrees, controlled by computer. Active systems are used on Italy's Pendolino family (developed by Fiat Ferroviaria, now Alstom), Germany's ICE T, Japan's many limited-express trains, and Britain's Class 390 Pendolino.

Tilting does not change the forces on the track itself, only the comfort and stability margins for the vehicle; curve-speed gains are typically 15–30 percent on winding legacy lines.

History

Experiments began in the 1930s–1950s with the Spanish Talgo concept. Serious development came in the 1970s: British Rail's Advanced Passenger Train (APT) pioneered active tilt but was abandoned in the 1980s after technical problems and a hostile press, though its technology was sold to Italy and fed into the Pendolino program, which became a worldwide commercial success beginning with the ETR 450 in 1988. Sweden's X 2000, launched in 1990, cut Stockholm–Gothenburg journey times significantly. Tilting trains are widely used in Japan, Finland, Switzerland (ICN), Portugal (Alfa Pendular), Slovenia, the Czech Republic, and on the United States' Acela (which tilts on the northern portion of the Northeast Corridor).

Advantages and disadvantages

Tilting allows substantial journey-time reductions on existing curvy routes without the enormous cost of building new straight high-speed lines. Drawbacks include greater mechanical complexity and cost, motion sickness in some passengers when tilt compensation is imperfect, and reduced benefit on purpose-built high-speed track, which is why dedicated Shinkansen and French LGV lines historically avoided tilt (though Japan later adopted it for some services).
