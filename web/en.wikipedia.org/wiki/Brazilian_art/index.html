Brazilian art

Brazilian art encompasses the visual arts produced in the territory of present-day Brazil, from pre-Columbian Indigenous ceramics and rock painting through colonial Baroque, 19th-century academic painting, and the modernist and contemporary movements for which the country is internationally known.

Indigenous and colonial art

The oldest traditions include the painted ceramics of Marajoara culture on Marajó Island and the rock art of sites such as Serra da Capivara. In the colonial period, Portuguese Catholic art dominated: gilded woodcarving (talha dourada), church architecture, and painting flourished especially in Minas Gerais during the gold cycle. The sculptor Antônio Francisco Lisboa, known as Aleijadinho ("the little cripple"), carved the soapstone Prophets at Congonhas (1800–1805), regarded as masterpieces of the Brazilian Baroque and Rococo.

19th century

The arrival of the Portuguese court in 1808 and the French Artistic Mission of 1816, led by Joachim Lebreton and including Jean-Baptiste Debret, founded academic art in Brazil. The Imperial Academy of Fine Arts trained painters such as Victor Meirelles and Pedro Américo, famed for vast historical canvases. Landscape painters of the Grimm Group broke with academic convention in the 1880s, painting outdoors.

Modernism

The Modern Art Week (Semana de Arte Moderna) in São Paulo in February 1922 launched Brazilian modernism. Painters Anita Malfatti and Tarsila do Amaral, and the poet Oswald de Andrade — whose Antropofagia ("cannibalism") manifesto of 1928 urged Brazilian culture to devour and transform foreign influences — defined the movement. Portinari became the country's best-known modern painter, author of the War and Peace panels at the United Nations. Later avant-gardes included the Concrete and Neo-Concrete movements of the 1950s, with artists such as Lygia Clark and Hélio Oiticica redefining participation in art.

Contemporary art

Brazil hosts the São Paulo Biennial, founded in 1951 and the second-oldest art biennial in the world, and the open-air museum Inhotim in Minas Gerais. Contemporary Brazilian artists with international reputations include Vik Muniz, Beatriz Milhazes, Ernesto Neto, and Adriana Varejão.
