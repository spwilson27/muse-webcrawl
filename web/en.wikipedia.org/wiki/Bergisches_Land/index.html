The Bergisches Land is a low mountain region in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany, east of the Rhine between the Ruhr and the Sieg rivers. Its name derives not from Berg ("mountain") but from the historical County of Berg, whose counts took their name from Burg Castle near Solingen. The region's hilly, forested landscape of slate uplands is drained by the Wupper and its tributaries.

History and industry

The County of Berg rose in the Middle Ages around Altenberg and Schloss Burg, becoming a duchy in 1380, and later part of the Napoleonic Grand Duchy of Berg and then Prussia. Abundant water power, iron ore and wood for charcoal made the valleys an early industrial region: Solingen became famous for blades and cutlery ("the City of Blades"), Remscheid for tools, and the Wupper valley towns of Barmen and Elberfeld — united in 1929 as Wuppertal — for textiles. Wuppertal's suspension railway, the Schwebebahn (1901), remains the region's emblem; the Müngsten Bridge (1897), at 107 m the highest railway bridge in Germany, links Solingen and Remscheid.

Geography and culture

The Bergisches Land is characterised by rolling, rain-soaked uplands, half-timbered and slate-clad houses with green shutters, and reservoirs (Talsperren) supplying the Ruhr. Landmarks include Altenberg Cathedral, Schloss Burg, and Beyenburg. The regional table tradition, the Bergische Kaffeetafel, serves coffee with waffles, rice pudding, black bread, quark and other dishes. The dialect belongs to the Ripuarian and Low Franconian groups.
