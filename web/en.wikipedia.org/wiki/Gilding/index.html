Gilding

Applying gold leaf to a picture frame

Gilding is a decorative technique for applying a very thin coating of gold to solid surfaces such as metal (most common), wood, porcelain, or stone. A gilded object is also called gilt. When applied to a metal surface it is described as gold leaf, and the process has been used since antiquity in Egypt, China, and the classical world.

Techniques

Water gilding on wood uses gesso and clay (bole) as a preparation, with gold leaf laid on a wet surface and burnished with an agate stone to a bright finish; it produces the brilliant surfaces of medieval altarpieces and fine frames but is not durable outdoors. Oil (mordant) gilding uses a slow-drying oil size and is matte but weather-resistant, and is preferred for exterior and architectural work.

On metal, historical methods include fire gilding (mercury gilding), in which a gold–mercury amalgam is applied and the mercury driven off by heat — producing a durable coating but at severe risk of mercury poisoning to the gilder — and depletion gilding, used in pre-Columbian America to enrich the surface of gold alloys. Modern methods include electroplating and mechanical application of gold leaf. A common base metal for gilt work is copper or silver; gilded silver is called silver-gilt or, in French, vermeil.

Applications

Gilding decorates picture frames, religious art and icons, domes and architectural details, book edges and bindings, furniture, and ceremonial objects. The phrase "gilding the lily" — adding ornament to something already beautiful — derives from a misquotation of Shakespeare's King John.
