Chlorogenic acid (CGA) is a polyphenol compound formed by the esterification of caffeic acid with quinic acid. Despite its name, it contains no chlorine; the name derives from the Greek chloros (pale green), referring to the green colour produced when the compound oxidises. It is one of the most abundant dietary polyphenols and is best known as a major component of coffee.

Occurrence

Chlorogenic acid and related caffeoylquinic acids occur widely in plants. Green coffee beans are an especially rich source, containing several percent by dry weight, though roasting degrades much of it into other compounds that contribute to coffee's flavour and bitterness. Other sources include sunflower seeds, potatoes, eggplant, apples, pears, blueberries, and the leaves of bamboo and honeysuckle; it is a significant constituent of the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Lonicera japonica and of Eucommia bark.

Chemistry and metabolism

The term "chlorogenic acids" also denotes the wider family of hydroxycinnamate–quinic acid esters, including the 3-, 4-, and 5-caffeoylquinic acids and dicaffeoylquinic acids. In humans, a portion is absorbed in the small intestine, while much reaches the colon, where gut microbiota hydrolyse it to caffeic and quinic acids and further metabolites such as dihydrocaffeic acid.

Health research

Chlorogenic acid has antioxidant activity in vitro, and studies have examined possible effects on glucose metabolism, blood pressure, and body weight; green coffee extract supplements were marketed for weight loss, though evidence is limited and some promotional claims drew regulatory action. Regulatory assessments have not approved disease-risk-reduction health claims for the compound.
