Progressive nonfluent aphasia (PNFA), now formally classified as the nonfluent/agrammatic variant of primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA), is a neurodegenerative syndrome in which the dominant, progressive disturbance is in the production of speech and grammar. It is one of the three recognised variants of primary progressive aphasia, alongside the semantic variant and the logopenic variant.

Signs and symptoms

The core features of PNFA are:

  • Agrammatism — spoken (and often written) language becomes simplified and telegraphic, with omission of grammatical morphemes and function words, and errors in verb tense and word order.
  • Apraxia of speech — effortful, halting speech production with distorted sound substitutions, groping articulatory movements and abnormal prosody.

Single-word comprehension and object knowledge are relatively preserved, distinguishing PNFA from the semantic variant, and episodic memory and visuospatial skills remain comparatively intact early on. As the disease progresses, many patients develop features of the movement disorders progressive supranuclear palsy or corticobasal syndrome, with which PNFA is pathologically linked, and some eventually become mute.

Causes and pathology

PNFA is caused by frontotemporal lobar degeneration. In most cases the underlying proteinopathy is an accumulation of abnormally phosphorylated tau protein (a tauopathy, including Pick's disease and the pathologies seen in corticobasal degeneration and progressive supranuclear palsy); a minority of cases are due to TDP-43 pathology or, rarely, Alzheimer's disease pathology. Neuroimaging shows focal atrophy of the left posterior frontal lobe and insula, including Broca's area.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic criteria published by Gorno-Tempini and colleagues in 2011 require a clinical diagnosis of primary progressive aphasia with either agrammatism in language production or apraxia of speech as a core feature, plus at least two supportive features such as impaired comprehension of complex sentences with spared single-word comprehension. Imaging-supported diagnosis requires predominant left posterior fronto-insular atrophy or hypometabolism.

Management

There is no disease-modifying treatment. Management focuses on speech and language therapy, communication strategies and assistive devices, treatment of mood symptoms, and support for patients and families as speech deteriorates.
