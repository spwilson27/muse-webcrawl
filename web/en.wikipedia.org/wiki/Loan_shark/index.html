Loan shark

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The loan shark in popular imagery

A loan shark is a person or organisation that lends money at extremely high rates of interest, typically outside the law, and enforces repayment through intimidation, threats or violence. The term is applied both to illegal criminal lenders — historically associated with organised crime syndicates such as the Mafia, whose "juice loans" charged weekly interest of several percent — and, more loosely and controversially, to legal high-cost lenders whose rates critics regard as predatory.

Practices and law

Illegal loan sharks usually require no paperwork or credit checks, relying instead on personal knowledge of the borrower and on credible menace: the debt itself is unenforceable in court, since usury laws in most jurisdictions cap lawful interest, and unlicensed moneylending is a criminal offence in countries including the United Kingdom (where "loan shark" units investigate illegal lenders under the Financial Conduct Authority's regime), India and Japan, where the sarakin consumer-lending crisis of the 1980s–2000s and the yakuza-linked yami-kingu lenders prompted strict rate caps. Borrowers are disproportionately people excluded from mainstream credit, and debts can spiral as interest and "fees" exceed the original sum many times over. Governments counter the trade with enforcement, debt advice and alternatives such as credit unions and community development finance. The separate debate over legal payday lending turns on whether very high annualised rates on very short loans are exploitative or a legitimate price for high-risk credit; regulators such as the UK FCA (price cap imposed in 2015) and the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau have restricted the sector accordingly.
