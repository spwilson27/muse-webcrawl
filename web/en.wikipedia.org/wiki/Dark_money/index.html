Dark money

Dark money is a term for funds spent to influence elections, political outcomes or public policy by organizations that are not required to disclose the identity of their donors. Such organizations may receive unlimited donations from corporations, individuals and unions, and spend funds to influence elections, but are not technically candidates and therefore are not regulated as campaign committees.

Dark money
Type: Political financing concept
Jurisdiction: Primarily United States
Key law: FECA, Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act
Court case: Citizens United v. FEC (2010)

Origins

The term emerged in United States politics in the 2000s and 2010s, as nonprofit organizations organized under sections 501(c)(4) (social welfare), 501(c)(5) and 501(c)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code became major spenders after the Supreme Court decisions in FEC v. Wisconsin Right to Life (2007) and Citizens United v. FEC (2010), which allowed corporations and unions to spend independently on elections.

Mechanisms

Dark money groups may run issue advertisements, fund super PACs, or transfer money among affiliated nonprofits, obscuring the original source. Unlike candidates and traditional PACs, they generally need not disclose donors to the Federal Election Commission, although they must report spending and file tax returns with some donor information redacted in public versions.

Scale and debate

Spending by dark money groups exceeded hundreds of millions of dollars in several recent U.S. election cycles according to OpenSecrets. Supporters argue donor anonymity protects free association, citing NAACP v. Alabama (1958). Critics argue it undermines transparency, accountability and equal political influence, and have proposed the DISCLOSE Act and similar disclosure legislation, largely unsuccessful at federal level though some states have acted.
