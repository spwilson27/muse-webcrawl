Universal life insurance

Universal life insurance (often shortened to UL) is a type of cash value life insurance, first introduced in the United States in the late 1970s and early 1980s. It provides permanent coverage with flexible premiums and an adjustable death benefit, and separates the policy into an insurance (mortality cost) component and a savings (cash value) component.

How it works

Premiums paid in excess of the cost of insurance and administrative charges accumulate in a cash value account, which is credited with interest at a rate declared by the insurer, typically subject to a guaranteed minimum. Policyholders may vary premium amounts and timing within limits, raise or lower the death benefit, and borrow or withdraw against the cash value — though insufficient premiums can cause the policy to lapse.

Variants

  • Indexed universal life (IUL): interest credited based on the performance of a stock index, with caps and floors.
  • Variable universal life (VUL): cash value invested in separate subaccounts resembling mutual funds, with investment risk borne by the policyholder.
  • Guaranteed universal life: emphasises a guaranteed death benefit with minimal cash value accumulation.
