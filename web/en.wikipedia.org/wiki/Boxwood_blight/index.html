Boxwood blight (also called box blight) is a serious fungal disease of boxwood (Buxus species) caused primarily by the fungus Calonectria pseudonaviculata. It causes leaf spots, black stem lesions, and rapid defoliation, and can kill plants, particularly in the humid conditions favoured by hedging and topiary. The disease has devastated box plantings in gardens, nurseries, and historic landscapes across Europe and North America since the 1990s.

History and distribution

The disease was first described in the United Kingdom in the mid-1990s and the causal fungus was formally named in the early 2000s, after similar outbreaks in New Zealand. It spread through the European nursery trade during the 2000s and was confirmed in North America in 2011, in Connecticut and North Carolina, quickly appearing in many eastern and some western states and in Canada. A second species, Calonectria henricotiae, occurs in Europe. Because the pathogen spreads mainly in infected plants and plant debris, movement of nursery stock is the chief route of long-distance spread.

Symptoms and disease cycle

Early symptoms are circular, tan to dark-brown leaf spots, often with darker borders, followed by black streak-like lesions on green stems. Under warm, wet conditions the undersides of infected leaves develop white fungal growth (spore masses). Infected leaves turn tan or straw-coloured and drop quickly, so plants may be almost completely defoliated within weeks; repeated defoliation weakens and can kill the plant, although root systems usually survive and may resprout. The fungus produces sticky spores spread short distances by splashing water, tools, clothing, and animals, and it can persist in fallen leaves and debris. A related older disease, Volutella blight (caused by Pseudonectria buxi), produces similar dieback but is generally less aggressive and is distinguished by salmon-pink spore masses.

Management

There is no cure once infection is established. Management relies on exclusion and sanitation: buying clean stock, quarantining and inspecting new plants, spacing and pruning for airflow, avoiding overhead irrigation, disinfecting tools, and promptly removing infected plants and fallen debris (which should be burned, buried, or sent to landfill rather than composted). Protectant fungicides—such as chlorothalonil and mancozeb, and systemic products including fludioxonil and tebuconazole—can prevent infection of healthy plants but must be applied before disease appears and re-applied through wet periods. Susceptibility varies among boxwood taxa: many cultivars of English boxwood (Buxus sempervirens 'Suffruticosa') and American boxwood are highly susceptible, while a number of Asian species and hybrid cultivars (including some derived from B. microphylla and B. harlandii) show useful tolerance. Breeding programmes have released blight-resistant selections, and many gardeners now substitute alternatives such as Japanese holly (Ilex crenata) for formal hedging.
