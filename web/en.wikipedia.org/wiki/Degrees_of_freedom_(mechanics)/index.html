Degrees of freedom (mechanics)

[Image: The six degrees of freedom of a rigid body]
The six degrees of freedom: three translations and three rotations

In physics, the degrees of freedom (DOF) of a mechanical system are the number of independent parameters that define its configuration or state. A single particle moving freely in three-dimensional space has three translational degrees of freedom; a rigid body has six — three translations of its centre of mass and three rotations about perpendicular axes. Constraints between bodies reduce the total degrees of freedom of an assembly.

Rigid bodies and mechanisms

In engineering, the mobility of a mechanism is computed from the degrees of freedom of its links minus the constraints imposed by its joints; the Kutzbach–Grübler criterion performs this count for linkages. A revolute or prismatic joint between two rigid bodies removes five degrees of freedom, leaving one; a spherical (ball-and-socket) joint leaves three. Robot arms are classified by their degrees of freedom: a typical industrial manipulator has six, matching the six needed to place and orient its end effector arbitrarily, while a human arm has seven, giving it redundancy.

Other uses

The concept extends beyond rigid-body mechanics: in statistical mechanics each independent quadratic term in the energy of a molecule contributes degrees of freedom relevant to heat capacity, and in structural engineering the degrees of freedom of a discretized model number in the millions for finite-element analyses. Ships and aircraft describe their motion with the six mechanical degrees of freedom: surge, sway, heave, roll, pitch and yaw.
