Mass attenuation coefficient

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The mass attenuation coefficient, or mass narrow beam attenuation coefficient, of a material is a measure of how readily a beam of radiation is reduced in intensity by absorption and scattering as it passes through that material. It is defined as the linear attenuation coefficient divided by the density of the material, and is normally expressed in units of cm²/g (or m²/kg).

dI = −μρ I dx,    mass attenuation coefficient = μ/ρ

Here μ is the linear attenuation coefficient, ρ is the density, I the beam intensity and x the path length. Because dividing by density removes the dependence on the physical state of the material, the mass attenuation coefficient of a compound can be computed as the weighted average of the coefficients of its constituent elements, which makes it convenient for tabulation. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes widely used tables (the XCOM database) of mass attenuation coefficients for elements and compounds across photon energies from 1 keV to 100 GeV.

Contributions

The total coefficient is the sum of contributions from the individual interaction processes, each dominant in a different energy range:

  • Photoelectric absorption — dominant at low photon energies, strongly dependent on atomic number (roughly as Z⁴ to Z⁵).
  • Compton scattering — dominant at intermediate energies.
  • Pair production — possible only above 1.022 MeV and dominant at high energies.
  • Coherent (Rayleigh) scattering, usually a small correction.

Mass energy-absorption coefficient

A related quantity, the mass energy-absorption coefficient (μen/ρ), counts only the energy actually deposited in the material by secondary charged particles, excluding energy carried away by secondary photons. It is used in radiation dosimetry, for example in calculating dose to tissue or air kerma, and in the design of shielding for X-ray and gamma-ray facilities.
