Heteronuclear single quantum coherence spectroscopy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heteronuclear single quantum coherence spectroscopy, usually abbreviated to HSQC, is a two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiment widely used in organic chemistry and structural biology. The experiment correlates the chemical shift of a nucleus (most commonly a proton, 1H) with that of a different, heteronucleus (most often 13C or 15N) to which it is directly bonded, so that each cross-peak in the spectrum corresponds to one bonded pair of atoms — for example, one C–H bond.

Principle

HSQC is an inverse-detection experiment: magnetisation is transferred from the sensitive proton to the heteronucleus and back again using sequences of radiofrequency pulses based on the INEPT (Insensitive Nuclei Enhanced by Polarization Transfer) block. The heteronucleus evolves during the indirect evolution period, giving the second frequency dimension, while detection is performed on the proton, which gives the experiment much higher sensitivity than direct observation of dilute nuclei such as 13C.

Variants and applications

Because it reports on one-bond correlations, an HSQC spectrum gives a fingerprint of a molecule's bonding framework: CH, CH2 and CH3 groups can be distinguished in edited versions of the experiment (such as multiplicity-edited HSQC), in which CH2 signals appear with opposite phase. The related HMBC experiment instead reports longer-range, two- and three-bond correlations.

In protein NMR, the 1H–15N HSQC is often the first experiment recorded on a labelled protein sample, since each backbone amide (except proline) gives a single peak, producing a characteristic fingerprint used to assess folding and to follow binding events or chemical shift perturbations. HSQC is also combined with other pulse sequences in triple-resonance experiments for resonance assignment, and forms the basis of techniques such as HSQC-TOCSY.
