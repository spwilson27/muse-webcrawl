Bell tower (Chinese)

A bell tower (Chinese: 鐘樓, zhōnglóu) in traditional Chinese architecture is a tower housing a large bell, used to mark the time and to signal the opening and closing of city gates. Bell towers typically stand paired with a drum tower near the centre of a historic city, the bell announcing the morning and the drum the evening — an arrangement echoed in the Chinese idiom "morning bell and evening drum" (晨鐘暮鼓). Many Buddhist temples also have bell towers, where the bell is struck for prayer and meditation.

Notable examples

The most famous civic bell tower is the Bell Tower of Xi'an, built in 1384 early in the Ming dynasty at what was then the centre of the walled city; it is a tall timber structure with triple eaves on a square brick base, housing a replica of the great Jingyun bell. Beijing's Bell Tower, the northern terminus of the city's central axis, was rebuilt in the 18th century and contains a huge bronze bell, about 7 metres high and weighing over 60 tonnes, once said to be audible for many kilometres. Other surviving towers include those of Nanjing, Lanzhou and numerous temple complexes.

Function and symbolism

Beyond timekeeping, bells announced alarms, ceremonies and curfews, and their sound carried ritual significance: temple bells are traditionally struck 108 times, a number associated with the defilements to be dispelled in Buddhist practice. After modern timekeeping arrived, the civic towers lost their practical role and became historic landmarks and tourist attractions.
