A specific phobia is an anxiety disorder, defined by the DSM-5 as a marked and persistent fear of a specific object or situation that is out of proportion to the actual danger, leading to avoidance or endurance with intense distress, and causing clinically significant impairment. Exposure to the phobic stimulus almost invariably provokes an immediate anxiety response. Specific phobias are among the most common anxiety disorders, with a lifetime prevalence of roughly 12% in some surveys, and are more frequently diagnosed in women than men.

Types

The DSM-5 specifies subtypes by the nature of the stimulus:

  • Animal — e.g. arachnophobia (spiders), ophidiophobia (snakes), cynophobia (dogs).
  • Natural environment — e.g. acrophobia (heights), astraphobia (storms), hydrophobia (water).
  • Blood-injection-injury — fear of blood, needles, or medical procedures; uniquely associated with a vasovagal fainting response.
  • Situational — e.g. claustrophobia (enclosed spaces), fear of flying, elevators, driving.
  • Other — e.g. fear of vomiting (emetophobia), choking, or loud sounds.

Causes

Specific phobias arise from a combination of genetic vulnerability, temperament (behavioural inhibition), and learning: direct traumatic conditioning, observing others' fear (vicarious learning), and information transmission. Evolutionary preparedness may explain why fears of snakes, spiders, heights, and blood are so much more common than fears of modern hazards.

Treatment

The treatment of choice is exposure therapy, a form of cognitive behavioural therapy in which the patient is gradually and systematically exposed to the feared stimulus, sometimes in a single extended session. Virtual-reality exposure is used for flying and height phobias. Medication is not generally first-line, though a short-acting anxiolytic may occasionally be used for unavoidable situations such as a necessary flight.
