Illuminati

The Illuminati (plural of Latin illuminatus, "enlightened") is the name given to several groups, both real and fictitious. Historically, it refers to the Bavarian Illuminati, an Enlightenment-era secret society founded on 1 May 1776 in Ingolstadt, Bavaria, by the professor of canon law Adam Weishaupt. The society promoted rationalism, secularism, and opposition to superstition, religious influence over public life, and abuses of state power. It was outlawed by the Bavarian government and effectively dissolved by the late 1780s. In modern popular culture, "the Illuminati" feature in numerous conspiracy theories alleging a secret world-controlling organisation.

The Bavarian Illuminati

Adam Weishaupt (1748–1830), the first professor of canon law at the University of Ingolstadt not drawn from the Jesuits (the order having been suppressed in 1773), founded the society initially as the Covenant of Perfectibility (Perfektibilisten), soon renamed the Order of the Illuminati. Influenced by the Enlightenment and by Freemasonry, Weishaupt's order recruited students and later, after the diplomat Adolph Freiherr Knigge joined in 1780, expanded widely by infiltrating and reorganising Masonic lodges across German-speaking Europe. Members adopted classical codenames: Weishaupt was "Spartacus", Knigge "Philo".

Structure and membership

The order was organised in three classes with multiple grades, from Novice and Minerval up to the "Mysteries" of Priest, Prince, Magus, and King, combining Masonic ritual forms with a political-pedagogical programme of moral improvement and gradual enlightenment of mankind. At its height it counted among its members officials, nobles, and intellectuals — possibly including, by some accounts, figures such as Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, though scholars debate individual memberships.

Suppression

Alarmed by secret societies, the Bavarian Elector Karl Theodor issued edicts in 1784–1785 banning unauthorised societies and then the Illuminati and Freemasons by name. Police raids seized internal papers, which the government published in 1787, exposing the order's rituals and correspondence and driving it to collapse; Weishaupt fled into exile and died in Gotha in 1830. Historians regard the historical order as defunct thereafter.

Conspiracy theories

In the 1790s, John Robison's Proofs of a Conspiracy and Abbé Augustin Barruel's Memoirs Illustrating the History of Jacobinism blamed the (already defunct) Illuminati for the French Revolution, founding a durable tradition of conspiracy literature. In the 20th and 21st centuries, fictional treatments — notably Robert Shea and Robert Anton Wilson's satirical Illuminatus! trilogy (1975) — merged with earnest conspiracy theories alleging that the Illuminati survived and secretly orchestrate world events toward a "New World Order". Symbols popularly associated with these theories include the Eye of Providence, which appears on the reverse of the Great Seal of the United States and the US one-dollar bill, though the eye was a common Christian and Enlightenment motif and no documentary link to the Bavarian order exists.

In popular culture

The Illuminati appear in novels such as Dan Brown's Angels & Demons, in films, video games, and music, almost always as an archetypal hidden world government rather than the historical Bavarian society.
