Social-emotional learning

Social-emotional learning (SEL) is an education-based process through which children and adults acquire and apply the knowledge, skills and attitudes needed to develop healthy identities, manage emotions, achieve personal and collective goals, feel and show empathy for others, establish and maintain supportive relationships, and make responsible and caring decisions.

Framework

The most widely used framework is that of the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL), founded in 1994, which identifies five interrelated competencies: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making. SEL is delivered through classroom instruction, integration into academic subjects, school-wide practices and family and community partnerships.

Evidence

A widely cited 2011 meta-analysis of 213 school-based SEL programs involving more than 270,000 students found improvements in social-emotional skills, attitudes, behaviour and academic performance (an 11-percentile-point gain in achievement). Follow-up analyses reported longer-term benefits, though effect sizes vary with implementation quality. Most U.S. states have adopted SEL standards or competencies, at least for early grades.

Criticism

SEL has attracted both pedagogical criticism — including concerns about measurement, developmental appropriateness, and diverting time from academics — and, in the 2020s, political controversy in the United States, where some groups have opposed SEL programs as vehicles for contested content. Supporters respond that SEL concerns widely shared skills such as self-control and cooperation.
