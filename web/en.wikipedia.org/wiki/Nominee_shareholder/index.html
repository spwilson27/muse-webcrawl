Nominee shareholder

A nominee shareholder is a person or company that is registered as the holder of shares in a company while in fact holding them on behalf of the actual owner, called the beneficial owner. The arrangement is usually documented by a declaration of trust or nominee agreement, under which the nominee agrees to act only on the instructions of the beneficial owner and to transfer the shares or pass on dividends as directed.

Legitimate uses

Nominee holdings are routine and lawful in many contexts. Most publicly traded shares are held through nominee accounts operated by brokers and central securities depositories — in the United Kingdom, for instance, shares are commonly held in CREST through a nominee so that trades can settle electronically, while the investor retains beneficial ownership. Nominees are also used in escrow, estate administration, and to allow professional firms to hold shares for clients.

Privacy and abuse concerns

In the offshore context, nominee shareholders (often paired with nominee directors) have been used to keep the true owner's name off public registers. While privacy can be legitimate, the practice has been criticised for facilitating money laundering, tax evasion and sanctions evasion. In response, many jurisdictions have introduced registers of beneficial ownership and requirements to disclose persons with significant control — such as the United Kingdom's PSC register and the beneficial-ownership reporting regime under the United States Corporate Transparency Act — reducing the anonymity nominee arrangements can provide.

Legal position

The nominee, as registered holder, is the legal owner in company law and appears on the register of members, but equity treats the beneficial owner as the true owner for most purposes; the nominee owes fiduciary duties and must not deal with the shares for their own benefit. Undisclosed nominee arrangements may breach disclosure rules, and nominees can be liable where they knowingly participate in wrongdoing.
