Japanese festivals

Japanese festivals (matsuri, 祭り) are traditional festive occasions held throughout Japan, most of them rooted in Shinto shrine worship, Buddhist observance and seasonal agricultural rites. Estimates put the number of festivals at 100,000 to 300,000 nationwide, ranging from great city spectacles drawing millions of spectators to small neighbourhood celebrations organised by a single shrine parish. Common features include processions of portable shrines (mikoshi), floats, dancing, drumming, traditional dress and street-food stalls.

Origins and character

The word matsuri derives from a verb meaning to worship or enshrine a kami (deity). A typical shrine festival invites the deity out of the shrine into a mikoshi, which parishioners carry through the neighbourhood to bless the community and entertain the god with music, dance and spectacle, before returning it to the shrine. Many festivals mark the agricultural calendar — planting in spring, protection from pests in summer, harvest thanks in autumn — while others commemorate historical events, ward off plague or honour the dead, as in the summer Obon season when ancestral spirits are welcomed back.

Major festivals

  • Gion Matsuri (Kyoto, July) — a month-long festival of Yasaka Shrine dating to 869, famous for its procession of towering wheeled floats (yamaboko), inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list.
  • Awa Odori (Tokushima, August) — Japan's largest dance festival, in which thousands of dancers in straw hats parade chanting "Odoru ahō ni miru ahō; onaji ahō nara odoranya son son!" ("The dancers are fools, the watchers are fools; both are fools, so why not dance!").
  • Aomori Nebuta Matsuri (August) — giant illuminated paper floats depicting warriors and mythic figures are paraded through the streets to taiko drums and flutes.
  • Tenjin Matsuri (Osaka, July) — one of Japan's "three great festivals", ending with a river procession of boats and fireworks.
  • Kanda Matsuri and Sanja Matsuri (Tokyo, May, in alternating years and annually respectively) — among the greatest Edo-period shrine festivals, with hundreds of mikoshi in Kanda and famously boisterous crowds in Asakusa.
  • Sapporo Snow Festival (February, since 1950) — a modern festival of monumental snow and ice sculptures drawing millions of visitors.
  • Takayama Matsuri (Gifu) — celebrated for its ornate yatai floats with mechanical dolls.

Seasonal observances

Beyond shrine matsuri, the year is punctuated by nationwide observances: New Year (Shōgatsu), Setsubun, cherry-blossom viewing (hanami), the doll festival Hinamatsuri, Children's Day, Tanabata star festival, Obon, moon-viewing (tsukimi), Shichi-Go-San for children, and countless local fire, snow and river festivals. Summer festivals typically feature fireworks displays (hanabi taikai), bon-odori dancing in yukata, and stalls selling yakisoba, takoyaki, shaved ice and goldfish-scooping games.

Cultural significance

Festivals remain a principal vehicle of local identity and community organisation in Japan, funded and staffed by neighbourhood associations and shrine parishioners. They attract major domestic and international tourism, and several — including the Yamaboko float processions — are inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
