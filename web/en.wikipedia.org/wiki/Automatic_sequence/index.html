Automatic sequence

In mathematics and theoretical computer science, an automatic sequence (also called a k-automatic sequence) is an infinite sequence of terms characterized by a finite automaton. The n-th term of an automatic sequence is a function of the representation of n in a fixed base k, read by a deterministic finite automaton with output.

The classic example is the Thue–Morse sequence, which is 2-automatic: the automaton reads the binary expansion of n and outputs the parity of the number of 1s. The Rudin–Shapiro sequence and the paperfolding sequence are other well-known examples.

Definition

Formally, a sequence (an)n≥0 over a finite alphabet is k-automatic if there is a deterministic finite automaton with output that, given the base-k representation of n as input, ends in a state whose output is an. An equivalent characterisation, Cobham's theorem of 1972, states that a sequence is k-automatic if and only if it is the image, under a letter-to-letter coding, of a fixed point of a k-uniform morphism.

Properties

LogicFirst-order theory decidable (Büchi)

Automatic sequences are never eventually periodic unless trivial, and they are not normal. Their factor complexity grows at most linearly. A celebrated application is that the first-order theory of the natural numbers together with a k-automatic sequence is decidable, which has enabled computer-assisted proofs of combinatorial theorems using theorem provers such as Walnut.
