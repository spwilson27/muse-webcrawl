Crystallite

A crystallite is a small or even microscopic crystal that forms, for example, during the cooling of many materials, especially metals and polymers. Most inorganic solids are polycrystalline, composed of many crystallites — also called grains — of varying sizes and orientations, joined at interfaces known as grain boundaries.

Formation and size

Crystallites form by nucleation and growth when a liquid solidifies or a solid recrystallises. Their size, which can range from a few nanometres to several millimetres, depends on processing: rapid cooling, deformation and heat treatment (annealing) all affect grain size. The average crystallite size can be estimated from X-ray diffraction line broadening using the Scherrer equation, or measured directly by electron microscopy and electron backscatter diffraction.

Properties

Grain size strongly influences mechanical behaviour. The Hall–Petch relationship describes how yield strength increases as grain size decreases, because grain boundaries impede dislocation motion. Conversely, at very small (nanocrystalline) grain sizes, creep and grain-boundary sliding can soften the material. Texture — the statistical distribution of crystallite orientations — affects properties such as magnetic behaviour and formability. See also Polycrystal and Grain boundary.
