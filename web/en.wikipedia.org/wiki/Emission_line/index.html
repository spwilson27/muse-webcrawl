Emission line

An emission line is a bright line at a specific wavelength in the spectrum of a light source, produced when atoms or molecules drop from a higher energy state to a lower one and release the energy difference as a photon. Emission lines are the counterpart of absorption lines, and together they form the basis of spectroscopy, the principal tool for determining the composition and physical conditions of astronomical objects and laboratory plasmas.

Formation

When a gas is heated or excited by radiation or collisions, electrons are raised to higher energy levels. As they fall back, they emit photons whose energies equal the level spacings, so each element and ion has a characteristic pattern of lines — a spectral fingerprint. Because the transitions are quantised, the emitted light concentrates at discrete wavelengths rather than forming a continuum. In dense gas in thermodynamic equilibrium, emission is balanced by absorption and a black-body continuum results; emission-line spectra typically arise in hot, low-density gas (such as nebulae) seen against a darker background.

Applications

Astronomers use emission lines to identify elements in stars, nebulae, galaxies and quasars, and to measure redshifts, temperatures, densities and velocities. Familiar examples include the Balmer lines of hydrogen (such as red H-alpha at 656.3 nm), the green "nebulium" lines of doubly ionised oxygen ([O III] at 495.9 and 500.7 nm) seen in planetary nebulae, and the 21-centimetre line of neutral hydrogen used in radio astronomy. On Earth, emission spectra underlie discharge lamps such as neon and sodium lights, as well as analytical techniques like flame tests and atomic emission spectroscopy.
