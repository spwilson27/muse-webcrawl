Combustion

The combustion of wood, producing flames

Combustion, or burning, is a high-temperature exothermic redox chemical reaction between a fuel (the reductant) and an oxidant, usually atmospheric oxygen, that produces oxidised, often gaseous products, in a mixture termed as smoke. Combustion does not always result in fire, because a flame is only visible when substances undergoing combustion vaporise, but when it does, a flame is a characteristic indicator of the reaction.

Types

In complete combustion, the reactant burns in oxygen and produces a limited number of products: a hydrocarbon burned in oxygen yields carbon dioxide and water. In incomplete combustion, limited oxygen leads to carbon monoxide, soot, and other partial oxidation products. Other forms include rapid combustion (fire and explosions), slow combustion (such as rusting-like oxidation and cellular respiration), turbulent combustion in engines, and flameless or catalytic combustion.

Chemistry

For a generic hydrocarbon CxHy, complete combustion follows:
CxHy + (x + y/4) O2 → x CO2 + (y/2) H2O.
The reaction proceeds by a radical chain mechanism and must be initiated by an ignition source supplying the activation energy. The heat released sustains the reaction, making combustion self-propagating once started.

Applications and hazards

Combustion powers most transport and much electricity generation through the burning of fossil fuels, and is used in internal combustion engines, gas turbines, rockets, furnaces, and stoves. Uncontrolled combustion causes fires and explosions, and combustion emissions — carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and particulates — are major drivers of climate change and air pollution.
