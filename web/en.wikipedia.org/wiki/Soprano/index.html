A soprano (Italian pronunciation: [soˈpraːno]) is a type of classical female singing voice with the highest vocal range of all voice types. The soprano's vocal range (using scientific pitch notation) is from approximately middle C (C4) to "high C" (C6) or higher, although very few sopranos are able to sing tones that high. The term derives from the Italian word sopra, meaning "above" or "over", as the soprano is the highest human voice, often given to the leading female role in operas.

History

In choral music before the 16th century, the highest part was often sung by boys (trebles) or by men using falsetto. As women were admitted to church choirs and to the stage in the 17th century, the soprano voice came to dominate the upper register. By the era of bel canto in the early 19th century, composers such as Rossini, Bellini and Donizetti wrote florid coloratura passages that showcased the agility of the soprano voice.

Subtypes

Soprano voices are commonly divided into subtypes according to range, weight and timbre:

  • Coloratura soprano — a light, agile voice capable of rapid runs, trills and very high notes, as in the Queen of the Night in Mozart's The Magic Flute.
  • Soubrette — a light, bright voice suited to youthful or coquettish roles.
  • Lyric soprano — a warm, full voice, the most common soprano type, suited to roles such as Mimì in La bohème.
  • Spinto soprano — a lyric voice with extra dramatic thrust, able to cut through a large orchestra.
  • Dramatic soprano — a powerful, heavy voice used for roles such as Brünnhilde in Wagner's Ring cycle and the title role of Puccini's Turandot.

Roles

Famous soprano roles include Violetta in La traviata, Lucia in Lucia di Lammermoor, Norma, Aida, Tosca, and the Countess in The Marriage of Figaro. Celebrated sopranos of the recording era include Maria Callas, Joan Sutherland, Renata Tebaldi, Leontyne Price, Birgit Nilsson (a dramatic soprano), and Montserrat Caballé.
