Decan

The decans as depicted on an ancient Egyptian astronomical ceiling.

The decans (Egyptian bakiu) are 36 groups of stars used in ancient Egyptian astronomy. Each decan rose successively above the horizon during the rotation of the Earth, and their sequential appearances were used to keep time at night and to organise the Egyptian calendar year. The system is at least as old as the Middle Kingdom, when decanal star tables were painted on coffin lids — the so-called "diagonal star clocks".

Timekeeping

Because the Earth orbits the Sun, a given star rises about four minutes earlier each night. Egyptian astronomers selected a set of 36 stars (or star groups) spaced so that a new decan rose roughly every ten days — hence the name, from the Greek dekanoi, "tenth parts". Each period of ten days formed a "week" of the Egyptian civil calendar, and three such weeks made a month. During the course of a night, twelve decans would rise in succession, allowing the hours of darkness to be tracked from star tables that listed which decan rose in each hour for each ten-day period of the year.

Astrological afterlife

In the Hellenistic period the decans were adopted into astrology and Hermetic magic. The zodiac was divided into 36 ten-degree segments, each ruled by a decan who acquired a name, an image and associated demons, angels and medicinal plants. Decanic imagery flourished in medieval and Renaissance astrology and magic — appearing, for example, in the Picatrix and in Giordano Bruno's writings — and the decans survive in modern astrology as the three "decantes" or faces of each zodiac sign.
