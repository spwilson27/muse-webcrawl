Fausto Coppi

Angelo Fausto Coppi (15 September 1919 – 2 January 1960) was an Italian racing cyclist, widely considered one of the greatest cyclists of all time. Nicknamed Il Campionissimo ("the champion of champions"), he won the Tour de France twice and the Giro d'Italia five times, and was world road race champion in 1953.

Career

Coppi won his first Giro in 1940 at age twenty, before the Second World War interrupted his career; he served in North Africa and was taken prisoner by the British. In 1949 he became the first rider to win the Giro and the Tour de France in the same year, repeating the double in 1952. His famous solo breakaways — commentators would announce that "a man alone is in the lead" — and his rivalry with Gino Bartali, which divided post-war Italy, made him a national symbol. He also set the world hour record in 1942 and won Milan–San Remo, Paris–Roubaix and the Tour of Lombardy.

Death and legacy

After a hunting trip to Upper Volta (now Burkina Faso) in late 1959, Coppi contracted malaria, which was misdiagnosed in Italy; he died on 2 January 1960, aged forty. The phrase Coppi è morto became a byword in Italy. A monument stands to him at the Passo Pordoi, and he remains a central figure in cycling history, though his career has also been discussed in the context of the era's widespread amphetamine use, which Coppi acknowledged openly.
