Shan shui

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shan shui (Chinese: 山水, "mountain-water") refers to a style of traditional Chinese painting that involves or depicts scenery or natural landscapes, using brush and ink rather than more conventional paints. Mountains, rivers, and waterfalls are common subjects, and the paintings are meant to convey a philosophical, often Daoist-influenced vision of humanity's place in nature rather than a realistic record of a particular view.

History

Landscape elements appeared in Chinese art from early times, but landscape emerged as an independent genre around the 4th–5th centuries CE. The critic Zong Bing (375–443) wrote an early treatise on landscape painting, arguing that a well-made painting could provide a spiritual journey for a viewer unable to travel. By the Tang dynasty, distinct manners had formed: the blue-and-green style of Li Sixun, using mineral colours on silk, and the ink-wash manner associated with the poet-painter Wang Wei.

During the Song dynasty (960–1279), shan shui reached a classical peak. Northern Song masters such as Fan Kuan (Travelers among Mountains and Streams) and Guo Xi created monumental, densely structured mountain landscapes, while Southern Song painters like Ma Yuan and Xia Gui favoured intimate, asymmetrical compositions with large areas of empty space. In the Yuan dynasty, scholar-painters (literati) such as Zhao Mengfu and the Four Masters — Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan, and Wang Meng — emphasized personal expression and calligraphic brushwork over descriptive accuracy.

Characteristics

Shan shui paintings typically employ shifting or "floating" perspective, so that the viewer's eye travels through the landscape along winding paths, rivers, and bands of mist. Human figures and buildings, when present, are small: a scholar's hut, a fisherman, or a temple half-hidden in the mountains, emphasizing the vastness of nature. Common compositional elements are grouped in threes — for example heaven, earth, and humanity, or foreground, middle ground, and distant peaks. Paintings are often accompanied by poems and calligraphy, and are mounted as handscrolls, hanging scrolls, album leaves, or fans.

Philosophy

The genre is deeply informed by Daoist ideas of harmony, emptiness, and the flow of qi, as well as Confucian and Buddhist thought. The painter traditionally sought not to imitate outward appearance but to capture the inner spirit or resonance of the landscape — an ideal summarized in Xie He's Six Principles of Chinese painting, the first of which is "spirit resonance through life movement."

Influence

Shan shui shaped landscape traditions across East Asia, including Korean sansuhwa and Japanese sansui painting. Its conventions continue to influence contemporary ink painting and are instantly recognizable in depictions of famous scenery such as the Yellow Mountains (Huangshan) and the Li River.
