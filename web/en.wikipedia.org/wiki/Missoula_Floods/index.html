Missoula Floods

The Missoula Floods (also known as the Spokane Floods or the Bretz Floods) were cataclysmic glacial lake outburst floods that swept periodically across eastern Washington and down the Columbia River Gorge at the end of the last ice age and the beginning of the Younger Dryas. These floods were the result of periodic sudden ruptures of the ice dam on the Clark Fork River that created Glacial Lake Missoula. After each rupture, the lake waters raced across the Idaho Panhandle and into eastern Washington, coursing over the top of the state toward the Pacific Ocean.

Geologist J Harlen Bretz first recognised evidence of the catastrophic floods in the 1920s, identifying the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington as their product. His hypothesis of catastrophic flooding was controversial for decades, because it seemed to violate the principle of uniformitarianism, but it was eventually vindicated and is now widely accepted. Researchers have estimated that the floods occurred between 15,000 and 13,000 years ago, and that the cycle of flooding and reformation of the lake lasted for several thousand years.

Glacial Lake Missoula

During the last glacial maximum, a lobe of the Cordilleran Ice Sheet advanced southward from Canada and dammed the Clark Fork River near present-day Lake Pend Oreille in Idaho. Behind the ice dam, water backed up into the valleys of western Montana, forming Glacial Lake Missoula, which at its largest held about 2,100 cubic kilometres (500 cubic miles) of water — roughly half the volume of modern Lake Michigan — and reached a depth of about 610 metres (2,000 ft) at the dam. Ancient shorelines of the lake are still visible on the mountainsides around Missoula, Montana.

Flood events

Periodically, the rising water either floated or overtopped the ice dam, causing it to fail catastrophically. The entire contents of the lake then drained in a matter of days. The peak discharge has been estimated at around 17 million cubic metres per second — roughly ten times the combined flow of all the rivers in the world today. The torrent raced across eastern Washington at speeds approaching 100 km/h, stripping away soil and bedrock and carrying house-sized boulders in icebergs that were stranded far downstream.

Evidence from rhythmic sediment layers (rhythmites), including the Touchet Beds, indicates that dozens of separate floods occurred — estimates commonly range from about 40 to as many as 100 — as the ice dam repeatedly re-formed and the lake refilled over intervals averaging roughly 30–55 years.

Effects on the landscape

The floods carved the Channeled Scablands, a network of enormous coulees, cataracts (including Dry Falls, among the largest known waterfalls by width), potholes, and giant gravel bars. Floodwaters funnelled through the Columbia River Gorge and backed up into Oregon's Willamette Valley as the temporary Lake Allison, depositing rich silt that underlies the valley's fertile farmland. Sediments from the floods can be traced far out into the Pacific Ocean.

The Bretz controversy

J Harlen Bretz began fieldwork in the Scablands in 1922 and by 1923 had concluded that only a flood of immense magnitude could explain the terrain. His 1927 presentation in Washington, D.C., was met with strong opposition from geologists committed to gradualist explanations. Support accumulated through the work of J. T. Pardee, who documented Glacial Lake Missoula and its giant current ripples, and through aerial and later satellite photography that made the flood-carved channels unmistakable. By the 1960s–1970s the catastrophic-flood interpretation was accepted; Bretz received the Penrose Medal in 1979, at the age of 96.
