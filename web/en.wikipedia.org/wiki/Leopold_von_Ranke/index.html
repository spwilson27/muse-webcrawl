Leopold von Ranke (21 December 1795 – 23 May 1886) was a German historian and a founder of modern source-based history. According to Caroline Hoefferle, "Ranke was probably the most important historian to shape historical writing in Europe and the United States during the 19th century." He was ennobled in 1865 (the "von" in his name) and for many years held the chair of history at the University of Berlin.

Method

Ranke introduced the seminar method of teaching and the rigorous critical examination of primary sources, especially archival documents, into historical practice. In the preface to his first book, Histories of the Latin and Germanic Nations (1824), he famously declared that history need not judge the past or instruct the present, but should merely show wie es eigentlich gewesen—"how it essentially was" (often rendered "how it actually happened"). His appendix to that work, a critique of earlier historians such as Guicciardini, pioneered modern source criticism. Research trips to the archives of Vienna, Venice, Rome, and Florence supplied the documentary basis for his multi-volume histories of the popes, England, France, and Prussia.

Historicism

Ranke's thought expressed the historicism of his age: every epoch, he held, is "immediate to God", possessing its own values that the historian must grasp on their own terms rather than measuring them against present standards. Though he aspired to scientific objectivity, Ranke's outlook was deeply shaped by Lutheran faith and political conservatism; he emphasized states, diplomacy, and great political forces, and his model of professional history—archival, seminar-based, monographic—was exported worldwide, including to the United States through students such as Herbert Baxter Adams.

Works

Among his major works are the History of the Popes (1834–1836), the History of England, the History of France, the History of Prussia, and a late, unfinished Universal History. His complete works run to more than fifty volumes.
