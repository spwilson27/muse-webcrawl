Provocatio

Provocatio (in full, provocatio ad populum, "appeal to the people") was a legal institution of the Roman Republic that protected a Roman citizen from summary punishment by a magistrate: a citizen condemned to death or subjected to corporal coercion could appeal to the people, whose assembly then judged the case. It is often described as one of the earliest safeguards of individual rights against state power, roughly analogous in spirit to habeas corpus.

History

Roman tradition attributed the right to a law of 509 BCE, the first year of the Republic, passed by the consul Publius Valerius Publicola (the lex Valeria de provocatione). Further Valerian and Porcian laws in the fourth through second centuries BCE strengthened the protection, extending it to citizens serving in the army and outside Rome, and prohibiting the scourging of citizens. In practice the right was repeatedly asserted and contested during the political struggles of the Republic; Cicero treated it as a cornerstone of Roman liberty.

Operation

By crying out provoco ad populum ("I appeal to the people"), a citizen could halt a magistrate's sentence of death or flogging within the city, and the case had to be heard before the comitia. The tribunes of the plebs, with their power of auxilium, could also intervene to protect citizens. The institution declined under the Empire, where appeals increasingly ran to the emperor, though the status of citizenship continued to confer protection: the apostle Paul's appeal to Caesar in the Acts of the Apostles reflects this later form of the right.
