Sarus crane

The sarus crane (Antigone antigone) is a large nonmigratory crane found in parts of the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, and northern Australia. The tallest of the flying birds, standing at a height of up to 1.8 m (5 ft 11 in), they are a conspicuous species of open wetlands in South Asia, seasonally flooded Dipterocarpus forests in Southeast Asia, and Eucalyptus-dominated woodlands and grasslands in Australia.

The sarus crane is easily distinguished from other cranes in the region by its overall grey colour and the contrasting red head and upper neck. They forage on marshes and shallow wetlands for roots, tubers, insects, crustaceans, and small vertebrate prey.

Description

Adults are grey with a bare red head and upper neck, a blackish band of bristle-like feathers behind the eyes, and long pinkish legs. Males and females are indistinguishable externally. In flight, the long neck is held straight, unlike that of a heron.

Behaviour and ecology

Unlike many other cranes, sarus cranes are largely nonmigratory. They are well known for their elaborate unison calling and dancing displays, and pairs are believed to mate for life — birds are celebrated in Indian culture as symbols of marital fidelity.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Threats include habitat loss and degradation of wetlands, hunting, and egg collection.
