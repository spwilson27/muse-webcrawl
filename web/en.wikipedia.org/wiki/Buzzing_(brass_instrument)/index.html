Buzzing (brass instrument)

Buzzing is the fundamental sound-production technique of brass instruments. The player vibrates their lips by blowing air through closed lips, producing a buzzing sound into the mouthpiece of the instrument. The lips act in a manner similar to a double reed, alternately opening and closing as air passes through, and the instrument's bore amplifies and shapes the resulting vibration into a musical tone.

Buzzing on a mouthpiece alone is a common practice exercise.

Technique

To buzz, the player forms an embouchure by tensing the corners of the mouth, keeping the lips together, and exhaling so that the lips vibrate against each other. Pitch is varied primarily by changing lip tension and air speed: greater tension and faster air produce higher pitches. Free buzzing (without any instrument) and buzzing on the mouthpiece alone are widely used in practice routines to develop embouchure strength, pitch accuracy, and tone control.

Pedagogy

Brass teachers differ in emphasis: some advocate extensive mouthpiece buzzing because the pitch produced is more apparent than when playing the full instrument, aiding ear training; others caution that the resistance of the mouthpiece alone differs from that of the instrument, so excessive mouthpiece practice may not transfer directly. Lip buzzing exercises are a staple of method books for trumpet, horn, trombone, and tuba.

Related vibration methods

Brass buzzing is related to the reed vibrations of woodwind instruments: the player's lips function as a pressure-controlled valve, analogous to the beating reed of a clarinet or the double reed of an oboe. Similar lip vibration is used in playing the didgeridoo and in "lip reed" natural instruments such as the alphorn.
