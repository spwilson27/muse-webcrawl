Samuel ben Judah ibn Tibbon (c. 1165 – c. 1232) was a Jewish physician, philosopher, and translator who lived in Lunel and Marseille in southern France. He is best known for his Hebrew translation of Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed (Moreh Nevukhim), completed in 1204 in correspondence with the author, which made the work accessible to the Jews of Christian Europe and fixed much of the philosophical vocabulary of medieval Hebrew.

A medieval manuscript of the Guide for the Perplexed in Samuel ibn Tibbon's Hebrew translation.

Life and work

Samuel was the son of Judah ben Saul ibn Tibbon, the "father of translators," from whom he learned Arabic and the translator's craft. Practising medicine in Marseille and travelling in Spain, Egypt, and the Levant, he translated a wide range of Arabic scientific and philosophical works into Hebrew, including Aristotle's Meteorology, writings of al-Fārābī and Averroes, and Maimonides' Book of Commandments, his treatise on resurrection, and his letter to Yemen.

For the Guide, Samuel corresponded with Maimonides in Fustat about difficult passages, and appended to his translation a glossary of philosophical terms (Perush ha-Millot ha-Zarot), coining Hebrew equivalents for Arabic technical vocabulary that shaped Jewish philosophy for centuries. A rival translation by Judah al-Ḥarizi was more literary but less precise, and Samuel's version became standard; through it (and the Latin version made from it) Maimonides' thought reached scholastic philosophers such as Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas.

Original writings

Samuel also wrote original works, notably Ma'amar Yikkavu ha-Mayim ("Let the Waters Be Gathered," 1231), a philosophical commentary on Genesis 1:9 that interprets the biblical creation account in Aristotelian-Maimonidean terms, and a commentary on Ecclesiastes. He was a central figure in the Maimonidean controversies that divided Provençal and Spanish Jewry in the 1230s, defending rationalist study against traditionalist critics. His son Moses ibn Tibbon continued the family's translation enterprise.
