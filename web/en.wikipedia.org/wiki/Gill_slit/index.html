Gill slit

Gill slits are individual openings to gills, which lack a single outer cover such as an operculum. They are characteristic of cartilaginous fishes — sharks, rays and chimaeras — and derive from the pharyngeal slits found in all chordate embryos. Most sharks and rays have five pairs of gill slits (some species have six or seven), opening separately on the sides of the head or, in rays and skates, on the underside. Water taken in through the mouth (or, in bottom-dwellers, through the spiracles) passes over the gill filaments in the gill pouches and exits through the slits, allowing gas exchange.

Five gill slits on a shark.

Anatomy and function

Each gill slit opens from a pharyngeal pouch lined with gill lamellae richly supplied with blood vessels. In most sharks the slits are lateral and increase in size from front to back; in rays they are ventral, matched to a bottom-feeding life in which water enters via large spiracles behind the eyes. Unlike bony fishes, whose four pairs of gills are hidden beneath a bony operculum with a single opening, elasmobranchs have no operculum, so the slits remain visible externally. The first modified gill slit in many fishes corresponds to the spiracle, and in tetrapod evolution the first pharyngeal pouch gives rise to the middle ear cavity and auditory tube.

Evolution and development

Pharyngeal slits are an ancestral chordate feature: filter-feeding invertebrate chordates such as lancelets have numerous slits used for feeding, and hemichordates also possess them. In vertebrate embryos, a series of pharyngeal arches and pouches develops in the neck region; in fishes these become the gill apparatus, while in land vertebrates they are remodelled into structures of the jaw, ear, throat and glands (such as the parathyroids and thymus). The embryonic similarity of these structures across vertebrates was a historically important line of evidence for evolution, although the strong recapitulation claims of 19th-century writers such as Ernst Haeckel are not accepted in their original form.

Numbers and variation

Five pairs is the norm across sharks and rays; the primitive sixgill and sevengill sharks (order Hexanchiformes) retain six and seven pairs respectively, a feature reflected in their names. Frilled sharks also have six. Lampreys have seven pairs of round gill openings, while hagfish have between five and fifteen pairs depending on species.
