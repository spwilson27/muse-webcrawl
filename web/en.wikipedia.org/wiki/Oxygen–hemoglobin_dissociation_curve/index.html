Oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve

The oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve, also called the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve or oxygen dissociation curve (ODC), is a curve that plots the proportion of hemoglobin in its saturated (oxygen-laden) form on the vertical axis against the prevailing oxygen tension (partial pressure of oxygen, pO2) on the horizontal axis. This curve is an important tool for understanding how blood carries and releases oxygen.

Shape

The curve is sigmoid (S-shaped) because of cooperative binding: as each oxygen molecule binds to hemoglobin, the molecule's affinity for the next oxygen molecule increases. In the lungs, where pO2 is high, hemoglobin becomes almost fully saturated; in the tissues, where pO2 is lower, the steep part of the curve allows large amounts of oxygen to be released in response to small falls in pO2.

The position of the curve is commonly described by the P50, the pO2 at which hemoglobin is 50% saturated, normally about 26–27 mmHg in adults.

Factors affecting the curve

  • Right shift (decreased affinity, easier unloading): increased temperature, increased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG), increased pCO2, and decreased pH — the Bohr effect.
  • Left shift (increased affinity): the opposite changes, as well as carbon monoxide poisoning and fetal hemoglobin, which binds oxygen more strongly than adult hemoglobin.
