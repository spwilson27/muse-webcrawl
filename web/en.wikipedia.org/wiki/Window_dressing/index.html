Window dressing

Window dressing refers to actions taken shortly before the end of a reporting period to make a financial statement, investment portfolio, or business appear more attractive than it actually is. The term originates from the practice of retailers arranging attractive shop-window displays.

In portfolio management

Mutual fund and portfolio managers may engage in window dressing by selling stocks that have performed poorly and buying recent winners just before reporting dates, so that the published holdings list appears to contain only successful investments. Critics argue this can mislead investors and may increase transaction costs without improving returns. Some studies have detected patterns of returns around quarter-ends consistent with the practice.

In accounting

In corporate accounting, window dressing can involve timing transactions — such as delaying payments, accelerating collections, or restructuring short-term borrowings — to improve reported liquidity ratios at the balance-sheet date. Aggressive forms shade into earnings management and, when they involve deception, financial fraud. Auditing standards require scrutiny of transactions near period ends for this reason.

Other uses

The phrase is also used generally for superficial measures intended to create a favorable impression, in retail display, real estate staging, and politics.
