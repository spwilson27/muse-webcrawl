RegionWestern United States and Canada

In the Western United States and Canada, open range refers to rangeland where cattle roam freely regardless of land ownership, without being enclosed by fences. Under open-range laws — a legacy of Spanish and English grazing customs — landowners who wanted to keep livestock out were responsible for fencing them out, rather than owners fencing animals in.

History

After the American Civil War, Texas cattlemen drove herds north to railheads in Kansas and to stock the northern plains, where cattle grazed public domain land year-round and were gathered in roundups, identified by brands. The system produced the classic cattle-drive era but invited overstocking and conflicts with homesteaders and sheep raisers.

Decline

The spread of cheap barbed wire from the late 1870s, the devastating winter of 1886–87, and federal land policy — culminating in the Taylor Grazing Act of 1934, which ended homesteading of grazing land and organised grazing districts — closed the open range on the public domain, though open-range statutes survive in some Western jurisdictions for highway liability and fencing law.
