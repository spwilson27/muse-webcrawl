Academic tenure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Academic tenure is a system of job security used in higher education under which a professor, after completing a probationary period, cannot be dismissed except for cause, such as gross professional incompetence, negligence, or moral turpitude. Tenure is intended to protect academic freedom, allowing scholars to pursue research and teaching, including on controversial topics, without fear of economic reprisal.

History

The modern system of tenure in the United States was formalized by the 1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure, issued jointly by the American Association of University Professors (AAUP) and the Association of American Colleges. The statement recommended a probationary period of no more than seven years, after which a faculty member would receive tenure or a terminal one-year appointment. Earlier antecedents include the medieval licentia docendi and protections for German university professors under the principle of Lehrfreiheit (freedom to teach).

The tenure track

Faculty hired on the tenure track typically begin as assistant professors. During a probationary period—commonly six or seven years—they are evaluated on research and scholarship, teaching, and service. At the end of this period they undergo a tenure review, involving evaluation by departmental colleagues, external reviewers, deans, and senior administrators. Successful candidates are promoted to associate professor with tenure; unsuccessful candidates usually receive a terminal year. Full professors have normally passed a further promotion review.

A widely cited safeguard is that tenure decisions are "up or out": a scholar denied tenure generally must leave the institution.

Purposes and arguments

In favor

  • Tenure protects academic freedom, enabling research and teaching on unpopular or controversial subjects.
  • It encourages scholars to take intellectual risks and pursue long-term research programs.
  • It helps universities attract and retain talented faculty despite salaries often lower than in industry.
  • It supports shared governance by allowing faculty to participate in institutional decisions without fear of retaliation.

Criticism

  • Critics argue tenure can shelter unproductive or incompetent faculty and make institutions inflexible.
  • The scarcity of tenure-track positions has coincided with growth in contingent, adjunct, and non-tenure-track appointments, creating a two-tier academic workforce.
  • Some argue that post-tenure review is needed to maintain accountability.

Around the world

Tenure-like systems exist in many countries. In the United Kingdom, the Education Reform Act 1988 abolished tenure for newly appointed academics while preserving academic freedom by statute. In Germany, professors appointed as Beamte (civil servants) enjoy strong job security. Many European systems hire academics as civil servants with permanent status from an early career stage.

Post-tenure review

Many institutions conduct periodic post-tenure review of tenured faculty. Such reviews typically assess continued productivity and, in cases of sustained unsatisfactory performance, may lead to development plans or, rarely, dismissal proceedings.
