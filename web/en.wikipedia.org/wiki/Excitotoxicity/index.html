Excitotoxicity is the pathological process by which neurons are damaged or killed by excessive stimulation by neurotransmitters such as glutamate and similar substances. When glutamate receptors — particularly the NMDA receptor and AMPA receptors — are overactivated, abnormally large amounts of calcium ions (Ca²⁺) enter the cell, triggering enzymes that damage cell structures and generate free radicals.

Mechanism

Under normal conditions glutamate is rapidly cleared from the synaptic cleft by transporters on neurons and astrocytes. When energy supply fails, as in stroke, transporters fail or reverse, extracellular glutamate accumulates, and sustained receptor activation produces a lethal calcium influx. The calcium overload activates proteases (calpains), phospholipases, and nitric oxide synthase, permeabilises mitochondria, and can lead to necrotic or apoptotic cell death.

History

The toxic effect of glutamate on the nervous system was noted by Lucas and Newhouse in 1957, and John Olney coined the term "excitotoxicity" in 1969 after showing that monosodium glutamate caused lesions in the mouse brain and retina. The concept was developed in the 1980s by Dennis Choi and others, who demonstrated glutamate-mediated neuronal death in culture and its role in ischaemic injury.

Clinical significance

Excitotoxic mechanisms are implicated in acute conditions (ischaemic stroke, traumatic brain and spinal cord injury, status epilepticus) and chronic neurodegenerative diseases including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Huntington's disease, and Alzheimer's disease. The NMDA antagonist memantine is approved for Alzheimer's disease, and riluzole, which reduces glutamate release, modestly extends survival in ALS; many other anti-excitotoxic drugs have failed in stroke trials.
