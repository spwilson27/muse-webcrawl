Facial expression

A facial expression is one or more motions or positions of the muscles beneath the skin of the face. These movements convey an individual's emotional state to observers and are a form of nonverbal communication. Facial expressions are produced by the facial muscles, innervated principally by the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII).

Basic emotions theory

Psychologist Paul Ekman proposed that expressions of a set of basic emotions — happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust and later contempt — are recognised across cultures. His Facial Action Coding System (FACS) describes expressions in terms of individual muscle actions ("action units"). Critics note that recognition is influenced by context and culture, and later research (e.g. Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion) disputes strict universality.

Communication and development

Faces are central to social signalling beyond emotion: gaze direction, expressions of dominance or appeasement, and conversational cues. Newborns imitate some facial gestures, and expression perception develops through infancy. Some expressions are voluntary and others involuntary; the Duchenne smile, involving the orbicularis oculi muscles around the eyes, is often cited as a marker of genuine enjoyment.

Disorders and technology

Conditions such as facial palsy, Parkinson's disease and Möbius syndrome impair expression. Automated facial-expression recognition is widely used in affective computing, with applications and controversies in surveillance and hiring.
