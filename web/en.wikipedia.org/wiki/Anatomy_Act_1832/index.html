The Anatomy Act 1832 (2 & 3 Will. 4. c. 75) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that provided for the licensing of anatomists and a legal supply of cadavers for medical research and anatomical study. It was enacted in response to public revulsion at grave robbery ("body snatching") and the Burke and Hare murders of 1828, in which sixteen people in Edinburgh were murdered so their bodies could be sold for dissection.

Background

Under the law inherited from the Murder Act 1752, the only bodies legally available for dissection were those of executed murderers. The expansion of medical schools in the early nineteenth century created a demand for hundreds of cadavers a year, far beyond that supply. "Resurrection men" exhumed freshly buried corpses and sold them to anatomy teachers; in London the gang known as the London Burkers imitated Burke and Hare's murders, and the 1831 "Italian Boy" case intensified pressure for reform. A select committee chaired by Henry Warburton had reported in 1828, and Warburton steered the eventual bill through Parliament.

Provisions

The Act allowed licensed teachers of anatomy to receive the unclaimed bodies of people who died in workhouses, hospitals, and other institutions, unless the deceased or their relatives objected; it also permitted individuals to donate their own bodies or those of relatives. It created inspectors of anatomy, required premises for dissection to be licensed, and effectively ended the dissection of executed criminals as a punishment. Grave robbery declined sharply once a lawful supply existed.

Controversy and legacy

The Act was fiercely resented by the poor, who feared that entering a workhouse hospital meant being dissected after death — a fate previously reserved for murderers — and it fed wider hostility to the New Poor Law of 1834. Historians such as Ruth Richardson (in Death, Dissection and the Destitute) have emphasised how the Act shifted the burden of supplying cadavers onto the destitute. Nevertheless, it established the framework of regulated anatomical donation that persisted until the Anatomy Act 1984, and later the Human Tissue Act 2004, replaced it.
