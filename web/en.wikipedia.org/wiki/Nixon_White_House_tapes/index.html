The Nixon White House tapes are audio recordings of conversations between U.S. President Richard Nixon and his administration officials, made by a secret taping system installed in the White House in 1971. The system's existence, revealed during the Senate Watergate hearings in July 1973, and the tapes' contents — above all the "smoking gun" recording of 23 June 1972 — proved decisive in ending Nixon's presidency.

The system

Nixon, who knew that earlier presidents had recorded selected conversations, ordered the Secret Service to install an extensive, voice-activated system in February 1971, covering the Oval Office, the Cabinet Room and his telephones; a second office in the Executive Office Building and the presidential retreat at Camp David were added later. Only a handful of officials — Nixon, H. R. Haldeman and supervising aide Alexander Butterfield among them — knew of it. Recordings ceased in July 1973, days before Butterfield's revelation, when the system was dismantled.

Subpoena and the Saturday Night Massacre

Special prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed nine tapes in July 1973. Nixon refused, citing executive privilege, and in October ordered Cox's dismissal when Cox would not accept summaries instead — the "Saturday Night Massacre", in which Attorney General Elliot Richardson and his deputy William Ruckelshaus resigned rather than fire Cox, before Solicitor General Robert Bork did so. The public storm nearly produced impeachment, and Nixon released transcripts ("expletive deleted") in April 1974 that badly damaged him.

One subpoenaed tape, of a 20 June 1972 conversation with Haldeman, contained an unexplained 18½-minute gap; Nixon's secretary Rose Mary Woods, who had transcribed it, famously demonstrated an awkward stretch — dubbed the "Rose Mary stretch" — while suggesting she might have erased part of it accidentally. A panel of experts concluded the gap resulted from multiple deliberate erasures.

Smoking gun and resignation

In United States v. Nixon (24 July 1974), the Supreme Court unanimously ordered Nixon to surrender the tapes. The 23 June 1972 tape released on 5 August 1974 showed Nixon approving a plan to use the CIA to block the FBI's Watergate investigation — destroying his remaining congressional support. Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974.

Legacy

Under the Presidential Recordings and Materials Preservation Act, the tapes are held by the National Archives and have been released in segments over decades; they remain an unmatched verbatim record of a presidency in crisis.
