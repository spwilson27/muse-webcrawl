Dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia

The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia proposes that the disorder results from abnormal dopamine neurotransmission — particularly hyperactivity of dopamine D2 receptor signalling in subcortical regions and hypoactivity in prefrontal cortex.

Dopamine hypothesis
Field: Psychiatry, neuroscience
Key receptor: D2
Proposed: 1960s
Related drug action: Antipsychotics block D2

Origins

The hypothesis arose when chlorpromazine and other early antipsychotics were found to block dopamine receptors, and amphetamines — which increase dopamine release — were observed to provoke psychosis. Arvid Carlsson and Jacques van Rossum were among its early proponents in the 1960s.

Revised versions

Modern versions focus on increased presynaptic dopamine synthesis and release capacity in the striatum, driven by genetic and environmental risk factors, with consequences for aberrant salience attribution (Kapur). Prefrontal hypodopaminergia is linked to negative and cognitive symptoms. The hypothesis now sits alongside glutamate (NMDA hypofunction) models, and has informed — but does not fully explain — antipsychotic drug action.
