Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation is inflammation of the nervous tissue, especially within the brain and spinal cord. It may be mediated by cytokines, chemokines, reactive oxygen species, and secondary messengers produced by resident central nervous system glia (microglia and astrocytes), endothelial cells, peripherally derived immune cells, and other factors. Unlike peripheral inflammation, neuroinflammation occurs behind the blood–brain barrier and is often chronic and self-sustaining.

Causes

Neuroinflammation can be triggered by infection, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, stroke, toxins, autoimmunity, and ageing. In neurodegenerative disease, aggregated proteins such as amyloid-beta and alpha-synuclein act as danger signals that keep glia in an activated state.

Mechanism

Microglia, the resident macrophages of the CNS, sense damage-associated and pathogen-associated molecular patterns through receptors including Toll-like receptors and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Activated microglia and reactive astrocytes release inflammatory mediators that recruit further immune activity, increase blood–brain barrier permeability, and can kill or support neurons depending on context. Short-lived inflammation assists debris clearance and repair, but chronic activation contributes to neuronal loss and synapse elimination.

Role in disease

Neuroinflammation is a recognised component of Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, multiple sclerosis, and Huntington's disease, and is implicated in depression, chronic pain, and the sickness behaviour that accompanies systemic infection. Genetic studies link Alzheimer's risk to microglial genes such as TREM2, strengthening the causal case.

Treatment approaches

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, minocycline, inflammasome inhibitors, and cytokine-blocking biologics have all been investigated; so far, results in neurodegeneration have been mixed, and timing and patient selection are considered crucial. Imaging with PET ligands for translocator protein (TSPO) allows inflammation to be measured in living patients.
