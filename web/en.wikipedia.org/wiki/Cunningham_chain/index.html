Cunningham chain

[Image: Diagram showing a sequence of primes each generated from the last by doubling and adding one]

In mathematics, a Cunningham chain is a sequence of prime numbers in which each member is generated from the previous one by a fixed linear rule. The chains are named after the mathematician Allan Joseph Champneys Cunningham, who studied them in the early twentieth century.

First kind

A Cunningham chain of the first kind of length n is a sequence of primes p1, p2, …, pn such that each subsequent term is pi+1 = 2pi + 1. Each prime in such a chain (after the first) is a Sophie Germain prime's successor: if p and 2p + 1 are both prime, then p is a Sophie Germain prime. An example of length 5 is 2, 5, 11, 23, 47.

Second kind

A Cunningham chain of the second kind uses the rule pi+1 = 2pi − 1. An example is 2, 3, 5, 7, 13. There is also a generalised Cunningham chain with rule pi+1 = a·pi + b for fixed integers a and b.

Records

The longest known Cunningham chains are found by large distributed computing searches; record chains of the first kind have reached lengths in the high teens, with the individual primes having thousands of digits. It is conjectured, on heuristic grounds related to the prime k-tuple conjecture, that arbitrarily long Cunningham chains exist, but none has been proved to be infinite — indeed, every individual chain must terminate, since the iterated sequence eventually hits a composite number.
