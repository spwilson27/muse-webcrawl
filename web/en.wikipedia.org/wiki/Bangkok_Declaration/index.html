Bangkok Declaration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bangkok Declaration is the name of two distinct instruments of Southeast Asian diplomacy. The first, signed on 8 August 1967 in Bangkok by Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand, is the founding declaration of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) — a brief document pledging cooperation in economic growth, social progress and regional peace, which served as ASEAN's charter until the ASEAN Charter of 2007.

The second is the Bangkok Declaration adopted on 2 April 1993 by Asian governments at their regional preparatory meeting for the Vienna World Conference on Human Rights. It asserted that human rights, while universal, “must be considered in the context of a dynamic and evolving process of international norm-setting, bearing in mind the significance of national and regional particularities and various historical, cultural and religious backgrounds” — the canonical statement of the “Asian values” position associated with leaders such as Lee Kuan Yew and Mahathir Mohamad, answered in parallel by Asian NGOs' own Bangkok declaration for a universalist reading. The Vienna Declaration (June 1993) split the difference: universality affirmed, particularities noted. The 1993 text remains the reference point for cultural-relativist arguments in human-rights diplomacy.
