Digital wellbeing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digital wellbeing (or digital well-being) refers to the state of physical and mental health of an individual in relation to their use of digital technologies, and to the tools, design practices, and habits intended to keep technology use balanced and intentional. The term gained prominence in the late 2010s as research and public debate grew about smartphone and social-media habits, attention, sleep, and adolescent mental health, and as major platform companies introduced built-in features to help users monitor and limit their screen time.

Platform features

In 2018 Google launched a "Digital Wellbeing" initiative for Android, including a dashboard of app usage, app timers, a "Wind Down" mode that greys the screen at bedtime, and notification controls; Apple introduced the similar Screen Time feature in iOS 12 the same year. Social platforms added their own activity dashboards and reminder prompts (for example, Instagram's "You're all caught up" and daily-limit tools). These features are commonly grouped with digital-detox practices such as notification batching, grayscale displays, and phone-free periods.

Research

Research on digital technology and wellbeing is extensive but contested. Large-scale studies generally find small average associations between screen time and measures such as life satisfaction, with effects varying by type of use (active communication versus passive scrolling), by individual, and by context. Some researchers and public-health bodies warn particularly about adolescents, social comparison, cyberbullying, and sleep displacement; others caution that correlations are weak, reverse causation is plausible, and headline claims outrun the evidence. Reviews by bodies such as the UK Chief Medical Officers and the US Surgeon General (which issued an advisory on social media and youth mental health in 2023) have recommended precautionary habits rather than blanket bans for most users.

Design and policy

Critics argue that persuasive design — infinite scroll, autoplay, streaks, and variable-reward notifications — is engineered to maximise engagement at odds with user wellbeing, and that self-regulation tools offered by the same companies are insufficient. Policy responses have included age-appropriate design codes (such as the UK's Children's Code), school phone restrictions, and requirements for default screen-time limits for minors in some countries. "Digital wellbeing" is also used in occupational health for the management of always-on work communication and the right to disconnect.
