Louis IX of France

Louis IX (Saint Louis)
Born: 25 April 1214, Poissy
Died: 25 August 1270, Tunis
Reign: 1226–1270
Canonised: 1297 by Boniface VIII

Louis IX (25 April 1214 – 25 August 1270), commonly known as Saint Louis, was King of France from 1226 until his death in 1270. He is the only canonised king of France, renowned for his piety, justice reforms and leadership of the Seventh and Eighth Crusades.

Reign

Crowned at 12 under the regency of his mother Blanche of Castile, Louis consolidated royal authority, defeated England at Taillebourg (1242), and by the Treaty of Paris (1259) settled claims with Henry III. He issued ordinances reforming administration, prohibited trial by ordeal and private warfare, established the Parlement's judicial role, and was famed for dispensing justice personally under an oak at Vincennes.

Crusades and death

Vowing a crusade after illness, Louis captured Damietta in 1249 but was defeated and captured in Egypt in 1250, later ransomed. He built the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris to house the Crown of Thorns. On the Eighth Crusade he died of dysentery at Tunis in 1270.

Legacy

Canonised in 1297, Louis became the ideal of the Christian monarch; cities such as St. Louis, Missouri bear his name.
