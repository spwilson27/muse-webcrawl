Sperner's theorem

Sperner's theorem, named after Emanuel Sperner, who published it in 1928, is a result in extremal combinatorics describing the largest possible antichains in the Boolean lattice of subsets of a finite set. It is one of the founding results of extremal set theory.

Statement

Let S be a set with n elements. A family ℱ of subsets of S is called an antichain (or Sperner family) if no member of ℱ contains another member. Sperner's theorem states that the largest antichain has size equal to the largest binomial coefficient:

|ℱ| ≤ C(n, ⌊n/2⌋).

Moreover, equality holds for the family of all subsets of size ⌊n/2⌋, and also (when n is odd) for those of size ⌈n/2⌉; these are essentially the unique extremal families.

Proof

The standard proof uses the LYM inequality (after Lubell, Yamamoto, and Meshalkin). Count maximal chains ∅ ⊂ {x1} ⊂ … ⊂ S through the Boolean lattice: there are n! such chains, one for each ordering of S, and a set A of size k lies on exactly k!(n − k)! of them. Since an antichain meets each maximal chain at most once,

ΣA ∈ ℱ 1 / C(n, |A|) ≤ 1.

Because each binomial coefficient C(n, k) is at most C(n, ⌊n/2⌋), the theorem follows. An alternative proof uses shifting (compression) or the Katona circle method.

Generalizations

Sperner's theorem was generalised by Erdős to families with no chain of k + 1 sets (the largest such family is the union of the k largest ranks of the lattice), and it has analogues for multisets, vector spaces over finite fields (q-analogues), and other ranked posets with the Sperner property. The theorem also underlies results on the width of partially ordered sets, related to Dilworth's and Mirsky's theorems.
