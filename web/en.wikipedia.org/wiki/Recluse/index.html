Recluse

A recluse is a person who lives in voluntary seclusion from the public and society. The word is from the Latin recludere, meaning "shut up" or "sequester". Historically, the word has referred to the Christian practice of anchorites, who were enclosed in cells attached to churches, as well as to hermits living alone in the wilderness. In modern usage it describes anyone who withdraws markedly from social life, whether for religious, artistic, personal or psychological reasons.

Religious reclusion

Solitude has been a valued spiritual discipline in many traditions. In early Christianity, the Desert Fathers and Mothers withdrew to the Egyptian desert; some anchorites, such as Julian of Norwich, lived permanently enclosed in a cell while offering counsel through a window. In Buddhism and Hinduism, periods of solitary retreat are common stages of religious life, and some Japanese Buddhist monks practised extreme forms of ascetic seclusion. In Roman Catholic canon law, hermits who profess vows in a diocese remain a recognised form of consecrated life.

Secular reclusion

Notable secular recluses have included inventors, writers and wealthy eccentrics. Howard Hughes spent his later years in near-total seclusion in hotel rooms. Author J. D. Salinger withdrew from public life for decades after the success of The Catcher in the Rye. Factors leading to reclusion can include a desire for uninterrupted creative work, misanthropy, social anxiety or other health conditions; in Japan, the phenomenon of severe social withdrawal among young people is known as hikikomori.
