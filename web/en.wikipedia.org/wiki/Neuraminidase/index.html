Neuraminidase

Neuraminidase (also called sialidase) is a family of glycoside hydrolase enzymes that cleave the glycosidic linkages of neuraminic acids (sialic acids). Neuraminidase enzymes are a large family found in a range of organisms; the best-known is viral neuraminidase, a target of antiviral drugs used against influenza. Together with hemagglutinin, it is one of the two surface glycoproteins of influenza A and B viruses and gives the "N" number in subtype names such as H1N1.

ReactionHydrolysis of α-(2→3), α-(2→6), α-(2→8) glycosidic linkages of terminal sialic acid residues
Found inInfluenza viruses, paramyxoviruses, bacteria, mammals

Function in influenza

Viral neuraminidase sits in the envelope as a mushroom-shaped tetramer. Its enzymatic activity destroys the sialic-acid receptors to which hemagglutinin binds, allowing newly formed virions to be released from the host cell instead of clumping at its surface, and helps the virus penetrate respiratory mucus. The balance between hemagglutinin binding and neuraminidase cleavage is important for viral fitness.

Structure

The enzyme's head is a six-bladed beta-propeller of about 400 amino acids held above the membrane on a thin stalk. The active site is highly conserved across influenza strains, which makes it an attractive drug target.

Neuraminidase inhibitors

Drugs that occupy the active site — oseltamivir (Tamiflu), zanamivir (Relenza), peramivir, and laninamivir — block progeny virus release and are used to treat and prevent influenza. Resistance can arise through mutations such as H274Y in N1 neuraminidase, which spread globally in seasonal H1N1 in 2007–2009 even in the absence of drug pressure.

Other organisms

Bacterial sialidases (for example in Vibrio cholerae and Clostridium perfringens) act as virulence factors by modifying host cell surfaces, while mammals have four neuraminidases (NEU1–NEU4) involved in lysosomal catabolism and cell signalling; deficiency of NEU1 causes the storage disease sialidosis.
