Creeping juniper (Juniperus horizontalis) is a low-growing, prostrate evergreen shrub in the cypress family, native to northern North America, from Alaska and Canada south to the northern United States. It grows in a wide variety of habitats — rocky slopes, sand dunes, prairies, cliffs and open woods — typically forming broad mats only 10–30 cm tall but spreading several metres across, rooting at the nodes where stems touch the ground.

Description

The foliage consists mainly of scale-like leaves pressed to the stems, with awl-shaped juvenile leaves on vigorous shoots; colour ranges from green to blue-green and often turns plum-purple in winter. The species is usually dioecious, with separate male and female plants. The seed cones are berry-like, dark blue with a whitish bloom, about 5–8 mm across, ripening over two seasons and eaten by birds, which disperse the seeds. The plant is closely related to the Rocky Mountain juniper (Juniperus scopulorum) and the eastern red cedar (J. virginiana), with which it can hybridise where ranges meet.

Cultivation

Because of its dense, ground-covering habit, hardiness and tolerance of poor, dry soils, creeping juniper is widely planted for erosion control on slopes and banks and in rock gardens. Numerous cultivars have been selected for foliage colour and habit, including 'Wiltonii' (Blue Rug), 'Bar Harbor', 'Blue Chip', 'Prince of Wales' and the golden 'Mother Lode'. It requires full sun and good drainage and is generally disease-tolerant, though it can serve as an alternate host for cedar-apple rust fungi.
