Mundell–Fleming model

FieldInternational macroeconomics
Developed byRobert Mundell and Marcus Fleming
PeriodEarly 1960s
Also known asIS–LM–BoP model

The Mundell–Fleming model, also known as the IS–LM–BoP model, is an economic model first set out by Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming in the early 1960s. It extends the closed-economy IS–LM analysis of short-run output and interest rates to a small open economy that trades with the rest of the world, adding a balance-of-payments condition to the goods-market (IS) and money-market (LM) equilibria.

Assumptions and structure

The model typically assumes a small open economy with perfect capital mobility, so that the domestic interest rate is tied to the world interest rate. Under these conditions the exchange-rate regime determines the effectiveness of policy: the model is usually presented with three curves — IS, LM, and a horizontal balance-of-payments (BP) line at the world interest rate.

Prices are assumed fixed in the short run, so nominal and real exchange rates move together.

Policy conclusions

The model's best-known result concerns the policy trilemma: an economy cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and an independent monetary policy. Under a floating exchange rate with perfect capital mobility, monetary policy is highly effective while fiscal policy is powerless, because expansion is offset by currency appreciation; under a fixed exchange rate the conclusions reverse, with fiscal policy effective and monetary policy impotent.

These results made the model the standard workhorse of international macroeconomics teaching and policy analysis for decades.

Criticism and legacy

The model has been criticised for its static expectations, its treatment of capital flows as perfectly mobile, and its neglect of stock-flow dynamics and exchange-rate expectations. Later approaches, including intertemporal open-economy models, sought to rebuild its insights on microeconomic foundations. Mundell received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1999 in part for this work.
