Singing

Singing is the act of creating musical sounds with the voice. A person whose profession is singing is called a singer, artist or vocalist (in jazz and popular music). Singers perform music (arias, recitatives, songs, etc.) that can be sung with or without accompaniment by musical instruments. Singing features in every known culture, in worship, ritual, work, storytelling and entertainment, and ranges from informal communal activity to highly trained professional performance.

Physiology and technique

Singing involves the coordinated use of the lungs (which supply air), the larynx and vocal folds (which vibrate to produce pitched sound), and the resonating cavities of the throat, mouth and nasal passages, shaped by the articulators — lips, tongue, jaw and soft palate — which form vowels and consonants. Vocal pedagogy addresses breath management ("support"), registration (chest voice, head voice, falsetto), intonation, diction and range. Adult voices are classified, from highest to lowest, as soprano, mezzo-soprano, alto (contralto), tenor, baritone and bass.

Styles and traditions

Styles of singing vary enormously across cultures and genres: Western classical singing (opera, oratorio, art song) emphasises projection and vibrato; folk traditions include ballad singing, throat singing in Tuva and Mongolia, and call-and-response work songs; popular styles include crooning, belting, rapping (rhythmic speech on pitch), and the melismatic singing of many Middle Eastern and South Asian traditions. Choral singing — from sacred polyphony to gospel and barbershop — is a major participatory form, with documented benefits for social bonding and wellbeing.

Learning and health

Singing may be learned formally, through lessons and choir training, or informally by imitation. Professional singers take care of vocal health, avoiding strain and treating conditions such as vocal nodules; the scientific study of the singing voice spans acoustics, medicine and pedagogy.
