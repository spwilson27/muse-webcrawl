Air

Air is the general name for the mixture of gases that makes up the atmosphere of Earth and is held around the planet by gravity. It is the air that humans and nearly all terrestrial life breathe, that weather acts within, and through which sound and, in part, light travel.

Composition

Dry air consists chiefly of nitrogen (about 78 percent by volume) and oxygen (about 21 percent), with argon making up most of the remaining one percent. Carbon dioxide, once about 0.028 percent, has risen to roughly 0.042 percent (about 420 parts per million) in the industrial era. Neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen, and nitrous oxide occur in trace amounts. Real air also contains variable amounts of water vapour — from near zero to about 4 percent by volume — which lowers the fractional share of the other gases.

Physical properties

Air is transparent, odourless, and tasteless in its pure state. It has mass and exerts pressure: at sea level the standard atmosphere is 101.325 kilopascals. Its density falls rapidly with altitude, so that about half the atmosphere's mass lies below 5.5 km. The blue colour of the sky results from preferential scattering of short wavelengths of sunlight by air molecules (Rayleigh scattering). Air transmits sound at about 343 metres per second at 20 °C, and its refractive index, slightly greater than that of vacuum, causes astronomical refraction and mirage effects.

Role in life and industry

Aerobic organisms extract oxygen from air for respiration, while plants and photosynthetic microbes fix its carbon dioxide and, via nitrogen-fixing pathways, its nitrogen. Air drives the weather, spreads seeds and spores, and is the working fluid of wind turbines. Industry compresses air for tools and braking systems, liquefies it to separate oxygen, nitrogen, and argon, and relies on controlled air (cleanrooms, HVAC) in manufacturing and medicine. Air quality — freedom from particulates, ozone, and pollutant gases — is a central measure of environmental health.

History of understanding

Air was classed as one of the four classical elements in Greek philosophy. Experiments by Torricelli, Pascal, and von Guericke in the 17th century demonstrated its weight and pressure; in the 18th century, Black, Priestley, Scheele, Cavendish, and Lavoisier showed that air is a mixture and identified oxygen and nitrogen, and Ramsay and Rayleigh isolated argon in the 1890s.
