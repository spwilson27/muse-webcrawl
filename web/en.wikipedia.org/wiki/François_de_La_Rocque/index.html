François de La Rocque

François de La Rocque (6 October 1885 – 28 April 1946) was a French soldier and political figure, leader of the Croix-de-Feu, a nationalist veterans' movement of the interwar period, and after its dissolution in 1936 of the French Social Party (Parti social français, PSF), one of the largest political parties in France before the Second World War.

Early life and military career

Born into a military family in Lorient, de La Rocque graduated from the Saint-Cyr military academy and served in North Africa and in the First World War, in which he was wounded and decorated. He left the army in 1928 with the rank of lieutenant-colonel.

Croix-de-Feu and the PSF

De La Rocque took over the Croix-de-Feu, originally a veterans' association, in 1930 and transformed it into a mass nationalist movement. The movement's role in the riots of 6 February 1934 remains debated by historians. When the Popular Front government dissolved the leagues in 1936, de La Rocque refounded his movement as the French Social Party, a conservative, nationalist and social-Catholic party that claimed over a million members by 1939.

Second World War and death

During the Occupation, de La Rocque's attitude toward Vichy and the Germans was ambiguous; he provided intelligence to the Allies through the Klan network and was arrested by the Gestapo in 1943 and deported to Austria. Liberated in 1945, he returned to France under house arrest and died in Paris in 1946, before he could be tried.
