Tepeyac

Tepeyac, also called Tepeyac Hill (Spanish: Cerro del Tepeyac), is a hill in northern Mexico City, in the borough of Gustavo A. Madero. It forms part of the Sierra de Guadalupe range. Tepeyac is famous throughout the Catholic world as the site where, according to tradition, the Virgin Mary appeared to the indigenous Mexican Saint Juan Diego in December 1531, under the title of Our Lady of Guadalupe.

The apparition tradition

According to the account preserved in the Nican Mopohua, a Nahuatl text published in 1649, Juan Diego was crossing Tepeyac on 9 December 1531 when a radiant lady spoke to him in Nahuatl, identifying herself as the Virgin Mary and asking that a church be built on the site. Bishop Juan de Zumárraga demanded proof; on 12 December, Juan Diego gathered Castilian roses — out of season — in his tilma (cloak) on the hill, and when he opened the cloak before the bishop the roses fell and an image of the Virgin was found imprinted on the cloth. The tilma and image are preserved in the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe at the foot of the hill, one of the most visited Catholic shrines in the world, receiving many millions of pilgrims a year, above all around the feast day of 12 December.

History

Before the Spanish conquest, Tepeyac was a place of worship of the Aztec mother goddess Tonantzin ("Our Revered Mother"), and a causeway connected it across Lake Texcoco to Tenochtitlan. After the apparitions, a succession of churches was built at the site: a first chapel in the 1530s, a larger church consecrated in 1622, and the "old basilica" completed in 1709. A small chapel, the Capilla del Cerrito, stands on the summit where the first apparitions are said to have occurred. The modern basilica, designed by Pedro Ramírez Vázquez and completed in 1976, was built because the old church was sinking into the soft lakebed soil; its circular, tent-like design allows up to 10,000 worshippers to see the image of the Virgin, which is displayed above a moving walkway.

Cultural significance

Tepeyac and the image of Guadalupe became central symbols of Mexican identity, carried into battle by the insurgents of Miguel Hidalgo in 1810 and by Emiliano Zapata's forces a century later. Pope John Paul II canonised Juan Diego in 2002.
