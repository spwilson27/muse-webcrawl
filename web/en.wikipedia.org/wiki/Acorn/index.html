Acorn

The acorn is the nut of the oak tree (genera Quercus, Lithocarpus and Notholithocarpus, in the family Fagaceae). Acorns usually contain a single seed enclosed in a tough, leathery shell, seated in a cup-shaped cupule. They range from 1 to 6 cm in length and take between 6 and 24 months to mature, depending on the species.

Ecology

Acorns are an important food source for wildlife including deer, wild boar, bears, squirrels, mice, and birds such as jays, pigeons, and ducks. In "mast years" oaks produce bumper crops that satiate predators and leave surplus seed for regeneration. Scatter-hoarding animals — especially the Eurasian jay and grey squirrel — disperse acorns and inadvertently plant them by burying more than they retrieve. Acorns are rich in carbohydrates but many contain bitter tannins, which discourage some herbivores.

Human use

Acorns were a staple food for many indigenous peoples, notably in California, Korea (where acorn jelly, dotorimuk, is still made), and parts of Europe and the Mediterranean, after leaching out the tannins in water. Ground acorn meal can be used as a flour or a coffee substitute. Acorns also serve as livestock fodder, famously for the Iberian pigs that produce jamón ibérico in the dehesa oak pastures of Spain and Portugal.

Cultural significance

The acorn, symbolising potential, growth, and strength in small beginnings, appears in heraldry, folklore, and proverbs ("great oaks from little acorns grow"). It is a national symbol of growth and renewal in several European traditions and the emblem of the National Trails of England and Wales.
