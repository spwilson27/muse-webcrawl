Air-to-surface missile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An air-to-surface missile (ASM) is a missile designed to be launched from military aircraft at targets on land or sea. Air-to-surface missiles are powered by rocket motors, turbojets, turbofans, or ramjets, and are guided by a variety of systems including laser guidance, infrared homing, optical guidance, satellite navigation, and radar homing. They range from small helicopter-launched anti-tank weapons to large stand-off cruise missiles carried by bombers.

History

Early air-to-surface guided weapons appeared during the Second World War, notably the German Henschel Hs 293 and Fritz X radio-controlled bombs, which sank several Allied warships. Post-war development produced weapons such as the American AGM-12 Bullpup, introduced in the late 1950s, and the AGM-65 Maverick, widely used for close air support from the 1970s. The Vietnam War spurred the development of precision-guided munitions, including laser-guided bombs and the AGM-45 Shrike and AGM-78 Standard ARM anti-radiation missiles for suppressing enemy air defences.

Types

  • Anti-tank guided missiles – e.g. the AGM-114 Hellfire, carried by helicopters and drones.
  • Anti-ship missiles – e.g. the AGM-84 Harpoon and the French Exocet.
  • Anti-radiation missiles – home on enemy radar emissions, e.g. the AGM-88 HARM.
  • Air-launched cruise missiles – long-range stand-off weapons such as the AGM-86 ALCM and Storm Shadow/SCALP.
  • Anti-runway and area weapons – designed to crater runways or deliver submunitions.
  • Decoys – such as the ADM-160 MALD, which imitates aircraft to saturate defences.

Guidance

Guidance methods include command guidance, beam riding, semi-active laser homing (in which a designator "paints" the target), electro-optical and imaging infrared seekers, active and semi-active radar homing, and GPS/INS navigation for fixed targets. Modern missiles frequently combine several methods and feature two-way data links allowing man-in-the-loop control or retargeting in flight.
