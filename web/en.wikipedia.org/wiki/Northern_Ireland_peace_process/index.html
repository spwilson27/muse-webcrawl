Northern Ireland peace process

Periodc. 1988–1998 (and ongoing implementation)
Key agreementGood Friday Agreement, 10 April 1998
PartiesBritish and Irish governments; Northern Ireland political parties
OutcomeEnd of large-scale violence of the Troubles; devolved power-sharing government

The Northern Ireland peace process was the long effort to end the Troubles, the conflict in Northern Ireland that from the late 1960s killed more than 3,500 people. It involved the British and Irish governments, Northern Ireland's unionist and nationalist parties, and paramilitary ceasefires, culminating in the Belfast (Good Friday) Agreement of 1998 and a devolved, power-sharing government at Stormont.

Background

Earlier initiatives, such as the Sunningdale Agreement (1973) and the Anglo-Irish Agreement (1985), failed to end the violence. In the late 1980s, contacts developed between SDLP leader John Hume and Sinn Féin leader Gerry Adams, while the British and Irish governments deepened cooperation. The Downing Street Declaration of December 1993 affirmed that Northern Ireland's status would change only with the consent of a majority of its people.

Ceasefires and talks

The Provisional IRA announced a ceasefire in August 1994, followed in October by loyalist paramilitaries. After the IRA ceasefire briefly broke down in 1996, it was restored in 1997. Multi-party talks, chaired by former U.S. Senator George Mitchell, produced the Good Friday Agreement on 10 April 1998. It created a Northern Ireland Assembly and Executive based on power-sharing, North–South and East–West institutions, prisoner releases, police reform, and the principle of consent.

Implementation

The agreement was endorsed by referendums in both parts of Ireland in May 1998. Devolution began in 1999 under First Minister David Trimble and deputy First Minister Seamus Mallon, but disputes over IRA decommissioning repeatedly suspended the institutions. The IRA completed decommissioning in 2005, and after the St Andrews Agreement of 2006, devolution was restored in 2007 with Ian Paisley of the DUP and Martin McGuinness of Sinn Féin leading the Executive — an outcome once thought impossible.
