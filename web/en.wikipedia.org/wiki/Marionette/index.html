Marionette

A marionette is a puppet controlled from above using wires or strings depending on regional variations of the form. A puppeteer who operates a marionette is called a marionettist. Marionettes are operated with the puppeteer hidden or revealed to an audience, from above the stage platform where the figure performs. The attachment of the strings varies with the character, but most strings are attached to a control device held in one or both of the operator's hands.

Construction

A marionette's body may be made of wood, cloth, papier-mâché or other materials, with jointed limbs. Strings run from the head, back, hands, arms and knees to a horizontal control bar (an "airplane" control) or a vertical control. The number of strings ranges from a handful to more than forty for complex figures, allowing correspondingly subtle movement. Some traditions use a rod through the head in place of a head string.

History

String-operated figures are ancient: jointed dolls have been found in Egyptian and Greek contexts, and string puppets were known in India, China and Southeast Asia. In Europe the form flourished from the early modern period; the word "marionette" derives from French, a diminutive of Marion, a pet form of Marie — little figures of the Virgin Mary were used in religious processions and plays.

Notable traditions include the Sicilian Opera dei Pupi, whose rod-and-string knights were inscribed by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage; the elaborate marionette theatres of Salzburg and Prague; Burmese yoke thé; and the English tradition culminating in the television work of Gerry Anderson ("Supermarionation") and classic series such as Thunderbirds.

In performance

Marionettes have been used for opera, satire, children's theatre and political commentary. Heinrich von Kleist's essay "On the Marionette Theatre" (1810) famously used the puppet as a figure for grace and self-consciousness, and marionette characters — from Punch and Judy adaptations to the creations of Jiří Trnka — recur throughout modern theatre and film.
