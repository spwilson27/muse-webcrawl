The Union of Lublin (Polish: Unia lubelska; Lithuanian: Liublino unija) was signed on 1 July 1569 in Lublin, Poland, and created a single state, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, by uniting the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in a real union. It replaced the previous personal union of the two states under one monarch dating back to the Union of Krewo (1385).

Background and negotiations

King Sigismund II Augustus, the last Jagiellonian king and childless, sought to perpetuate the union. Lithuanian magnates feared absorption and walked out of the Sejm in March 1569; the king responded by annexing the Lithuanian voivodeships of Podlachia, Volhynia, Bracław and Kiev to the Polish Crown, forcing the Lithuanians to return and accept terms.

Terms

The act created a single Sejm meeting in Warsaw, a jointly elected monarch crowned king of Poland and grand duke of Lithuania, and common foreign policy and currency, while the two states retained separate armies, treasuries, administrations and legal systems — Lithuania keeping its Statutes. The Commonwealth became one of Europe's largest and most populous states, lasting until the Third Partition of 1795.

Legacy

The Union is commemorated by Jan Matejko's monumental painting (1869) and, in 2019, by the Lublin Union's addition to UNESCO's Memory of the World register and the European Heritage Label.
