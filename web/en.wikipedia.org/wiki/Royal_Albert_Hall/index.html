The Royal Albert Hall is a concert hall on the northern edge of South Kensington, London. One of the United Kingdom's most treasured and recognisable buildings, it was opened by Queen Victoria in 1871 as a memorial to her late husband Prince Albert, and forms the practical centrepiece of the cultural quarter nicknamed "Albertopolis". The hall is owned and operated by a registered charity, the Corporation of the Hall of Arts and Sciences.

Architecture

The circular hall was designed by Captain Francis Fowke and Major-General Henry Scott of the Royal Engineers, and built in red brick with an elaborate terracotta frieze depicting the "Triumph of Arts and Sciences". Its great glass-and-iron dome, 41 metres high, was a landmark of Victorian engineering. Originally gas-lit, the auditorium was plagued by a notorious echo — it was said to be the only place where a composer could be sure of hearing his work twice — which was largely cured by the "mushroom" acoustic discs hung from the ceiling in 1969.

Events

Since 1941 the hall has been the home of the Proms, the BBC's summer season of orchestral concerts, whose Last Night is a national institution. Beyond classical music it has hosted almost everything: the first Sumo tournament outside Japan for 1,500 years, Eurovision (1968), boxing and tennis, rock and pop concerts from the Beatles and the Rolling Stones to Cream's farewell and reunion concerts and Eric Clapton's record-breaking residencies, and the annual Festival of Remembrance. Debenture holders — including many of the original seat owners of 1871 — retain ownership of about a quarter of the seats.

The Grand Organ, built by Henry Willis and once the largest in the world, dominates the stage. Outside, at the hall's south steps, stands the Albert Memorial in Kensington Gardens, facing the hall across Kensington Gore.
