In mathematical optimization, the method of Lagrange multipliers is a strategy for finding the local maxima and minima of a function subject to equation constraints (that is, subject to the condition that one or more equations have to be satisfied exactly by the chosen values of the variables). It is named after the mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange.

The basic idea is to convert a constrained problem into a form such that the derivative test of an unconstrained problem can still be applied. The relationship between the gradient of the function and the gradients of the constraints rather naturally leads to a reformulation of the original problem, known as the Lagrangian function.

Statement

To find the maximum or minimum of a function f(x) subject to the equality constraint g(x) = 0, one forms the Lagrangian ℒ(x, λ) = f(x) − λ·g(x) and seeks its stationary points, where the scalar λ is the Lagrange multiplier. At a constrained optimum, the gradient of f is parallel to the gradient of g: ∇f = λ∇g. The method extends to several variables and several constraints, with one multiplier per constraint.

Interpretation

The value of the multiplier has an important interpretation: it measures the sensitivity of the optimal value of the objective to a relaxation of the constraint. If the constraint is written g(x) = c, then λ equals the rate of change of the optimal objective value with respect to c. In economics this is the "shadow price" of the constrained resource.

Applications

Lagrange multipliers are used throughout mathematics, physics and economics: in deriving equations of motion under constraints in classical mechanics, in maximising entropy in statistical mechanics, in consumer theory (maximising utility subject to a budget constraint), and in machine learning, where the dual problem of support vector machines is obtained through a Lagrangian formulation. The generalisation to inequality constraints is given by the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions.

Limitations

The method finds candidate stationary points but does not by itself determine whether they are maxima, minima or saddle points, and it requires the constraint surfaces to satisfy regularity conditions (constraint qualification). For problems with inequality constraints, in economics and optimal control the KKT conditions or the maximum principle are used instead.
