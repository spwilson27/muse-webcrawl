Azide

In chemistry, an azide is a compound containing the linear anion N3−, consisting of three nitrogen atoms, or the corresponding functional group –N3 in organic compounds. Azides are best known for their use in explosives, propellants, and the "click chemistry" azide–alkyne cycloaddition.

Properties

The azide anion is linear and isoelectronic with carbon dioxide. Azide salts of heavy metals, such as lead azide, are shock-sensitive primary explosives used in detonators. Sodium azide is used as the gas-generating agent in automobile airbags and as a laboratory preservative; it is acutely toxic, comparable to cyanide.

Organic azides

Organic azides undergo characteristic reactions including the Staudinger reaction with phosphines, thermal or photochemical decomposition to nitrenes, and the copper-catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC), the archetypal "click" reaction that yields 1,2,3-triazoles and is widely used in bioconjugation.

Safety

Many azides are hazardous: heavy-metal azides detonate on shock or friction, and low-molecular-weight organic azides can be explosively unstable. Hydrazoic acid is both highly toxic and explosive.
