Algebraic geometry

Algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that studies geometric structures defined by polynomial equations. It grew out of the classical study of curves and surfaces defined by polynomials and, in the twentieth century, was rebuilt on the foundations of commutative algebra through the work of Oscar Zariski, André Weil, Jean-Pierre Serre, and Alexander Grothendieck.

Basic notions

The fundamental objects of algebraic geometry are algebraic varieties: solution sets of systems of polynomial equations over a field. Affine and projective varieties are studied through their coordinate rings, and the correspondence between ideals in a polynomial ring and algebraic sets is given by Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. Grothendieck's theory of schemes generalised varieties by allowing arbitrary commutative rings as local models, making it possible to treat arithmetic and geometry uniformly.

Applications

Algebraic geometry underlies much of modern number theory, including the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem via elliptic curves, and it has applications in cryptography, robotics, string theory, and computer-aided design. Key concepts include sheaves, cohomology, divisors, and algebraic cycles.
