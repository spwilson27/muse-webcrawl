Algebraic geometry

Algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that solves systems of polynomial equations using abstract algebraic techniques, classically studying the zero sets of multivariate polynomials. Modern algebraic geometry is based on the use of abstract algebraic techniques, mainly from commutative algebra, for solving geometrical problems about these sets of zeros. Its fundamental objects of study are algebraic varieties, subsets of affine or projective space defined by polynomial equations, and more generally schemes, introduced by Alexander Grothendieck in the 20th century.

History

The field has roots in the analytic geometry of Descartes and the study of conic sections in antiquity. In the 19th century, Riemann, and later the Italian school of Castelnuovo, Enriques, and Severi, developed the geometry of algebraic curves and surfaces. The foundations were rebuilt rigorously in the 20th century by van der Waerden, Zariski, and Weil, and revolutionized by Grothendieck's theory of schemes, which unified algebraic geometry with number theory.

Basic notions

An affine variety is the set of common zeros of a collection of polynomials in affine space; the corresponding algebraic object is a finitely generated algebra over a field, and Hilbert's Nullstellensatz relates the geometry of zero sets to ideals in polynomial rings. Projective varieties live in projective space and are better behaved with respect to intersection theory: for example, Bézout's theorem counts the intersections of projective curves, taking multiplicities into account. Morphisms between varieties are maps given by polynomials or rational functions.

Subfields and applications

Within the subject, algebraic cycles and their equivalence relations underpin intersection theory and the theory of motives, while moduli spaces parametrize families of geometric objects. Algebraic geometry has deep connections to number theory — culminating in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem via elliptic curves and modular forms — as well as to complex analysis, topology, mathematical physics (mirror symmetry and string theory), and applied areas such as robotics, coding theory, and computer vision.
