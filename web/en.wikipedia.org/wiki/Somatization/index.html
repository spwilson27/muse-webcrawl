Somatization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Somatization is a tendency to experience and communicate psychological distress in the form of somatic (physical) symptoms and to seek medical help for them. Commonly reported symptoms include pain (headache, back pain, joint pain), gastrointestinal complaints, fatigue, and cardiopulmonary symptoms. The symptoms are genuinely experienced but are not fully explained by a general medical condition, substance use, or another mental disorder; psychological factors are judged to play an important role in their onset, severity, or persistence.

Diagnosis and classification

The psychiatric classification of somatization has changed over time. Earlier editions of the DSM included somatization disorder, a chronic condition with numerous unexplained symptoms beginning before age 30, historically related to the older diagnosis of hysteria and to Briquet's syndrome. The DSM-5 (2013) replaced most of these categories with somatic symptom disorder, which emphasizes distressing symptoms plus excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviours related to them, rather than requiring that symptoms be medically unexplained. The ICD-11 uses the term bodily distress disorder. Somatization is common in primary care worldwide and is associated with high health-care utilization, functional impairment, and comorbid depression and anxiety.

Causes and treatment

Somatization is understood biopsychosocially: heightened attention to bodily sensations, catastrophic interpretation of symptoms, illness behaviour learned in family and culture, stress, and trauma can all contribute; cultures differ in the extent to which distress is expressed physically rather than emotionally. Management rests on a stable relationship with a single clinician, regular scheduled visits, limiting unnecessary investigations, and treatment of comorbid conditions. Cognitive behavioural therapy and other psychological treatments, and antidepressant medication where indicated, have evidence of benefit.
