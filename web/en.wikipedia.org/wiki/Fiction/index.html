Fiction is any creative work, chiefly a narrative work, that portrays people, places, or events that are imaginary or partly imaginary rather than factual. Although fiction is conventionally contrasted with nonfiction — which presents only true, factual information — the boundary is porous: fiction may incorporate real places and historical events, and factual works may use narrative techniques borrowed from fiction.

The term derives from the Latin fictio, "a making or fashioning." Fiction's oldest surviving forms are epic poems and myths; its most widespread modern form is the novel.

History

Storytelling is universal in human cultures. Early written fiction includes the ancient Egyptian tales, the Greek epics of Homer, and romances such as The Tale of Genji (11th century, Japan) and Don Quixote (1605), often called the first modern novel. The rise of printing, literacy, and a commercial book market in the 18th century — with writers such as Defoe, Richardson, and Fielding — made prose fiction the dominant literary form in the West.

Elements of fiction

  • Plot — the sequence of events, often structured as exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution.
  • Characters — the people (or personified beings) who act and are acted upon; protagonists, antagonists, and supporting figures.
  • Setting — the time and place of the action, including social milieu.
  • Theme — the central ideas or questions the work explores.
  • Point of view — the narrative perspective (first person, third person, omniscient, limited, unreliable).
  • Style — diction, syntax, imagery, and tone.

Formats and genres

By length, prose fiction ranges from flash fiction and the short story through the novella to the novel. Genres include mystery, science fiction, fantasy, romance, horror, historical fiction, and the thriller, among many others. Fiction also thrives in drama, film, radio, television, comics, and video games, each medium shaping narrative possibilities differently.

Fiction and reality

Readers typically approach fiction with a "willing suspension of disbelief," granting the invented world provisional reality. Historical fiction, roman à clef, and autofiction deliberately blur fact and invention. Philosophers and literary theorists debate how fictional statements can be "true in the story" while false of the world, and how fiction nonetheless conveys genuine knowledge about human experience.
