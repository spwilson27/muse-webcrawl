Luwians

The Luwians were an ancient Anatolian people who spoke Luwian, an Indo-European language of the Anatolian branch closely related to Hittite. They inhabited large parts of Anatolia (modern Turkey) during the Bronze Age and early Iron Age, in regions including western and southern Anatolia and northern Syria.

History

Luwian-speaking populations are attested from the second millennium BCE. Within the Hittite Empire, Luwian was widely spoken and used in religious texts, and Luwian hieroglyphic inscriptions appear on seals and monuments. After the collapse of the Hittite Empire around 1200 BCE, Luwian-speaking Neo-Hittite (Syro-Hittite) states persisted in southeastern Anatolia and northern Syria, leaving monumental inscriptions and sculptures at centres such as Carchemish, Melid (Malatya), and Sam'al, until they were absorbed into the Assyrian Empire by the late 8th century BCE.

Language and script

Luwian was written both in cuneiform and in a distinctive hieroglyphic script (Anatolian hieroglyphs), which was deciphered in the 20th century. The hieroglyphic inscriptions, often bilingual with Phoenician (as at Karatepe), have been a major source for Luwian history and religion; notable deities include the storm god Tarhunt.
