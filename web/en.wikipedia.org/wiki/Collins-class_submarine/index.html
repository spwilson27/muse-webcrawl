Collins-class submarine

The Collins class is a class of six Australian-built diesel-electric submarines operated by the Royal Australian Navy (RAN). Named for Vice Admiral Sir John Augustine Collins, the lead boat HMAS Collins was commissioned in 1996 and the last of the class, HMAS Rankin, in 2003. Designed with Swedish assistance from Kockums (an enlarged development of the Västergötland class) and constructed by the Australian Submarine Corporation at Osborne, South Australia, they replaced the Oberon class and at their introduction were among the largest conventionally powered submarines in the world.

Design

The boats displace about 3,400 tonnes submerged, are roughly 77.8 m long, and are crewed by about 58 personnel. Armament consists of six 21-inch bow torpedo tubes capable of firing Mark 48 heavyweight torpedoes and launching UGM-84 Harpoon anti-ship missiles, or carrying mines. Their combat system was originally of mixed Australian–American origin; after early problems, it was replaced by a version of the AN/BYG-1 system used by the United States Navy.

Early problems and remediation

The class became notorious in the late 1990s for technical difficulties publicized by the media and the McIntosh–Prescott report of 1999: excessive noise, propeller and periscope defects, diesel unreliability, and a combat system that did not meet requirements. A remediation program of roughly a billion dollars followed, and by the mid-2000s the class was widely regarded as an effective, quiet platform well suited to long-range operations and intelligence gathering in the region. Crew shortages periodically limited availability.

Future

The Collins class has been the subject of a life-of-type extension program to keep it operational into the 2030s and 2040s, pending delivery of nuclear-powered submarines planned under the AUKUS partnership, which superseded the cancelled Attack-class program with France.
