Sinbad the Sailor (Arabic: سندباد البحري, Sindibādu l-Bahriyy, "Sinbad of the Sea") is a fictional mariner and the hero of a story-cycle of Middle Eastern origin, set in the Abbasid Caliphate. The tales recount the fantastic adventures he encountered on seven voyages throughout the seas east of Africa and south of Asia. The cycle is included in many recensions of One Thousand and One Nights, though it appears to have originated as an independent collection and was incorporated relatively late.

Frame story

In the frame narrative, a poor porter named Sinbad (Hindbad) rests outside a rich merchant's house in Baghdad and laments his lot; the merchant, also named Sinbad, invites him in and over seven evenings recounts the voyages by which he won — and repeatedly lost and regained — his fortune, rewarding the porter each night.

The seven voyages

  • First voyage: Sinbad's ship anchors on a supposed island that is actually a whale, which dives; he survives on a wooden tub and reaches a kingdom whose king befriends him.
  • Second voyage: Marooned in an uninhabited valley, he finds the valley of diamonds, escaping by clinging to meat thrown down for eagles, as in the legend of the roc's valley.
  • Third voyage: Captured by a monstrous giant resembling Polyphemus, Sinbad blinds him with heated spits and escapes on a raft.
  • Fourth voyage: Shipwrecked among cannibal pepper-eaters and later made wealthy in a kingdom where he is obliged to bury himself alive with his dead wife, escaping from the burial cave.
  • Fifth voyage: His crew breaks the egg of the roc, whose parents destroy the ship with boulders; on an island he outwits the Old Man of the Sea, who rides on his shoulders until made drunk with wine.
  • Sixth voyage: Shipwrecked on Serendib (Sri Lanka), he builds a raft down a river running through a cavern and is honored by the king, carrying gifts back to Caliph Harun al-Rashid.
  • Seventh voyage: On his final, reluctant voyage he is captured, sold into service hunting elephants, redeemed by his master, and returns to Baghdad to live at ease.

Origins and influence

The tales draw on real Arab and Persian seafaring lore of the Indian Ocean, Greek romance motifs, and folk tales. Sinbad has become a staple of children's literature and popular culture, inspiring films including The 7th Voyage of Sinbad (1958) with Ray Harryhausen's stop-motion creatures, animated adaptations, and the Japanese series Arabian Nights: Sinbad's Adventures.
