Mugham

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mugham (Azerbaijani: Muğam) is one of the many classical and folk musical traditions of Azerbaijan, and one of the country's principal art forms. It is a highly complex modal art combining classical poetry — often ghazals by poets such as Nizami, Fuzuli, and Khagani — with melodic improvisation. In 2008, UNESCO inscribed Azerbaijani mugham on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, having earlier proclaimed it a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2003.

Structure and performance

A mugham performance is organised in suites built around a single mode (such as Rast, Shur, Segah, Chargah, Bayati-Shiraz, Shushtar and Humayun). Each suite proceeds through contrasting sections: improvised vocal passages over the mode, interspersed with rhythmic songs (tasnif) and instrumental pieces (reng). The classic ensemble, the mugham trio, consists of a singer (khananda) who also plays the daf (frame drum), a tar (long-necked lute) player, and a kamancha (spike fiddle) player. The singer builds intensity gradually, moving through the mode's melodic stations (shobe), and the emotional power of the performance is central to the tradition.

History

Mugham's roots lie in the modal music of the medieval Islamic world and in Azerbaijani folk song and ashig minstrelsy. It flourished in the khanates of the Caucasus and was codified in the early 20th century by composer Uzeyir Hajibeyov, who also fused mugham with Western opera in works such as Leyli and Majnun (1908), the first opera of the Muslim East. Renowned performers include khanandas such as Jabbar Garyagdioglu, Khan Shushinski and Seyid Shushinski, tar player Gurban Pirimov, and in modern times the singer Alim Qasimov, who brought mugham to international audiences. Baku hosts a biennial World of Mugham festival.
