Barbara Smith (born 16 November 1946) is an American feminist author, activist, and scholar, widely regarded as a foundational figure in Black feminism. She was a co-founder of the Combahee River Collective, whose 1977 statement is credited with introducing the concept of identity politics and with articulating an early analysis of interlocking systems of oppression — race, class, gender, and sexuality — later developed under the name intersectionality.

Life and work

Smith was born in Cleveland, Ohio, and educated at Mount Holyoke College. In 1974 she helped found the Combahee River Collective in Boston, a Black feminist lesbian organization named for Harriet Tubman's 1863 raid on the Combahee River. The Collective's statement insisted that the liberation of Black women required dismantling all systems of oppression simultaneously, because those systems are experienced together rather than separately.

In 1980 Smith co-founded Kitchen Table: Women of Color Press, the first United States publisher devoted to writing by women of color, which published landmark works including This Bridge Called My Back and Home Girls: A Black Feminist Anthology (1983), which Smith edited. Her essay "Toward a Black Feminist Criticism" (1977) helped establish Black feminist literary criticism as a field, arguing for the recognition of a distinct Black women's literary tradition.

Later life

Smith later served two terms on the city council of Albany, New York, working on issues of violence prevention and equity. Her writings are collected in The Truth That Never Hurts: Writings on Race, Gender, and Freedom (1998), and her oral history Ain't Gonna Let Nobody Turn Me Around appeared in 2014.
