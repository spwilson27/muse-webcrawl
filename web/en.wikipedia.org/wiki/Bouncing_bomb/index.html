Bouncing bomb

A bouncing bomb is a bomb designed to bounce or skip across the surface of water to avoid torpedo nets and other defences, and to detonate adjacent to a target such as a dam wall. The concept was developed by the British engineer Barnes Wallis during the Second World War, inspired by the practice of skipping stones and by experiments with bouncing spherical and cylindrical bombs.

Upkeep and the Dambusters raid

The best-known bouncing bomb, codenamed Upkeep, was a large cylindrical depth charge given backspin before release from a Lancaster bomber flying at exactly 60 feet (18 m) at 220 mph (355 km/h). The backspin made the bomb skip over torpedo nets, strike the dam wall, and then sink along the wall before a hydrostatic fuze detonated it at depth, where the water tamped the explosion against the masonry. Upkeep was used on the night of 16–17 May 1943 in Operation Chastise by No. 617 Squadron RAF — the "Dambusters" — breaching the Möhne and Eder dams in the Ruhr valley.

Other variants

The smaller Highball was a spherical bouncing bomb intended for use against ships, to be delivered by Mosquito aircraft; it was tested extensively but never used operationally. The concept also influenced later skip-bombing tactics and the large conventional "earthquake bombs" Wallis went on to design, such as Tallboy and Grand Slam.
