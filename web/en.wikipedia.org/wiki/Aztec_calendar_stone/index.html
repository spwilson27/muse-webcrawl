The Aztec calendar stone, also known as the Sun Stone (Spanish: Piedra del Sol), is a massive Mexica (Aztec) basalt sculpture carved in the early 16th century. It is one of the most famous works of Aztec art and an iconic symbol of Mexico. Despite its popular name, the stone is not a functioning calendar; it is generally interpreted as a cuauhxicalli-type sacrificial monument or a temalácatl (gladiatorial combat stone) whose carved surface presents a cosmological diagram centred on the sun god Tonatiuh and the myth of the Five Suns.

History

The stone was carved during the reign of Moctezuma II, probably between 1502 and 1521, and may have been intended for the Templo Mayor precinct of Tenochtitlan. After the Spanish conquest it was buried beneath what became Mexico City's main square (the Zócalo). It was rediscovered on 17 December 1790 during repaving works, near the also-recently unearthed statue of Coatlicue. Displayed for a century against the wall of the Metropolitan Cathedral, it was transferred to the Museo Nacional in 1885 and, in 1964, to the new National Museum of Anthropology, where it forms the centrepiece of the Mexica hall.

Description

The disc, 3.6 metres in diameter and about 98 cm thick, weighs roughly 24.6 tonnes. At its centre is the face of Tonatiuh, the sun god, tongue extended as a sacrificial knife, flanked by clawed hands grasping human hearts. Around the centre are four squares representing the four previous cosmic eras or "Suns" (Jaguar, Wind, Rain of Fire, and Water), each destroyed by catastrophe; the central era is the current Fifth Sun, 4 Movement (Nahui Ollin), whose sign encloses the god's face. Two concentric rings follow: the inner ring contains the twenty day signs of the 260-day ritual calendar (tonalpohualli), and the outer ring depicts two great fire serpents (xiuhcoatl) whose tails meet at the bottom and whose faces, at the base, frame the date 13 Reed — associated in tradition with the birth of the Fifth Sun.

Interpretation

Early antiquarians, including Antonio de León y Gama, who published the first study in 1792, treated the stone as an astronomical calendar. Modern scholarship reads it primarily as a political-cosmological monument proclaiming Mexica dominion and the sacrificial economy that sustained the sun. Its imagery appears on Mexican coinage, including the 10-peso coin, and it remains a potent national symbol.
