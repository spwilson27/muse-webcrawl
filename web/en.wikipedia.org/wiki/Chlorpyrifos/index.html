Chlorpyrifos (CPS) is an organophosphate pesticide, acaricide and miticide used primarily to control foliage and soil-borne insect pests on a variety of food and feed crops. Introduced in 1965 by Dow Chemical Company, it was for decades among the most widely used insecticides in agriculture and, formerly, in homes and gardens. It acts on the nervous systems of insects by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase.

Uses

Chlorpyrifos has been applied to corn, soybeans, fruit trees, nut trees, Brussels sprouts, cranberries, broccoli and other crops, as well as on golf courses and as a mosquito adulticide. It was sold for agriculture under trade names including Lorsban and Dursban; residential uses in the United States were phased out in 2000–2001.

Toxicity and health effects

As an organophosphate, chlorpyrifos inhibits acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate and overstimulate nerves. Acute exposure can cause cholinergic symptoms ranging from headache and nausea to convulsions and respiratory failure. Epidemiological studies have associated prenatal and early-childhood exposure with developmental effects, which became central to regulatory reviews.

Regulation

The European Union did not renew approval of chlorpyrifos in 2020. In the United States, after years of contested rulemaking, the Environmental Protection Agency revoked all food tolerances in 2021 (effective 2022); a 2023 federal court decision vacated that rule in part and returned the matter to the agency, which subsequently proposed allowing a reduced set of uses. Many states imposed their own bans or restrictions.

Environmental effects

Chlorpyrifos is highly toxic to fish, aquatic invertebrates, bees and birds. It binds to soil and has moderate persistence; its breakdown product TCP (3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol) is more persistent and mobile.
