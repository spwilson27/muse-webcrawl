Little People

Little People is a toy brand for small children, best known for the line of chunky miniature human figures and playsets produced by Fisher-Price since 1959. This article is about the toy line. The term "little people" is also used in folklore studies for small human-like beings (fairies, dwarfs and similar), and is a self-designation of some people with dwarfism; these senses are treated in their own articles.

History of the toy line

The first Little People figures appeared in Fisher-Price's Safety School Bus in 1959: simple peg-shaped figures with cylindrical bodies and round heads, made to fit into holes in vehicles and buildings. Through the 1960s the line expanded with the Play Family sets — houses, farms, garages, airports, a village and a castle — and the figures became known generically as "Play Family people".

The original peg design, barely 5 cm tall, was later judged a choking hazard for toddlers. In 1991 Fisher-Price redesigned the figures with much chunkier proportions — rounder, wider bodies that could not be swallowed — and these remain the basis of the modern line. Contemporary Little People sets include licensed tie-ins and diverse character figures, and the brand has extended into animated videos and short web features.

Cultural impact

The original wooden and early plastic Little People are collectible, and the figures are frequently used by toy photographers, diarists and stop-motion animators. The line's longevity — over six decades — makes it one of Fisher-Price's flagship brands, alongside the Chatter Telephone and the See 'n Say.

Other uses of the term

  • Folklore — "little people" is a generic term for the small supernatural peoples of world folklore: the fairies of Britain and Ireland, the Icelandic huldufólk, the Hawaiian menehune, the Filipino duwende, Native American little people traditions, and others.
  • People with dwarfism — "little people" (singular "little person") is a term preferred by some individuals and by the organisation Little People of America for people of short stature due to medical conditions such as achondroplasia.
  • Archaeology/anthropology — media reporting on the small-bodied hominin Homo floresiensis sometimes borrows the term.
