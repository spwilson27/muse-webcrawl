Hafnium is a chemical element with the symbol Hf and atomic number 72. A lustrous, silvery grey, tetravalent transition metal, hafnium chemically resembles zirconium and is found in many zirconium minerals. Its existence was predicted by Dmitri Mendeleev in 1869, and it was identified in 1923 by Dirk Coster and George de Hevesy in Copenhagen — from whose Latin name, Hafnia, the element takes its name — making it the last stable element to be discovered.

Properties

Hafnium is a ductile metal with excellent corrosion resistance, protected by a tenacious oxide film. Because of the lanthanide contraction its atomic radius is nearly identical to zirconium's, and the two elements are among the hardest pairs in chemistry to separate; commercial zirconium always contains 1–3 per cent hafnium. Unlike zirconium, hafnium is an extremely strong absorber of thermal neutrons, with a capture cross-section some 600 times greater — the decisive difference that drives most of its applications and makes its removal essential for nuclear-grade zirconium.

Applications

Hafnium is used in control rods for nuclear reactors, especially in naval reactors, because it absorbs neutrons efficiently and resists corrosion in hot water. Hafnium alloys with niobium, tantalum and tungsten withstand very high temperatures, and hafnium-containing superalloys are used in jet engines and rocket nozzles; hafnium carbide is among the most refractory binary compounds known. In microelectronics, hafnium dioxide (HfO2) became the standard high-κ gate dielectric in transistors from Intel's 45 nm generation (2007) onward. Naturally occurring 174Hf is very slightly radioactive with an immense half-life, and the radioactive isomer 178m2Hf is notable for storing unusually large amounts of energy per gram.

Occurrence

Hafnium makes up about 5.8 ppm of the Earth's crust by weight. It never occurs free in nature and is obtained as a by-product of refining zircon (zirconium silicate) sands mined mainly in Australia and South Africa; annual world production of the metal is only on the order of tens of tonnes.
