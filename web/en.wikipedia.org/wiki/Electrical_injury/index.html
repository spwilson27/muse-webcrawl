Electrical injury

Electrical injury is physiological damage caused by electric current passing through the body. The injury depends on the density of the current, tissue resistance and the duration of contact. The passage of electric current can cause superficial, partial-thickness and full-thickness thermal burns, the destruction of nerves and muscles due to electroporation, and mechanical trauma from falls or involuntary contractions.

Types

Electrical injuries are traditionally classified as low-voltage (less than 1,000 volts) and high-voltage (1,000 volts or more) injuries, the latter tending to cause deep tissue destruction, compartment syndrome, and rhabdomyolysis. Arc flash and flame burns can occur without current passing through the body. Lightning strikes are a special category involving extremely high voltage over a very short duration.

Mechanisms of harm

Current passing through the body converts electrical energy to heat as it meets resistance, burning tissue along its path, especially in high-resistance tissues such as bone. Current through the heart can induce ventricular fibrillation — the most common cause of death from electric shock at household voltages — while current through the chest can arrest breathing by tetanic contraction of the respiratory muscles or damage to the brainstem.

Epidemiology and prevention

Electrical injuries are a significant occupational hazard for utility workers and electricians, and a household danger to children. Prevention relies on insulation, grounding, residual-current devices (ground-fault circuit interrupters), lockout-tagout procedures, and safety standards from bodies such as OSHA and the IEC.
