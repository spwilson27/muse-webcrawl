François Couperin

François Couperin (10 November 1668 – 11 September 1733) was a French Baroque composer, organist and harpsichordist. Known as Couperin le Grand ("Couperin the Great") to distinguish him from other members of his musical family, he is regarded as one of the founders of the French harpsichord school and a master of the character piece.

Born: 10 November 1668, Paris, France
Died: 11 September 1733, Paris, France
Era: Baroque
Known for: Pièces de clavecin; L'art de toucher le clavecin

Life

Couperin was born in Paris into a family that would hold the organ loft of the church of Saint-Gervais for nearly two centuries. Orphaned at eleven, he succeeded his father Charles as organist of Saint-Gervais at eighteen. In 1693 Louis XIV appointed him organist of the Royal Chapel, and he later taught music to royal children at Versailles and composed chamber music for the king's Sunday concerts. He was made a chevalier of the Order of Latran in 1717.

Music

His fame rests on the four books of Pièces de clavecin (1713–1730) — over 230 pieces arranged in suites called ordres — and his treatise L'art de toucher le clavecin (1716), a landmark of performance practice with detailed instructions on fingering and ornamentation. Many pieces bear evocative titles portraying people, moods and scenes: Les barricades mystérieuses, Le rossignol en amour and Les petits moulins à vent remain favourites. Couperin sought to unite French and Italian styles, a project announced in chamber works such as Les goûts réunis ("The Styles Reunited") and the trio sonatas Le Parnasse, ou L'apothéose de Corelli and L'apothéose de Lully. He also composed motets and other sacred music for the Royal Chapel.

Legacy

J. S. Bach admired and copied his works; Brahms championed him in the 19th century, and Debussy and Ravel — who dedicated a movement of Le tombeau de Couperin to his memory — drew on his clarity and refinement.
