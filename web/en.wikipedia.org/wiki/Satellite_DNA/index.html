Satellite DNA

Satellite DNA consists of very large arrays of tandemly repeating, non-coding DNA. Satellite DNA is the main component of functional centromeres and forms the main structural constituent of heterochromatin.

The name "satellite DNA" refers to the phenomenon that repetitions of a short DNA sequence tend to produce a different frequency of the bases adenine, cytosine, guanine and thymine, and thus have a different density from bulk DNA such that they form a second or "satellite" band when genomic DNA is separated in a density gradient.

Types of satellite DNA

Satellite DNA, together with minisatellite and microsatellite DNA, constitute the tandem repeats. The major satellite DNA families in humans include:

  • α-satellite (alphoid DNA) — found in all chromosomes, made of 171 bp repeat units, the main constituent of centromeres.
  • β-satellite — 68 bp repeat units found mainly in the centromeres of chromosomes 1, 9, 13, 14, 15, 21, 22 and Y.
  • Satellite 1, 2 and 3 — classical satellites with short repeat units, abundant in pericentromeric heterochromatin.

Length and structure

A repeated pattern can be between 1 base pair long (a mononucleotide repeat) and several thousand base pairs long, and the total size of a satellite DNA block can be several megabases without interruption. Most satellite DNA is localized to the telomeric or the centromeric region of the chromosome. The nucleotide sequence of the repeats is fairly well conserved across species, although variation in repeat length is common.

Origin and function

Satellite DNA is thought to have originated by slipped mispairing during DNA replication, or by unequal crossing-over and rolling-circle amplification of extrachromosomal circles. Although satellite DNA does not code for proteins, it is not inert: centromeric alpha satellite is essential for chromosome segregation, and satellite sequences contribute to the organisation of heterochromatin, chromosome pairing, and regulation of nearby genes. Altered amounts or methylation of satellite DNA have been reported in some cancers and in the immunodeficiency syndrome ICF.
