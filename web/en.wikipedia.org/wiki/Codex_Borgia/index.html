Codex Borgia

Codex Borgia
Created: Late Postclassic, before 1519
Place of origin: Puebla–Tlaxcala region, Mexico
Material: Deerskin, gesso
Pages: 39 leaves, 76 painted pages
Location: Vatican Library, Rome

The Codex Borgia (also known as the Codex Yoalli Ehēcatl) is a pre-Columbian Mesoamerican pictorial manuscript, written before the Spanish conquest of Mexico. It is one of the most famous members of the group of manuscripts known collectively as the Borgia Group, which also includes the Codex Cospi, the Codex Fejérváry-Mayer, the Codex Laud, and the Codex Vaticanus B.

Description

The codex is made of animal skins folded into 39 sheets; each sheet measures roughly 27 by 26.5 cm, and the folded manuscript extends to almost 11 metres in length. Both sides of the leaves are painted, giving 76 pages in total, read from right to left. The pages are painted in the polychrome style characteristic of the Puebla–Tlaxcala region, with figures outlined in black and filled with brilliant flat colour.

Contents

The contents of the Codex Borgia are primarily religious and divinatory. Its longest and most famous section, pages 29 to 46, depicts a series of rituals and ceremonies whose precise meaning remains debated among scholars. Other sections include almanacs for the 260-day ritual calendar (the tonalpohualli), prognostications for travellers, merchants, and farmers, the ceremonies of the Venus cycle, and depictions of the gods of the cardinal directions and the world trees.

The codex also contains material concerning the cult of Quetzalcoatl in his aspect as Ehēcatl, the wind god, and elaborate imagery of birth, marriage, and the ballgame.

History

The early history of the manuscript is obscure. It appears to have reached Europe in the sixteenth century, and by the eighteenth century it was in the collection of Cardinal Stefano Borgia, from whom it takes its name. After Borgia's death in 1804, the codex passed to the Vatican Library, where it remains. The Mexican scholar Alfonso Caso proposed that its original Nahuatl name may have been Yoalli Ehēcatl, "Night and Wind".

Scholarship

Eduard Seler published the first full commentary on the codex at the turn of the twentieth century. Modern facsimile editions, including that of Gisele Díaz and Alan Rodgers with a commentary by Bruce Byland, have made the manuscript a cornerstone of the study of Mesoamerican religion and calendrics.
