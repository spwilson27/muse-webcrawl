Dinghy

A dinghy is a type of small boat, often carried or towed by a larger vessel for use as a tender. Utility dinghies are usually rowboats or have an outboard motor. Some are rigged for sailing but they differ from sailing dinghies, which are designed first and foremost for sailing. A dinghy's main use is for transfers from larger boats, especially when the larger boat cannot dock in a suitably sized port or marina.

Etymology

The term is a loanword from the Bengali ḍiṅgi, Urdu ḍīngī, and Hindi ḍeṅgī, denoting a small boat used on Indian rivers and coasts; it entered English through the Royal Navy in the early nineteenth century.

Types

  • Rigid dinghies — traditionally wood, now usually fibreglass or aluminium; rowed, motored, or occasionally sailed.
  • Inflatable dinghies — made of rubberised or PVC fabric with inflatable tubes; popular as yacht tenders because they are stable and easily stowed.
  • Rigid inflatable boats (RIBs) — a rigid hull with inflatable buoyancy tubes.
  • Sailing dinghies — lightweight centreboard boats such as the Optimist, Laser (ILCA), and Mirror, used for training and racing including Olympic classes.

Uses

As ship's tenders, dinghies ferry crew and supplies between an anchored vessel and shore, and may double as lifeboats in emergencies. Small dinghies are also used for fishing, harbour transport, and as starter boats in sailing schools — the Optimist alone is sailed by children in more than 120 countries.

Safety

Because of their small size and low freeboard, dinghies are easily swamped or capsized; regulations and good practice call for buoyancy, lifejackets, and care not to overload.
