Macedonia (Greek: Μακεδονία, Makedonía), also called Macedon, was an ancient kingdom on the periphery of Archaic and Classical Greece, later the dominant state of Hellenistic Greece. It emerged in the 7th century BC under the Argead dynasty and rose to supremacy under Philip II (359–336 BC) and his son Alexander the Great, whose conquests spread Greek culture across the Near East.

Early kingdom

The Macedonians inhabited the fertile plains of the Haliacmon and Axios (Vardar) rivers, north of Mount Olympus. Long regarded by southern Greeks as semi-barbarian rustics, the kingdom was reorganised during the 5th and 4th centuries BC; kings such as Archelaus built roads, founded Pella as the capital, and patronised Greek culture — Euripides spent his last years at the Macedonian court.

Philip II and Alexander

Philip II transformed Macedonia's army, creating the sarissa-armed phalanx and combined-arms tactics that defeated the Greek city-states at Chaeronea (338 BC), uniting Greece in the League of Corinth. Assassinated in 336 BC, he was succeeded by his twenty-year-old son Alexander, who in twelve years destroyed the Persian Empire, conquering Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia, and reaching India before dying at Babylon in 323 BC.

Hellenistic period

Alexander's empire broke apart in the Wars of the Diadochi. Macedonia itself eventually fell to the Antigonid dynasty, which ruled Greece until defeat by Rome at the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC ended the monarchy; in 148 BC Macedonia became a Roman province. The conquests of Alexander nonetheless inaugurated the Hellenistic age, during which Greek language and culture spread from the Mediterranean to Central Asia.

Legacy

Macedon's legacy includes the Koine Greek language, the spread of Greek cities across the Near East, and the Vergina royal tombs, rediscovered in 1977, whose treasures — including the tomb widely attributed to Philip II — are among the greatest archaeological finds of the 20th century.
