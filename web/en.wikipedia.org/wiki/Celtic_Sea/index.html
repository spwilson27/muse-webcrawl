Celtic Sea

The Celtic Sea (Irish: An Mhuir Cheilteach; Welsh: Y Môr Celtaidd; Cornish: An Mor Keltek; Breton: Ar Mor Keltiek; French: La mer Celtique) is the area of the Atlantic Ocean off the southern coast of Ireland bounded to the east by Saint George's Channel, the Bristol Channel, the English Channel, and the Bay of Biscay, as well as adjacent portions of Wales, Cornwall, Devon, and Brittany.

Geography

The sea's northern limit is a line from St David's Head in Wales to Carnsore Point in Ireland; its southern limit is defined by the International Hydrographic Organization by a line from Île Vierge, Brittany, to Hartland Point in Devon and on to St Agnes in the Isles of Scilly. The Celtic Sea overlies the Celtic Shelf, part of the continental shelf of Europe, and is mostly shallower than 100–200 m, though the Celtic Deep and other channels cut through it. The seabed features sand ridges shaped by tidal currents.

Name

The name "Celtic Sea" was introduced in 1921 by the fisheries researcher E. W. L. Holt, reflecting the shared Celtic heritage of the surrounding lands. It came into common usage among chart makers and the oil and fishing industries in the following decades.

Ecology and industry

The Celtic Sea supports important fisheries for mackerel, herring, hake, and Nephrops, and hosts grey seals and cetaceans. It has seen oil and gas exploration, offshore wind development (including floating wind projects off Cornwall and Wales), and busy shipping routes serving the ports of Cork, Milford Haven, and the English Channel approaches. Proposals for a Celtic Interconnector power cable link Ireland with France across the sea.
