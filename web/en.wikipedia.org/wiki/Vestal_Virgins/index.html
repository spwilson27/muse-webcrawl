Vestal Virgins

Statues of Vestals in the House of the Vestal Virgins, Roman Forum.

In ancient Rome, the Vestal Virgins (Latin: Vestālēs) were priestesses of Vesta, goddess of the hearth. The College of the Vestals was regarded as fundamental to the continuance and security of Rome. The Vestals tended the sacred fire in the Temple of Vesta that was not allowed to go out, prepared ritual substances such as mola salsa, and guarded sacred objects including the Palladium.

Selection and service

Vestals were selected as girls between the ages of six and ten from aristocratic families, initially by the king and later by the pontifex maximus, and served for thirty years: ten years learning the rites, ten performing them, and ten teaching novices. During their service they took a vow of chastity; violation of the vow (incestum) was punishable by being buried alive, a penalty designed to avoid the shedding of a Vestal's blood. If the sacred fire went out, the responsible Vestal could be scourged.

Privileges

In Roman society the Vestals enjoyed exceptional independence and prestige for women: they could own property, make a will, vote in effect through influence if not formally, and were escorted by lictors. Their persons were inviolable, and condemned criminals who met a Vestal on the way to execution were pardoned. After thirty years of service a Vestal could retire and marry, though few did.

History

The order, traditionally founded by Numa Pompilius, lasted for well over a thousand years, until the Christian emperor Theodosius I closed the Temple of Vesta and disbanded the college in 394 CE. The last chief Vestal, Coelia Concordia, is recorded stepping down as the cult was suppressed.
