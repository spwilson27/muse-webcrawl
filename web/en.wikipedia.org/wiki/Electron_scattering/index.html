Electron scattering

Electron scattering
Field: particle and condensed-matter physics
Processes: elastic, inelastic, deep inelastic
Uses: probing nuclei, quarks, materials

Electron scattering is the deflection of electrons by electromagnetic interactions with atoms, nuclei, or other particles. Because electrons are point-like leptons that interact cleanly through the electromagnetic force, scattering experiments with electron beams have been a central tool for probing the structure of matter.

Types

In elastic scattering the electron loses no energy and the target recoils intact; the angular distribution reveals the target's charge distribution through its form factor. In inelastic scattering the target is excited or broken up. Deep inelastic scattering, at high energy and momentum transfer, resolves the internal constituents of nucleons.

Historical importance

Experiments at SLAC in the late 1960s scattered high-energy electrons from protons and found evidence of point-like constituents inside — the quarks — a discovery that helped establish quantum chromodynamics. Earlier, Robert Hofstadter's electron-scattering measurements mapped the charge distributions of nuclei, earning a Nobel Prize.

In materials science

Low-energy electron scattering underlies techniques such as electron diffraction, scanning and transmission electron microscopy, and electron energy loss spectroscopy, used to study surfaces and nanostructures.
