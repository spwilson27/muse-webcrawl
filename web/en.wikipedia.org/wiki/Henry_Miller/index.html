Henry Miller, whose once-banned novels reshaped American literary freedom.

Henry Valentine Miller (1891–1980) was an American novelist and essayist known for developing a new kind of semi-autobiographical novel blending fiction, memoir, social criticism, and mysticism in a frank, exuberant first-person voice. His most famous works, Tropic of Cancer (1934) and Tropic of Capricorn (1939), were banned for obscenity in the United States for decades, and their eventual publication was a landmark in American free-expression law.

Life

Miller grew up in Brooklyn, the son of a tailor. After restless years in New York — including a stint as personnel manager at Western Union, dramatised in Tropic of Capricorn — he moved to Paris in 1930, living in poverty among the expatriate literary scene and forming a famous creative and personal bond with Anaïs Nin. Tropic of Cancer was published there by Obelisk Press in 1934. He spent 1939 in Greece, producing the travel book The Colossus of Maroussi, and settled in 1944 at Big Sur, California, which became a bohemian magnet around him.

The bans and their fall

Grove Press's 1961 US publication of Tropic of Cancer triggered more than 60 obscenity trials; in 1964, in the wake of Grove Press, Inc. v. Gerstein, the Supreme Court effectively ended the ban. Miller's trilogy The Rosy Crucifixion — Sexus, Plexus, and Nexus — and books like Black Spring and The Air-Conditioned Nightmare secured his reputation as a liberating influence on the Beat Generation and later writers, though feminist critics have contested aspects of his treatment of women.
