Res judicata

Res judicata
Latin"a matter judged"
BranchCivil and criminal procedure
ComponentsClaim preclusion, issue preclusion (collateral estoppel)
OriginRoman law

Res judicata or res iudicata, also known as claim preclusion, is the Latin term for "a matter decided" and refers to either of two concepts in common law and civil law legal systems: a case in which there has been a final judgment that is no longer subject to appeal, and the legal doctrine meant to bar (or preclude) relitigation of claims or issues that were already adjudicated.

Purpose

The doctrine serves the public interest in finality (interest rei publicae ut sit finis litium — "it is in the public interest that there be an end to litigation"), protects defendants from harassment by repeated suits, and preserves judicial economy and the authority of judgments. Without it, losing parties could relitigate indefinitely, and inconsistent judgments could result.

Claim and issue preclusion

In United States law, res judicata is commonly divided into:

  • Claim preclusion (true res judicata): a final judgment on the merits bars the same parties from relitigating the same claim or cause of action, including claims that could have been raised in the first action — even if based on new evidence or new legal theories.
  • Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel): an issue of fact or law that was actually litigated and necessarily decided in a prior action cannot be relitigated between the same parties (and in some circumstances by or against non-parties) in a later action on a different claim.

Requirements

Generally, claim preclusion requires: (1) a final judgment on the merits in the prior action; (2) the same claim or cause of action (often tested by whether it arises from the same transaction or series of transactions); and (3) the same parties, or those in privity with them. Judgments by consent (settlements entered as judgments) usually count as on the merits; dismissals for lack of jurisdiction or improper venue usually do not.

Exceptions

Courts recognise limited exceptions: fraud on the court, newly discovered evidence in some configurations, changes in controlling law for ongoing equitable decrees, and jurisdictional defects may allow attack on a judgment through appeal, motions for relief from judgment (e.g. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 60(b)), or independent actions. In criminal law, the cognate protection is the prohibition on double jeopardy.

Civil law systems

Civil law jurisdictions recognise a comparable doctrine — French autorité de la chose jugée, German materielle Rechtskraft — though often construed more narrowly, generally attaching only to what was actually requested and decided (dispositif) between the same parties with the same object and cause. The concept descends from Roman law, where the exceptio rei iudicatae barred a second action on an adjudicated matter.
