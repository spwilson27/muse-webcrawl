Statute of frauds

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A statute of frauds is a statute requiring certain categories of contracts to be in writing (and, typically, signed) to be legally enforceable. The term takes its name from the English Statute of Frauds of 1677, "An Act for Prevention of Frauds and Perjuries", and general principles descended from it survive in the law of the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia and other common-law jurisdictions.

Application in the United States

Every U.S. state has a statute of frauds. Common categories requiring a writing include contracts for the sale of real property, agreements that cannot be performed within one year, promises to pay another's debt (suretyship), promises by an executor, agreements in consideration of marriage, and—under UCC §2-201—sales of goods priced at $500 or more.

Requirements of the writing

The writing need not be the contract itself: a memorandum noting the essential terms and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought usually suffices. Modern statutes and the UCC's Statute of Frauds provisions accommodate electronic records and signatures.

Effect of non-compliance

A contract within the statute that lacks a sufficient writing is generally unenforceable rather than void, and the defence must be raised by the party to be charged. Exceptions for part performance, promissory estoppel and admission may bar a party from invoking the statute.
