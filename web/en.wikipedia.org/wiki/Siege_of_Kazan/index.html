The Siege of Kazan in 1552 was the final battle of the Russo-Kazan Wars, leading to the fall of the Khanate of Kazan and its annexation by the Tsardom of Russia. The Russian army of Tsar Ivan IV ("the Terrible"), numbering by contemporary accounts as many as 150,000 men with a large artillery train, besieged the Tatar capital for over a month before storming it on 2 October 1552 (Old Style). The city's fall opened the middle Volga to Russian expansion and is traditionally regarded as a turning point in the history of Eurasia, ending centuries of Tatar political dominance in the region.

Background

The Khanate of Kazan, founded in the 1430s as a successor state of the Golden Horde, controlled the Volga trade route and frequently raided Russian territory for slaves and plunder. Ivan IV launched unsuccessful campaigns against Kazan in 1547–48 and 1549–50. In 1551 the Russians built the fortress of Sviyazhsk on an island upstream of Kazan in a matter of weeks, providing a forward base and cutting the khanate off from its eastern allies. In June 1552 Ivan's great army marched east, pausing to defeat a Crimean Tatar diversion at Tula before arriving before Kazan on 23 August.

The siege

The defenders, led by Khan Yadegar Mokhammad and the imam Kul Sharif, rejected Ivan's demand for surrender. The Russians encircled the city, built siege works and movable wooden towers, and opened fire with some 150 cannon. Engineers, including the specialist Ivan Vyrodkov and foreign experts, drove mines beneath the walls; a huge explosion on 30 September breached the defences near the Nogai gate. Tatar defenders counter-mined and sortied repeatedly, and bitter fighting continued amid rain that turned the approaches to mud.

The final assault

On 2 October, after two further mine explosions destroyed sections of wall and the city's water supply, Russian assault columns stormed the breaches. The fighting in the streets was ferocious; Kul Sharif and his students died defending a madrasa, and most of the male defenders were killed. Khan Yadegar was captured alive and later baptised. Ivan entered the ruined city on 3 October. A general massacre of the surviving male population followed, and Russian colonization of the ханate's lands began.

Aftermath

The khanate was formally annexed, though resistance in the countryside—the so-called Kazan Wars—continued until 1556. The fall of Kazan was followed in 1556 by the conquest of Astrakhan, giving Russia control of the entire Volga. To commemorate the victory Ivan ordered the construction of the Cathedral of the Intercession, better known as Saint Basil's Cathedral, on Red Square in Moscow. The conquest is still commemorated—and mourned—in Tatar historical memory, where 1552 marks the loss of independence.
