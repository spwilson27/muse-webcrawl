Chateaubriand steak

Chateaubriand steak, sliced to show a rare centre

Chateaubriand steak (also called chateaubriand) is a thick cut of beef taken from the centre of the tenderloin, traditionally roasted or grilled and served with a rich sauce. The dish is named after the French writer and diplomat François-René de Chateaubriand (1768–1848), and is generally credited to his personal chef, Montmireil, who is said to have created it while Chateaubriand served as ambassador to England in the 1820s.

Because a single tenderloin yields only a short length of suitably thick meat, the chateaubriand is among the most prized and expensive steak cuts. It is usually large enough to serve two people and is carved at the table.

Cut and preparation

The chateaubriand is cut from the thick central portion of the whole beef tenderloin, the same muscle that provides filet mignon steaks at its narrower end. Cuts are typically at least 5 cm (2 in) thick and may be considerably thicker. The meat is trimmed of silverskin and surface fat, seasoned, and seared, then finished by grilling, broiling or roasting to rare or medium-rare so that the centre remains tender.

Historically the steak was sometimes cooked between two thinner steaks, which were discarded, so that the chateaubriand itself was heated without being charred on the surface. Modern preparations instead rely on high-heat searing followed by gentle finishing, often in a sauce such as béarnaise or the classic chateaubriand sauce: a reduction of white wine, shallots, tarragon and demi-glace finished with butter.

Service

Chateaubriand is traditionally presented whole on a platter, rested, and sliced diagonally at the table. Classical accompaniments include béarnaise sauce, château potatoes (small potatoes browned in butter), and watercress. The centre slices, pink and exceptionally tender, are the choicest part of the serving.

Etymology

The dish takes its name from Vicomte François-René de Chateaubriand, author of Atala and Mémoires d'Outre-Tombe. By the later nineteenth century the name had become generic for the preparation, and "chateaubriand" came to denote the cut and method rather than the man. The name is also applied, by extension, to the sauce served with the steak and, occasionally, to other thick centre-cut portions prepared in a similar fashion.
