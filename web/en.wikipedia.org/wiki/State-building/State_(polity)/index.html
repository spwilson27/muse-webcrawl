State (polity)

A state is a polity under a system of governance with a monopoly on the legitimate use of force within a defined territory. Commonly understood, a state is a political organization with a centralized government that maintains institutions such as armed forces, civil services, courts, and taxation. The creation and consolidation of such institutions in a territory is the process known as state-building.

Definitions

The sociologist Max Weber famously defined the state as a human community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory. Under international law, the Montevideo Convention (1933) lists four criteria of statehood: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Political science distinguishes the state — the enduring apparatus of rule — from the government of the day and from the nation, a community of identity.

Forms of state

States may be unitary or federal, republics or monarchies, democratic or authoritarian. Sovereign states are the principal actors of the international system; constituent states (such as those of the United States or Germany) exercise delegated authority within a federation. Failed states are those whose central government has lost effective control of its territory.

Origins and state-building

Theories of state formation emphasize warfare and taxation ("war made the state, and the state made war," in Charles Tilly's phrase), agricultural surplus, and social contract traditions from Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau. In modern development practice, state-building refers to efforts — often after conflict — to build functioning institutions, a legitimate political order, and the capacity to deliver security and public services; scholars stress that durable states grow from internal political settlements rather than external templates alone.
