Heat stress

Heat stress is the physiological strain placed on the body when it cannot dissipate heat fast enough to maintain a safe core temperature. It occurs when high air temperature, humidity, radiant heat, heavy physical work and insulating clothing combine to overwhelm the body's cooling mechanisms — chiefly sweating and increased skin blood flow. Heat stress is a major occupational and public-health hazard, and the precursor to the spectrum of heat-related illnesses.

Heat-related illnesses

Conditions caused by heat stress range from mild to life-threatening:

  • Heat rash and heat cramps — skin irritation and painful muscle cramps from sweat and electrolyte loss.
  • Heat syncope — fainting from blood pooling and dehydration.
  • Heat exhaustion — heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache and cool, clammy skin; body temperature normal or moderately raised.
  • Heat stroke — a medical emergency in which core temperature exceeds about 40 °C (104 °F) and the central nervous system fails, causing confusion, seizures or coma; the skin may be hot and dry. Untreated heat stroke is frequently fatal, and rapid cooling is the critical intervention.

Measurement

Occupational hygienists assess environmental heat stress with the Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) index, which combines natural wet-bulb, black-globe and dry-bulb temperatures to account for humidity, sun and radiant heat. Standards from bodies such as NIOSH, OSHA and ACGIH specify work–rest cycles, hydration schedules and acclimatisation periods for given WBGT values and workloads. The heat index ("feels-like" temperature) serves a similar purpose for the general public.

Risk factors and vulnerable groups

Risk is higher for outdoor workers in agriculture, construction and firefighting, soldiers, athletes, the elderly, young children, people with cardiovascular disease, and those taking certain medications. Acclimatisation over 7–14 days substantially increases tolerance. Urban heat islands and climate change are increasing the frequency, intensity and duration of dangerous heat events worldwide; studies attribute growing numbers of excess deaths during heatwaves to heat stress.

Prevention

Controls follow the hierarchy used in occupational safety: engineering controls (shade, ventilation, insulation of hot surfaces), administrative controls (scheduling work for cooler hours, work–rest regimens, buddy systems, training), adequate water and electrolytes, gradual acclimatisation, and appropriate clothing or cooling garments. In agriculture, heat stress also reduces livestock productivity and crop yields, making it a significant economic and food-security issue.
