Expulsion from the United States Congress

Expulsion is the most serious disciplinary sanction available to the United States Congress against one of its members. Under Article I, Section 5 of the Constitution, each House may, with the concurrence of two-thirds of its members, expel a member.

History

The Senate has expelled 15 members, most famously William Blount in 1797 and fourteen senators in 1861–1862 for supporting the Confederacy. The House has expelled only six members: three in 1861 for Confederate support, Michael Myers in 1980 after his Abscam bribery conviction, James Traficant in 2002 after a criminal conviction, and George Santos in 2023 following an ethics investigation, the first expulsion without a prior conviction or Confederate allegiance.

Procedure

Expulsion proceedings typically follow an investigation by the ethics committee of the respective chamber and require a two-thirds floor vote. Expulsion is distinct from censure and reprimand, which require only a majority and do not remove the member.

Other removals

Members may also leave office through resignation, death, defeat, or—in disputes over elections—exclusion by majority vote, a power exercised sparingly since the Supreme Court's decision in Powell v. McCormack (1969).
