Vivisection

Vivisection (from Latin vivus 'alive' and sectio 'cutting') is surgery conducted for experimental purposes on a living organism, typically animals with a central nervous system, to view living internal structure. The word is more broadly used as a pejorative catch-all term by animal rights advocates for any experimentation on live animals.

Vivisection has been practised since antiquity and remains one of the most contested practices in biomedical research.

History

Experiments on living animals date to ancient Greece and Rome. Galen of Pergamon (2nd century CE) performed public vivisections on pigs and goats that founded much of Western physiology. In the 17th century, William Harvey used vivisection to demonstrate the circulation of the blood. Claude Bernard's Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine (1865) defended the practice as essential to physiology, while the Brown Dog affair of 1903–1910 in London galvanised the British anti-vivisection movement.

Regulation

Modern animal research is governed in many countries by the principles of the Three Rs — replacement, reduction and refinement — first articulated by W. M. S. Russell and R. L. Burch in 1959. The United Kingdom's Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 and the United States Animal Welfare Act are among the principal regulatory frameworks.

Opposition

Opposition to vivisection was a founding concern of organisations such as the RSPCA (1824) and the National Anti-Vivisection Society (1875). Critics argue the practice is both cruel and scientifically unreliable; defenders maintain it remains indispensable for understanding disease and testing treatments where no alternative exists.
