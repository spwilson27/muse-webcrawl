The Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria is an Oriental Orthodox Christian church based in Egypt, with adherents throughout the world. According to tradition, it was founded by Saint Mark the Evangelist in Alexandria in the first century, around 42 AD. It is headed by the Pope of Alexandria and Patriarch of All Africa on the Holy See of St. Mark — since 2012, Pope Tawadros II, the 118th pope in the church's reckoning.

The church separated from the wider imperial church following the Council of Chalcedon in 451, rejecting that council's two-natures Christological definition in favour of the miaphysite position associated with Cyril of Alexandria. It forms, with the Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean and Malankara churches, the Oriental Orthodox communion.

History

Alexandria was one of the great centres of early Christianity, home to the Catechetical School and to theologians such as Clement, Origen, Athanasius (who defended Nicene teaching against Arianism) and Cyril. After Chalcedon, the majority of Egyptian Christians followed their own patriarch, and the church endured periods of persecution under Byzantine, and later varying conditions under Arab and Ottoman rule. The Copts — the word derives from the Greek Aigyptios via Arabic Qibṭ — preserved their language, written in a script adapted from Greek with Demotic characters, long after Arabic became the spoken language of Egypt.

Under Pope Cyril VI (1959–1971) the church experienced a revival marked by a Sunday school movement and renewed monasticism in the Wadi El Natrun desert monasteries. His successor, Pope Shenouda III (1971–2012), oversaw expansion to the growing diaspora in North America, Europe and Australia.

Theology and practice

The church confesses one united nature of God the Word incarnate (miaphysitism), following Cyril's formula "one nature of God the Word incarnate". It accepts the first three ecumenical councils (Nicaea, Constantinople and Ephesus). Its sacramental life centres on the Divine Liturgy, baptism by immersion, chrismation, confession, and a demanding calendar of fasts totalling more than 210 days a year for the observant, including Lent and the Fast of the Apostles.

Liturgy and languages

The principal liturgy is that of Saint Basil, with liturgies of Saint Gregory and Saint Cyril also in use. Services combine Coptic — the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language — with Arabic and, in the diaspora, local vernaculars. Coptic chant, largely unaccompanied apart from cymbals and triangle, is held to preserve very ancient melodic traditions.

Monasticism

Egypt's deserts gave birth to Christian monasticism: Anthony the Great withdrew to the Eastern Desert in the late third century, and Pachomius founded communal (cenobitic) monasteries in Upper Egypt. Historic monasteries such as St Anthony's, St Macarius, St Bishoy and the Monastery of Saint Mary (Deir el-Surian) remain active centres of prayer and pilgrimage.

Modern era and diaspora

Copts form the largest Christian community in the Middle East, concentrated in Egypt, where their numbers are disputed between official statistics and church claims. Emigration since the 1960s has created substantial parishes abroad — several hundred churches in the United States and Canada alone — governed by diocesan bishops under the Alexandrian patriarchate. The church has also faced episodes of sectarian violence in Egypt, including church bombings in the 2010s, which spurred further emigration.
