Hooghly River

The Hooghly River (also spelled Hugli; Bengali: হুগলি নদী) is a distributary of the Ganges River in West Bengal, India, approximately 260 kilometres (160 mi) long. It branches from the Ganges near Murshidabad, where the Bhagirathi–Hooghly channel takes off from the main river, and flows south past Kolkata (Calcutta) and Howrah before emptying into the Bay of Bengal. In its upper course it is often called the Bhagirathi; below Kolkata it becomes a broad tidal estuary.

Course and importance

The river is the commercial lifeline of West Bengal. The twin cities of Kolkata and Howrah, joined by bridges including the cantilever Howrah Bridge (Rabindra Setu, 1943) and the cable-stayed Vidyasagar Setu, stand on its banks, and the ports of Kolkata and Haldia handle much of eastern India's trade. The Farakka Barrage, completed in 1975, diverts Ganges water into the Bhagirathi–Hooghly to flush silt from Kolkata port—an engineering benefit for India that has been a longstanding source of dispute with Bangladesh over dry-season flows.

History

European trading posts lined the Hooghly in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: the Portuguese at Hooghly town, the Dutch at Chinsurah, the French at Chandannagar, and the Danes at Serampore, reflecting the river's role as the gateway to Bengal's textile and opium trades. The East India Company's settlement at Calcutta grew into the capital of British India. The river remains central to Bengali religious life: pilgrims bathe at its ghats, and at Sagar Island, where it meets the sea, the Ganga Sagar Mela draws millions of devotees each January.

Challenges

Heavy silting, industrial and municipal pollution, and declining dry-season discharge threaten navigation and ecosystems. The estuary supports the famous hilsa fishery and the northern edge of the Sundarbans mangrove delta.
