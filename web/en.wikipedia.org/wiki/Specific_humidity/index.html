Specific humidity

Specific humidity (or moisture content) is the ratio of the mass of water vapor to the total mass of the air parcel. It is one of several measures of the humidity of air used in meteorology, climatology, and engineering. Specific humidity is approximately equal to the mixing ratio, another common measure, which is the ratio of the mass of water vapor to the mass of dry air.

Specific humidity
Symbolq
SI unitkg/kg (dimensionless ratio, often g/kg)
Typical range0–30 g/kg in the atmosphere

Definition

Specific humidity is defined as:

q = mv / (mv + md)

where mv is the mass of water vapor and md is the mass of dry air in a given volume. It is related to the mixing ratio r by q = r / (1 + r), and can be approximated from vapor pressure e and total pressure p as:

q ≈ 0.622 e / (p − 0.378 e)

Properties

An important advantage of specific humidity is that it remains constant when an air parcel rises or sinks adiabatically, changing only when moisture is added (by evaporation or mixing) or removed (by condensation and precipitation). This makes it a conservative tracer widely used in weather forecasting models and climate analyses. In contrast, relative humidity varies strongly with temperature even when the actual moisture content does not change.

Measurement and typical values

Specific humidity is not measured directly; it is computed from measurements of temperature, pressure, and dew point or relative humidity, obtained by radiosondes, surface stations, and satellites. Saturated tropical air near the surface may hold 20–30 g/kg, while cold polar air and the upper troposphere contain less than 1 g/kg.

Applications

Specific humidity fields are central to numerical weather prediction, studies of the hydrological cycle, and climate change research: because warmer air can hold more moisture, global average specific humidity has increased with warming temperatures, intensifying precipitation extremes. In building engineering and HVAC design, humidity ratio calculations derived from specific humidity govern latent cooling loads.
