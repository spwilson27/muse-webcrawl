Anti-dumping duty

Anti-dumping duty
TypeTrade remedy; import tariff
Legal basisGATT Article VI; WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement
ConditionsDumping, material injury, causal link
Typical durationFive years, subject to sunset review

An anti-dumping duty is a protectionist tariff that a domestic government imposes on foreign imports that it determines are priced below their normal value — a practice known as dumping. The duty is intended to offset the price difference and protect domestic producers from material injury caused by unfairly low-priced imports.

Legal framework

Anti-dumping measures are authorised under Article VI of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1947) and are governed in detail by the WTO Agreement on Implementation of Article VI, commonly called the Anti-Dumping Agreement. Before imposing a duty, an investigating authority must establish three things:

  • the imports are dumped — their export price is less than the normal value of the like product in the exporting country;
  • the domestic industry suffers material injury or threat of injury; and
  • a causal link exists between the dumped imports and the injury.

The duty may not exceed the margin of dumping. Duties are normally imposed for five years and expire unless a "sunset review" finds that dumping and injury would be likely to recur. Investigations are usually initiated on a complaint from the domestic industry, and provisional duties may be applied during the investigation.

Practice

The most active users of anti-dumping measures have included India, the United States, the European Union and China, and the most frequent targets are steel, chemicals and other basic industrial goods. Investigations in the United States are split between the Department of Commerce, which determines dumping, and the International Trade Commission, which determines injury.

Criticism

Economists often criticise anti-dumping law as a vehicle for protectionism: selling below the price charged in one's home market is common competitive behaviour in domestic markets and is rarely unlawful there, while the calculation of "normal value" — sometimes based on constructed costs or third-country prices — can produce large margins for methodological rather than economic reasons. Defenders reply that dumping may reflect subsidies, state ownership or closed home markets that make the comparison genuinely unfair.
