Global citizenship

Global citizenship is the idea that every person is a member of, and bears rights and responsibilities within, a worldwide political community, rather than being a citizen of a particular nation alone. Unlike legal citizenship, world citizenship has no single granting state; it is a moral and political identity grounded in doctrines from ancient Stoic cosmopolitanism to modern human rights and global-governance advocacy.

History

Diogenes the Cynic, asked where he came from, reportedly replied that he was a "citizen of the world" (kosmopolitês), and Stoic philosophers developed the thought into a doctrine of universal moral community. Kant's Perpetual Peace (1795) gave cosmopolitanism a juridical form in a right of universal hospitality. In the twentieth century, figures such as Garry Davis — who in 1948 renounced his US citizenship and declared himself a citizen of the world, issuing "world passports" — turned the idea into a movement; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) gave every person rights irrespective of nationality, while leaving enforcement to states.

Contemporary uses

Today "global citizenship" is used in education (UNESCO's Global Citizenship Education programme teaches knowledge, values, and skills for participation in an interdependent world), in business (the globally mobile "global citizen" executive), and by NGOs promoting climate action, global health, and refugee protection. Supporters argue that problems such as pandemics, financial crises, and climate change cannot be governed at national level alone. Critics respond that world citizenship without a world state is empty rhetoric, that it disproportionately describes the privileged, or that it dilutes the democratic accountability that only real polities provide; others ask whether global and national loyalties genuinely conflict, or whether the first grows out of the second.
