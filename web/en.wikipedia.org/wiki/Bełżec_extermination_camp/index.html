Bełżec was a Nazi German extermination camp built by the SS for the purpose of implementing the secretive Operation Reinhard, the plan to murder all Polish Jews, part of the "Final Solution" which, applying to all of German-occupied Europe, eventually murdered around six million Jews. The camp operated from 17 March 1942 to the end of June 1943.

History

Bełżec was the first of the three Operation Reinhard killing centres to begin operations. It was built near the railway station at Bełżec, on the Lublin–Lwów line, beginning in November 1941 under camp commandant Christian Wirth. Victims, deported by train from ghettos across the Lublin district, Galicia and Kraków, were killed in gas chambers using carbon monoxide produced by a large internal-combustion engine. Unlike Auschwitz, Bełżec had no significant role as a labour or concentration camp; nearly every deportee was murdered on arrival.

Only a handful of people are known to have survived Bełżec, among them Rudolf Reder and Chaim Hirszman, whose testimony records the camp's operation. After the last transports in December 1942, the camp was dismantled during 1943: mass graves were exhumed and the bodies cremated on pyres as part of Sonderaktion 1005, and the site was planted over with trees to conceal the crime.

Estimates of the toll

The Höfle telegram, a German wartime message intercepted and decrypted by the British, records 434,508 people sent to Bełżec by the end of 1942. Historians' estimates of the total number murdered range from about 430,000 to 600,000.

Commemoration

For decades the site was neglected. A new memorial, built jointly by the Polish government and the American Jewish Committee, was opened in 2004; it covers the entire camp area with a field of iron-bearing slag, with a path cut through it bearing the names of the victims' home towns.
