Emmert's law

Emmert's law states that objects that generate retinal images of the same size will look different in physical size (linear size) if they appear to be located at different distances. Specifically, the perceived linear size of an object increases as its perceived distance from the observer increases. This is a law of visual perception first described by the German physiologist Emil Emmert in 1881.

Afterimages

The effect is classically demonstrated with afterimages. If a viewer stares at a bright light or image and then looks at a nearby surface, the afterimage appears small; if the viewer then looks at a distant wall, the same afterimage appears much larger, although the image on the retina has not changed in size. The perceived size grows in proportion to the distance at which the afterimage is projected.

Relationship to size constancy

Emmert's law is closely related to size constancy, the perceptual tendency for objects to appear of constant size despite changes in viewing distance. In algebraic terms, perceived size is approximately proportional to the product of retinal image size and perceived distance. The law also helps explain the Moon illusion, in which the Moon appears larger near the horizon than high in the sky.
