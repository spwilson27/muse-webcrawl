Air medical services

Air medical services are the use of aircraft — helicopters and fixed-wing aeroplanes — to transport patients, medical personnel, organs, and medical supplies. Air medical transport is a component of emergency medical services and is provided by dedicated air ambulance operators, hospital-based programmes, charities, and military medical evacuation units. The two principal roles are emergency scene response and inter-hospital transfer of critically ill or injured patients.

A helicopter air ambulance departing a hospital helipad.

Helicopter emergency medical services

Helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) developed from military experience in the Korean and Vietnam Wars, where rapid evacuation by helicopter dramatically improved survival of the wounded. Civilian programmes expanded from the 1970s onward, notably with hospital-based services in the United States, the physician-staffed air ambulances of Germany's ADAC and DRF Luftrettung, Switzerland's Rega, and Britain's charity-funded air ambulances such as London's Air Ambulance. HEMS crews typically consist of a pilot, a paramedic, and — in many European systems — an emergency physician, and can deliver advanced interventions at the scene of major trauma, including anaesthesia, blood transfusion, and surgical procedures, before rapid transfer to a major trauma centre.

Fixed-wing air ambulance

Fixed-wing aircraft are used for longer distances, typically over about 240 km (150 miles), for repatriation of injured travellers, and for planned transfers between hospitals. Equipped as flying intensive-care units with stretchers, monitors, ventilators, and sometimes incubators, these aircraft are operated by commercial air ambulance companies, insurers, and the military. Unlike helicopters they require runways, but they offer greater range, speed, weather capability, and cabin space.

Benefits and controversy

The case for air medical transport rests on speed and on bringing senior clinical capability to the patient, exploiting the "golden hour" concept in trauma care. Its use is debated where journey times by road are short, and in the United States the high cost of helicopter transport — and the practice of balance billing patients — has drawn sustained criticism and regulatory attention, including provisions of the No Surprises Act of 2020. Safety is also a concern: air medical flying, often at night, in poor weather, and to unimproved landing sites, has historically had a higher accident rate than other aviation sectors, prompting stricter regulation of equipment, night-vision systems, and crew resource management.
