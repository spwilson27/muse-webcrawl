Deadstick landing

[Image: Aircraft gliding to a forced landing with its engine shut down]

A deadstick landing, also called a dead-stick landing, is a type of forced landing when an aircraft loses all of its propulsive power and is forced to land. The "stick" does not refer to the flight controls, but to the traditional wooden propeller, which without power would simply be a "dead stick". The term is now applied to power-off landings in any aircraft, including jets and helicopters (in the latter case performed as an autorotation).

Description

When an engine fails, a pilot's immediate priorities are to maintain airspeed and establish the best glide, then select a suitable landing site. Aircraft glide at a characteristic ratio of forward distance to altitude lost; for a typical light aircraft this is roughly 9:1, while modern airliners can glide about 17:1. From cruising altitude an airliner may therefore glide well over 100 kilometres.

Notable successful deadstick landings by airliners include the "Gimli Glider" (Air Canada Flight 143, 1983), which ran out of fuel and landed at a former air force base in Manitoba; Air Transat Flight 236 (2001), which glided about 120 km to the Azores after fuel exhaustion—the longest passenger jet glide on record; and US Airways Flight 1549 (2009), ditched in the Hudson River after bird strikes disabled both engines.

Training

Power-off landing practice is a standard part of pilot training. Instructors simulate engine failure by retarding the throttle to idle, and students practise judging glide distance and energy management. Competition "spot landing" events also reward accurate power-off approaches.
