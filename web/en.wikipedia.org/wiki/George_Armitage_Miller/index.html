George Armitage Miller

George A. Miller

Born: 3 February 1920, Charleston, West Virginia, USA

Died: 22 July 2012, Plainsboro, New Jersey, USA

Known for: "The Magical Number Seven", WordNet, cognitive revolution

George Armitage Miller (3 February 1920 – 22 July 2012) was an American psychologist who was one of the founders of cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience. He is widely regarded as one of the most important psychologists of the 20th century.

The Magical Number Seven

Miller's 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two: Some Limits on Our Capacity for Processing Information" is one of the most cited papers in psychology. It showed that short-term memory can hold roughly seven "chunks" of information, and that recoding items into larger chunks is the key to expanding effective capacity.

Cognitive revolution

At Harvard, Miller co-founded the Center for Cognitive Studies (1960) with Jerome Bruner, helping to shift psychology away from behaviourism toward the study of mental processes. With Eugene Galanter and Karl Pribram he wrote Plans and the Structure of Behavior (1960), introducing the TOTE (Test-Operate-Test-Exit) unit as an alternative to the reflex arc.

WordNet and language

At Princeton, Miller directed the development of WordNet, a large lexical database of English in which words are organised by meaning into networks of synonyms and semantic relations; it became a foundational resource for computational linguistics and natural language processing. His books include Language and Communication (1951) and The Science of Words (1991). He received the National Medal of Science in 1991.
