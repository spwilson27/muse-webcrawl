Computational complexity theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Nested regions labelled P, NP and PSPACE as a Venn-style diagram]

Computational complexity theory classifies problems by how the resources needed to solve them — time, memory — grow with input size. It asks not whether a problem is computable (see computability) but whether it is feasible.

The field took shape in the 1960s (Hartmanis and Stearns, 1965) and revolves around classes such as P (solvable in polynomial time) and NP (whose proposed solutions can be checked in polynomial time). Whether P equals NP is the most famous open question in computer science.

Methods

Reductions compare problems' difficulty: a problem is NP-complete if every NP problem reduces to it, making it a hardest representative of the class — thousands of practical problems, from scheduling to protein folding subproblems, are NP-complete.

Complexity theory also underpins cryptography, which banks on certain problems (inverting one-way functions) being hard in practice.
