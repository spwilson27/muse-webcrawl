r/K selection theory

In ecology, r/K selection theory describes a spectrum of reproductive strategies. r-selected species produce many small offspring with little parental care and thrive in unstable environments, while K-selected species produce few, larger offspring with high parental investment near the environment's carrying capacity (K).

Origins and critique

The terminology derives from the logistic equation parameters r (growth rate) and K (carrying capacity) and was popularised by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson in 1967, and by Eric Pianka. Modern ecologists treat it as a useful heuristic within broader life-history theory (such as fast–slow continua) rather than a strict dichotomy, since traits often vary independently.

Examples

Typical r-strategists include insects, oysters, and weeds; K-strategists include elephants, large whales, and humans.
