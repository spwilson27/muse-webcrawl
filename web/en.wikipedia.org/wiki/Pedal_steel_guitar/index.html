Pedal steel guitar

The pedal steel guitar is a type of electric steel guitar which is built on legs and uses pedals and knee levers to change the pitch of certain strings while being played, allowing more varied and complex chord voicings than a simple lap steel guitar. Like all steel guitars, it can play unlimited glissandi (gliding pitches), which are an important part of its characteristic sound, and deep vibrato. The instrument is played horizontally with a metal or glass slide (tone bar) in one hand while the other hand plucks the strings, often with thumb and finger picks.

History

The pedal steel evolved from the Hawaiian lap steel guitar that became popular in American music in the early 20th century, through the multi-neck console steels of the western swing era. Players seeking to change tunings mid-song prompted inventors to add pedals mechanically linked to the strings; early experiments date to the late 1930s and 1940s, with instruments such as the Gibson Electraharp. In the 1950s, players including Bud Isaacs — whose playing on Webb Pierce's 1954 hit "Slowly" popularised the pedal sound in country music — and builders such as Zane Beck, and later Shot Jackson and Buddy Emmons with the Sho-Bud company, standardised the design. Emmons and Jimmy Day popularised the E9 and C6 two-neck configuration that remains standard.

Description

A typical pedal steel has one or two necks of ten strings each, mounted in a frame on legs, with (commonly) three to eight foot pedals and up to eight knee levers. Pedals and levers pull or release strings via a mechanical changer mechanism at the bridge, raising or lowering pitches by set amounts, usually a semitone or whole tone. The most common tuning for the front neck is E9, used for country playing; the rear neck is usually C6, favoured for western swing and jazz. A volume pedal is used almost universally to shape attack and sustain.

Use

The pedal steel is most strongly associated with American country music, where its crying, sliding tones became a signature of the Nashville sound, but it is also used in western swing, sacred steel gospel traditions, blues, rock and ambient music. Notable players include Buddy Emmons, Lloyd Green, Paul Franklin, Robert Randolph and Susan Alcorn.
