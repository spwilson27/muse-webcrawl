Hereford Cathedral, formally the Cathedral Church of St Mary the Virgin and St Ethelbert the King, is the Anglican cathedral of the Diocese of Hereford in Herefordshire, England. Built mainly in the 12th and early 13th centuries, it is home to two celebrated medieval treasures: the Hereford Mappa Mundi, the largest surviving medieval world map, and one of the finest surviving chained libraries, dating from the 17th century.

History

A church stood on the site from at least the 7th century, associated with St Ethelbert, a king of East Anglia murdered in 794 whose relics made Hereford a pilgrimage centre. The present building was begun under Bishop Robert de Losinga around 1079 and consecrated in the 1140s. The nave is Norman, the choir and Lady Chapel Early English Gothic. The western tower collapsed in 1786, destroying the west front, which was rebuilt; a further restoration in the early 20th century, directed by John Oldrid Scott, gave the cathedral its present west front, consecrated in 1908.

Treasures

Mappa Mundi

The Mappa Mundi, drawn about 1300 and attributed to Richard of Holdingham, is drawn on a single sheet of vellum and depicts the inhabited world of medieval Christian geography, with Jerusalem at its centre. It has been held at the cathedral since its creation and is now displayed in a dedicated exhibition building opened in 1996.

Chained library

The cathedral's chained library, set up in the early 17th century, preserves some 1,500 volumes, including about 227 medieval manuscripts, with the books still secured by their original chains to the presses. It is the largest surviving chained library of its kind.

Music

Hereford is one of the three host cathedrals of the Three Choirs Festival, held jointly with Gloucester and Worcester since the early 18th century. The organist Edward Elgar had close associations with the city and festival.
