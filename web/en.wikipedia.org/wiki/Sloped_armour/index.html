Sloped armour

Sloped armour is armour that is neither in a vertical nor a horizontal position. Such "angled" armour is often mounted on tanks and other armoured fighting vehicles. Sloping armour plate increases its effective thickness against incoming projectiles, because a projectile striking at an angle must travel through more material, and also increases the chance of deflection or ricochet.

Principle

The effective thickness of a sloped plate is the nominal thickness divided by the cosine of the angle from vertical: a plate 50 mm thick sloped at 45 degrees presents a line-of-sight thickness of about 70 mm. In addition, sloped surfaces can cause kinetic-energy penetrators and shaped-charge jets to deflect, deform or fail to "bite" into the plate. Sloping also allows a given level of protection to be achieved with a thinner, lighter plate, or conversely greater protection for the same weight — though the sloped layout can reduce usable internal volume.

History

Angled armour appeared on warships and early armoured vehicles, but its most famous application was the Soviet T-34 medium tank of 1940, whose steeply sloped glacis plate and turret sides gave it protection far beyond what its plate thickness suggested, strongly influencing later designs such as the German Panther. Earlier French designs, including the SOMUA S35 and the FCM 36, had also used sloped and cast armour. After the Second World War, sloped armour became standard on main battle tanks, and modern vehicles combine sloping with composite and explosive reactive armour.

Limitations

Against modern long-rod penetrators, excessive obliquity can be counterproductive in some regimes, and normalization (the tendency of a projectile to turn into the plate) can reduce the benefit. Sloped sides also reduce internal volume and can complicate vehicle layout, which is why many modern designs slope mainly the frontal arc.
