Alfred, Lord Tennyson

Alfred Tennyson, 1st Baron Tennyson
Born: 6 August 1809, Somersby, Lincolnshire
Died: 6 October 1892, Lurgashall, Sussex
Occupation: Poet
Poet Laureate: 1850–1892
Notable works: In Memoriam A.H.H.; The Charge of the Light Brigade; Ulysses; Idylls of the King; Crossing the Bar

Alfred Tennyson, 1st Baron Tennyson (1809–1892) was an English poet and Poet Laureate for over forty years of Queen Victoria's reign — the longest tenure of the office. He remains one of the most popular British poets, famed for his mastery of metre and melancholy lyricism.

Early career

Born in Lincolnshire and educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, Tennyson won the Chancellor's Gold Medal for "Timbuktu" (1829). Early volumes (1830, 1832) drew mixed reviews; the death of his closest friend Arthur Hallam in 1833 plunged him into a decade of grief and relative silence.

Major works

The two-volume Poems (1842) — including "Ulysses", "Morte d'Arthur", and "Locksley Hall" — established his reputation. In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850), a long elegy for Hallam containing "Tis better to have loved and lost", made him Laureate after Wordsworth's death. Other famous poems include "The Charge of the Light Brigade" (1854), the Arthurian cycle Idylls of the King (1859–1885), "The Lady of Shalott", "Maud", and the late "Crossing the Bar" (1889).

Later life

Tennyson was raised to the peerage in 1884. He lived at Farringford on the Isle of Wight and Aldworth in Sussex, avoided publicity, and made some of the earliest poetry voice recordings for Edison. He died in 1892 and was buried in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey.
