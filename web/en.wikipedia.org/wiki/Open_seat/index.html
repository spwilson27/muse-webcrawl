Open seat

An open seat is an elected office or legislative seat for which the current holder, the incumbent, is not seeking re-election. Open-seat elections are contrasted with contests in which an incumbent faces a challenger. Because no candidate enjoys the advantages of incumbency, open seats are usually the most competitive races in an election cycle and attract the greatest campaign spending.

Causes

A seat becomes open for several reasons:

  • The incumbent retires voluntarily, sometimes anticipating defeat or scandal;
  • The incumbent is barred by term limits, a rule common for governors and state legislators in the United States;
  • The incumbent runs for a different office, such as a representative seeking a Senate seat;
  • The incumbent dies or resigns before the end of the term and the succession rules require a fresh election rather than an appointment;
  • A new seat is created by redistricting or reapportionment without a natural incumbent.

Competitiveness

Open seats typically draw crowded primary fields, because ambitious politicians regard them as rare opportunities: in legislatures with high incumbency re-election rates, most members first win office through an open seat. Parties therefore treat open seats in marginal districts as top targets, and control of closely divided legislatures often turns on a handful of open-seat contests. A retiring incumbent's party is generally more likely to lose an open seat than to lose a seat defended by the incumbent personally.
