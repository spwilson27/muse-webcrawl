Abdication

Abdication is the act of formally relinquishing monarchical authority. It differs from deposition, in which a monarch is forcibly removed, and from demission, a term used in canon law. Abdications may be voluntary, made under pressure, or required by law; in some monarchies the constitution makes no provision for abdication at all, while in others it is a routine and expected part of the succession.

History

Voluntary abdication has ancient precedents. The Roman emperor Diocletian abdicated in 305 CE and retired to grow cabbages at Salona, an exceptionally rare case of a Roman ruler leaving power alive. In medieval and early modern Europe abdications were uncommon and often coerced, as with the forced abdications of Richard II of England (1399) and Mary, Queen of Scots (1567). Charles V, Holy Roman Emperor, abdicated his many crowns in stages in 1555–56, dividing them between his son Philip II of Spain and his brother Ferdinand I. Christina of Sweden's abdication in 1654, so she could convert to Catholicism, shocked Europe.

Modern practice

In the Netherlands, abdication became a normal convention: Wilhelmina (1948), Juliana (1980) and Beatrix (2013) each abdicated in favour of their heirs. Belgium's Leopold III abdicated in 1951 after the Royal Question crisis, and Albert II abdicated in 2013; Luxembourg's grand dukes have also abdicated by tradition. In Britain, Edward VIII's abdication in December 1936 — to marry the American divorcée Wallis Simpson — precipitated a constitutional crisis settled by His Majesty's Declaration of Abdication Act 1936. Pope Benedict XVI's resignation in 2013, the first papal abdication since Gregory XII in 1415, and Emperor Akihito of Japan's abdication in 2019 (enabled by special legislation) are prominent recent examples.

Legal aspects

The legal effect of an abdication varies. Some constitutions require a statute to give it effect, others treat it as a personal act of the sovereign. Where no mechanism exists, a monarch who wishes to step down may be unable to do so, or the act may be regarded as a vacancy filled by the ordinary rules of succession. Abdication is distinct from regency, in which the monarch retains the title while another exercises power.
