The Duchy of Burgundy (Latin: Ducatus Burgundiae; French: Duché de Bourgogne) was a medieval state in what is now eastern France, centred on Dijon. Under the Valois dukes in the 14th and 15th centuries, Burgundy assembled a composite state — the Burgundian Netherlands and the Franche-Comté as well as the duchy itself — that rivalled the Kingdom of France in wealth and power.

Origins

The duchy emerged in the late 9th century from the breakup of the Carolingian world, ruled first by the Bosonid and then the Capetian dukes, a cadet line of the French royal house. In 1361 the duchy reverted to the crown, and in 1363 King John II granted it to his youngest son, Philip the Bold, founding the Valois line of dukes.

The Valois dukes

Through marriage and inheritance the dukes acquired Flanders, Artois, the Franche-Comté, Brabant, Holland, and Luxembourg. Their courts at Dijon and Bruges were famed for art and chivalry — patrons included the sculptor Claus Sluter and the painter Jan van Eyck — and the dukes founded the Order of the Golden Fleece in 1430. During the Hundred Years' War, Burgundy allied with England after the assassination of John the Fearless in 1419, a feud with the Armagnacs that shaped French politics until the Treaty of Arras (1435).

Fall

Charles the Bold sought a royal crown and a continuous territory linking Burgundy to the Netherlands, but was defeated by the Swiss and killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477. Louis XI of France seized the duchy proper, while Charles's daughter Mary married Maximilian of Habsburg, carrying the Low Countries and Franche-Comté into the Habsburg inheritance.
