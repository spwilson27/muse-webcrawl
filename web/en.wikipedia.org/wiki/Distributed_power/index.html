Distributed power

A unit train using distributed power, with remote locomotives placed mid-train and at the rear.

Distributed power is a concept in railway operations in which additional locomotives are placed at intermediate points or at the rear of a train, rather than all being coupled at the front, and are controlled remotely from the lead locomotive. The remote units are commonly called distributed power units (DPUs). The system is widely used on long, heavy freight trains, particularly unit trains carrying coal, ore and grain in North America and Australia.

Operation

The engineer in the lead locomotive controls the remote locomotives by radio link, with systems such as GE's Locotrol synchronising throttle and braking commands. Distributed power reduces the in-train forces acting on couplers, allowing longer and heavier trains to be operated safely, and improves braking because air-brake signals are applied from multiple points in the train rather than propagating from the front alone.

Advantages

By spreading tractive effort along the train, distributed power reduces draft forces, lessens the risk of derailment on curves, improves fuel efficiency and can increase line capacity without additional crews. Remotely controlled locomotives at the rear also assist on gradients where helper locomotives were traditionally stationed.
