Double movement

The double movement is a concept in political economy introduced by Karl Polanyi in his 1944 book The Great Transformation. It describes the dialectical interplay in modern market societies between two opposing movements: the expansion of free markets and economic liberalisation on the one hand, and the protective countermovement — social legislation, regulation, welfare provision and other measures by which society shields itself from the disruptive effects of the self-regulating market — on the other.

Polanyi's argument

Polanyi argued that the attempt to create a fully self-regulating market economy in the 19th century treated land, labour and money as "fictitious commodities", subjecting society to intolerable strains. The protective countermovement arose spontaneously across class lines — factory acts, trade unions, social insurance, central banking and tariffs — as society sought to protect itself. For Polanyi, the collapse of the 19th-century order, and the rise of fascism and the New Deal, could be understood as outcomes of this double movement.

Legacy

The concept remains influential in sociology, international political economy and globalisation studies, where scholars apply it to contemporary cycles of market liberalisation and backlash, including responses to financial crisis and austerity.
