Plain people

Plain people is a term used in North America for Christian groups — most notably the Amish, Old Order Mennonites, Old German Baptist Brethren, and some conservative Quakers — who practise plain dress, plain speech, and a deliberately simple way of life as expressions of humility, modesty, and separation from worldly society.

Beliefs and practice

Plain churches ground their customs in biblical injunctions against conformity to the world and pride. Members wear distinctive, modest clothing without ornament: men typically wear broad-brimmed hats, beards (among the Amish) without moustaches, and plain-cut coats; women wear head coverings or prayer caps, aprons, and cape dresses in solid colours. Many groups traditionally restrict technology — cars, electricity from the grid, telephones in the home — according to community rules known among the Amish as the Ordnung.

History

The movement's roots lie in the Radical Reformation: the Swiss Brethren and the Anabaptist followers of Menno Simons in the sixteenth century, and the Amish division of 1693 led by Jakob Ammann. Plain communities emigrated to Pennsylvania from the early 1700s and spread across the American Midwest and Canada, with the largest concentrations today in Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Indiana.

Distinctions

"Plain" describes a spectrum: horse-and-buggy Old Orders at one end, and "car-driving" conservative groups at the other, all sharing plain dress and worship styles. The term distinguishes these Anabaptist traditions from other groups that also value simplicity but dress conventionally.
