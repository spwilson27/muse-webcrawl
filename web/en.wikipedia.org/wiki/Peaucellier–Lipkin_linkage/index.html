Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage

The Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage, invented independently by Charles-Nicolas Peaucellier in 1864 and Yom Tov Lipman Lipkin in 1871, was the first true planar straight-line mechanism, capable of transforming circular motion into precise straight-line motion and vice versa.

Geometry

The linkage consists of six bars forming a rhombus together with a seventh bar. Two adjacent vertices of the rhombus are constrained at equal distances from a fixed pivot, so that as one free vertex traces a circle passing through the pivot, the other free vertex traces an exact straight line. The mechanism is a physical implementation of circle inversion in geometry.

Importance

Before its discovery, engineers relied on approximate straight-line mechanisms such as the Watt linkage used in steam engines. An exact solution had been a long-standing problem in kinematics; James Joseph Sylvester championed the Peaucellier linkage and demonstrated it widely in Britain.
