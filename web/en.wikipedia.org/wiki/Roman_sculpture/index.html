Roman sculpture

Roman sculpture is the art of sculpture in ancient Rome and the territories of the Roman Empire, from roughly the 6th century BC to the 5th century AD. Although strongly influenced by Greek and Etruscan art, it developed distinctive strengths, above all in portraiture and historical relief.

Portraiture

Roman portrait sculpture is famous for its verism — an unsparing realism that recorded wrinkles, warts and baldness — rooted in the Republican custom of keeping wax ancestor masks (imagines maiorum). Under Augustus, portraiture became more idealised and served as mass political propaganda, with standardised imperial types distributed across the empire. Portrait styles changed fashionably with each emperor and are a key tool for dating Roman art.

Relief and monuments

Large narrative reliefs adorned public monuments such as triumphal arches and columns: the Ara Pacis, the Arch of Titus, Trajan's Column with its helical frieze depicting the Dacian Wars, and the Column of Marcus Aurelius. Sarcophagi with elaborate mythological and biographical reliefs were produced in huge numbers from the 2nd century AD.

Materials and copies

Romans worked in marble and bronze and made many copies of celebrated Greek originals, through which much Greek sculpture is known today. In late antiquity, sculpture became more abstract and frontal, a style that passed into early Christian and Byzantine art.
