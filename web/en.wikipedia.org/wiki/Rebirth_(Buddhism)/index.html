The Bhavacakra (wheel of life) depicts the six realms into which beings are reborn.

Rebirth in Buddhism refers to the teaching that the actions of a sentient being lead to a new existence after death, in an endless cycle called samsara. The cycle is driven by ignorance (avidya), craving, and aversion, and it is considered a domain of suffering (dukkha) from which liberation — nirvana — is sought.

Rebirth without a soul

Buddhist rebirth differs from the doctrine of reincarnation found in Hinduism: Buddhism denies the existence of a permanent, unchanging soul (atman) that transmigrates. The doctrine of anatta (non-self) holds that what continues from one life to the next is not an essence but a causal continuum of consciousness and karmic tendencies. Classical texts illustrate this with the analogy of a flame passed from one candle to another: the final flame is neither the same as, nor wholly different from, the first. The mechanism is explained by the twelve links of dependent origination (pratitya-samutpada).

The six realms

Rebirth may occur in any of six realms, depicted on the Bhavacakra (wheel of life):

  • Gods (devas) — long-lived and blissful, but complacent and still mortal.
  • Demigods (asuras) — powerful, driven by jealousy and conflict.
  • Humans — a balanced realm considered the most favorable for attaining enlightenment.
  • Animals — dominated by instinct, fear, and ignorance.
  • Hungry ghosts (pretas) — afflicted by insatiable craving.
  • Hell beings (naraka) — realms of intense suffering.

Where one is reborn is conditioned by one's karma — intentional actions of body, speech, and mind. In Tibetan Buddhism, an intermediate state between death and rebirth, the bardo, is described in texts such as the Bardo Thodol (the so-called Tibetan Book of the Dead), and certain realized teachers (tulkus) are held to direct their own rebirth and be recognized in childhood.
