Felix Bloch (23 October 1905 – 10 September 1983) was a Swiss-American physicist who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics with Edward Mills Purcell for the development of new methods for nuclear magnetic precision measurements—i.e., nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), the basis of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Life and career

Bloch studied engineering and then physics at ETH Zürich, and completed his doctorate under Werner Heisenberg at the University of Leipzig in 1928. His dissertation founded the quantum theory of electrons in crystal lattices, introducing what are now called Bloch waves or Bloch functions: solutions of the Schrödinger equation in a periodic potential. He worked with Heisenberg, Pauli, Bohr, and Fermi, and developed the Bethe–Bloch formula for the stopping power of charged particles in matter.

Of Jewish descent, Bloch left Germany in 1933 after the Nazi rise to power and emigrated to the United States in 1934, joining Stanford University, where he spent most of his career. During the Second World War he worked on the atomic bomb project at Los Alamos and on radar countermeasures at Harvard. In 1946, working independently of Purcell's group, he demonstrated NMR in bulk matter. He served as the first director-general of CERN (1954–1955).

Contributions

His name is attached to Bloch's theorem, Bloch waves, the Bloch sphere representation of a two-level quantum system, the Bloch equations describing nuclear magnetization dynamics, the Bloch–Grüneisen law of electrical resistivity, and the Bloch wall between magnetic domains.
