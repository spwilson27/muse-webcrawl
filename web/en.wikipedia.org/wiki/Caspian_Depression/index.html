Caspian Depression

The Caspian Depression is a low-lying flatland region encompassing the northern part of the Caspian Sea and surrounding land in Kazakhstan and Russia. It is one of the lowest points on Earth's land surface, with areas lying around 28 metres below sea level near the Caspian shore.

Geography

The depression covers roughly 200,000 square kilometres of steppe, semi-desert and salt marsh around the northern Caspian. The Volga and Ural rivers cross it to enter the sea, building broad deltas. Much of the terrain is flat, salty and sparsely vegetated, with sand massifs such as the Ryn Desert.

Geology and resources

The region overlies the deep Pricaspian sedimentary basin, famous for giant salt domes and major oil and gas fields, including Tengiz and Kashagan offshore. Salt tectonics have shaped both the landscape and the hydrocarbon traps.

Climate and ecology

The climate is sharply continental and arid. The wetlands of the Volga delta are internationally important for migratory birds and sturgeon, while the dry steppes support saiga antelope. Fluctuations in Caspian Sea level periodically flood or expose large areas of the depression.
