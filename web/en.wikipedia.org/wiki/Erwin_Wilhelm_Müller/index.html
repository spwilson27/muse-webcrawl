Erwin Wilhelm Müller

Erwin Wilhelm Müller (13 June 1911 – 17 May 1977) was a German-born American physicist who invented the field ion microscope and the atom probe.

Erwin Wilhelm Müller (13 June 1911 – 17 May 1977) was a German physicist who invented the field emission microscope (1936), the field ion microscope (1951) — the first instrument able to image individual atoms — and, with his student J. A. Panitz, the atom-probe field ion microscope, which can identify single atoms chemically.

Early life and field emission

Müller was born in Berlin and studied at the Technical University of Berlin under Gustav Hertz. Working in Hertz's laboratory he developed the field emission microscope, in which electrons tunnel from a sharply pointed cathode under a high electric field and project a magnified image of the tip's surface onto a screen. He worked in German industrial laboratories, including Siemens, through the 1930s and the war years, then held posts in Berlin and at the Fritz Haber Institute of the Max Planck Society.

Field ion microscopy

Replacing electrons with ions of an imaging gas such as helium, and cooling the tip to cryogenic temperatures, Müller achieved in 1951 the first images showing individual atoms on a metal surface — a landmark in microscopy. In 1952 he emigrated to the United States, joining Pennsylvania State University, where he remained for the rest of his career and became a US citizen. His images of crystal lattices, vacancies and dislocations opened atomic-scale metallurgy to direct observation.

The atom probe

In 1967 Müller and Panitz combined the field ion microscope with a time-of-flight mass spectrometer to create the atom probe: individual atoms are field-evaporated from the tip, their identity determined from flight time and their original positions recorded. The technique matured after his death into atom-probe tomography, now a standard tool for three-dimensional atomic-scale materials analysis. Müller died in Washington, D.C., in 1977.
