Cutlass

Placeholder image: Cutlass

A cutlass is a short, broad sabre or slashing sword, with a straight or slightly curved blade sharpened on the cutting edge, and a hilt often featuring a solid cupped or basket-shaped guard. It was a common naval weapon during the Age of Sail.

Description

The cutlass is characterized by a blade typically 60–80 cm (24–31 in) long, shorter than infantry sabres, an advantage in the confined spaces of a ship and in boarding actions. Its sturdy guard protected the hand during close combat. The word derives from the French coutelas, a form of couteau ("knife"), related to earlier machete-like weapons.

History

Cutlasses were widely used by naval forces and merchant crews from the 17th through the 19th centuries, as well as by pirates and privateers in the Caribbean. Navies issued standardized patterns; the British Royal Navy and the United States Navy adopted successive models through the 19th and early 20th centuries. The U.S. Navy's Model 1917 cutlass was among the last combat issues, and cutlasses remained in naval inventories into the 1940s. Today they survive as ceremonial weapons: U.S. Navy chief petty officers are authorized to wear a ceremonial cutlass with dress uniforms.

Related weapons

The cutlass is related to the hanger, the short hunting sword, and the machete used as a tool and weapon in agriculture across the Caribbean and Latin America, where the cutlass remains a common implement known by names such as cutlass in Trinidad and Guyana.
