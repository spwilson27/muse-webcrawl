Optimism bias

Optimism bias
FieldCognitive psychology, behavioural science
Also known asUnrealistic optimism, comparative optimism
Key researcherNeil Weinstein; Tali Sharot

Optimism bias (also known as the "illusion of invulnerability", unrealistic optimism, and comparative optimism) is a cognitive bias that causes someone to believe that they themselves are less likely to experience a negative event. It is also known as unrealistic optimism or comparative optimism. Optimism bias is common and transcends gender, ethnicity, nationality, and age.

The bias was first systematically documented by Neil D. Weinstein in 1980, who found that students rated their chances of experiencing positive life events (such as owning their own home or living past 80) as above average, and their chances of negative events (such as developing a drinking problem or being fired) as below average.

Causes

Four factors have been identified as contributing to optimism bias: the desired end state, cognitive mechanisms such as single-target focus, overall evaluations versus individual evaluations, and interpersonal distance. When updating beliefs, people tend to incorporate good news readily but discount bad news — a phenomenon studied by Tali Sharot and colleagues, who found associated activity in the inferior frontal gyrus. The bias is also related to controllability: people are more optimistic about events they believe they can control, such as car accidents (when they are driving) than when they are passengers.

Consequences

Optimism bias can be beneficial — it is associated with better mental health, greater persistence, and reduced anxiety — but it also leads to underestimation of risk. Smokers who underestimate their personal risk are less likely to quit; investors may take excessive risks; and planners commit the planning fallacy, underestimating the time and cost of projects. During the COVID-19 pandemic, optimism bias was linked to reduced compliance with preventive measures among some groups who judged their personal risk as low.

Measurement

The bias is typically measured by asking participants to estimate their own probability of experiencing an event compared with that of an average peer (the comparative method), or by comparing absolute estimates with actual population base rates. Effect sizes vary by event valence, rarity, and perceived controllability; unrealistic optimism is stronger for negative events that are perceived as rare and controllable.
