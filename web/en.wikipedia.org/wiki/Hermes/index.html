Hermes, messenger of the gods

Hermes is the Greek god of heralds, travellers, roads and boundaries, trade and thieves, language and luck — the messenger of Olympus and guide of souls to the underworld. Son of Zeus and the nymph Maia, he is depicted as a swift youth in winged sandals and a broad traveller's hat, carrying the caduceus, a herald's staff entwined with two serpents. Cunning, charming and morally ambidextrous, he is the patron of everyone who crosses boundaries: merchants and orators, heralds and highwaymen.

The infant trickster

The Homeric Hymn to Hermes gives him mythology's busiest first day: born at dawn, by midday he had invented the lyre from a tortoise's shell, and by evening he had stolen Apollo's cattle, driving them backwards to confuse the trail, slaughtered two, and invented fire-sticks — then, confronted, lied with such precocious charm that Zeus laughed, and settled the matter by giving Apollo the lyre, receiving the caduceus and the herald's office in exchange. The hymn establishes his eternal character: the boundary-crosser whom even gods cannot quite pin down.

Roles

As herald, Hermes carries Zeus's commands through the epics; as psychopompos he conducts the dead — the suitors' ghosts in the Odyssey, and shades generally — to Hades. He helps heroes quietly: guiding Priam through the Greek camp to Achilles, giving Odysseus the herb moly against Circe, lending Perseus his sickle and sandals. Stone heaps and later squared pillars called herms, set at roadsides and doorways, marked his protection of travellers and thresholds; their mutilation at Athens in 415 BC was the great sacrilege scandal of the Peloponnesian War. In late antiquity he merged with Egyptian Thoth as Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary author of the Hermetica.
