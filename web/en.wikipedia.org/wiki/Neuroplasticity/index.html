Neuroplasticity


Synaptic connections are continually strengthened, weakened and remodelled by experience.

Neuroplasticity, also known as neural plasticity or brain plasticity, is the ability of neural networks in the brain to change through growth and reorganization. These changes range from individual neurons making new connections, to systematic adjustments such as cortical remapping. It is the process by which the nervous system encodes experience, learns new behaviours and recovers, in part, from injury.

The idea that the brain changes throughout life ran counter to the older view of a static, unchanging adult brain. Santiago Ramón y Cajal described neuronal plasticity conceptually, Jerzy Konorski coined the term plasticity in this context in 1948, and Donald Hebb's 1949 theory — that cells which "fire together wire together" — provided an influential mechanism, later confirmed in the cellular phenomena of long-term potentiation and long-term depression. William James had earlier discussed the brain's "plasticity" in The Principles of Psychology (1890).

Types

Synaptic plasticity

At the smallest scale, the strength of individual synapses can be increased (long-term potentiation) or decreased (long-term depression) in response to patterns of activity, a mechanism thought to underlie learning and memory. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity refines this idea: the order and timing of pre- and post-synaptic firing determines whether a synapse strengthens or weakens.

Structural and functional plasticity

Neurons can grow new dendritic spines and axonal branches, and entire cortical maps can be reorganised. Classic experiments by Michael Merzenich and others showed that the somatosensory cortex of monkeys reorganises after digit amputation or training. In humans, London taxi drivers were famously found to have enlarged posterior hippocampi associated with navigational experience, and violinists show expanded cortical representations of the fingers of the left hand. After stroke or brain injury, rehabilitation exploits plasticity as surviving regions take over lost functions.

Developmental plasticity

Plasticity is greatest in early life, when "critical" or "sensitive" periods exist during which particular experiences — such as binocular vision or exposure to language — are needed for normal development. Hubel and Wiesel's work on visual deprivation in kittens demonstrated such periods experimentally.

Clinical significance

Plasticity underlies recovery after stroke, the effects of constraint-induced movement therapy, and the fitting of cochlear implants. It can also be maladaptive, contributing to chronic pain, phantom limb phenomena, tinnitus and focal dystonia. Claims made for commercial "brain training" products are often exaggerated relative to the scientific evidence.
