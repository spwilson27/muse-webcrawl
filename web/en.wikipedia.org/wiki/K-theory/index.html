K-theory

K-theory is a branch of mathematics that studies rings and abelian groups generated by vector bundles and related structures. Originating in algebraic geometry and topology in the late 1950s, it has developed into algebraic K-theory, topological K-theory, and operator K-theory, with applications ranging from index theorems to string theory and condensed matter physics.

Origins

The subject began with Alexander Grothendieck's 1957 reformulation of the Riemann–Roch theorem, in which he defined the group K(X) of a scheme X as the Grothendieck group of coherent sheaves. Michael Atiyah and Friedrich Hirzebruch then defined topological K-theory K(X) for a compact space X as the Grothendieck group of (complex) vector bundles over X. The resulting generalized cohomology theory exhibits Bott periodicity, making its groups computable and tying it to stable homotopy theory.

Algebraic K-theory

For a ring R, the groups Kn(R) begin with K0(R), the Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective modules, and K1(R), related to the abelianization of the general linear group. Hyman Bass developed K0 and K1 in the 1960s; John Milnor defined K2 via Steinberg relations. Daniel Quillen's plus construction and Q-construction (1973) defined the higher groups Kn(R) for all n, work for which he received the Fields Medal. Quillen also proved that the K-groups of finite fields are finite groups computable from the field's order, and the Quillen–Lichtenbaum conjectures linked K-theory to étale cohomology.

Applications

K-theory underlies the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, the classification of D-brane charges in string theory, and the periodic table of topological insulators and superconductors. Operator-algebraic K-theory for C*-algebras — developed to study elliptic operators — appears in the Baum–Connes conjecture and noncommutative geometry.
