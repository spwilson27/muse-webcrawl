Naval architecture

A traditional lines plan, the naval architect's geometric definition of a hull.

Naval architecture is the engineering discipline concerned with the design, construction, maintenance and operation of ships and other marine structures. It combines elements of mechanical, civil, electrical and materials engineering with hydrodynamics, and its practitioners are known as naval architects.

Branches of the discipline

Hull geometry and hydrostatics. The naval architect defines the hull form and computes displacement, buoyancy and the centres of buoyancy and gravity, from which the vessel's stability — its ability to right itself after heeling — is assessed, both intact and after damage.

Resistance and propulsion. Predicting the power required to drive a hull through water involves model tests in towing tanks, computational fluid dynamics, and the design of propellers and other propulsors to work efficiently behind the hull.

Structures and dynamics. Ships must withstand still-water and wave bending loads, slamming and fatigue over decades of service. Naval architects also study seakeeping — the motions of a vessel in waves — manoeuvring, vibration and noise.

Practice

Design typically proceeds through a "design spiral" of increasingly detailed iterations, from concept sketches to classification-society approval and construction drawings. Modern practice relies heavily on computer-aided design and simulation. Professional bodies such as the Royal Institution of Naval Architects (RINA) and the Society of Naval Architects and Marine Engineers (SNAME) accredit practitioners, and international rules from the International Maritime Organization govern safety standards including the SOLAS convention.
