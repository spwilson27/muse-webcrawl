Butsudan

A butsudan (Japanese: 仏壇, "Buddhist altar") is a small shrine cabinet found in Japanese homes and temples, used to honor the Buddha and to memorialize deceased family members. It typically consists of a lacquered wooden cabinet with doors that open to reveal a central sacred object — a statue or image of the Buddha or a sect's main deity, or a calligraphic mandala (gohonzon) — flanked by memorial tablets (ihai) bearing the posthumous names of ancestors.

Arrangement

Inside the cabinet, the principal object of worship sits on the highest level. Below it are arranged candles, an incense burner, a bell, flower vases, and offerings of rice, water, fruit, and sweets. Photographs of the deceased are often placed nearby in modern homes.

Practice

Family members open the doors each morning, light incense and candles, offer food, ring the bell, and recite sutras or the nembutsu/damoku depending on sect. The butsudan is the focus of memorial observances on death anniversaries and during Obon, when ancestors' spirits are welcomed home.

History

Household altars became widespread in the Edo period, when the Tokugawa shogunate's temple-registration (danka) system tied every family to a Buddhist temple, partly to suppress Christianity. Elaborate gold-lacquered butsudan became status symbols and centers of regional craft traditions.
