Admiralty law, or maritime law, is the body of law governing nautical matters and private maritime disputes: the carriage of goods and passengers by sea, shipping contracts, marine insurance, collisions, salvage, towage, seafarers' rights, and liability for oil pollution and other maritime torts. It is a distinctive blend of domestic law and international conventions administered historically by specialised admiralty courts.

History

Maritime law is among the oldest bodies of law: the Byzantine Rhodian law codified principles such as general average (shared sacrifice of cargo), and medieval codes — the Rolls of Oléron, the Consolato del Mare and the laws of Wisby — standardised rules across European ports. In England, the High Court of Admiralty developed procedures including actions in rem against the ship itself, allowing a vessel to be arrested and sold in satisfaction of claims. Distinctive concepts persist today: maritime liens, limitation of shipowners' liability, and the personification of the ship as defendant.

Modern scope and conventions

Modern admiralty practice rests heavily on international conventions prepared by the Comité Maritime International (founded 1897) and adopted under the International Maritime Organization, including the Hague-Visby Rules on bills of lading, the COLREGs on preventing collisions, SOLAS on safety of life at sea, the Salvage Convention 1989, and conventions on limitation of liability and oil-pollution compensation. In the United States, admiralty is a federal subject under Article III of the Constitution and cases are heard without juries unless a statute (such as the Jones Act for injured seamen) provides otherwise; the Death on the High Seas Act and general maritime law govern wrongful death and personal injury at sea.
