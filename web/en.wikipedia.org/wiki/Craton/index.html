Craton

A craton (from Greek krátos, "strength") is an old and stable part of the continental lithosphere that has survived cycles of continental merging and rifting largely undeformed since the Precambrian. Cratons consist of an exposed or buried crystalline basement — a shield where it outcrops, and a platform where it is covered by sedimentary rock.

Formation and structure

Cratons have deep, cold, chemically depleted lithospheric roots (keels) extending to around 200–250 km, which give them buoyancy and rigidity and explain their billions of years of stability. Most cratonic crust formed during the Archean eon by accretion of volcanic arcs and terranes, a process studied in the context of how plate tectonics began on the early Earth.

Distribution and resources

Major cratons include the Canadian Shield of North America, the Kaapvaal and Congo cratons of Africa, the Yilgarn and Pilbara cratons of Western Australia, the Siberian and East European cratons, and the São Francisco craton of Brazil. Their deep roots are the source of most of the world's diamonds, brought to the surface by kimberlite pipes, and cratons host major deposits of gold, iron and nickel.
