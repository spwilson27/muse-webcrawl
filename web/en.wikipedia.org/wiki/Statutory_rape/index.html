Statutory rape

In common law jurisdictions, statutory rape is nonforcible sexual activity in which one of the individuals is below the age of consent — the age at which a person is legally deemed capable of consenting to sex. Because a minor is considered legally incapable of consent, the offence can apply even where the younger person ostensibly agreed; force is not an element of the crime. The term is most used in the United States; other jurisdictions have equivalent offences such as unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor or sexual activity with a child.

Ages of consent

Ages of consent vary: internationally they commonly fall between 14 and 18, and within federations such as the United States they differ by state (16, 17, or 18 in most states). Many jurisdictions provide "Romeo and Juliet" close-in-age exemptions for partners of similar age, and some increase the age threshold where the older person is in a position of trust or authority, such as a teacher.

Legal character

Historically, statutory rape was a strict liability offence: reasonable mistake about the victim's age was no defence, a rule upheld in many places though some jurisdictions now allow a mistake-of-age defence above certain ages. Penalties range widely, from misdemeanours to long prison terms, and convictions typically carry sex-offender registration. Laws originally protected female minors under gender-specific statutes; modern laws are generally gender-neutral.

Debate

Debate concerns the fairness of strict liability, the severity of penalties for consensual peer relationships, and the appropriateness of the label "rape" for nonforcible conduct. Critics of strict enforcement argue that harsh penalties for near-age partners distort the law's protective purpose.
