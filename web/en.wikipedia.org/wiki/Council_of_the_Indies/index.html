The Council of the Indies (Spanish: Consejo de Indias), formally the Royal and Supreme Council of the Indies, was the principal administrative organ of the Spanish Empire for its overseas territories from 1524 until 1834. Based at the royal court in Spain, it exercised legislative, executive and judicial authority over Spanish America and the Philippines, second only to the monarch himself.

History

Charles V formally established the Council on 1 August 1524, building on the earlier work of Juan Rodríguez de Fonseca and the Casa de Contratación (1503), which handled trade and navigation. For three centuries the Council supervised the viceroys, audiencias and governors of the Indies, drafted colonial legislation, approved officials, heard appeals, oversaw the royal patronage of the Church (patronato real) and commissioned official histories and cosmography — employing chroniclers such as Antonio de Herrera and cosmographers who maintained the secret master map, the Padrón Real.

Functions and legislation

The Council produced the great compilations of colonial law, notably the Recopilación de Leyes de los Reynos de las Indias (1680), which codified thousands of decrees. It also examined the conduct of officials through residencias and visitas. Under the Bourbon reforms of the eighteenth century, its powers were progressively transferred to secretaries of state and new ministries, and the Council, already hollowed out, was abolished in 1834, after most of mainland Spanish America had won independence.

Legacy

The Council's immense archive survives principally in the Archivo General de Indias in Seville, one of the world's great repositories for colonial history.
