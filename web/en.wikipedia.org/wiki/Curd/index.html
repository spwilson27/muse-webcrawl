Curd

Curd
Fresh cheese curds separated from whey
Main ingredientMilk
Produced byCoagulation (acid or rennet), separating curds from whey
UsesCheese-making; eaten fresh (cheese curds); South Asian dahi (yogurt)

Curd is the solid mass obtained by coagulating milk — causing its proteins, chiefly casein, to clump together — and separating the solids from the liquid whey. Coagulation is triggered either by acid (as in lemon juice or lactic acid produced by bacterial cultures) or by the enzyme complex rennet. Curds are the essential intermediate from which almost all cheeses are made, and the word is also used for several foods eaten in curd form, notably fresh cheese curds and, in South Asia, the set yogurt called dahi.

Formation

In fresh milk, casein micelles are kept in suspension by their surface charge. Adding acid lowers the pH toward casein's isoelectric point (about 4.6), neutralising the charge so the micelles aggregate into a gel. Rennet works differently: its enzyme chymosin clips the protective κ-casein layer, allowing the micelles to bind into a firm curd that can be cut, cooked, drained and pressed. The method used determines the texture — acid-set curds are soft and fragile, as in cottage cheese and paneer, while rennet curds are elastic and suitable for ageing into hard cheeses.

Uses

  • Cheese-making — curds are cut, salted, drained and pressed, then eaten fresh or aged into the thousands of cheese varieties.
  • Cheese curds — in the American Midwest and Québec, fresh cheddar curds are eaten as a snack; in Québec they top poutine, where their fresh "squeak" is prized.
  • South Asian curd — dahi, a yogurt set by culturing warm milk, is a daily staple eaten with rice, made into lassi and raita, and used in marinades and curries.
  • Fruit curds — lemon curd and similar spreads take their name from their curd-like texture, though they are made from eggs, butter, sugar and citrus juice rather than milk.

In culture

The separation of "curds and whey" is familiar from the nursery rhyme Little Miss Muffet. Curdling is also a classic image of spoilage: milk "turns" when naturally occurring bacteria acidify it spontaneously, producing the sour curd of spoiled milk.
