Jesuit reduction

The Jesuit reductions were a type of settlement for indigenous peoples established by Jesuit missionaries in colonial South America, principally among the Guaraní of present-day Paraguay, north-eastern Argentina and southern Brazil. The Spanish word reducción (Portuguese redução) referred to "reducing" — gathering — scattered native populations into ordered towns where they could be instructed in Christianity and European crafts and agriculture.

History

The first Guaraní reductions were founded in the early 17th century; the mission of San Ignacio Guazú dates to 1609. By the mid-18th century there were some thirty mission towns with a combined population of well over 100,000. Each reduction was typically run by two Jesuit priests but governed day-to-day through native caciques and an elected town council. Guaraní language was retained for worship and instruction, printing presses in the missions produced books in Guaraní, and the missions became famous for their music, sculpture and stone churches. The reductions also protected their inhabitants, at least in part, from enslavement by Portuguese bandeirante slave-raiders from São Paulo — after devastating raids in the 1620s and 1630s, the missions were permitted to arm and train a Guaraní militia, which famously defeated the raiders at the Battle of Mbororé in 1641.

Decline and legacy

The Treaty of Madrid (1750), which transferred seven mission towns east of the Uruguay River to Portugal, provoked the Guaraní War of 1754–56, in which mission Indians resisted forced relocation. In 1767–68 Charles III of Spain expelled the Jesuits from the Spanish Empire; the reductions passed to other orders and civil administrators, declined economically, and their populations dispersed. Magnificent ruins remain at sites such as San Ignacio Miní, Trinidad and Jesús de Tavarangue in Paraguay, and São Miguel das Missões in Brazil; several are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Historians debate whether the reductions were protective utopias, theocratic labour regimes, or something in between; they have been celebrated in literature and film, notably the 1986 film The Mission.
