E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial is a 1982 American science fiction film produced and directed by Steven Spielberg and written by Melissa Mathison. It tells the story of Elliott, a boy who befriends an extraterrestrial, dubbed E.T., who is accidentally left behind on Earth. Elliott and his siblings help E.T. return home while evading government agents who seek to capture him.

Plot

A spacecraft collecting botanical specimens flees government agents, accidentally leaving one alien behind. The creature is taken in by ten-year-old Elliott, who forms a psychic and emotional bond with him. E.T. assembles a communication device from household objects to "phone home". As E.T.'s health deteriorates — and Elliott's with it — government scientists quarantine the house. E.T. appears to die, then revives when his people return. In a famous chase sequence, the children escape on bicycles, which E.T. levitates across the face of the full moon, before a tearful farewell at the spaceship.

Production

Spielberg conceived the story from an imaginary friend he created after his parents' divorce. Carlo Rambaldi designed the E.T. creature, operated by puppeteers and performed in part by actors including Pat Bilon and Matthew De Meritt. John Williams composed the score. The film was shot largely in chronological order to help the young cast's performances.

Release and reception

The film premiered as the closing entry of the 1982 Cannes Film Festival and was released by Universal Pictures. It surpassed Star Wars to become the highest-grossing film of all time, a record it held for eleven years until Jurassic Park. It was nominated for nine Academy Awards, winning four, including Best Original Score and Best Visual Effects. Critics praised its themes of childhood, friendship and loss, and it is routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made. A 20th-anniversary edition in 2002 altered some effects and dialogue, changes Spielberg later expressed regret over.

Legacy

The image of the bicycle silhouetted against the moon became the logo of Spielberg's Amblin Entertainment. The film's tie-in video game for the Atari 2600 became notorious as a commercial failure. In 1994 the film was selected for the United States National Film Registry as culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant.
