Isaac Bashevis Singer (1902/1904 – 1991) was a Polish-born Jewish-American writer who wrote in Yiddish. One of the leading figures of Yiddish literature, he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1978, cited "for his impassioned narrative art which, with roots in a Polish-Jewish cultural tradition, brings universal human conditions to life."

Life

Singer was born in Leoncin, Poland, the son of a Hasidic rabbi, and grew up in Warsaw and in the shtetl of Biłgoraj, settings that pervade his fiction. Encouraged by his elder brother, the novelist Israel Joshua Singer, he began writing for the Yiddish press in the 1920s. In 1935 he emigrated to the United States, joining the Yiddish newspaper Forverts (The Jewish Daily Forward), for which he wrote for the rest of his life — serialising novels and publishing thousands of stories under pen names including Yitskhok Bashevis, Segal and Warszawski.

Work

Singer's fiction recreates the vanished world of Polish Jewry — its rabbis, marketplaces, dybbuks and imps — while engaging modern themes of faith, doubt, desire and betrayal. His best-known works include the story "Gimpel the Fool" (translated by Saul Bellow, 1953), the novels The Family Moskat, The Manor, The Magician of Lublin, Enemies, A Love Story, and Shosha, and the story collection A Crown of Feathers, which won the National Book Award. He also wrote acclaimed children's books, including Zlateh the Goat. Singer supervised English translations of his work, treating them as "second originals". A lifelong vegetarian, he famously asked, "How can we speak of right and justice if we take an innocent creature and shed its blood?"
