Ant colony

An ant colony is a population of ants, typically from a single species, that are capable of maintaining their complete lifecycle. Ant colonies are eusocial, meaning they have reproductive and non-reproductive castes. A colony is the fundamental unit of ant life; individual ants are often compared to cells of a superorganism, since no single ant can survive and reproduce independently for long.

Structure

A typical colony consists of a queen (or several queens), who is the sole reproductive female, a large number of sterile female workers, and, seasonally, winged males and virgin queens (alates) that leave the nest on mating flights. Workers perform all other tasks: foraging, brood care, nest construction and defence. Colonies of some species number only a few dozen individuals, while mature colonies of leafcutter ants (Atta) or army ants may contain millions.

Nests

Most colonies occupy a nest, which may be a simple excavation in soil, a complex structure of chambers and tunnels, a mound, or a cavity in wood or vegetation. Some species are nomadic and form temporary bivouacs of living ants. Nest architecture regulates temperature and humidity and protects the brood and stored food.

Life cycle

New colonies are usually founded after a nuptial flight, when a mated queen lands, sheds her wings and excavates a first chamber, raising the first workers from her own body reserves. The colony then grows through ergonomic stages, eventually producing its own reproductives. Some species also reproduce by budding, in which queens walk to a new nest site accompanied by workers.

Supercolonies

In a few invasive species, such as the Argentine ant (Linepithema humile), separate nests may show no aggression toward one another and form vast supercolonies extending over hundreds of kilometres.
