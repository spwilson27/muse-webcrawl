Three-phase electric power

Three-phase electric power is a type of polyphase alternating current (AC) system used worldwide for the generation, transmission and distribution of electric power, and for powering heavy industrial loads. It uses three conductors carrying alternating currents of the same frequency whose phases are offset by one-third of a cycle (120°).

Principles

In a balanced three-phase system the sum of the instantaneous currents is zero, so no return conductor is needed for the balanced component, and total power delivered is constant rather than pulsating, as in single-phase systems. This produces smooth torque in rotating machines. Three-phase supplies can deliver more power for a given amount of conductor material than single-phase systems, and the rotating magnetic field needed by induction motors arises naturally.

Connections

Generators and loads may be connected in star (wye, Y) or delta (Δ) configurations. A star system with a neutral conductor provides two voltages — line-to-neutral and the √3-times-larger line-to-line voltage — allowing simultaneous supply of domestic single-phase loads and industrial three-phase loads, as in the 230/400 V systems common in Europe.

History

The system was developed in the late 1880s, with key contributions by Galileo Ferraris, Nikola Tesla, Mikhail Dolivo-Dobrovolsky and others. The 1891 Lauffen–Frankfurt transmission demonstrated long-distance three-phase power and established the standard that dominates grids today.
