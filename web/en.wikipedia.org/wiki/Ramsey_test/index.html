Ramsey test

The Ramsey test is a procedure for evaluating the acceptability of a conditional statement ("if P, then Q"), proposed by the philosopher Frank P. Ramsey in his 1929 paper "General Propositions and Causality".

The test

Ramsey wrote that when we evaluate "if P, then Q", we add the antecedent P hypothetically to our stock of knowledge and argue on that basis about Q. In his frequently quoted formulation: "If two people are arguing 'If P, then Q?' and are both in doubt as to P, they are adding P hypothetically to their stock of knowledge and arguing on that basis about Q."

Influence

The test became a cornerstone of theories of conditionals in philosophy and of belief revision in logic and artificial intelligence. Robert Stalnaker built his semantics for conditionals partly on the idea, and the AGM theory of belief revision uses a closely related criterion: a conditional is accepted in a belief state if the consequent is believed after revising by the antecedent. The test also underlies the connection between conditional probability and the probability of conditionals explored by Ernest Adams, and it figures in discussions of the paradoxes of material implication and of subjunctive conditionals.

Criticisms

Critics note that the test may not apply uniformly to indicative and subjunctive conditionals, and that it can validate undesirable inferences when combined with certain assumptions about revision. Nevertheless it remains a standard benchmark for theories of conditionals.
