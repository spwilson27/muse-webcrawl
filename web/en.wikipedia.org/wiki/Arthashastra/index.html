The Arthashastra (Sanskrit: Arthaśāstra) is an ancient Indian Sanskrit treatise on statecraft, political science, economic policy and military strategy. Tradition attributes it to Kautilya, also identified as Chanakya or Vishnugupta, the adviser traditionally credited with founding the Maurya Empire; modern scholarship regards the surviving text as a composite work by several authors, redacted over centuries between roughly the 2nd century BCE and the 3rd century CE.

Contents

Divided into 15 books, the text covers the training and discipline of the king; the duties of ministers, officials and superintendents of every state department (mines, mints, storehouses, trade, agriculture, liquor, prostitutes, shipping); civil and criminal law; the administration of justice; foreign policy through the mandala ("circle of states") theory and the six measures of policy; espionage on a vast scale; and the conduct of war, sieges and secret operations. It prescribes taxation, market regulation, weights and measures, and forensic and accounting controls in remarkable detail.

Rediscovery

The work was lost to modern scholarship until a manuscript was identified by the librarian R. Shamasastry at the Oriental Research Institute, Mysore, in 1905; he published the Sanskrit text in 1909 and an English translation, Kautilya's Arthashastra, in 1915, transforming understanding of ancient Indian political thought.

Reputation

Often compared with Machiavelli's The Prince for its unsentimental realpolitik — it recommends spies, disinformation and assassination where useful — the Arthashastra equally stresses the king's duty to the welfare of subjects, holding that "in the happiness of his subjects lies the king's happiness". It remains a classic of political realism and public administration.
