Wicksellian cumulative process

The Wicksellian cumulative process is a theory of inflation and deflation developed by the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell, principally in his work Interest and Prices (1898). It explains sustained movements in the general price level through a gap between two interest rates: the natural rate of interest, at which saving equals investment in real terms, and the money (bank) rate of interest actually charged by banks.

Mechanism

If the bank rate is set below the natural rate, borrowing for investment becomes profitable, credit expands, demand for goods and factors of production rises, and prices increase. Because the process feeds on itself — rising prices raise expected returns and validate further borrowing — the inflation continues cumulatively so long as the gap persists, rather than self-correcting as simple quantity-theory reasoning might suggest. Conversely, if the bank rate exceeds the natural rate, credit contracts and a cumulative deflationary spiral follows.

Influence

The cumulative process was a foundational contribution to monetary macroeconomics. It influenced the Stockholm school of economics, was taken up by Keynes in his Treatise on Money (1930), and anticipated modern ideas about monetary policy transmission through real interest-rate gaps. Wicksell concluded that price stability required banks to adjust the money rate to track the unobservable natural rate, guided by movements in prices themselves.
