Five Families

The Five Families is the name given to the five major New York City organized crime families of the Italian-American Mafia (or La Cosa Nostra). The term was first used in 1931, when Salvatore Maranzano formally organized the previously warring gangs into five autonomous families, each with a fixed territory and structure. The Five Families are the Bonanno, Colombo, Gambino, Genovese, and Lucchese families. Together they dominated organized crime in New York and much of the United States through the 20th century, coordinating through the Commission, the Mafia's governing body.

History

The families emerged from the Sicilian and southern Italian criminal networks established in New York in the early 1900s. The Castellammarese War (1930–31) between the factions of Joe Masseria and Salvatore Maranzano ended with both men murdered in 1931; Maranzano briefly declared himself capo di tutti capi before Charles "Lucky" Luciano had him killed and replaced personal overlordship with the Commission. Prohibition (1920–33) had enriched the families through bootlegging; afterwards they expanded into labour racketeering, gambling, loansharking, extortion, narcotics, and infiltration of industries including construction, waste hauling, and the garment trade.

Structure

Each family follows the same pyramid: a boss (don), an underboss, a consigliere (adviser), several caporegimes (captains) each commanding a crew of soldiers ("made men" formally inducted into the Mafia), and a wider penumbra of associates who work for the family without membership. Membership is restricted to men of Italian descent and is sealed by an induction ceremony and the code of silence, omertà.

Decline

From the 1970s the families were progressively weakened by the RICO Act (1970), aggressive federal prosecution, long prison sentences, and a stream of informants and cooperating witnesses — including underbosses Sammy Gravano (Gambino, 1991) and, unprecedentedly, Bonanno boss Joseph Massino (2004). The Commission Case (1985–87) convicted the leadership of most of the families at once. All five families still exist and remain subjects of law-enforcement attention, but with far less power than at their mid-20th-century peak; the Genovese family is generally considered the most intact and disciplined.
