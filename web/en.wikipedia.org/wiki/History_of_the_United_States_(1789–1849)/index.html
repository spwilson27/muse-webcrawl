History of the United States (1789–1849)

The history of the United States from 1789 to 1849 covers the period from the inauguration of George Washington as the first president under the new Constitution through the territorial expansion and sectional tensions of the mid-nineteenth century, ending shortly after the Mexican–American War.

The Federalist era (1789–1801)

Washington's administration established the federal judiciary, the cabinet system, and — under Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton — a national bank and the assumption of state debts. Political parties emerged: Hamilton's Federalists and Thomas Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans. Tensions with revolutionary France and the Alien and Sedition Acts (1798) marred John Adams's presidency before the peaceful transfer of power in the "Revolution of 1800".

Jefferson and expansion (1801–1817)

Thomas Jefferson doubled the nation's size with the Louisiana Purchase (1803) and commissioned the Lewis and Clark Expedition. Trade conflicts with Britain and France culminated in the War of 1812 under James Madison, which ended in the status quo ante but boosted American nationalism and destroyed Federalist influence.

Era of Good Feelings and Jacksonian democracy

James Monroe's presidency (1817–1825) proclaimed the Monroe Doctrine. The election of Andrew Jackson in 1828 ushered in mass democracy, the spoils system, the Nullification Crisis with South Carolina, the Bank War, and the forced removal of Native American peoples along the Trail of Tears under the Indian Removal Act of 1830.

Westward expansion and war with Mexico

The ideology of Manifest Destiny drove annexation of Texas (1845), the Oregon Treaty with Britain (1846), and the Mexican–American War (1846–1848), which added California and the Southwest to the United States. The discovery of gold in California in 1848 set off the California Gold Rush. The question of whether slavery would expand into the new territories dominated politics and set the stage for the coming sectional crisis.
