Blood glucose monitoring is the ongoing measurement of blood sugar (glucose) levels, most commonly performed by people with diabetes mellitus to guide treatment decisions such as insulin dosing, food intake and exercise. Regular monitoring helps maintain glycaemic control and reduce the risk of both acute complications (hypoglycaemia, diabetic ketoacidosis) and long-term complications of diabetes.

Methods

Fingerstick testing

In self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG), a small drop of blood obtained by pricking the fingertip with a lancet is applied to a disposable test strip inserted in a glucose meter. The strip contains enzymes (glucose oxidase or glucose dehydrogenase) whose reaction with glucose generates an electrical current proportional to the glucose concentration; results appear within seconds. Meters are regulated for accuracy, with standards generally requiring most results within ±15% of laboratory values.

Continuous glucose monitoring

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) uses a small sensor inserted under the skin to measure glucose in interstitial fluid every few minutes, transmitting readings to a receiver or smartphone. CGMs report trends, provide alarms for high and low glucose, and reduce the need for fingersticks. Metrics such as "time in range" (the percentage of time spent between 70 and 180 mg/dL) are derived from CGM data. Real-time CGMs are widely used with insulin pumps in automated insulin delivery ("artificial pancreas") systems.

Laboratory and other measures

Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) testing reflects average glucose over roughly three months and complements day-to-day monitoring. Hospital and laboratory plasma glucose measurements, flash glucose monitoring, and urine glucose testing (now largely obsolete) are also used in specific settings.

Clinical use

Monitoring frequency is individualised: people with type 1 diabetes or those on intensive insulin regimens test most frequently or use CGM, while the benefit of routine SMBG in type 2 diabetes not treated with insulin is smaller and debated. Structured monitoring around meals, exercise, illness and driving is recommended to detect hypoglycaemia and guide therapy.
