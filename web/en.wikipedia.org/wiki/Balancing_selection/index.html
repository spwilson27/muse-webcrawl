Balancing selection

Balancing selection refers to a number of selective processes by which multiple alleles (different versions of a gene) are actively maintained in the gene pool of a population at frequencies greater than expected from genetic drift alone. Unlike directional selection, which favours one allele until it is fixed, balancing selection preserves genetic polymorphism — sometimes for millions of years, so that the same alleles may persist across speciation events (trans-species polymorphism).

Mechanisms

Heterozygote advantage

When heterozygotes have higher fitness than either homozygote (overdominance), both alleles are maintained. The classic example is sickle-cell trait: individuals heterozygous for the sickle-cell haemoglobin allele are resistant to malaria, while one homozygote suffers severe sickle-cell anaemia and the other lacks the protection — a balance that keeps the allele common in malaria-endemic regions.

Frequency-dependent selection

In negative frequency-dependent selection, an allele's fitness decreases as it becomes more common, so rare types are always favoured. This mechanism operates in host–parasite coevolution, predator search images (apostatic selection), and the self-incompatibility systems of plants, which prevent self-fertilisation and maintain dozens of alleles at a single locus.

Spatially and temporally varying selection

Selection that favours different alleles in different habitats, seasons, or years can also preserve variation, provided migration or storage effects (such as seed banks or overlapping generations) link the environments.

Examples

The best-documented cases occur in the immune system. The major histocompatibility complex (MHC) genes of vertebrates and their human counterparts, the HLA genes, are among the most polymorphic loci known, maintained by pathogen-driven balancing selection. Plant disease-resistance (R) genes and the ABO blood group polymorphisms also show signatures of long-term balancing selection.

Detection

Genomic evidence for balancing selection includes elevated polymorphism around the selected site, an excess of intermediate-frequency alleles (positive Tajima's D), deep coalescence times, and alleles shared between species. Distinguishing these signals from population history is a central problem in population genetics.
