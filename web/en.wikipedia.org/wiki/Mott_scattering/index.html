Mott scattering

Elastic scattering of an electron by an atomic nucleus

Mott scattering, also referred to as spin–orbit coupling scattering, is the elastic scattering of a charged spin-1/2 particle, usually an electron, from a massive charged target, typically an atomic nucleus. It is named after Nevill Mott, who derived the relativistic cross-section in 1929.

The Mott formula

Mott scattering generalises Rutherford scattering by including the effects of special relativity and the electron's spin and magnetic moment. The resulting Mott cross-section contains a factor (1 − β² sin²(θ/2)) relative to the Rutherford result, which suppresses backscattering for relativistic electrons. The formula is valid when the target is treated as a point charge; departures from it reveal the finite size and internal structure of the nucleus, parametrised by form factors.

Applications

Because the scattered electron's behaviour depends on its spin polarisation, Mott scattering is used in Mott polarimeters to measure the polarisation of electron beams. It is also important in electron microscopy and in the interpretation of electron–nucleus scattering experiments in nuclear and particle physics.
