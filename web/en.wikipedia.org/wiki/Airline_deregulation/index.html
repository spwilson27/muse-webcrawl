Airline deregulation is the process of removing government-imposed entry and price restrictions on airlines, so that carriers may compete freely on routes and fares. The landmark case is the United States, where the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 phased out the Civil Aeronautics Board's control over routes and fares, which it had exercised since 1938.

Background

Under regulation, airlines required government approval to enter or leave routes and to set prices, which limited competition. Economists argued that intrastate airlines operating outside federal control, such as those in California and Texas, offered markedly lower fares. Reformers including Senator Edward Kennedy and economist Alfred Kahn, appointed to head the Civil Aeronautics Board, pressed the case for change.

Effects

Deregulation produced a wave of new entrants, the spread of hub-and-spoke networks, frequent-flyer programmes and substantial long-run declines in average real fares, alongside greatly increased passenger numbers. It also brought bankruptcies of established carriers, labour disputes, industry consolidation into a few large network airlines, and the growth of low-cost carriers.

Deregulation worldwide

Other countries and blocs followed with their own liberalisation programmes: Canada in the 1980s, the European Union's phased single aviation market in the 1990s, Australia, India and much of Asia and Latin America, as well as "open skies" agreements between states.
