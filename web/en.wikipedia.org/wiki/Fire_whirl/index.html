Fire whirl

A fire whirl or fire devil is a whirlwind induced by fire, often made up of flame and ash. Fire whirls most commonly form during wildfires and firestorms, when intense rising heat and turbulent winds combine into a rotating column of air that entrains burning debris.

Formation

A fire whirl begins when a column of superheated air rising above a fire starts to rotate, drawing in flames and combustible gases. As the column stretches vertically, conservation of angular momentum intensifies the spin. Most fire whirls are only a few metres wide and last a few minutes, but large ones during firestorms can reach tens of metres across with winds rivaling a tornado.

Notable events

During the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake, a giant fire whirl in Tokyo killed an estimated 38,000 people in fifteen minutes, the deadliest single event of that disaster. The 2018 Carr Fire in California generated a vortex with winds estimated at 143 mph (EF3 tornado strength), which destroyed buildings and killed a firefighter — an event often called a "firenado".

Related phenomena

Fire whirls are related to dust devils, which form in clear weather over heated ground, and to the swirling vortices that can appear in large volcanic eruptions and mass fires such as the bombing firestorms of the Second World War.
