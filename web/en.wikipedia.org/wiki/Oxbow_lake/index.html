Oxbow lake

An oxbow lake is a U-shaped lake or pool that forms when a wide meander of a river is cut off, creating a free-standing body of water. The term "oxbow" refers to the U-shaped bow of the yoke used with oxen. Oxbow lakes are common along mature, low-gradient rivers such as the Mississippi and the Amazon.

Formation

As a river meanders, erosion on the outer bank and deposition on the inner bank cause the bend to migrate and tighten. During a flood, the river may cut through the narrow neck of the meander (a neck cutoff or chute cutoff), taking the straighter, steeper course. Sediment then seals the ends of the abandoned bend, isolating it as a crescent-shaped lake. Over time the lake silts up, becoming a marsh or meander scar and eventually dry land.

Ecology and significance

Oxbow lakes are rich wetland habitats for fish, amphibians and waterbirds, acting as nurseries and refuges during floods. They also serve archaeologically as records of former channels. Examples include Carter Lake, Iowa — an oxbow of the Missouri River that left a piece of Iowa on the Nebraska side of the river — and the many billabongs of Australia, which are oxbow lakes of ephemeral rivers.
