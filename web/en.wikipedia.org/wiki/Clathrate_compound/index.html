A cage-like host lattice of a clathrate compound, trapping a guest molecule inside.

A clathrate is a chemical substance consisting of a lattice that traps or contains molecules. The word clathrate is derived from the Latin clathratus (κλᾶθρον), meaning "with bars, latticed". Most clathrate compounds are polymeric and completely envelop the guest molecule, but in modern usage clathrates also include host–guest complexes and inclusion compounds. Clathrates can be divided into molecular clathrates and polymeric clathrates depending on their structure and bonding.

History and materials

Gas hydrates — clathrates of water with small gas molecules such as methane — were first observed by Humphry Davy, who reported chlorine hydrate in 1810–1811. Michael Faraday studied chlorine clathrate hydrate in 1823, and in 1922 Waser and Schwyzer determined the first structure of a clathrate. The hydroquinone clathrates studied by D. E. Palin and H. M. Powell in the 1940s established the classic cage structures.

Important clathrate families include:

  • Clathrate hydrates — ice-like frameworks of water molecules enclosing guests such as CH4, CO2 or H2S. Methane clathrates occur in vast quantities beneath ocean sediments and in permafrost.
  • Intermetallic clathrates — silicon, germanium and tin frameworks (e.g. Ba8Ga16Ge30) studied as thermoelectric materials under the "phonon glass–electron crystal" concept.
  • Molecular clathrates — hosts such as hydroquinone, urea, cyclodextrins and calixarenes that enclose guests in cavities.

Applications and significance

Methane hydrates are a potentially enormous unconventional energy resource, but also a climate concern, since destabilisation could release methane, a potent greenhouse gas. Clathrate formation blocks oil and gas pipelines ("hydrate plugs"), a major problem in flow assurance. Conversely, clathrate chemistry is exploited in gas storage and separation, desalination research, and thermoelectric devices.
