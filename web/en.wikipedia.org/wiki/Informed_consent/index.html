Informed consent

Informed consent is the process in which a health care provider educates a patient about the risks, benefits, and alternatives of a given procedure or intervention, after which the patient voluntarily agrees to undergo it. The concept also applies in research, where participants must be informed about the nature and risks of a study before agreeing to take part, and in other contexts where one party must understand and accept the consequences of an action before it proceeds. Informed consent is grounded in the ethical principle of respect for autonomy and in legal doctrines protecting bodily integrity.

Elements

Valid informed consent generally requires that the person be given relevant information about the proposed intervention, including its nature, purpose, foreseeable risks and benefits, and reasonable alternatives. The person must have the capacity (competence) to make the decision, must understand the information, and must decide voluntarily, without coercion or manipulation. Consent is commonly documented by a signed form, but the form is evidence of the process rather than a substitute for it.

History

The doctrine developed through a series of legal cases and responses to research abuses. The term "informed consent" gained prominence after the Nuremberg trials, which exposed Nazi medical experiments and produced the Nuremberg Code (1947), whose first principle states that the voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential. Revelations about studies such as the Tuskegee syphilis study, in which treatment was withheld from participants without their knowledge, led to stronger protections, including the Belmont Report (1979) in the United States and the requirement of institutional review board (IRB) oversight of research.

In clinical law, the 1914 case Schloendorff v. Society of New York Hospital articulated the principle that every adult of sound mind has the right to determine what shall be done with his or her own body. Later cases, such as Canterbury v. Spence (1972), established standards for how much a physician must disclose, measured by what a reasonable patient would want to know.

Exceptions and limitations

Exceptions to the requirement include emergencies where immediate treatment is needed to save life or prevent serious harm and consent cannot be obtained, patients who lack decision-making capacity (for whom a surrogate decides), and rare invocations of therapeutic privilege. Children generally cannot give legal consent; a parent or guardian consents on their behalf, though the child's assent is sought where appropriate.

In research

In human-subjects research, informed consent is regulated by frameworks such as the U.S. Common Rule. Participants must be told about the study's purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, confidentiality protections, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Particular care is required with vulnerable populations, including children, prisoners, and persons with cognitive impairments.
