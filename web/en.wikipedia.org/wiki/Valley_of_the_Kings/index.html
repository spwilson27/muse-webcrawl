The Valley of the Kings is a wadi on the west bank of the Nile opposite Luxor (ancient Thebes) where, for nearly 500 years during Egypt's New Kingdom (c. 1539–1075 BC), the pharaohs and powerful nobles of the 18th, 19th and 20th Dynasties were buried in rock-cut tombs. Abandoning the conspicuous pyramids of earlier ages, the kings chose this secluded valley beneath the pyramid-shaped peak of al-Qurn, sacred to the goddess Meretseger, hoping — largely in vain — that concealment would protect them from tomb robbers.

Tombs

Sixty-five tombs and chambers are catalogued with the prefix KV. Their corridors descend through painted halls decorated with funerary texts — the Amduat, the Book of Gates, the Litany of Ra — mapping the sun god's nightly journey through the underworld. Notable tombs include KV17 of Seti I, the longest and most finely decorated; KV9, begun for Ramesses V and usurped by Ramesses VI; and KV34 of Thutmose III, one of the earliest. The workforce lived in the village now called Deir el-Medina.

Robbery and rediscovery

Nearly all the tombs were robbed in antiquity; in the late New Kingdom priests gathered the royal mummies into caches (at Deir el-Bahari and in the tomb of Amenhotep II) for safekeeping. European exploration began in the Napoleonic era, and Giovanni Belzoni discovered Seti I's tomb in 1817. The valley's fame rests on Howard Carter and Lord Carnarvon's discovery in November 1922 of KV62, the tomb of Tutankhamun — the only royal tomb found substantially intact, its treasure of gold and furniture becoming a global sensation.

Tourism and conservation

The valley is one of Egypt's premier tourist sites, and a rotating selection of tombs is open to visitors. Conservation challenges include humidity and carbon dioxide from visitors, flash flooding, and the cracking of the soft rock, and ongoing surveys (notably the Theban Mapping Project) continue to document the necropolis.
