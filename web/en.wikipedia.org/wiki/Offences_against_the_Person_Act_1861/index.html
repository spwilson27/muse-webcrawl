The Offences Against the Person Act 1861 (24 & 25 Vict. c. 100) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that consolidated the law relating to offences against the person in England and Wales and Ireland. Although much of it has been repealed, several of its provisions — notably those on grievous bodily harm — remain central to the criminal law of England and Wales.

Key provisions

Section 18 creates the offence of wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent, punishable with life imprisonment. Section 20 covers unlawfully and maliciously wounding or inflicting grievous bodily harm. Section 47 creates the offence of assault occasioning actual bodily harm. Other surviving provisions deal with assaults on police officers, child cruelty by exposure or abandonment, and administering poison. Sections 58 and 59 criminalised abortion; in England and Wales their effect was modified by the Abortion Act 1967, while in Northern Ireland the provisions were repealed in 2019.

History and reform

The Act was one of the great Victorian consolidation statutes associated with Robert Peel's earlier reforms and the Criminal Law Consolidation Acts of 1861. The Law Commission has repeatedly criticised the Act's archaic language and recommended its replacement; draft reform bills were published in 1998 and again proposed by the Law Commission in 2015, but the Act remains substantially in force.
