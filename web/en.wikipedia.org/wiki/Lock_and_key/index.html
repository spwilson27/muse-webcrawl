A lock is a mechanical or electronic fastening device that is released by a physical object (such as a key, keycard, fingerprint, RFID card, security token), by supplying secret information (such as a number or letter permutation or password), or by a combination thereof. A key is a device that operates a lock and is used to lock and unlock it. Most keys are made of metal and have a shaped blade whose cuts or bitting correspond to the internal mechanism of a specific lock, so that only the correct key aligns the tumblers and turns the plug.

History

Locks operated by keys are more than 4,000 years old: wooden pin locks were used in ancient Egypt and in the palace of Khorsabad in Assyria, where a large wooden key lifted pins so a bolt could be withdrawn. Roman locksmiths developed metal locks and keys small and ornate enough to be worn as rings, along with warded locks whose fixed internal obstructions ("wards") block wrong keys. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, a series of English and American inventions — Barron's double-acting lever lock (1778), Bramah's safety lock (1784), Chubb's detector lock (1818) and Linus Yale Jr.'s pin tumbler cylinder lock (1861–1865) — produced the mechanisms that dominate to this day.

Types of locks

  • Pin tumbler lock — key pins and spring-loaded driver pins must be aligned at the shear line by the key's cuts
  • Wafer tumbler lock — flat wafers; common in cabinets and older cars
  • Lever tumbler lock — a set of levers lifted to precise heights; used in safes and many UK doors
  • Warded lock — relies on obstructions only; offers low security
  • Tubular, disc-detainer, combination and time locks
  • Electronic locks — keycards, PIN pads, biometrics and smartphone credentials

Keys and key control

Keys are cut by locksmiths and hardware stores from blanks using key-cutting machines that copy an existing key or decode the lock's bitting. Restricted keyways and patented blanks limit unauthorised duplication, and master key systems arrange locks so that individual keys open one lock while a master key opens many. The security of key locks is challenged by lock picking, bumping, impressioning and destructive entry; high-security cylinders add sidebars, magnetic elements and hardened inserts to resist attack.

Symbolism

"Under lock and key" denotes secure custody, and keys figure widely in heraldry and symbolism — the crossed keys of Saint Peter, the ceremonial keys of a city, and the "key to the city" honour.
