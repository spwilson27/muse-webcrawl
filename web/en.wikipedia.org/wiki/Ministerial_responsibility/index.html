Ministerial responsibility
Constitutional convention in Westminster systems
Forms: Individual and collective

Ministerial responsibility is a constitutional convention in governments using the Westminster system under which ministers are accountable to the legislature for their actions and for those of their departments. It has two main forms: collective responsibility, under which the whole government stands or falls together and ministers must publicly support cabinet decisions, and individual responsibility, under which a minister answers for the conduct of their ministry.

Individual responsibility

Under the individual doctrine, a minister is expected to explain and defend their department's policies and mistakes in parliament, and may be expected to resign for serious personal misconduct or major departmental failure. The classic statement is associated with the 1954 Crichel Down affair in Britain, after which a minister resigned over errors by officials, though scholars debate how strictly the convention has ever applied to purely administrative failings.

Collective responsibility

Collective ministerial responsibility requires cabinet members to support government policy in public or resign; it underpins votes of confidence, in which defeat on a major measure can bring down the government.
