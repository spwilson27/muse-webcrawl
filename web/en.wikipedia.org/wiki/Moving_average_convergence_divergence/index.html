Moving average convergence divergence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

Moving average convergence divergence (MACD) is a trend-following momentum indicator used in technical analysis of stock and other asset prices. It was developed by Gerald Appel in the late 1970s and shows the relationship between two exponential moving averages (EMAs) of a security's price. The MACD is calculated by subtracting the 26-period EMA from the 12-period EMA.

Components

The indicator has three main elements. The MACD line is the 12-period EMA minus the 26-period EMA. The signal line is a 9-period EMA of the MACD line itself, plotted on top of it. The histogram plots the difference between the MACD line and the signal line, making turning points easier to see. Because it is derived from moving averages — lagging indicators — the MACD tends to follow trends rather than anticipate them, and it is unbounded, oscillating around zero rather than within a fixed range.

Interpretation

Traders watch for signal-line crossovers: when the MACD crosses above the signal line, the indicator suggests buying momentum, and vice versa. Divergence — when the price makes a new high or low that the MACD fails to confirm — is read as a warning that the trend is weakening. Crossings of the zero line show the short-term average passing the long-term average. MACD generally works best in trending markets and can give false signals in sideways trading. Thomas Aspray later added the histogram, and Gerald Appel's original parameters (12, 26, 9) remain the standard settings.
