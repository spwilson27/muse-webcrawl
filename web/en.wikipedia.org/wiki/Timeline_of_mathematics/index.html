Timeline of mathematics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This is a timeline of pure and applied mathematics history. It is divided here into slanted sections, each of which stretches over many millennia, from prehistory to the present day.

Prehistory and ancient world

PeriodDevelopment
c. 35,000 BCThe Lebombo bone, a baboon fibula with tally marks, is notched in Swaziland.
c. 20,000 BCThe Ishango bone, found near Lake Edward, bears grouped tally marks that some interpret as early counting or lunar reckoning.
c. 3400 BCThe Sumerians develop a sexagesimal (base-60) numeral system, later inherited by Babylonian mathematics.
c. 2000 BCBabylonian scribes solve quadratic equations and tabulate Pythagorean triples, as on the tablet Plimpton 322.
c. 1650 BCThe Egyptian Rhind Mathematical Papyrus records arithmetic, geometry and unit-fraction methods.
c. 600 BCThales of Miletus is credited with the first deductive theorems in geometry.
c. 300 BCEuclid compiles the Elements, the foundational axiomatic treatment of geometry.
c. 250 BCArchimedes approximates pi and develops methods anticipating integral calculus.

Medieval developments

Around 628 AD the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta gave the first systematic rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. In the 9th century, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi's treatise on al-jabr founded algebra as a discipline, and his name gave rise to the word "algorithm". Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) introduced Hindu–Arabic numerals to Europe.

Early modern era

The 17th century saw Descartes' analytic geometry (1637), Fermat's and Pascal's founding of probability theory, and the independent invention of calculus by Newton and Leibniz in the 1670s–1680s. Euler dominated the 18th century, standardising much modern notation.

19th and 20th centuries

Gauss, Riemann and others developed non-Euclidean geometry and modern number theory. Cantor founded set theory in the 1870s; Hilbert posed his 23 problems in 1900. Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) reshaped the foundations of mathematics, and Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994. The classification of finite simple groups, completed in the early 2000s, remains one of the largest collaborative proofs in history.
