Response surface methodology

Response surface methodology (RSM) explores the relationships between several explanatory variables and one or more response variables. The method was introduced by George E. P. Box and K. B. Wilson in 1951. The main idea of RSM is to use a sequence of designed experiments to obtain an optimal response. Box and Wilson suggested using a second-degree polynomial model to do this; they acknowledged that this model is only an approximation, but used it because such a model is easy to estimate and apply, even when little is known about the process.

Basic approach

Response surface methodology uses statistical models, and therefore practitioners need to be aware that even the best statistical model is an approximation to reality. In practice both the models and the parameter values are unknown, and subject to uncertainty on top of ignorance. Of course, an estimated optimum need not be optimum in reality, because of the errors in estimates and inadequacies of the model.

Nonetheless, response surface methodology has an effective track-record of helping industrial engineers improve products and processes. For example, Box's original response-surface modeling enabled chemical engineers to improve a process that had been stuck at a saddle point for years. The engineers had not been able to afford to fit a cubic three-dimensional model, and their biased linear models estimated the gradient to be zero. Box's design reduced the costs of original experiments so that a model with a quadratic form could be fit, which led to a (long-sought) ascent direction.

Optimal designs

Optimal designs for response surface models depend on the model to be fit, on the region of operability and the region of interest, and on the total number of runs. Common experimental designs used in RSM include central composite designs and Box–Behnken designs. The central composite design, introduced by Box and Wilson, combines a factorial or fractional factorial design with center points and a set of axial (star) points that allow estimation of curvature.

Applications

RSM is used extensively in industrial experimentation, chemical engineering, food science, and other fields where a small number of influential factors affect a measurable performance quality. It is closely related to the design of experiments and to regression analysis.
