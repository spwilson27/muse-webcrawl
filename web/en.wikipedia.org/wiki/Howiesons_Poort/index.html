Howiesons Poort

Backed stone artefacts characteristic of the Howiesons Poort.

Howiesons Poort (also spelled Howieson's Poort) is a Middle Stone Age lithic technology or "industry" in southern Africa, named after the Howiesons Poort rock shelter near Grahamstown (Makhanda) in the Eastern Cape, South Africa, where it was first described in the 1920s. Dated to roughly 65,000–60,000 years ago, it is one of the earliest known examples of a backed-blade (microlithic) technology and is widely discussed as evidence for modern human behaviour in the African Middle Stone Age.

Characteristics

The industry is defined by small, carefully shaped stone segments — crescents, trapezoids and backed blades — produced mainly on fine-grained materials such as silcrete and hornfels, often with the aid of heat treatment. Residue and use-wear studies indicate that many of these segments were hafted with compound adhesives (mixtures of ochre, resin and other materials) onto wooden handles to form composite tools, probably including the tips and barbs of arrows or spears. Associated finds at some sites include engraved ochre and perforated shell beads.

Significance

Howiesons Poort assemblages occur at major sites including Klasies River, Diepkloof Rock Shelter, Sibudu Cave and Border Cave. The industry appears abruptly, flourishes for a few thousand years, and then disappears, replaced by more expedient technologies — a pattern sometimes called the "Howiesons Poort enigma". Its sophisticated hafting technology and symbolic artefacts are central to debates about when and how behaviourally modern cognition emerged among early Homo sapiens.
