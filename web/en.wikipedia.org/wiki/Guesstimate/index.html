Guesstimate

A guesstimate is an estimate made without using complete information, or an estimate formed by a mixture of guesswork and calculation. The word is a portmanteau of "guess" and "estimate" and entered American English in the 1930s.

Guesstimates are common in business, engineering and policy, where decisions must be made before precise data are available. Related techniques include Fermi problems, in which a quantity is broken down into factors that can each be approximated, often producing a surprisingly accurate result. Books such as Douglas Hubbard's How to Measure Anything argue that even rough, structured estimation greatly improves decision-making compared with intuition alone.

While sometimes used dismissively, a guesstimate differs from a pure guess in that it incorporates whatever partial information and reasoning is available.
