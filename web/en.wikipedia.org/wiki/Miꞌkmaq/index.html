Miꞌkmaq

The Miꞌkmaq (also spelled Micmac, Mi'kmaq or Mi'gmaq) are a First Nations people of the Atlantic coast of Canada and the northeastern United States. Their traditional territory, Miꞌkmaꞌki, encompasses present-day Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, the Gaspé Peninsula of Quebec, and parts of New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Maine. The Miꞌkmaq language is an Eastern Algonquian language.

Territory and governance

Miꞌkmaꞌki was traditionally divided into districts, each with its own chief, overseen by a Grand Chief and the Grand Council (Santeꞌ Mawioꞌmi). The Miꞌkmaq were a maritime people, skilled in canoe travel, fishing — including for salmon, cod and eel — and hunting moose and caribou. Their birchbark canoes and, later, distinctive wooden crafts such as baskets and quillwork were widely traded.

History

The Miꞌkmaq were among the first Indigenous peoples in North America to have sustained contact with Europeans, trading furs with French fishermen and settlers from the early 1500s. They formed a long alliance with France and were members of the Wabanaki Confederacy. Treaties of peace and friendship signed with the British Crown in the 18th century (1725–1779) are still invoked in Canadian courts; the Supreme Court of Canada's 1999 decision in R v Marshall affirmed Miꞌkmaq treaty rights to a moderate livelihood from fishing, which has led to ongoing negotiation and occasional conflict over fisheries.

Language and culture

The Miꞌkmaq language was historically written in a distinctive hieroglyphic script (komqwejwiꞌkasikl) adapted by missionaries, and is today written in several Latin-based orthographies. Despite pressures from residential schools and assimilation policies, revitalisation efforts — immersion schools, digital resources and community teaching — support a growing number of speakers. The Miꞌkmaq flag, the Grand Council's role, and annual gatherings such as those at Chapel Island (Potlotek) remain central to cultural life.
