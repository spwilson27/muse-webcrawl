Werewolf


The werewolf of folklore transforms under the full moon.

A werewolf (from Old English werwulf, "man-wolf") is a mythological and folkloric human with the ability to shapeshift into a wolf or wolf-like creature, either purposely or after being placed under a curse or the bite or scratch of another werewolf. The condition, called lycanthropy, is a central figure of European folklore; beliefs about werewolves developed in parallel with, and sometimes combined with, witchcraft beliefs during the late medieval and early modern periods, when werewolf trials took place in several European countries.

Folklore and beliefs

Traditions varied widely: in some regions the transformation was said to occur at the full moon, in others the werewolf could change at will, sometimes by donning a wolfskin belt or rubbing on a magical salve. Common motifs include vulnerability to silver, the werewolf returning to human form at dawn, and wounds inflicted on the wolf appearing on the human body. Ancient antecedents include the story of Lycaon in Greek mythology, whom Zeus transformed into a wolf.

In popular culture

Werewolves became staples of horror fiction and film, notably in movies such as The Wolf Man (1941), which codified modern conventions including the full moon transformation and silver bullets, and they remain popular in literature, television, games, and role-playing settings.

Clinical lycanthropy, a rare psychiatric delusion in which a person believes they can transform into an animal, takes its name from the legend.
