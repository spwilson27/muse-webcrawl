Thrombin

Thrombin (EC 3.4.21.5, historically also called fibrinogenase) is a serine protease enzyme that in humans is encoded by the F2 gene. Thrombin is the central enzyme of blood coagulation: it is generated from its zymogen prothrombin (factor II) by activated factor X (factor Xa) in the presence of factor Va, calcium and phospholipid, and it converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble strands of fibrin, forming the mesh of a blood clot together with the transglutaminase factor XIII.

Functions

Besides cleaving fibrinogen, thrombin activates factors V, VIII, XI and XIII, providing positive feedback that accelerates its own generation; activates platelets; and, when bound to thrombomodulin on intact endothelium, activates protein C, an anticoagulant pathway — so thrombin has both procoagulant and anticoagulant roles. It also acts as a signalling molecule through protease-activated receptors (PARs) on many cell types.

Regulation and clinical relevance

Thrombin activity is controlled by antithrombin (whose action is greatly accelerated by heparin), by the protein C system, and by direct inhibitors. Excess thrombin generation underlies thrombosis; the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran is used as an anticoagulant, and hirudin from leeches is the prototypical natural inhibitor. Purified thrombin is also used clinically as a topical haemostatic agent in surgery, and the "thrombin time" laboratory test measures its action on fibrinogen.
