Agglomeration economies

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cities such as New York exemplify the benefits and costs of agglomeration

Agglomeration economies are the benefits that firms obtain from locating near each other and near large concentrations of population. They help explain why cities exist and why certain industries cluster in particular places, from financial services in London and New York to technology firms in Silicon Valley. The concept is central to urban economics and economic geography.

Mechanisms

Following Alfred Marshall's analysis of industrial districts, economists usually group the sources of agglomeration economies into three channels, sometimes called the Marshallian externalities:

  • Labour market pooling: a thick local labour market reduces search costs for employers and workers and provides insurance against firm-specific shocks.
  • Input sharing: clusters support specialized suppliers and shared infrastructure that no single firm could sustain alone.
  • Knowledge spillovers: proximity facilitates the exchange of ideas, imitation, and innovation.

Localization and urbanization economies

Localization economies arise from the concentration of firms within the same industry, while urbanization economies arise from the overall size and diversity of a city, benefiting firms across industries. There are also offsetting "agglomeration diseconomies" — congestion, high rents, and pollution — so that the net benefit of concentration rises and then falls with city size, a balance studied in the new economic geography associated with Paul Krugman.
