The Mosque of Muhammad Ali is a mosque situated in the Citadel of Cairo in Egypt. It was commissioned by Muhammad Ali Pasha, the ruler of Egypt in the first half of the 19th century, between 1830 and 1848. It is commonly known as the Alabaster Mosque because of the extensive use of alabaster in its construction.

History

Muhammad Ali, having consolidated his power in Egypt — including through the massacre of the Mamluks in the Citadel in 1811 — built the mosque on the site of former Mamluk palaces, choosing an Ottoman style in deliberate defiance of his nominal overlords in Istanbul. Construction took place from 1830 until 1848, under the direction of the architect Yusuf Bushnak. Muhammad Ali died in 1849 and was buried in a tomb carved from Carrara marble in the mosque's courtyard.

Architecture

The mosque is modelled on Istanbul's Ottoman imperial mosques, such as the Sultan Ahmed Mosque. Its great central dome is 21 metres in diameter and 52 metres high, flanked by four semi-domes and four smaller corner domes. The two slender minarets, in the Ottoman pencil style, rise 82 metres, the tallest in Egypt. The courtyard features an ornate ablutions fountain, and the famous clock tower in the courtyard was a gift from King Louis-Philippe of France in 1845, given in exchange for the Luxor obelisk now standing in the Place de la Concorde in Paris.

Significance

Perched at the summit of the Citadel, the mosque is the most visible mosque in Cairo and one of the city's most prominent landmarks, visible across much of the old city.
