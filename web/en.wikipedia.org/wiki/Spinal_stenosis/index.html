Spinal stenosis

Spinal stenosis is an abnormal narrowing of the spinal canal or neural foramina that places pressure on the spinal cord or nerve roots. It most commonly results from age-related degenerative changes — osteoarthritis, thickened ligaments, bulging discs and bone spurs — and usually affects the lumbar (lower back) or cervical (neck) spine. It is entirely different in cause from spinal muscular atrophy, a genetic motor-neuron disease.

Symptoms

Lumbar stenosis classically produces neurogenic claudication: pain, numbness or weakness in the legs and buttocks brought on by walking or standing and relieved by sitting or bending forward (the "shopping cart sign"). Cervical stenosis can compress the cord itself, causing myelopathy with gait disturbance and hand clumsiness.

Treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by MRI. Initial management is conservative — exercise, physiotherapy, analgesia and sometimes epidural steroid injections. For persistent or severe symptoms, surgical decompression such as laminectomy, with or without spinal fusion, is a common and generally effective option in older adults.
