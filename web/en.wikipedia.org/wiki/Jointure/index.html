Jointure

Jointure, in English common law, was a form of land tenure historically used to make provision for a wife, typically in connection with marriage. A jointure was a joint estate granted for the lives of the husband and wife, or, more commonly in practice, a separate estate settled on the wife for her life, to take effect after the husband's death, in lieu of dower. The word derives from the French joindre, "to join".

Historical development

Before the Statute of Uses (1535), a jointure typically consisted of land conveyed to trustees to the use of the husband and wife jointly for their lives, with the survivor taking the whole. Because such interests were equitable "uses", the courts of equity enforced them even though common law did not recognise them directly.

The Statute of Uses converted uses into legal estates, and the jointure developed into the standard mechanism by which a husband's family provided for a widow. A jointure settled before marriage, if expressed to be in satisfaction of dower, barred the widow's common-law dower rights under the statute, provided it consisted of lands of freehold tenure taking effect immediately on the husband's death and was for the wife's own life at least.

Jointure and dower

At common law a widow was entitled to dower — a life interest in one third of the lands of which her husband had been seised during the marriage. Dower was uncertain and inconvenient, since it attached to lands the husband might have alienated. A jointure, by contrast, gave the widow a defined income, commonly charged on specified lands, and was therefore preferred by landed families making marriage settlements.

If a jointure was made after marriage, the widow could elect between the jointure and her dower. A jointure made before marriage was generally an absolute bar to dower, though equity would relieve where the jointure was obtained by fraud or proved illusory.

Modern position

The institution became obsolete in England following property law reforms of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, including the Married Women's Property Acts and the Law of Property Act 1925, which abolished dower and transformed the law of settlements. The term survives mainly in historical and literary contexts, where a widow's "jointure" is frequently mentioned in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century novels as the measure of her independence.
