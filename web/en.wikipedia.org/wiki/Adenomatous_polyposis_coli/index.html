Adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) is a protein that in humans is encoded by the APC gene, a tumor suppressor gene located on the long arm of chromosome 5. The APC protein is a negative regulator of beta-catenin and participates in cell adhesion, signal transduction, and the organization of the cytoskeleton. Mutations in APC are among the earliest and most common genetic events in colorectal cancer.

Function

APC forms a "destruction complex" together with the proteins axin, glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3β), and casein kinase 1. In the absence of Wnt signaling, this complex phosphorylates beta-catenin, marking it for ubiquitination and degradation by the proteasome, thereby preventing it from entering the nucleus and activating transcription of growth-promoting genes such as MYC and CCND1 (cyclin D1). When APC is lost or truncated, beta-catenin accumulates and drives constitutive expression of these targets, a hallmark of colorectal tumorigenesis.

Clinical significance

Germline mutations in APC cause familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), an autosomal dominant syndrome in which hundreds to thousands of adenomatous polyps develop in the colon and rectum during adolescence and early adulthood; without prophylactic colectomy, progression to colorectal cancer is nearly inevitable. Milder, later-onset disease is known as attenuated FAP. Somatic APC mutations are found in the great majority of sporadic colorectal cancers and are widely regarded as the initiating "gatekeeper" lesion of the adenoma–carcinoma sequence. Specific mutations in the gene's "mutation cluster region" correlate with the severity of polyposis; certain alleles are also associated with desmoid tumors and, in the historical Gardner syndrome, with extracolonic features such as osteomas and dental abnormalities.

Interactions

Beyond the destruction complex, APC binds microtubules and the plus-end tracking protein EB1, influencing cell polarity and chromosomal segregation during mitosis; loss of these functions may contribute to the chromosomal instability seen in APC-deficient tumors. The protein also interacts with the adherens junction component beta-catenin at the cell membrane, linking signaling and adhesion roles.
