Milled coinage

Milled coinage refers to coins struck by machinery — a mill or press — rather than by hand hammering. Machine striking, introduced progressively in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, produced coins of uniform diameter and weight, typically with reeded or lettered edges that discouraged clipping.

History

Early experiments included Leonardo da Vinci's designs and the screw presses used in Augsburg and at the Segovia mint in Spain. In France, Jean Varin introduced milled coinage in the 1640s; England adopted it permanently with the Great Recoinage of 1696, when Isaac Newton was Warden of the Mint. The milled edges with inscriptions such as Decus et tutamen ("an ornament and a safeguard") made tampering evident.

Significance

Uniform machine-made coins underpinned modern monetary systems, making counterfeiting and clipping harder and enabling reliable large-scale circulation.
