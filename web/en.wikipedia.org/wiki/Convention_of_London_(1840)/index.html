Convention of London (1840)

Signed15 July 1840
LocationLondon, United Kingdom
SignatoriesUnited Kingdom, Austria, Prussia, Russia, Ottoman Empire
ContextSecond Egyptian–Ottoman War; Oriental Crisis of 1840

The Convention of London of 1840 was an international treaty signed on 15 July 1840 between the United Kingdom, Austria, Prussia, and Russia on one side and the Ottoman Empire on the other. It was designed to settle the Oriental Crisis of 1840, the confrontation that had erupted during the Second Egyptian–Ottoman War between the Ottoman Sultan Abdülmecid I and his ambitious vassal, Muhammad Ali of Egypt.

Background

In 1839 the Ottoman army had been crushed by Egyptian forces under Ibrahim Pasha at the Battle of Nezib, and the Ottoman fleet had defected to Alexandria. Muhammad Ali demanded hereditary rule over Egypt and Syria. The European great powers, fearing the collapse of the Ottoman Empire and the disruption of the balance of power, intervened diplomatically. France, under Adolphe Thiers, supported Muhammad Ali, while Britain, led in foreign affairs by Lord Palmerston, sought to preserve Ottoman integrity and limit French influence in the Levant.

Terms

Under the convention, Muhammad Ali was offered hereditary governorship of Egypt and, for his lifetime, administration of the southern part of Syria (the eyalets of Sidon and Acre), provided he accepted the terms within ten days and withdrew his forces from the rest of Syria. If he refused, the offer of Syria would lapse; if he still refused after a further ten days, even the Egyptian offer would be withdrawn. The signatories agreed to enforce these terms, including by naval force if necessary.

Consequences

Muhammad Ali, encouraged by France, rejected the ultimatum. An allied British, Austrian, and Ottoman force bombarded Beirut and captured Acre in the autumn of 1840, compelling Muhammad Ali to accept a settlement. The resulting arrangement was formalised by the London Straits Convention of 1841, in which Muhammad Ali received hereditary rule of Egypt under Ottoman suzerainty while relinquishing Syria. The convention is regarded as a landmark in the Eastern Question and in the Concert of Europe diplomacy of the period.
