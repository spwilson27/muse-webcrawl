Computational mathematics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Numerical simulation, a central tool of computational mathematics

Computational mathematics is an area of mathematics devoted to the interaction between mathematics and computer computation. It involves mathematical research in science and engineering where computing plays a central and essential role, emphasizing algorithms, numerical methods, and symbolic methods. A large part of computational mathematics consists roughly of using mathematics for allowing and improving computer computation in areas of science and engineering where mathematics is useful, and conversely of using computation to explore mathematical problems that are too large for analytical treatment.

Areas

  • Numerical analysis — algorithms for solving continuous problems approximately: root-finding, interpolation, numerical integration, and the numerical solution of ordinary and partial differential equations.
  • Scientific computing — large-scale simulation of physical systems, such as weather prediction, fluid dynamics, and structural mechanics, using methods like finite differences, finite elements, and spectral methods.
  • Symbolic computation — exact manipulation of mathematical expressions by computer algebra systems.
  • Computational number theory and algebra — algorithms for factoring, primality testing, and algebraic structures, with applications in cryptography.
  • Optimization — computational methods for linear, nonlinear, and combinatorial optimization.
  • Computational statistics and data science — simulation-based inference, Monte Carlo methods, and machine learning.

Character and methods

Because computers work with finite precision, computational mathematics studies error, stability, and convergence: how rounding and truncation errors accumulate, and how algorithms can be designed so that approximate answers are provably close to true ones. Verification and validation — checking both that a computation solves the equations right and that the equations model reality — are standard concerns. Software ecosystems such as MATLAB, NumPy/SciPy, and the computer algebra systems Mathematica and Maple are the everyday instruments of the field, and high-performance computing clusters and GPUs make very large problems tractable.

History

Computation has accompanied mathematics since antiquity (tables of logarithms and trigonometric values were early "computed" products), but the modern field dates from the electronic computer era of the 1940s: John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine's 1947 paper on numerical inverting of matrices is a founding document, and the postwar development of numerical weather prediction, nuclear engineering, and aerospace drove rapid growth. Today computational mathematics is a standard discipline with its own journals and degree programs, sitting between pure mathematics, computer science, and the application sciences.
