Reproductive labor

Reproductive labor or work is often associated with care giving and domestic housework roles including cleaning, cooking, child care, and the unpaid domestic labor force. It is about the work required to maintain and reproduce the labour force — not only physical reproduction through childbirth, but the daily and generational work of feeding, cleaning, socializing, and emotionally sustaining people so that they can participate in productive (paid) labour.

The term has been used in feminist and Marxist theory to argue that such work, performed predominantly by women and frequently unpaid or underpaid, is essential to the functioning of capitalist economies yet is rendered invisible in standard economic accounting such as GDP.

Theoretical development

Marxist feminists such as Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Silvia Federici, prominent in the 1970s Wages for Housework campaign, argued that housework produces and reproduces labour power itself, and demanded recognition of its economic value. Later scholars developed the related concepts of social reproduction theory, "care work", and emotional labour. Evelyn Nakano Glenn and others showed how reproductive labour is stratified by race and class, with paid domestic and care work disproportionately performed by women of colour and migrant women, a phenomenon later analysed as global care chains.

Debates

Scholars debate how reproductive labour should be measured and valued, whether wages for housework would entrench or subvert gender roles, and how policies such as parental leave, public childcare, and shorter working hours redistribute care work between households, markets, and the state.
