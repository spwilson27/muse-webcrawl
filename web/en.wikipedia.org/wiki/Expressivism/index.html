Expressivism

Expressivism in meta-ethics is the view that the primary function of moral sentences is not to describe facts but to express the speaker's attitudes, emotions, or plans. Saying "stealing is wrong", on this view, expresses disapproval of stealing rather than stating a moral fact, which makes expressivism a form of non-cognitivism.

Historical roots include A. J. Ayer's emotivism and Charles Stevenson's work; modern versions include Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism and Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism, which attempts to earn back the right to talk of moral truth while retaining an expressivist semantics.

The best-known challenge is the Frege–Geach problem: explaining how moral sentences behave in embedded, unasserted contexts, such as the antecedent of a conditional ("If stealing is wrong, then..."), where no attitude is expressed.
