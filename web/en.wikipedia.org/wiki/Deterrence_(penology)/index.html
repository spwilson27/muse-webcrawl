Deterrence (penology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Deterrence in penology is the theory that punishment discourages crime: general deterrence discourages the public by example; specific (or individual) deterrence discourages the punished offender from reoffending. Classical theorists (Beccaria, 1764; Bentham) held that deterrent effect depends more on the certainty and swiftness of punishment than on its severity — a proposition modern criminology broadly endorses: reviews (notably the US National Research Council's work and Daniel Nagin's syntheses) find credible evidence that increases in the likelihood of apprehension deter, and weak or no reliable evidence that harsher sentences, including the death penalty, add deterrent effect beyond long imprisonment.

Deterrence is one of the classical aims of punishment alongside retribution, incapacitation, rehabilitation and restoration, and it structures sentencing policy from drink-driving enforcement to mandatory minima. Its limits are equally studied: many offences are impulsive, committed under intoxication or desperation, or by offenders who discount future consequences; marginal deterrence can fail where added severity for worse crimes removes incentives to stop short (the problem that penalties should increase with gravity to deter escalation).
