The Mi'kmaq (also spelled Micmac, Mi'gmaq or Mi'kmaw) are a First Nations people of the Northeastern Woodlands, indigenous to the areas now known as Canada's Atlantic Provinces and the Gaspé Peninsula of Quebec, as well as the northeastern United States, particularly Maine. Their traditional territory, Mi'kma'ki, encompasses the present-day Maritime provinces and parts of Quebec, Newfoundland and New England.

Language

The Mi'kmaq language is an Eastern Algonquian language with several dialects. It was historically written with a distinctive hieroglyphic writing system (komqwejwi'kasikl) adapted by missionaries, and later with Latin-based orthographies, including the Francis–Smith orthography commonly used today. Language revitalisation efforts are under way in many communities.

History

The Mi'kmaq were among the first peoples encountered by European explorers and settlers in northeastern North America, and they formed early alliances with the French. Through the 18th century they concluded a series of Peace and Friendship Treaties with the British Crown (1725–1779), which — unlike many later treaties — did not cede land. The Grand Council (Sante' Mawio'mi), led by the Grand Chief, remains the traditional governing body, alongside elected band councils under Canadian law. The Supreme Court of Canada recognised Mi'kmaq treaty rights in decisions such as R v Simon (1985) and the Marshall decisions (1999) concerning fishing rights.

Culture

Traditional Mi'kmaq spirituality centres on figures such as Glooscap (Kluskap), a hero and transformer. The patron saint of the Mi'kmaq is Saint Anne, whose feast day on 26 July is a major gathering. Symbols of Mi'kmaq identity include the eight-pointed star and the Mi'kmaq flag.
