Nontrinitarianism

Nontrinitarianism is a form of Christianity that rejects the mainstream Christian doctrine of the Trinity — the teaching that God is one being in three coeternal, consubstantial persons (Father, Son and Holy Spirit). Nontrinitarians differ widely in their beliefs about Jesus, variously holding that he was a prophet or created being (Arianism), that God is a single person (modalism, Oneness), or that Jesus is distinct from and subordinate to God the Father (biblical unitarianism).

History

Early Christian movements such as the Ebionites, and later Arius in the fourth century, held views condemned as heretical by the councils that defined Nicene orthodoxy. Modalistic monarchianism (Sabellianism) taught that the Father, Son and Spirit are modes of a single divine person. In the Reformation era, Michael Servetus and the Socinians revived anti-trinitarian theology; Servetus was executed in Geneva in 1553. Transylvanian and Polish Unitarian churches emerged in the sixteenth century, and modern Unitarianism developed in England and America in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Contemporary groups

Today's largest nontrinitarian bodies include the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (whose theology of the Godhead holds the Father, Son and Holy Ghost to be three distinct beings), Jehovah's Witnesses, Oneness Pentecostals, Christadelphians, the Iglesia ni Cristo, and Unitarian Universalists. Because their doctrines diverge from the Nicene Creed, mainstream churches generally do not regard some of these groups' baptisms as valid, and the question of whether they are "Christian" is contested.
