Lithium aluminium hydride, commonly abbreviated to LAH, is an inorganic compound with the chemical formula Li[AlH4]. It was discovered by Finholt, Bond and Schlesinger in 1947. It is a powerful reducing agent used in organic synthesis, able to reduce esters, carboxylic acids, acyl chlorides, aldehydes and ketones to the corresponding alcohols, and amides and nitriles to amines.

Properties

LiAlH4 is a white crystalline solid, though commercial samples are often grey due to impurities. It reacts violently with water, liberating hydrogen, and can ignite in moist air; it is therefore handled in dry diethyl ether or tetrahydrofuran solutions or suspensions under inert atmosphere. The solid is pyrophoric in finely divided form.

Preparation

It was originally prepared by reacting lithium hydride with aluminium chloride in ether (the Schlesinger process): 4 LiH + AlCl3 → LiAlH4 + 3 LiCl. Industrial production may use sodium aluminium hydride with lithium chloride or lithium hydride under pressure in a metathesis reaction. The related compound sodium borohydride is a much milder reagent; LAH's greater reactivity stems from the more polar Al–H bond.

Applications

In organic chemistry, LAH reduces most carbonyl compounds and many other functional groups; work-up with water and aqueous base (the Fieser workup) converts aluminium salts into a filterable granular precipitate. It also reduces epoxides, lactones and nitro compounds (to azo compounds or amines), and converts alkyl halides and tosylates to alkanes. It is used in the preparation of other hydrides, in hydrogen-storage research as a high-capacity hydrogen carrier (it is about 10.6 wt% hydrogen), and in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals.

Safety

LAH reacts exothermically with water, alcohols and other protic substances, evolving flammable hydrogen gas. Fires involving LAH must be extinguished with dry powder, sand or soda ash — never water or carbon dioxide extinguishers.
