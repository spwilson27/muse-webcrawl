Russian colonization of North America

From the 1730s to 1867, the Russian Empire carried out the colonization of North America, claiming territory principally in what is now the U.S. state of Alaska. The colonial possessions, known as Russian America, were administered from 1799 by the Russian-American Company, a state-sponsored monopoly. The colonization ended when the United States purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867 for $7.2 million.

Exploration

The Second Kamchatka Expedition of Vitus Bering and Aleksei Chirikov reached Alaska in 1741, returning with valuable sea otter pelts. This launched the maritime fur trade, as promyshlenniki (fur traders) pushed eastward along the Aleutian Islands, often violently exploiting and enslaving the Aleut and other Native peoples for hunting labour.

Russian-American Company

Grigory Shelikhov founded the first permanent Russian settlement at Three Saints Bay on Kodiak Island in 1784. In 1799 Tsar Paul I chartered the Russian-American Company under Alexander Baranov, who founded New Archangel (Sitka) as the colonial capital. At its height, Russian America included a settlement, Fort Ross in California (1812–1841), and posts in Hawaii.

Decline and sale

Sea otter depletion, the costs of administration and Russia's defeat in the Crimean War made Alaska seem a liability vulnerable to British seizure. Negotiations with the United States led to the Alaska Purchase of 1867; the transfer ceremony took place at Sitka on 18 October 1867, after which most Russian settlers departed, though a Russian Orthodox presence and Creole population remained.
