Indian reservation

An American Indian reservation is an area of land held in trust by the United States federal government and managed by a Native American tribal government under the US Bureau of Indian Affairs, rather than by the government of the state in which it is located. There are 326 Indian reservations in the United States, associated with the 574 federally recognised tribes; not all tribes have a reservation, and some tribes have more than one, while others share reservations.

History

The reservation system grew out of treaties between the United States and tribal nations, by which tribes ceded most of their lands and retained smaller areas for their exclusive use. From the 1830s, the policy of Indian removal forcibly relocated eastern tribes to lands west of the Mississippi. The Dawes Act of 1887 broke up many reservations into individual allotments and opened "surplus" land to non-Native settlement, costing tribes some 90 million acres before allotment was ended by the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934.

Governance and jurisdiction

Reservations are part of Indian Country in law. Tribal governments exercise sovereignty over them, providing services such as police, courts, housing, and healthcare, though jurisdiction over crimes involving non-Natives is limited by federal law, including the Supreme Court's decision in Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978). In McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the Court held that much of eastern Oklahoma remains reservation land for purposes of federal criminal law.

Life on reservations

Many reservations face high rates of poverty and unemployment, legacies of federal policy and remoteness, alongside cultural revitalisation efforts, language programmes, and tribal enterprises such as gaming authorised under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988. The largest reservation is the Navajo Nation, at roughly 27,000 square miles, while many California rancherías are only a few acres.
