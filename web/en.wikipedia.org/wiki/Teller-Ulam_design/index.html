Teller–Ulam design

The Teller–Ulam design is the technical concept behind most modern thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs). It is a two-stage implosion design in which the energy of a fission "primary" stage is used to compress and ignite a separate fusion "secondary" stage, producing yields far beyond those of single-stage fission weapons. The design is credited to Polish-American mathematician Stanisław Ulam and Hungarian-American physicist Edward Teller, working at Los Alamos in 1951.

History

Earlier "Super" concepts for a hydrogen bomb proved impractical when calculations showed the fusion fuel would cool too quickly. Ulam proposed staged compression using one bomb to implode a second, and Teller added the key insight of radiation implosion: channelling the primary's X-ray energy to compress the secondary. The design was validated by the Ivy Mike test of 1 November 1952, a 10.4-megaton device detonated at Enewetak Atoll, followed by deliverable weapons such as the Castle Bravo device (1954).

Basic principle

Published accounts describe the primary and secondary housed in a casing that reflects and contains radiation. On detonation, soft X-rays from the primary briefly fill the casing and ablate the secondary's outer surface, driving it to extreme density and temperature until its fusion fuel ignites; a fission "spark plug" at the core may assist ignition. Details remain classified in all nuclear-weapon states, which independently developed equivalent staged designs.
