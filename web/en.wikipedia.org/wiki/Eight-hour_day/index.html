Eight-hour day

[Hero image placeholder: eight-hour day trade union banner]

The eight-hour day (or 40-hour week) was a social movement to regulate the length of a working day, preventing excesses and abuses of working time, and became a central demand of the labour movement in the 19th and 20th centuries. Where the movement originated, workers' time was divided by the slogan advanced by Robert Owen in 1817: "Eight hours' labour, Eight hours' recreation, Eight hours' rest."

History

Early advocates included the Welsh social reformer Robert Owen, who coined the eight-hour slogan at his New Lanark mills. In 1840, the carpenter Samuel Parnell secured an eight-hour day for tradesmen in Wellington, New Zealand, often cited as the first such victory. In Australia, stonemasons in Melbourne won the eight-hour day in 1856 after downing tools, an achievement commemorated by the Eight Hours Monument.

In the United States, the demand galvanized the labour movement after the Civil War. The Haymarket affair of 1886 in Chicago grew out of a general strike for the eight-hour day. May 1st — International Workers' Day — commemorates these events and the campaign.

Legislation

The International Labour Organization's first convention, adopted in 1919, established the eight-hour day and 48-hour week in industry. In the United States, the Adamson Act (1916) set an eight-hour day for interstate railroad workers, and the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 established the 40-hour week with overtime pay across most employment. Henry Ford's Ford Motor Company famously adopted the eight-hour day and five-day week in 1926, arguing that leisure time increased workers' purchasing power.

Many countries later moved to shorter standard weeks, such as 35 hours in France. The eight-hour day remains the standard reference for full-time work in much of the world, though gig work, salaried overtime exemptions, and always-on digital communication have blurred its boundaries.
