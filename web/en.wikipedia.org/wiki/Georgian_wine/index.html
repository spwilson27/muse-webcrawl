Georgian wine has one of the longest continuous histories of any wine in the world. Archaeological evidence from Neolithic settlements south of Tbilisi — grape pips and wine residues in clay jars dated to about 6000 BC — indicates that Georgia was among the very first places where wine was made, roughly 8,000 years ago. Wine is central to Georgian identity, feasting and Orthodox Christian ritual, and the country preserves more than 500 indigenous grape varieties.

The qvevri method

The defining feature of traditional Georgian winemaking is the qvevri: a large egg-shaped clay vessel, coated inside with beeswax, that is buried in the earth of the wine cellar (marani) up to its neck. Crushed grapes — often including skins, pips and stems — are poured in and ferment with wild yeasts at a naturally stable temperature. White wines made this way emerge a deep amber or "orange" colour with pronounced tannin from extended skin contact; red production follows the same principle. In 2013 UNESCO inscribed the ancient Georgian qvevri winemaking method on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Varieties and regions

The eastern region of Kakheti produces the great majority of Georgia's wine. The most important grape varieties are the red Saperavi — a rare teinturier grape with coloured flesh, used for the dry reds of Mukuzani and the semi-sweet Kindzmarauli and Khvanchkara blends (the latter from Racha, blended with Aleksandrouli and Mujuretuli) — and the white Rkatsiteli, one of the world's most widely planted white grapes, used for Tsinandali (blended with Mtsvane) and for amber qvevri wines. In western Georgia, regions such as Imereti use less skin contact and varieties such as Tsolikouri and Tsitska.

Modern history

Under the Soviet Union Georgia supplied wines to the whole USSR, but production was industrialised and quality often neglected. Russia's 2006 import ban, imposed during political tensions, forced Georgian producers to raise quality and diversify into European, American and Asian markets; the ban was lifted in 2013. Since the 2000s a revival of small-scale qvevri production has made Georgian amber wines fashionable in the natural-wine world, and wine has again become one of the country's leading exports.

The supra

Wine in Georgia is inseparable from the supra, the traditional feast at which a toastmaster, the tamada, leads a ritual sequence of toasts — to God, peace, ancestors, children, friendship — drunk from glasses and, on grand occasions, from polished horns.
