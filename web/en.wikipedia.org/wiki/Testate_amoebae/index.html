Testate amoebae

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Testate amoebae (formerly Thecamoebians) are a polyphyletic group of unicellular amoeboid protists characterised by the presence of a shell (called a test) from which pseudopodia emerge through a single opening (aperture). They are found in soils, leaf litter, freshwater sediments, mosses and especially in peatlands, where they are often the dominant group of protozoa.

The test

The test may be secreted by the cell (autogenous — composed of protein, or of siliceous or calcareous plates called idiosomes, as in Euglypha), or built from collected material (xenogenous — sand grains, diatom frustules and debris cemented together, as in Difflugia), or a combination of the two. Test shape — flask-shaped, hemispherical, elongate — and the form of the aperture are key identification characters.

Classification

The group is not a natural clade: lobose testate amoebae (Arcellinida) belong to the Amoebozoa, while filose forms such as the euglyphids (with siliceous plate tests) belong to the Rhizaria (Cercozoa). Genera include Arcella, Difflugia, Centropyxis, Euglypha, Trinema, Hyalosphenia and Nebela.

Ecology and uses

Testate amoebae feed on bacteria, fungi, algae and other microorganisms and occupy an important place in soil and peatland food webs and carbon cycling. Because many species have narrow tolerance ranges for water-table depth and pH, and their tests preserve in peat and lake sediments, they are widely used as bioindicators — particularly for quantitative reconstruction of past peatland hydrology and climate (transfer-function studies), and in forensic and monitoring contexts.
