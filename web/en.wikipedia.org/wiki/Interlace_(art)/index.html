Interlace (art)

Interlace is a decorative motif in art, architecture and manuscript illumination in which bands, ribbons or straps are woven over and under one another in a regular pattern. Interlace is characteristic of Insular art, Viking art and Islamic art, and appears in media from carved stone crosses to illuminated manuscripts and metalwork.

Origins

Plaitwork patterns occur in late Roman art, and interlace developed further in the early medieval period. In the British Isles, the Insular style of the seventh and eighth centuries produced extraordinarily intricate knotwork, as in the Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells. Scandinavian artists developed their own varieties in the Borre, Jellinge and later styles, often incorporating elongated animal bodies into the weave.

Varieties

Simple plaitwork consists of unbroken repeating bands; more elaborate forms break and rejoin the bands, or merge them with spirals, key patterns and zoomorphic figures. In Islamic art, geometric interlace (girih) became a vehicle for complex tessellation.

Legacy

Interlace fell from favour in the later Middle Ages in Northern Europe but was revived by the Arts and Crafts movement and the Celtic Revival, and remains a popular motif in jewellery, tattooing and graphic design.
