Pitch correction

A graphical pitch-correction editor, showing a detected pitch curve being adjusted against a piano-roll scale.

Pitch correction is an electronic audio effect unit or audio editor software that changes the intonation (highness or lowness in pitch) of an audio signal so that all pitches will be in a given key — for example, in tune with an equal-tempered scale. Pitch correction devices do this without otherwise altering the character of the sound, in contrast with pitch shifting effects, which intentionally alter timbre.

Pitch correction first detected the pitch of the recording, then calculated the desired change and modified the audio signal accordingly. The widest use of pitch correction devices is in Western popular music for the correction of vocal lines.

History

Before the invention of pitch correction, errors in vocal intonation in recordings could only be corrected by re-recording the whole song (which most bands would do up until the 1980s), or by splicing in the correct note from another take. Pitch correction was made possible by the invention of the digital audio workstation.

In 1997, the Antares Audio Technology Auto-Tune pitch-correction plug-in was released. Originally intended as a subtle tool for correcting off-key performances, it became notorious after its aggressive use as a deliberate effect on Cher's 1998 hit "Believe", in which fast pitch transitions produced an unnatural, robotic vocal sound. This "Auto-Tune effect" subsequently became a signature sound in hip hop and pop, used prominently by artists such as T-Pain, and a staple of genres including trap and hyperpop.

Use and criticism

Used subtly, pitch correction smooths small intonation errors while preserving expressive devices such as vibrato; correction speed settings determine how quickly notes are pulled to target pitches. Critics have argued that ubiquitous correction raises unrealistic expectations of live performance and homogenises singing, while defenders note that it is only one of many studio tools and that skilled engineers apply it transparently. Alternatives and competing products include Celemony Melodyne, which allows direct note-by-note manipulation of polyphonic audio, and Waves Tune.
