Circular economy

[Image: workers refurbishing returned appliances at a remanufacturing plant]

A circular economy is an economic model that aims to eliminate waste and keep products and materials in use as long as possible — through design for durability, reuse, repair, remanufacturing and recycling — in contrast to the linear 'take–make–dispose' model. It is promoted by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, the EU (Circular Economy Action Plans under the European Green Deal) and China (Circular Economy Promotion Law, 2009).

Rationale and practice

The model's economic logic rests on resource-price volatility, supply security (critical minerals), waste externalities and new service models — selling illumination rather than bulbs, leasing rather than owning equipment — that align producers with longevity. Industrial symbiosis (Kalundborg, Denmark) turns one plant's waste into another's input. Analysts caution that recycling cannot repeal thermodynamics and that efficiency gains can rebound into higher consumption (Jevons paradox), so circularity complements rather than replaces broader sustainability policy in environmental economics; measuring circularity (material-flow accounts, circular material use rates ~12% in the EU) remains a developing statistical field within Economics.
