John Willard Milnor (born 20 February 1931) is an American mathematician known for his work in differential topology, algebraic K-theory and low-dimensional holomorphic dynamical systems. He is one of only a few mathematicians to have won the Fields Medal, the Wolf Prize and the Abel Prize — the three most prestigious awards in mathematics.

Early life and education

Milnor was born in Orange, New Jersey, and studied at Princeton University. As an undergraduate he twice won the Putnam competition, and in 1950, still a student, he proved the Fáry–Milnor theorem on the total curvature of knots. He completed his doctorate at Princeton in 1954 under Ralph Fox, with a dissertation on link groups.

Exotic spheres

In 1956 Milnor astonished the mathematical world by showing that the 7-dimensional sphere admits smooth structures different from the standard one — "exotic spheres". This result, which opened the modern field of differential topology, demonstrated that topological and smooth structures on a manifold can diverge. He went on to develop surgery theory and, with Michel Kervaire, classified exotic sphere structures in higher dimensions.

Other work

Milnor made major contributions to algebraic K-theory (including the Milnor conjecture, later proved by Vladimir Voevodsky), to the theory of quadratic forms, and to complex dynamics, where his books and lecture notes helped define the modern study of one-dimensional holomorphic maps. He taught at Princeton and the Institute for Advanced Study, and later at Stony Brook University, where he co-founded the Institute for Mathematical Sciences.

Awards

Milnor received the Fields Medal in 1962 for his work in differential topology, the Wolf Prize in Mathematics in 1989, and the Abel Prize in 2011 "for pioneering discoveries in topology, geometry and algebra". His expository books, including Morse Theory and Topology from the Differentiable Viewpoint, are celebrated for their clarity and remain standard references.
