Narmer (Ancient Egyptian: nꜥr-mr) was an ancient Egyptian pharaoh of the Early Dynastic Period, reigning around 3150 BC. He is conventionally regarded as the king who unified Upper and Lower Egypt and founded the First Dynasty, though some scholars identify him with, or as the successor of, the semi-legendary King Menes credited with the unification in later tradition.

The Narmer Palette

Narmer is best known from the Narmer Palette, a ceremonial carved siltstone palette discovered at Hierakonpolis in 1897–98 and now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. On one side the king, wearing the White Crown of Upper Egypt, smites a captive identified with Lower Egypt; on the other he wears the Red Crown of Lower Egypt and inspects rows of decapitated enemies. The palette is widely interpreted as a commemoration of Egypt's unification and is a foundational artefact of pharaonic iconography, establishing motifs — the smiting king, the intertwined serpopards, the falcon of Horus subduing a papyrus land — that endured for three millennia.

Reign and evidence

Narmer's name, written with the catfish (nꜥr) and chisel (mr) signs inside a serekh, is attested on seal impressions, ivory labels, pottery, and stone vessels from sites across Egypt and into the southern Levant, suggesting trade or campaigning as far as Canaan. A tomb at Umm el-Qa'ab in Abydos (B17/B18) is attributed to him. Debate continues over whether unification was a single event under Narmer or a gradual process completed by his predecessors or successors, and over his relationship to Queen Neithhotep and to Hor-Aha, who followed him.
