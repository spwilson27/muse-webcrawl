Automotive Industry in Canada

The automotive industry in Canada consists primarily of assembly plants owned by foreign automakers, most with headquarters in the United States or Japan, and hundreds of parts manufacturers, many of which are Canadian-owned. The industry is concentrated in southern Ontario, close to the United States border and to Detroit, the historic centre of the American industry.

History

Canadian automobile manufacturing began in 1904 when Gordon McGregor of Walkerville, Ontario, formed the Ford Motor Company of Canada under licence from Henry Ford. Other American firms followed, drawn by tariff protection within the British Empire and by access to the Canadian market. Indigenous firms such as McLaughlin of Oshawa were absorbed into General Motors by the 1920s.

The industry's modern shape dates from the 1965 Canada–United States Automotive Products Agreement, known as the Auto Pact, which eliminated tariffs on cars and parts between the two countries in exchange for production safeguards. The Auto Pact rationalized production within North America and greatly expanded Canadian assembly and exports. It was later superseded by the Canada–US Free Trade Agreement of 1989 and NAFTA in 1994, and the pact itself was ruled inconsistent with trade rules by the World Trade Organization in 2000.

Manufacturers and production

Major assemblers with Canadian plants have included General Motors (Oshawa and Ingersoll), Ford (Oakville and St. Thomas), Chrysler/Stellantis (Windsor and Brampton), Toyota (Cambridge and Woodstock) and Honda (Alliston). Canadian-owned parts giant Magna International, founded by Frank Stronach, grew into one of the world's largest automotive suppliers, alongside firms such as Linamar and Martinrea.

Canada has typically ranked among the top dozen vehicle-producing countries, building roughly two million vehicles a year in strong years, though production fell sharply after the 2008 financial crisis and during the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent semiconductor shortages. More recently, federal and provincial governments have committed large subsidies to attract electric-vehicle battery plants, including Volkswagen's gigafactory at St. Thomas and the Stellantis–LG plant in Windsor.
