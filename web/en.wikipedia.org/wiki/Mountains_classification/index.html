Mountains classification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

The mountains classification is a secondary competition in many professional road bicycle stage races, especially the Grand Tours, rewarding the riders who reach the summits of categorised climbs first. The overall winner is commonly called the King of the Mountains (French: Roi de la Montagne). Points are awarded at the top of each significant climb, with more points for harder climbs; the classification leader wears a distinctive jersey.

Climb categorisation

Climbs are graded by difficulty. The Tour de France uses, in ascending order, categories 4, 3, 2 and 1, with the hardest climbs classed hors catégorie ("beyond categorisation", HC). The grades consider length, gradient, altitude and position in the stage. Summit finishes typically award double points. The Giro d'Italia uses categories 1–4 plus the Cima Coppi, the highest point of the race, named for Fausto Coppi.

Jerseys

At the Tour de France the leader wears the famous white jersey with red polka dots (maillot à pois rouges), introduced in 1975 after sponsorship by a chocolate company whose wrapper inspired the design. The Giro d'Italia awards the blue jersey (maglia azzurra), formerly green, and the Vuelta a España a white jersey with blue dots. A rider who wins both the general and mountains classifications wears the more prestigious jersey, with the runner-up in the mountains wearing the polka dots.

History

The Tour de France first recognised its best climber in 1933, won by Vicente Trueba, though the polka dot jersey came decades later. The title has been won by great climbers such as Federico Bahamontes, Eddy Merckx, Lucien Van Impe, and Richard Virenque, who holds the record of seven Tour mountains titles. Specialists who target the classification often attack in breakaways on mountain stages to harvest summit points rather than contend for overall victory.
