Sinking of the RMS Titanic

Sinking of RMS Titanic
Date14–15 April 1912
LocationNorth Atlantic Ocean, about 370 miles (600 km) south-southeast of Newfoundland
CauseCollision with iceberg
DeathsAbout 1,500
Survivors710

The RMS Titanic sank in the early morning hours of 15 April 1912 in the North Atlantic Ocean, four days into her maiden voyage from Southampton to New York City. The largest ocean liner then in service, she had an estimated 2,224 people on board when she struck an iceberg at around 23:40 (ship's time) on Sunday, 14 April 1912. Her sinking two hours and forty minutes later at 02:20 on Monday, 15 April, resulted in the deaths of more than 1,500 people, making it one of the deadliest peacetime maritime disasters in history.

Background

Titanic was the second of three Olympic-class liners built by Harland and Wolff for the White Star Line. She received several warnings of sea ice on 14 April but was travelling near maximum speed when her lookouts sighted an iceberg. Unable to turn quickly enough, the ship suffered a glancing blow that buckled her starboard hull, opening several compartments to the sea. Designed to remain afloat with any four of her sixteen watertight compartments flooded, she could not survive the flooding of five.

Evacuation and rescue

The ship carried only enough lifeboats for about half of those on board. In the evacuation, boats were launched partially filled, and about 710 people — mostly women and children — survived. The liner RMS Carpathia arrived about two hours after the sinking and rescued the survivors. More than 1,500 people, including Captain Edward Smith and designer Thomas Andrews, died.

Aftermath

The disaster led to major reforms, including the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) in 1914, requirements for sufficient lifeboats for everyone aboard, and the establishment of the International Ice Patrol. The wreck, discovered in 1985 by a team led by Robert Ballard, lies about 12,500 feet (3,800 m) below the surface and has become the subject of extensive exploration and cultural fascination.
