Date262 – 260 BC
LocationChangping, northwest of modern Gaoping, Shanxi
ResultDecisive Qin victory

The Battle of Changping (長平之戰) was a military campaign in ancient China, fought from 262 to 260 BC between the states of Qin and Zhao during the Warring States period. It was one of the largest and deadliest battles of the ancient world: the Qin general Bai Qi encircled and destroyed the entire Zhao field army, and ancient historians report that the hundreds of thousands of surrendered Zhao soldiers were massacred. The disaster broke Zhao's power permanently and left Qin without a serious rival on the road to unifying China in 221 BC.

Background and campaign

The campaign began over the Shangdang commandery, whose governor, cut off by Qin, offered the territory to Zhao instead. King Xiaocheng of Zhao accepted, sending the veteran general Lian Po, who built fortified lines at Changping and refused battle, holding the Qin army at bay for three years while its supply lines stretched ever longer. Qin agents spread rumours in the Zhao capital that Lian Po was timid and that Qin feared only the young Zhao Kuo, son of a famous general. The king took the bait and replaced Lian Po with Zhao Kuo, who abandoned the defensive strategy.

Encirclement and massacre

Qin's king had secretly given command to Bai Qi, the most feared general of the age. Feigning retreat, Bai Qi lured Zhao Kuo's army forward, then closed a double encirclement: cavalry cut the Zhao line of retreat while a second force sealed the supply route. Besieged on a hill for 46 days and starving, Zhao Kuo was killed in a final breakout attempt, and his army surrendered. According to Sima Qian's Shiji, Bai Qi, judging that the prisoners could not be fed and might revolt, had them buried alive, sparing only 240 of the youngest to carry the news home. The figure of 450,000 dead given by ancient sources is generally regarded by modern scholars as inflated, but the scale of the catastrophe is not in doubt; mass graves excavated near Gaoping in the 1990s corroborate a great slaughter.

Aftermath

Zhao never recovered its strength, though Qin's subsequent siege of the Zhao capital Handan failed when Wei and Chu intervened. The Chinese idiom "talking warfare on paper" (zhǐ shàng tán bīng), meaning armchair theorising divorced from reality, derives from Zhao Kuo's reputation. Changping remains a byword in Chinese culture for catastrophic military disaster — an outcome made possible by the total-mobilisation state built a century earlier by Shang Yang.
