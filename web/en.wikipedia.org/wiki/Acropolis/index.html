Acropolis
EtymologyGreek ἄκρον (akron, "highest point") + πόλις (polis, "city")
FunctionFortified citadel and sanctuary on elevated ground
Most famous exampleAcropolis of Athens

An acropolis is the settlement of an upper part of an ancient Greek city, especially a citadel, and frequently a hill with steep sides, chosen for purposes of defence. The term (from Greek akron, "highest point, extremity," and polis, "city") came to refer to the fortified, elevated core of a Greek city-state, which typically housed temples, treasuries and public buildings and served as a refuge in wartime and as the religious heart of the city.

The Acropolis of Athens, crowned by the Parthenon, is the most famous acropolis in the world.

The Acropolis of Athens

The best-known acropolis is that of Athens, a flat-topped limestone rock rising about 150 metres above the city. Inhabited since the Neolithic period and fortified in Mycenaean times, it became the site of the great Classical sanctuaries built under Pericles in the fifth century BC: the Parthenon, the Propylaea, the Erechtheion and the Temple of Athena Nike. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a symbol of ancient Greece and democracy.

Other acropoleis

Many Greek and Mediterranean cities had acropoleis, including Corinth (Acrocorinth), Argos, Thebes (the Cadmea), Sparta, and Pergamon, whose steep acropolis carried royal palaces, temples and a famous library. Similar elevated citadels are found in non-Greek contexts around the ancient world.
