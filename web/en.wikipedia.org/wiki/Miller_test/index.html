Miller test

The Miller test is the standard used by courts in the United States to determine whether speech or expression can be classified as obscene and therefore unprotected by the First Amendment. It was established by the Supreme Court in the 1973 case Miller v. California, 413 U.S. 15.

The test

Under the Miller test, material is obscene if all three of the following prongs are satisfied:

  1. Whether the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find that the work, taken as a whole, appeals to the prurient interest;
  2. Whether the work depicts or describes, in a patently offensive way, sexual conduct specifically defined by applicable state law; and
  3. Whether the work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.

The third prong is assessed by a "reasonable person" standard rather than by local community standards, a clarification made in Pope v. Illinois (1987).

Background

The test replaced the earlier standard from Roth v. United States (1957) and the fractured plurality in Memoirs v. Massachusetts (1966), which had required that material be "utterly without redeeming social value" to be obscene. Chief Justice Warren Burger's majority opinion in Miller sought to give states greater latitude to regulate pornography while retaining protection for works of serious value.

Criticism

The Miller test has been criticized for vagueness, particularly its reliance on local "community standards," which critics argue produces inconsistent results and a chilling effect on speech, especially in the era of the internet, where material is accessible across jurisdictions with differing standards. Attempts by Congress to regulate online indecency, such as the Communications Decency Act, were largely struck down in Reno v. ACLU (1997).
