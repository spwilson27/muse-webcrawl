Bryophytes are a group of non-vascular land plants comprising the liverworts (Marchantiophyta), mosses (Bryophyta), and hornworts (Anthocerotophyta) — together some 20,000 species. They are characterized by life cycles dominated by the haploid gametophyte generation, by the absence of true roots, stems, leaves, and lignified vascular tissue, and by dependence on liquid water for sexual reproduction, since their sperm must swim to the egg. Bryophytes are generally small and inhabit moist environments, though many tolerate desiccation and occur from the tropics to the polar regions.

Life cycle

A bryophyte begins as a spore that germinates into a filamentous or thalloid protonema, from which the mature gametophyte develops. Gametes form in multicellular organs — antheridia (male) and archegonia (female). Fertilization produces a diploid sporophyte that grows out of the archegonium and remains permanently attached to and nutritionally dependent on the gametophyte, typically consisting of a foot, a stalk (seta), and a capsule (sporangium) in which spores form by meiosis.

Ecology

Bryophytes are pioneer colonizers of rock, bark, and disturbed soil; regulate moisture in forests; and provide habitat for invertebrates. Peat mosses (Sphagnum) dominate northern peatlands, waterlogging their surroundings and locking away vast stores of carbon — peatlands cover about 3% of land area but hold more carbon than all the world's vegetation. Bryophytes are sensitive bioindicators of air pollution and climate change.

Evolution and classification

Bryophytes are among the earliest-diverging lineages of land plants, with a fossil record extending to the Ordovician–Silurian in the form of spores. Whether the three groups form a single clade ("Bryophyta sensu lato") or a grade relative to vascular plants has been debated; recent molecular phylogenies increasingly support bryophyte monophyly, with hornworts sister to a clade uniting liverworts and mosses (the Setaphyta hypothesis).

Human uses

Sphagnum peat is used in horticulture and historically as an absorbent surgical dressing. Mosses feature in Japanese moss gardens and in the florist trade, and bryophytes serve as model organisms in plant developmental genetics (e.g. Physcomitrium patens).
