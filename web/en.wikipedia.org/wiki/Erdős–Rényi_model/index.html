Erdős–Rényi model

In the mathematical field of graph theory, the Erdős–Rényi model refers to two closely related models for generating random graphs, introduced by Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi in papers beginning in 1959. In the G(n, M) model a graph is chosen uniformly at random from all graphs with n vertices and M edges; in the G(n, p) model each of the possible edges is included independently with probability p.

Properties

The model exhibits sharp threshold phenomena studied by Erdős and Rényi in their landmark 1960 paper "On the evolution of random graphs". As the average degree passes 1, a giant component suddenly emerges containing a positive fraction of all vertices; at average degree ln n the graph becomes connected with high probability. Degrees in G(n, p) follow a binomial distribution (approximately Poisson), and typical distances are short, of order log n.

Comparison with real networks

Erdős–Rényi graphs are the baseline of network science, but real networks usually differ from them: real networks show heavy-tailed degree distributions, high clustering and community structure, which motivated later models such as the Watts–Strogatz small-world model and the Barabási–Albert preferential-attachment model.

Applications

The model is used in the probabilistic method in combinatorics, in percolation theory, in epidemiology (where the giant component predicts the size of a possible epidemic), and as a null model in the statistical analysis of networks.
