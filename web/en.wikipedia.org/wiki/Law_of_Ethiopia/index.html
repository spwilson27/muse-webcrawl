The law of Ethiopia is a federal legal system based principally on the civil law tradition. Ethiopia is notable as one of the few African states never formally colonised, and its legal development was shaped by indigenous codifications alongside imported European models.

History

Traditional law was embodied in the Fetha Nagast ("Law of the Kings"), a legal code of Coptic origin used for centuries. In the mid-20th century, Emperor Haile Selassie commissioned a comprehensive set of modern codes — including the Civil Code of 1960, drafted under the guidance of the French jurist René David, and codes on commerce, criminal law, and civil and criminal procedure. The socialist Derg regime (1974–1991) overlaid these with its own proclamations.

Modern system

The 1995 Constitution of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia establishes a federal state in which legislative power is shared between the federal government and ethnically based regional states, which may maintain their own courts and laws. The Federal Supreme Court sits at the apex of the judiciary, with a cassation division whose interpretations bind lower courts. Alongside the formal system, customary dispute resolution and, in some regions, Sharia courts for personal matters remain important.
