An electric light, electric lamp or electric light bulb is an electrical device that produces visible light from electric current. It is the most common form of artificial lighting and a defining technology of the modern world, providing light indoors and outdoors after dark. Electric lighting is conventionally divided into incandescent lamps, gas-discharge lamps (including fluorescent and high-intensity discharge lamps), and solid-state lamps, chiefly light-emitting diodes (LEDs).

History

Humphry Davy demonstrated the electric arc lamp in the first decade of the 19th century, and arc lamps lit some streets and public spaces from the 1870s, but they were too bright and maintenance-heavy for homes. The race for a practical incandescent lamp culminated in 1879–1880, when Thomas Edison in the United States and Joseph Swan in England independently produced long-lived lamps with carbonised filaments in evacuated glass bulbs; Edison's achievement was to build a complete system — generators, wiring, switches and meters — around the lamp. Fluorescent tubes, commercialised in the late 1930s, offered far higher efficiency and dominated offices and industry, while compact fluorescents (CFLs) entered homes in the 1990s.

LED era and efficiency

Since the 2010s, LED lamps — based on the blue LED developed by Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura, recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics — have displaced older types as prices fell. LEDs convert a much larger share of energy into light (over 100 lumens per watt in typical products, versus about 15 for incandescent bulbs) and last 15,000–50,000 hours. Many countries have phased out general-service incandescent lamps through minimum efficiency standards, cutting global electricity demand for lighting, though concerns remain about light pollution and blue-rich light at night.
