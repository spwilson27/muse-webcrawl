Tự Đức (Hán tự: 嗣德; 22 September 1829 – 19 July 1883) was the fourth emperor of the Nguyễn dynasty of Vietnam, reigning from 1847 until his death in 1883 — the longest reign of any Nguyễn emperor. A scholarly, Confucian ruler devoted to literature and history, he presided over the first decades of French encroachment on Vietnam and is often judged, fairly or not, as the emperor who lost Cochinchina.

Reign

Tự Đức succeeded his father Thiệu Trị in preference to his elder brother, a choice that provoked an attempted revolt. His reign was beset by internal rebellions — including Catholic-led and Lê restorationist movements — epidemics, and banditry from the remnants of China's Taiping turmoil. His court's persecution of Catholic missionaries and converts gave France a pretext for intervention: Franco-Spanish forces seized Da Nang in 1858 and Saigon in 1859, and the Treaty of Saigon (1862) ceded the three eastern provinces of Cochinchina to France. Further conquests followed, formalised in the treaties of 1874 and, after Tự Đức's death, those of 1883–84 that made Vietnam a French protectorate.

Character and legacy

Tự Đức wrote poetry, commissioned historical compilations, and is credited with the saying that he was "a sick man whose burden was too heavy for his strength". Childless — possibly owing to a youthful illness — he adopted three nephews, a succession arrangement whose failure triggered the chaotic "four emperors in one year" crisis after his death. His tomb complex at Huế, built during his lifetime as a retreat with lakes and pavilions, is among the finest of the Nguyễn imperial tombs.
