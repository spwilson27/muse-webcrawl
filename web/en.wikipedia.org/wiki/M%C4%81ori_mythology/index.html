Māori mythology
Tradition: Māori, New Zealand
Key figures: Ranginui, Papatūānuku, Tāne, Tangaroa, Māui
Themes: Creation, separation of sky and earth, trickster feats

Māori mythology comprises the creation narratives, gods (atua), and heroic legends of the Māori people of New Zealand, preserved orally in song, carving, and recitation and recorded extensively by nineteenth-century collectors. Versions vary between iwi, but a common sequence of cosmogony is widely shared.

Creation

In the beginning was Te Kore (the void) and Te Pō (the night), from which emerged Ranginui (the Sky Father) and Papatūānuku (the Earth Mother), locked in an embrace with their children trapped in darkness between them. The children, led by Tāne (god of forests), forced their parents apart, bringing light into the world; Tāne later fashioned the first human woman, Hineahuone, from earth.

Gods and heroes

The children of Rangi and Papa became departmental gods: Tangaroa of the sea, Tāwhirimātea of storms, Rongo of cultivated food, Haumia of wild food, and Tūmatauenga of war and humanity. The demigod Māui, the great Polynesian trickster, fished up the North Island (Te Ika-a-Māui) from the sea, slowed the sun, stole fire for humankind, and died attempting to win immortality by passing through the goddess Hine-nui-te-pō.

Legacy

These traditions continue to structure Māori art, oratory, and environmental values, and they frame the genealogical connection (whakapapa) between people, gods, and the natural world.
