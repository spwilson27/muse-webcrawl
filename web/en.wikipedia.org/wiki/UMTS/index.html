The Universal Mobile Telecommunications System (UMTS) is a third-generation (3G) mobile cellular system for networks based on the GSM standard. Developed and maintained by the 3GPP (3rd Generation Partnership Project), UMTS uses wideband code-division multiple access (W-CDMA) as its underlying air interface, and forms part of the International Telecommunication Union's IMT-2000 standard set.

Technology

UMTS specifies a complete network system, including the radio access network (UTRAN), the core network — evolved from GSM's Mobile Application Part — and authentication of users via USIM cards. The most common form of UMTS uses W-CDMA with time-division duplex and frequency-division duplex variants. Theoretical peak data rates in the original standard were around 384 kbit/s for mobile users and up to 2 Mbit/s for stationary users.

Later 3GPP releases added HSDPA and HSUPA — collectively HSPA — and HSPA+, which raised peak download speeds first to 14.4 Mbit/s and eventually, with dual-carrier and MIMO techniques, to 42 Mbit/s, greatly extending the commercial life of UMTS networks.

Deployment and decline

The first commercial UMTS network was launched by NTT DoCoMo in Japan in 2001 under the FOMA brand, with European launches following from 2003. UMTS became the dominant 3G technology worldwide. From the 2010s onward, operators began refarming UMTS spectrum for 4G LTE and 5G NR, and many networks were shut down during the 2020s as carriers completed their 3G sunsets.
