Schist

Schist is a medium-grained metamorphic rock showing pronounced schistosity — a layered arrangement of platy or elongated mineral grains, typically micas, that allows the rock to split into thin, wavy flakes or slabs. The name derives from the Greek schistos, "divided".

Mica schist showing characteristic foliation.

Formation

Schists form under medium-grade regional metamorphism, at temperatures and pressures higher than those producing slate or phyllite but lower than those producing gneiss. Common parent rocks are shales and mudstones, though schists can also derive from fine-grained igneous rocks. The grains of mica grow large enough to be visible to the naked eye and align perpendicular to the direction of maximum pressure.

Types

Schists are named for their dominant visible minerals: mica schist, garnet schist, chlorite schist, talc schist, glaucophane (blue) schist, and staurolite schist among them. They frequently contain porphyroblasts — larger crystals of garnet, staurolite or kyanite embedded in the finer matrix.

Uses

Schist has been used as a building stone, for roofing and paving, and Manhattan's bedrock — Manhattan schist — famously supports the foundations of the city's skyscrapers.
