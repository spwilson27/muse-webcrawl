Spin ice

A spin ice is a magnetic substance that does not reach an ordered ground state even at very low temperatures, because its magnetic moments (spins) are geometrically frustrated. The canonical spin ices are the rare-earth titanate pyrochlores dysprosium titanate (Dy2Ti2O7) and holmium titanate (Ho2Ti2O7), in which the rare-earth moments sit on a lattice of corner-sharing tetrahedra.

The ice rule

In these materials the spins are strongly anisotropic, forced to point along the local axis joining the centre of each tetrahedron to its vertices. Ferromagnetic coupling then favours arrangements in which, on every tetrahedron, two spins point inward and two point outward — the "two-in, two-out" rule. This is directly analogous to the arrangement of hydrogen atoms around oxygen in water ice, where each oxygen has two hydrogens near and two far (the Bernal–Fowler ice rules). Just as ordinary ice retains a residual entropy at absolute zero (measured by Giauque and calculated by Linus Pauling), spin ice retains a macroscopic ground-state degeneracy and a residual entropy close to Pauling's estimate of (R/2) ln(3/2) per mole of spins.

Magnetic monopoles

Flipping a single spin in the ice-rule background creates a pair of defects: one tetrahedron with three spins in and one out, and a neighbouring one with one in and three out. These defects carry an effective magnetic charge and can move apart through the crystal as deconfined quasiparticles resembling magnetic monopoles. This picture, proposed theoretically in 2008 and supported by neutron scattering, muon spin relaxation, and heat-capacity experiments, made spin ice a leading example of emergent fractionalised excitations in condensed matter.

History

The spin-ice behaviour of Ho2Ti2O7 was identified by Harris and collaborators in 1997, who noted the analogy with the proton disorder in water ice. Ramirez and co-workers measured the residual entropy of Dy2Ti2O7 in 1999, confirming the Pauling-like value. Related "artificial spin ice" systems, made by lithographically patterning arrays of ferromagnetic nano-islands, reproduce the same frustrated physics in two dimensions and allow individual moments to be imaged directly.
