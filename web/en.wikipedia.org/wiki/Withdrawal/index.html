Withdrawal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Withdrawal refers to the physical and psychological symptoms that a person has when they suddenly stop taking or reduce their intake of a substance on which the body has become dependent. Symptoms can vary depending on the type of drug use, and withdrawal generally involves effects opposite to those of the substance itself, since the body attempts to regain homeostasis after adapting to the drug's presence.

Substances known to cause dependence and withdrawal include alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, nicotine, caffeine, stimulants and certain antidepressants. Some withdrawal syndromes — notably alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal — can be medically dangerous or fatal if unmanaged, whereas opioid withdrawal, though severely distressing, is rarely life-threatening in healthy adults.

Mechanism

With repeated exposure, the nervous system compensates for a drug's effects — for example, down-regulating receptors or reducing production of endogenous neurotransmitters. When the drug is removed, these adaptations are unopposed, producing a rebound state: sedative withdrawal yields hyper-excitability and anxiety, while stimulant withdrawal yields fatigue and low mood.

Symptoms by substance

Alcohol

Symptoms begin within hours of the last drink: tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea and insomnia, potentially progressing to seizures and delirium tremens, which carries significant mortality without treatment.

Opioids

Opioid withdrawal resembles a severe flu: muscle aches, diarrhoea, dilated pupils, yawning, gooseflesh (the origin of "cold turkey"), intense craving and dysphoria, peaking at two to three days.

Benzodiazepines

Abrupt cessation after prolonged use can cause rebound anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbances and seizures; gradual tapering is standard practice.

Nicotine and caffeine

Nicotine withdrawal produces irritability, difficulty concentrating and increased appetite. Caffeine withdrawal — headache, fatigue, depressed mood — is recognised in the DSM-5.

Treatment

Management ranges from supportive care and gradual dose tapering to substitution therapy: methadone or buprenorphine for opioid dependence, benzodiazepine tapers for alcohol withdrawal, and nicotine replacement for smoking cessation. Medically supervised detoxification is recommended for severe dependence on alcohol or sedatives.

Protracted withdrawal

Some individuals experience protracted (post-acute) withdrawal symptoms — mood disturbance, sleep problems, reduced stress tolerance — lasting weeks or months after acute withdrawal resolves.
