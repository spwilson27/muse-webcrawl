The Eureka Rebellion, also known as the Eureka Stockade, was an armed uprising by gold miners at Ballarat in the Colony of Victoria, Australia, culminating in a brief battle on 3 December 1854. The rebellion grew out of grievances over the expensive miner's licence, taxation without representation, and the actions of the government and police on the goldfields. It is widely regarded as a foundational event in the development of Australian democracy.

Background

The Victorian gold rush, which began in 1851, drew tens of thousands of prospectors ("diggers") to Ballarat. To mine legally, each digger had to purchase a licence for 30 shillings a month, a heavy burden for those who found little gold, and licence hunts by police were frequent and often brutal. Resentment intensified in October 1854 after the murder of digger James Scobie near the Eureka Hotel and the acquittal of the hotel's owner, a magistrate's associate; a mob burned the hotel, and three men were imprisoned for the riot. In November the Ballarat Reform League was formed, demanding manhood suffrage, abolition of the licence, and reform of goldfields administration.

The stockade

After a mass meeting on 29 November at which licences were burned, the diggers swore allegiance to the Eureka Flag — a blue flag bearing the Southern Cross — and, under the leadership of Peter Lalor, built a rough stockade at the Eureka lead. In the early hours of Sunday, 3 December 1854, a force of about 276 soldiers and police stormed the stockade, which was defended by roughly 120–150 diggers. The fighting lasted about 15 minutes. At least 22 diggers and six soldiers were killed, and Lalor was wounded and lost an arm.

Aftermath

Thirteen rebels were tried for high treason in Melbourne, but juries acquitted all of them amid public sympathy. A royal commission recommended sweeping reforms: the licence was replaced by a cheaper annual "miner's right", which carried the vote, and diggers gained representation in the Legislative Council. Peter Lalor was himself elected to parliament in 1855 and later became Speaker of the Victorian Legislative Assembly. The Eureka Flag, preserved at Ballarat, remains a potent symbol used by Australian democratic, labour and nationalist movements.
