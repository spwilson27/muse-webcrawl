Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), was a United States Supreme Court case in which the Court held that the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act's interim provision commanding state officers to conduct background checks on prospective handgun purchasers violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court ruled that Congress cannot compel — "commandeer" — state executive officers to implement a federal regulatory program.

Background

The Brady Act required the chief law enforcement officer (CLEO) of each local jurisdiction to conduct background checks on prospective handgun buyers until a federal instant-check system became operational. Jay Printz, sheriff of Ravalli County, Montana, and Richard Mack, sheriff of Graham County, Arizona, challenged the provision, arguing that it conscripted state officials into federal service contrary to the Constitution's structure of dual sovereignty.

Decision

Justice Antonin Scalia, writing for a 5–4 majority, concluded from historical evidence, constitutional structure, and precedent — including New York v. United States (1992), which had barred Congress from commandeering state legislatures — that the federal government likewise may not compel state executive officers to administer federal law. The opinion invoked the Tenth Amendment and The Federalist Papers. Justice Stevens dissented, arguing that the provision was a permissible exercise of Congress's commerce and necessary and proper powers.

Legacy

Together with New York v. United States, Printz forms the core of the anti-commandeering doctrine, later applied in Murphy v. NCAA (2018). The background-check mandate itself was temporary and expired when the permanent National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS) began operating in November 1998.
