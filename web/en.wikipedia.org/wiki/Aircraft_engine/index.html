Aircraft engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A huge turbofan engine with wide fan blades on a test stand]
A modern high-bypass turbofan.

An aircraft engine is the component of an aircraft propulsion system that generates mechanical power. Types include piston (reciprocating) engines driving propellers, turboprops, turbojets and turbofans, turboshafts for helicopters, and increasingly electric motors. Modern airliner turbofans — such as the GE9X, the largest and most powerful jet engine (about 110,000 lbf thrust, fan diameter 3.4 m) — achieve remarkable efficiency and reliability, with on-wing times exceeding a decade between overhauls.

Piston and turboprop

Aero piston engines evolved from car-like water-cooled V12s (Rolls-Royce Merlin) and air-cooled radials (Pratt & Whitney Wasp family) to today\'s avgas and diesel light-aircraft engines. Turboprops use a gas turbine geared to a propeller — efficient at regional speeds and dominant in commuter and military transport (C-130) roles.

Jet engines

Turbofans generate most thrust from a huge front fan bypassing air around the core; high bypass ratios (10:1+) slash fuel burn and noise. Engine makers Rolls-Royce, GE Aerospace, CFM (GE–Safran) and Pratt & Whitney compete on efficiency, durability and services contracts (\'power by the hour\').

Electric and future

Battery-electric propulsion serves trainers (Pipistrel Velis Electro, the first certified electric aircraft, 2020) and eVTOL prototypes; hydrogen combustion and fuel-cell concepts are in development for the 2030s–40s, constrained by hydrogen storage volume and infrastructure.
