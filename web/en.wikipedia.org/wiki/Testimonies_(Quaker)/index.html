Testimonies (Quaker)

Testimonies are the central expressions of Quaker faith and practice in the Religious Society of Friends. Rather than a creed, testimonies are outward expressions of the inward spiritual leadings Friends believe they experience, lived out in everyday conduct. They are not rules to be obeyed but witnesses — "testimonies" — to truth as Friends understand it, and have historically been a major source of Quaker social activism.

The main testimonies

Modern Friends often summarize the testimonies with the acronym SPICES: Simplicity, Peace, Integrity, Community, Equality, and Stewardship (of the Earth). The peace testimony, dating to a 1660 declaration to Charles II, leads Friends to refuse participation in war; the testimony of integrity underlies the Quaker tradition of plain speech and refusal of oaths; the equality testimony motivated early Quaker opposition to slavery and advocacy of women's ministry; and the testimony of simplicity discourages ostentation and excess consumption.

Historical development

The testimonies grew out of the practices of the first Friends in the 1650s — refusing hat honor, using "thee" and "thou" to all, and declining tithes and oaths — for which many were imprisoned. Over the centuries they have generated distinctive Quaker contributions to prison reform, abolition, conscientious objection, humanitarian relief, and environmental stewardship.
