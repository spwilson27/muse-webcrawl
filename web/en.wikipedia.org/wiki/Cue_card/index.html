Cue card


Cue cards held by a production assistant during a television taping

Cue cards, also known as idiot cards or idiot boards, are cards with words written on them that help actors and speakers remember what they have to say. They are typically used in television productions where they can be held off-camera so as to appear invisible to the audience. Cue cards are most commonly used for television work, and today are often replaced by the teleprompter, though they remain in use where a teleprompter is impractical, such as for some live stage work, public speaking, and certain comedy shows.

History

Cue cards came into widespread use with early television in the late 1940s and early 1950s. Because early television was broadcast live, and because the medium borrowed heavily from theatre and radio, performers often had to memorise large amounts of new material quickly. Barney McNulty, a CBS page and former military officer, is generally credited with popularising the practice of writing lines on card stock and holding them up beside the camera. The practice was quickly adopted by hosts such as Bob Hope, who made cue cards a signature part of his act, and by talk-show hosts who conducted interviews and delivered monologues.

Use

Cue cards are usually written in black marker or printed in large type on stiff boards, roughly the size of a placard. A cue-card operator, sometimes called a "cue card girl" or "idiot card holder" in old industry slang, holds the cards in sequence next to the camera lens and flips through them as the speaker progresses. For a live audience show, several sets may be prepared so that cards can be placed near each camera position.

In popular culture

The comedian Bob Hope was famously dependent on cue cards throughout his long career. Saturday Night Live and other sketch and talk shows continue to use cue cards as a backup to teleprompters; The Tonight Show host Jimmy Fallon and others have been shown joking about relying on them. The phrase "idiot card" also survived in British variety theatre usage.
