The Miocene is the first geological epoch of the Neogene Period and extends from about 23.03 to 5.333 million years ago (Ma). The Miocene was named by Scottish geologist Charles Lyell; its name comes from the Greek μείων (meiōn, "less") and καινός (kainos, "new") and means "less recent" because it has 18% fewer modern sea invertebrates than the Pliocene has. The Miocene is preceded by the Oligocene and is followed by the Pliocene.

Climate

As Earth went from the Oligocene through the Miocene and into the Pliocene, the climate slowly cooled towards a series of ice ages. The early Miocene saw a warming phase culminating in the Mid-Miocene Climatic Optimum (about 17–14.5 Ma), when global temperatures were several degrees warmer than today and Antarctic ice was reduced. A major cooling step, the Middle Miocene Climate Transition (~14 Ma), expanded the East Antarctic Ice Sheet. By the late Miocene the climate was cooler and drier, and the first Northern Hemisphere ice sheets began to form.

Life

Grasslands expanded at the expense of forests as the climate dried, driving the evolution of grazing animals. Horses diversified greatly in North America, and elephants, apes and many modern mammal families spread across the continents. The Miocene saw the first appearance of the great ape radiation and, near its end, early hominins such as Sahelanthropus. In the oceans, kelp forests and coral reefs supported diverse marine life, including the giant shark Otodus megalodon. Roughly 95% of modern seed plant families existed by the end of the Miocene.

Subdivisions

The Miocene is divided into six ages (from oldest): Aquitanian, Burdigalian, Langhian, Serravallian, Tortonian and Messinian. The epoch ends with the Messinian salinity crisis, when the Mediterranean Sea nearly dried up, and the Zanclean flood that refilled it marks the start of the Pliocene.
