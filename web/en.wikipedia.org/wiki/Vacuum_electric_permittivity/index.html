Vacuum electric permittivity

Vacuum electric permittivity (ε₀)
Value: 8.8541878188(14)×10⁻¹² F/m
Unit: farad per metre (C²·N⁻¹·m⁻²)
Also called: Electric constant; permittivity of free space

The vacuum electric permittivity, commonly denoted ε₀ (pronounced "epsilon nought" or "epsilon zero"), is the value of the absolute dielectric permittivity of classical electromagnetism's vacuum. It quantifies how an electric field affects, and is affected by, a vacuum, and it appears in Coulomb's law, Gauss's law and the capacitance of structures in free space.

Value

Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units, ε₀ is no longer an exactly defined constant; it is determined experimentally from the fine-structure constant. Its measured value is approximately:

ε₀ ≈ 8.8541878188(14) × 10⁻¹² F·m⁻¹

Historically, when the ampere was defined by the force between current-carrying wires, ε₀ was exactly 1/(μ₀c²) ≈ 8.854187817×10⁻¹² F/m.

Relations

The permittivity of free space is linked to the vacuum magnetic permeability μ₀ and the speed of light c by ε₀μ₀c² = 1, i.e. c = 1/√(ε₀μ₀) — the relation by which Maxwell deduced that light is an electromagnetic wave. It also sets the strength of the electrostatic force in Coulomb's law, F = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r²), and appears in the definition of the electric displacement field D = ε₀E in vacuum. The relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of any material is measured as a multiple of ε₀.

The value of ε₀ can also be expressed through the fine-structure constant: ε₀ = e²/(2αhc), connecting it to quantum electrodynamics and the quantum vacuum.
