The Hawaii Emergency Management Agency (HI-EMA) is the emergency management agency of the U.S. state of Hawaii, housed within the state Department of Defense and led by the adjutant general's organisation. Based in an operations centre inside Diamond Head crater in Honolulu, it coordinates the state's preparation for, response to, and recovery from natural and human-caused disasters, and operates Hawaii's outdoor warning siren system — the largest such system in the United States.

History

The agency descends from Hawaii's territorial civil defense organisation, created in the Second World War era and shaped by the territory's devastating tsunamis (1946 and 1960) and the Cold War missile threat. Its modern form was established as the Hawaii Emergency Management Agency by state statute in 2014, replacing the old Civil Defense Division with a broader all-hazards mandate covering hurricanes (such as Iniki in 1992), volcanic activity on Hawaii Island, flooding, and wildfires.

2018 false missile alert

On 13 January 2018, during a drill, an HI-EMA employee selected the wrong template in the alert system, sending a statewide wireless emergency alert warning of an incoming ballistic missile: "BALLISTIC MISSILE THREAT INBOUND TO HAWAII. SEEK IMMEDIATE SHELTER. THIS IS NOT A DRILL." Because no pre-written false-alarm message existed, a correction took 38 minutes to reach phones, causing widespread panic. The incident — investigated by the FCC and the state — led to the resignation of administrator Vern Miyagi, the firing of the employee involved, new two-person confirmation rules, and reforms echoed in alerting systems nationwide.

Recent operations

HI-EMA has since played central roles in the 2018 Kīlauea eruption and flooding responses, the COVID-19 emergency, and the August 2023 Maui wildfires, after which the agency's warning-siren policies and leadership again came under scrutiny; its administrator resigned amid criticism that sirens were not sounded for the Lahaina fire.
