Rosslyn Chapel

Location: Roslin, Midlothian, Scotland

Founded: 1446

Founder: William Sinclair, 1st Earl of Caithness

Denomination: Scottish Episcopal

Rosslyn Chapel, formally the Collegiate Church of St Matthew, is a fifteenth-century chapel in the village of Roslin, Midlothian, Scotland, about seven miles south of Edinburgh. Founded in 1446 by William Sinclair, 1st Earl of Caithness, it is celebrated for the extraordinary richness of its stone carving — hundreds of figures, plants, and symbols that have inspired centuries of legend, including proposed links to the Knights Templar and the Holy Grail.

Architecture

The chapel is only the choir of a much larger cruciform collegiate church that was never completed; building stopped at William Sinclair's death in 1484. Its interior is densely carved: biblical scenes, angels, the Seven Deadly Sins facing the Seven Virtues, a Dance of Death, and over a hundred "Green Men" foliate heads. Some carvings appear to depict New World plants such as maize and aloe, carved before Columbus's voyage — a claim historians treat cautiously, regarding them as stylised wheat, strawberries, or lilies.

The most famous feature is the Apprentice Pillar, a richly spiralling column. Legend holds that the master mason, having travelled to Rome for the design, returned to find his apprentice had completed the pillar himself, and killed the apprentice in a jealous rage with a blow to the head — an echo, some have claimed, of the Masonic legend of Hiram Abiff.

History and legend

After the Scottish Reformation the chapel fell out of use and into decay; Oliver Cromwell's troops stabled horses there in 1650. Queen Victoria's visit in 1842 prompted a Victorian restoration. From the 1980s onward, speculative literature connected the chapel with Freemasonry, the Templars, and buried treasure or the Grail; its role in Dan Brown's 2003 novel The Da Vinci Code and the 2006 film greatly increased visitor numbers. A major conservation programme in the 2000s, including a temporary canopy that dried out the interior, restored the stonework, and a visitor centre opened in 2011. The chapel remains a working Scottish Episcopal church.
