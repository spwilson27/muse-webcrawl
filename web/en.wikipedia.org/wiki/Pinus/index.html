Pinus

A pine (Pinus sp.) with mature cones

Pinus, the pines, is a genus of coniferous trees in the family Pinaceae and the only genus in the subfamily Pinoideae. The World Flora Online accepts around 187 species names, with roughly 120 species commonly recognised. Pines are native throughout most of the Northern Hemisphere, from the Arctic Circle to Central America, North Africa and Southeast Asia, and have been planted worldwide.

Description

Pines are evergreen, resinous trees (rarely shrubs), ranging from small species such as the Siberian dwarf pine to giants over 80 metres tall such as the sugar pine. Their needle-like leaves grow in clusters (fascicles) of two, three or five, a character used to divide the genus into subgenera. Pines are monoecious, bearing separate male and female cones on the same tree; the woody seed cones mature over two or three years, and seeds are typically winged and wind-dispersed, though some species depend on birds or fire to release them.

Ecology

Many pines are adapted to poor, dry or cold soils where other trees struggle, and several are fire-adapted, with serotinous cones that open only after a blaze. Some of the longest-lived organisms on Earth are pines: Great Basin bristlecone pines (Pinus longaeva) in California and Nevada exceed 4,800 years of age.

Uses

Pines are among the most commercially important trees in the world, grown in plantations for softwood timber (often simply called "pine"), pulp and paper. They also yield pine nuts, resin, turpentine and tar, and are widely planted as ornamentals and Christmas trees. Scots pine, Monterey pine (radiata), loblolly and slash pines are especially significant plantation species; outside their native ranges some have become invasive.
