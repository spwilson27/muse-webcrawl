Visual cortex

The visual cortex of the brain is the area of the cerebral cortex that processes visual information. It is located in the occipital lobe. Sensory input originating from the eyes travels through the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and then reaches the visual cortex. The area that receives the sensory information directly is called the primary visual cortex, also called visual area 1 (V1), the striate cortex, or Brodmann area 17.

Primary visual cortex (V1)

V1 is organised retinotopically: neighbouring neurons respond to neighbouring parts of the visual field, with a disproportionately large representation of the fovea (cortical magnification). In the 1960s David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel showed that V1 neurons respond selectively to edges of particular orientations, arranged in orientation columns; they later received the Nobel Prize for this work. V1 also contains blobs sensitive to colour.

Extrastriate areas

Beyond V1, processing continues in areas V2, V3, V4 and V5/MT. Two broad pathways emerge: the ventral stream ("what" pathway), running into the temporal lobe and involved in object recognition, including the colour-sensitive V4; and the dorsal stream ("where/how" pathway), running into the parietal lobe and involved in spatial awareness and motion, including area MT/V5, specialised for motion perception.

Clinical significance

Damage to V1 produces scotomas or cortical blindness, although some residual unconscious visual capacity ("blindsight") may remain. Lesions of the ventral stream can cause visual agnosias such as prosopagnosia (inability to recognise faces), while dorsal lesions can produce akinetopsia (inability to perceive motion).
