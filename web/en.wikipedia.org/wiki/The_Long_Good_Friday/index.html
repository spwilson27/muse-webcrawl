The Long Good Friday is a 1980 British gangster film directed by John Mackenzie from a screenplay by Barrie Keeffe. Bob Hoskins stars as Harold Shand, an East End gangster who aspires to become a legitimate businessman through a huge London Docklands redevelopment deal with the American Mafia — just as, over one Good Friday, an unseen enemy begins murdering his associates and bombing his businesses.

Plot

Shand, returning from a trip to America with his girlfriend Victoria (Helen Mirren), is preparing to host the Mafia emissary Charlie (Eddie Constantine). Meanwhile a bomb damages his casino and destroys his pub, his chauffeur and his oldest friend are killed, and his mother is targeted at church. Only late in the day does Shand learn the attacks are the work of the IRA, retaliating because one of his lieutenants had double-crossed them over an arms payment. The final shot — a long, near-silent close-up of Hoskins in the back of a car driven by an IRA gunman (an early role for Pierce Brosnan) — is famous as one of British cinema's great endings.

Reception and legacy

The film premiered at the London Film Festival in 1980, though its release was delayed by its owner Lew Grade's displeasure at its violence and politics. It is now regarded as one of the finest British films of its era — the British Film Institute ranked it 21st in its list of the top 100 British films — and its vision of Docklands redevelopment and moneyed criminality proved prescient of 1980s London.
