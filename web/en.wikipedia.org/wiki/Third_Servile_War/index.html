Third Servile War

Date73–71 BCE
LocationRoman Italy
Rebel leadersSpartacus, Crixus, Oenomaus, Gannicus, Castus
Roman commandersGaius Claudius Glaber, Publius Varinius, Marcus Licinius Crassus, Pompey, Lucullus
ResultRevolt suppressed; mass crucifixions along the Appian Way

The Third Servile War (73–71 BCE), also called the Spartacus revolt or Gladiator War, was the last and most famous of the major slave uprisings against the Roman Republic. It began with the escape of gladiators from a training school at Capua and grew into a mobile army that defeated Roman forces across Italy for two years. Its suppression by Marcus Licinius Crassus, with Pompey arriving to share credit, exposed both the violence of Roman slavery and the Republic’s gathering crisis of military strongmen.

Outbreak

In 73 BCE about seventy gladiators seized kitchen tools and weapons and escaped from the ludus of Lentulus Batiatus at Capua. Ancient sources name Spartacus, a Thracian, and the Gauls Crixus and Oenomaus among the leaders. The fugitives took refuge on Mount Vesuvius and defeated a hurried force under Gaius Claudius Glaber by descending the mountain on ropes of vines and attacking from an unexpected side. Slaves, herdsmen and rural poor joined as news spread and Roman detachments under Publius Varinius were beaten in turn.

The rebels’ aims are hard to reconstruct because all surviving narratives are Roman or later. Escape from Italy across the Alps, plunder and survival, revenge, and perhaps for some a return home, likely mattered more than a programme to abolish slavery. Ancient authors present Spartacus as comparatively humane and farsighted; modern historians read such praise cautiously, as a literary device that also sharpens Roman disgrace at being humbled by slaves.

Campaigns in Italy

The rebel army—tens of thousands at its height by uncertain ancient estimates—moved through Campania, Lucania and Bruttium. Crixus separated with a largely Gallic and Germanic force and was defeated and killed by Lucius Gellius near Mount Garganus in 72 BCE. Spartacus defeated consular armies and reached Cisalpine Gaul, where he defeated Gaius Cassius Longinus near Mutina. Why he then turned south instead of crossing the Alps remains debated: sources speak of followers preferring plunder in Italy, strategic hesitation or pressure from events.

In the toe of Italy Spartacus negotiated with Cilician pirates for passage to Sicily; the pirates took payment and left. Crassus, given command with extraordinary resources, tried to wall off the rebels across the Bruttian peninsula. Spartacus broke out, but discipline and unity were fraying. Separate forces under Gannicus and Castus were destroyed. In 71 BCE Spartacus faced Crassus in a final battle, probably in Lucania near the river Silarus; he was killed, though his body was reportedly never found.

Aftermath

Pompey, returning from Spain, annihilated a fleeing group and wrote to the Senate that while Crassus had defeated the slaves in battle, he had torn the war out by the roots—a boast that infuriated Crassus. About 6,000 captured rebels were crucified along the Via Appia from Capua to Rome as a spectacle of deterrence. Crassus and Pompey both sought a triumph or ovation and advanced toward the joint consulship of 70 BCE, accelerating the pattern by which armies made political careers.

Legacy

No later Roman slave revolt matched its scale. Spartacus became a symbol in modern politics and art—for Marxists, abolitionists, nationalists and filmmakers—as an emblem of resistance by the oppressed. Howard Fast’s novel and the 1960 film Spartacus fixed a heroic image in popular culture. Historians balance that image against the fragmentary evidence: a revolt formidable because Roman Italy was full of enslaved people and because its leaders learned to defeat Rome at war, not because it left a manifesto.
