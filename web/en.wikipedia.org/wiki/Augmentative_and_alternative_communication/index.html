Augmentative and alternative communication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) encompasses the communication methods used to supplement or replace speech or writing for those with impairments in the production or comprehension of spoken or written language. AAC is used by people with a wide range of congenital conditions (such as cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability) and acquired conditions (such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, stroke and traumatic brain injury).

Types of AAC

Unaided AAC uses no external equipment: manual signs, gestures, facial expressions and vocalisations. Aided AAC uses tools ranging from low-tech picture boards, communication books and alphabet boards to high-tech speech-generating devices (SGDs) — dedicated devices or tablet apps that produce synthesised or digitised speech. Access may be by direct pointing, eye-gaze tracking, head pointers, or switch scanning for users with severe motor impairments.

Symbols and language systems

Many AAC systems use graphic symbols, such as Picture Communication Symbols (PCS), SymbolStix, or the open-source ARASAAC set, organised by vocabulary frameworks like Unity/Minspeak, PODD (Pragmatic Organisation Dynamic Display) or core-word boards emphasising a small set of high-frequency words usable across contexts.

Assessment and outcomes

AAC assessment is typically led by a speech-language pathologist with an interdisciplinary team, matching technology to the user's motor, sensory, cognitive and linguistic abilities. Research indicates that AAC does not hinder speech development and may facilitate it; outcomes depend heavily on partner training and opportunities to communicate.

History

The field coalesced in the 1970s–1980s with the founding of the International Society for Augmentative and Alternative Communication (ISAAC) in 1983. Stephen Hawking's computer-based voice system became its most famous example. Controversial facilitated communication is regarded as pseudoscientific by major professional bodies and is distinguished from legitimate AAC.
