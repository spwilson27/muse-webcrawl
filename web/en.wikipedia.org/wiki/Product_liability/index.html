Product liability

Product liability is the area of law in which manufacturers, distributors, suppliers and retailers are held responsible for injuries caused by defective products they place into the stream of commerce. Claims typically rest on three theories: negligence, breach of warranty, and strict liability in tort. Most jurisdictions recognise three categories of defect: manufacturing defects, design defects, and defects in marketing, such as inadequate instructions or warnings.

Historical development

At common law the doctrine of privity, exemplified by Winterbottom v. Wright (1842), barred injured consumers from suing manufacturers with whom they had no contract. New York's decision in MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. (1916), written by Judge Benjamin Cardozo, abolished the privity barrier for negligence where a defective product was reasonably certain to endanger others. Justice Roger Traynor's concurrence in Escola v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co. (1944) argued for liability without fault, a position adopted by the California Supreme Court in Greenman v. Yuba Power Products (1963) and generalised in section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts (1964–65), which imposed strict liability on commercial sellers of products in a defective condition unreasonably dangerous to users.

Theories and defects

A manufacturing defect exists when a product departs from its intended design; a design defect when the foreseeable risks of the whole product line could have been reduced by a reasonable alternative design, judged by consumer-expectations or risk–utility tests; and a warning defect when foreseeable risks are not adequately disclosed. Defendants in the chain of distribution may all be liable under strict liability, subject to doctrines such as comparative fault, state-of-the-art defences, and statutes of repose. The Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability (1998) reorganised the field, notably requiring proof of a reasonable alternative design for most design claims.

Comparative regimes

The European Union's Product Liability Directive (85/374/EEC), implemented in the United Kingdom by the Consumer Protection Act 1987, imposes strict liability for defective products with a development-risks defence, and was modernised by a new directive adopted in 2024 covering software and digital products. Mass product litigation — over asbestos, tobacco, the Ford Pinto fuel tank, silicone breast implants, Vioxx, opioids and talc — has driven the growth of class actions and multidistrict litigation, particularly in the United States, and recurring political debates about tort reform and punitive damages.
