Stellar parallax

Stellar parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby star against distant background stars as Earth orbits the Sun, used to measure stellar distances. The parallax angle p is half the annual angular shift; distance in parsecs equals 1/p when p is in arcseconds — one parsec is about 3.26 light-years.

History

The absence of observable parallax was an argument against heliocentrism from antiquity through Tycho Brahe: if Earth moved, stars should shift, implying either Earth was still or the stars were immensely distant. Friedrich Bessel first succeeded in 1838, measuring 61 Cygni at about 0.314 arcseconds (roughly 10.4 light-years); Thomas Henderson (Alpha Centauri) and Friedrich Struve (Vega) followed shortly.

Space astrometry

Ground-based parallax is limited by atmospheric seeing to about 0.01 arcsecond. The Hipparcos satellite (1989–93) measured over 100,000 stars to milliarcsecond precision; its successor Gaia (launched 2013) has measured positions and parallaxes for nearly two billion stars, revolutionising the cosmic distance ladder. Parallax remains the only direct, geometric method of measuring stellar distances and calibrates all others. Related measurement puzzle: the solar neutrino problem.
