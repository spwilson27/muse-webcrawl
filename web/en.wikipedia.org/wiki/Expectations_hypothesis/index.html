Expectations hypothesis

The expectations hypothesis (or expectations theory) of the term structure of interest rates holds that the long-term interest rate is determined by the market's expectations of future short-term interest rates. In its pure form, it states that the expected total return from holding a long-term bond to maturity equals the expected return from rolling over a sequence of short-term bonds over the same period — so that long rates are an average (in geometric terms) of current and expected future short rates, with no term premium.

Implications

If the expectations hypothesis holds, the slope of the yield curve reveals the market's expectations: an upward-sloping curve implies that investors expect short-term rates to rise, while an inverted curve implies expected declines. Forward rates would then be unbiased predictors of future spot rates.

Empirical evidence

Extensive empirical work, notably by Eugene Fama and by John Campbell and Robert Shiller, has generally rejected the pure expectations hypothesis: forward rates and yield spreads predict excess bond returns rather than only future rate changes, indicating time-varying term premia. Modified versions of the theory allow a constant or slowly varying term premium and remain foundational in finance and monetary economics, underpinning how central banks interpret the yield curve as a signal of market expectations for policy and growth.
