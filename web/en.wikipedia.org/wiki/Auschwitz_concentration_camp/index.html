Auschwitz was a complex of over 40 concentration and extermination camps operated by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland (in an area annexed by Germany in 1939) during the Second World War and the Holocaust. It consisted of Auschwitz I, the main camp (Stammlager) in Oświęcim; Auschwitz II-Birkenau, a concentration and extermination camp with gas chambers; and Auschwitz III-Monowitz, a labour camp for the IG Farben chemical conglomerate, along with numerous subcamps.

History

Established in 1940 initially for Polish political prisoners, Auschwitz grew into the largest of the Nazi camps. From early 1942 it became the principal site of the mass murder of Europe's Jews: transports arrived by rail from across the continent, and most deportees were killed in gas chambers using Zyklon B shortly after selection on the Birkenau ramp. Registered prisoners endured forced labour, starvation, disease and medical experiments, including those of Josef Mengele. Of the at least 1.3 million people deported to Auschwitz, at least 1.1 million were murdered — around a million Jews, about 74,000 Poles, 21,000 Roma, 15,000 Soviet prisoners of war and thousands of others.

Liberation and remembrance

As Soviet forces approached, the SS evacuated some 58,000 prisoners on death marches in mid-January 1945 and blew up the crematoria. The Red Army liberated the camp on 27 January 1945, finding about 7,000 surviving prisoners. In 1947 Poland established the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum on the site; in 1979 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List under the World Heritage Convention. The name "Auschwitz" has become a universal symbol of the Holocaust and of crimes against humanity.
