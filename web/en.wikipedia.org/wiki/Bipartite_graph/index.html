Bipartite graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a bipartite graph (or bigraph) is a graph whose vertices can be divided into two disjoint and independent sets U and V such that every edge connects a vertex in U to one in V. Equivalently, a bipartite graph is a graph that contains no odd-length cycles, and is exactly a graph whose chromatic number is at most two.

Examples

Every tree is bipartite, as are all even cycles (cycles of even length) and all hypercubes. Königsberg-style problems aside, familiar examples include the incidence graph of points and lines in finite geometries and the "utility graph" K3,3, the complete bipartite graph with three vertices in each part, famous from the three utilities problem.

Characterization and properties

By Kőnig's theorem, a graph is bipartite if and only if it contains no odd cycle. A complete bipartite graph Km,n has all possible edges between parts of size m and n. In bipartite graphs, the size of a minimum vertex cover equals the size of a maximum matching (Kőnig's theorem on bipartite matching), and Hall's marriage theorem gives a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a matching that covers one part.

Applications

Bipartite graphs model matching problems throughout computer science and operations research: assigning workers to jobs, applicants to schools, or advertisers to keywords, where the Hopcroft–Karp algorithm finds maximum matchings efficiently. They also appear in coding theory (Tanner graphs) and in the analysis of recommendation systems.
