Peace testimony

The peace testimony is one of the best-known testimonies of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers). It expresses the belief that violence and war are contrary to the will of God and to the teachings of Jesus, and it commits Friends to nonviolence in personal conduct and to working for peace in society. Although the wording has varied over the centuries, the peace testimony has shaped Quaker positions on military service, arms, capital punishment and social reform.

Historical roots

The testimony is most famously expressed in the 1660 declaration presented to King Charles II of England, in which early Quakers stated: "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end, or under any pretence whatsoever." The declaration was partly intended to distance Friends from violent plots against the restored monarchy, but it articulated a conviction grounded in their reading of the New Testament and in the experience of the "Inward Light" of Christ.

Practice

Because of the peace testimony, many Quakers have been conscientious objectors to military service, and during both World Wars Friends organised alternative service through bodies such as the Friends' Ambulance Unit and the American Friends Service Committee (AFSC), founded in 1917. The AFSC and the British Friends Service Council received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947 on behalf of Quakers worldwide. The testimony extends beyond refusal to fight: Friends have campaigned for disarmament, against the arms trade, and for nonviolent approaches to conflict, and many understand it as requiring the removal of the causes of war, including injustice and poverty.

Variation

Not all Quakers interpret the testimony in the same way. Some serve in the armed forces, and liberal, conservative and evangelical branches of the Society of Friends place differing emphasis on it. Nevertheless, the peace testimony remains a defining feature of Quaker identity and witness.
