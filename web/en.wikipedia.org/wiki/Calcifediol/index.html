Calcifediol, also known as calcidiol or 25-hydroxyvitamin D (abbreviated 25(OH)D), is a form of vitamin D produced in the liver by hydroxylation of vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). It is the main circulating form of vitamin D and the form measured in blood tests to assess a person's vitamin D status.

Physiology

Vitamin D obtained from the skin or diet is converted in the liver, principally by the enzyme CYP2R1, into calcifediol. Calcifediol is then converted in the kidneys by CYP27B1 into calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), the biologically active hormonal form that regulates calcium and phosphate metabolism. Because calcifediol has a much longer half-life (about two to three weeks) than calcitriol, its serum concentration is used clinically as the marker of vitamin D sufficiency.

Medical use

Calcifediol is used as a medication (for example, extended-release formulations) to treat secondary hyperparathyroidism in adults with stage 3 or 4 chronic kidney disease and vitamin D insufficiency, and in some countries for vitamin D deficiency. Compared with ordinary cholecalciferol supplements, calcifediol raises serum 25(OH)D more rapidly and predictably because it bypasses hepatic hydroxylation.
