Inverted sugar syrup

Inverted sugar syrup is an edible mixture of the monosaccharides glucose and fructose, produced by hydrolysing the disaccharide sucrose. The name refers to the reversal ("inversion") of the direction in which the solution rotates polarised light after hydrolysis. Common commercial forms include golden syrup and some treacles; honey is a natural analogue rich in fructose.

Production and properties

Inversion is carried out by heating sucrose syrup with a small amount of acid (such as cream of tartar or citric acid) or with the enzyme invertase. Because fructose is sweeter and more hygroscopic than sucrose, invert syrup tastes sweeter and helps baked goods and confectionery retain moisture. It also resists crystallisation, which makes it valuable in fondants, ganache, sorbets and jams; brewers and beekeepers use it as well. Highly inverted syrups are sometimes called "trimoline" in professional pâtisserie.

Composition

"Partially inverted" syrups retain some sucrose alongside glucose and fructose; fully inverted syrup consists of roughly equal parts glucose and fructose — similar in composition to high-fructose corn syrup, though made from cane or beet sugar rather than corn starch.
