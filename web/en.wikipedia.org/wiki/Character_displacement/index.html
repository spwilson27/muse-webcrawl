Character displacement

Character displacement is the evolutionary process by which two similar species that compete for the same resources diverge in traits — such as beak size, body size, or feeding behaviour — when they live together (in sympatry), reducing competition between them. First clearly articulated by William L. Brown and Edward O. Wilson in 1956, it predicts that competing species should be more different in appearance or resource use where their ranges overlap than where each occurs alone (in allopatry).

Traits diverge where competitors co-occur

Evidence

Classic examples come from Darwin's finches in the Galápagos: ground finch species have more divergent beak sizes on islands where they co-occur than on islands where each lives alone, matching differences in the seed sizes they can crack. Long-term field studies by Peter and Rosemary Grant documented beak-size shifts in Geospiza fortis after the larger G. magnirostris established on Daphne Major, especially after a drought intensified competition for large seeds. Other well-supported cases involve stickleback fish, Anolis lizards, snails, and carnivores.

Criteria and criticism

Because similar patterns can arise from ecological sorting or chance, researchers require several criteria before attributing divergence to character displacement: the trait differences must exceed chance expectations, relate to resource use, be greater in sympatry than allopatry, occur at sites alike in other respects, and have a heritable basis. Alternative processes, such as a species simply failing to colonize where a too-similar competitor lives, can mimic the pattern and are controlled for in modern comparative studies.

Related processes

Character displacement can also be driven by reproduction rather than resources: where two species risk costly hybridization, selection favours divergence in mating signals or mate recognition, a process called reproductive character displacement or reinforcement.
