Industry 4.0

A "smart factory" illustrating the Industry 4.0 vision

Industry 4.0 (German: Industrie 4.0) refers to the digital transformation of manufacturing and related industries through the integration of cyber-physical systems, the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, big data and artificial intelligence into production. The term was coined as part of a strategic initiative of the German federal government, publicised at the Hannover Fair in 2011, and describes a "fourth industrial revolution" following mechanisation (steam power), mass production (electricity and assembly lines) and computerised automation.

Principles

Four design principles are commonly identified: interoperability, the ability of machines, devices and people to connect and communicate via the IoT; information transparency, in which sensor data builds a virtual copy—a digital twin—of the physical factory; technical assistance, where systems support humans with information and, in the form of collaborative robots, with physical tasks; and decentralised decisions, in which cyber-physical systems make simple decisions autonomously and report exceptions upward. Consequences sought include mass customisation, predictive maintenance, flexible small-batch production and integration along the whole value chain.

Technologies

Enabling technologies include industrial sensors and actuators, industrial robots and cobots, additive manufacturing (3D printing), augmented reality for maintenance and training, simulation and digital twins, horizontal and vertical system integration, cloud and edge computing, cybersecurity for operational technology, and data analytics and machine learning. Related national programmes include "Made in China 2025", the US Industrial Internet Consortium and Japan's "Society 5.0" concept.

Criticism and challenges

Critics note the vagueness of the term and its use in marketing, the cost and skills barriers for small and medium-sized manufacturers, concerns about employment displacement and job quality, and the security risks of connecting previously isolated industrial control systems to networks.
