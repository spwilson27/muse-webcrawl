Asuka, Nara

Asuka (明日香村, Asuka-mura) is a village located in Takaichi District, Nara Prefecture, Japan. It occupies the area that was the site of several imperial capitals of Japan during the period from 538 to 710 CE, which takes its name — the Asuka period — from this place. The whole village is preserved as a historic landscape, and development is strictly limited by special legislation.

History

During the Asuka period the imperial court moved among a succession of palaces within the Asuka basin, including the Toyura, Oharida, Okamoto, Asuka Itabuki and Asuka Kiyomihara palaces. It was here that Buddhism was officially introduced to Japan, that the Taika Reforms were set in motion, and that Prince Shōtoku promulgated the Seventeen-Article Constitution. After the capital moved to Fujiwara-kyō and then Heijō-kyō (Nara), Asuka reverted to countryside, preserving its ancient remains beneath farmland.

Archaeological sites

Asuka is famous for its megalithic stone structures and ancient tombs (kofun), including the Ishibutai Kofun, a huge stone burial chamber traditionally identified as the tomb of Soga no Umako; the Takamatsuzuka Tomb, whose vivid wall paintings of court ladies and the four directional deities were discovered in 1972; and the Kitora Tomb, with its astronomical ceiling chart. Other sites include the Asuka-dera temple, home to the oldest great Buddha statue in Japan (the Asuka Daibutsu, 609), and the carved stones such as the Sakafune-ishi and the "turtle stone".

Preservation

The Ancient Capitals Preservation Law of 1966 and the Asuka Law of 1980 designate the entire village for conservation, compensating residents for restrictions on construction. The village is a candidate, together with related sites in the Asuka-Fujiwara area, for inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
