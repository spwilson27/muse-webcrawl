Screw

A typical screw, showing the head, unthreaded shank and helical thread.

A screw is an externally helical threaded fastener, usually made of metal, used to hold objects together or to position them. Screws are typically turned into a material with a screwdriver, wrench, or other tool, and the helical thread converts rotational motion into linear motion while generating a strong clamping force. Screws are one of the six classical simple machines.

Type: Fastener / simple machine

Components: Head, shank, thread, tip

Materials: Steel, stainless steel, brass, titanium, plastic

Drive types: Slotted, Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx, hex socket, Robertson

Description

A screw consists of a head, which is engaged by the driving tool, a cylindrical or tapered shaft called the shank, and a helical ridge called the thread that wraps around the shaft. The pointed tip allows the screw to penetrate material, and as the screw is rotated the thread advances it into the substrate. Unlike a bolt, which is generally used with a nut, a screw usually forms its own mating thread in the material it enters, or is used in a pre-tapped hole.

History

The principle of the screw was known in antiquity; the water screw is attributed to Archimedes in the 3rd century BC. Metal screws for fastening were rare until the late Middle Ages, when they were used in clocks and armour, and became cheap only with the development of mass-production lathes and thread-cutting machinery in the late 18th and 19th centuries. Standardisation of thread forms, such as the Whitworth thread (1841) and later metric ISO threads, made screws interchangeable worldwide.

Types

Common varieties include wood screws, machine screws, self-tapping screws, sheet metal screws, drywall screws, set screws (grub screws), lag screws, and threaded inserts. Screws are distinguished by thread pitch, diameter (gauge), length, head shape (flat, pan, round, oval, hex, button), and drive type.

Mechanics

The screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. The mechanical advantage depends on the pitch of the thread: a finer pitch requires more turns but produces greater axial force for a given torque. Friction in the thread makes most screws self-locking, so they do not unwind under load.
