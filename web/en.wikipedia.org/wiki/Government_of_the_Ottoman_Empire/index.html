Government of the Ottoman Empire

The government of the Ottoman Empire was centred on the sultan, who combined the roles of head of state, head of government (in the early centuries) and caliph of Islam (from 1517). The central administration was known as the Sublime Porte, a name derived from the gate of the Grand Vizier's offices, and operated from Topkapı Palace and later the palace complexes of Istanbul. Over six centuries the system evolved from a patrimonial household administration into a modern bureaucratic state, and finally — after 1876 — a constitutional monarchy.

The sultan and the imperial council

Ultimate authority rested with the sultan of the House of Osman, whose laws (kanun) complemented Islamic law (sharia). From the fifteenth century, day-to-day government was exercised by the Grand Vizier (sadrazam), the sultan's absolute deputy and holder of the imperial seal, presiding over the Imperial Council (Divan-ı Hümayun). The Divan included the viziers, the chief judges of Rumelia and Anatolia (kazaskers), the treasurers (defterdars) and the chancellor (nişancı); the Şeyhülislam, chief mufti, stood at the head of the religious establishment and could authorise — or, by fatwa, legitimise the deposition of — sultans.

Provincial administration

The empire was divided into provinces (eyalets, later vilayets) governed by beylerbeys and, after the 1864 Vilayet Law, by valis under a codified hierarchy of provinces, sanjaks, kazas and nahiyes. Classical land revenue was organised through the timar system, in which cavalrymen (sipahis) held conditional grants of tax revenue in return for military service. Non-Muslim religious communities — Orthodox Christians, Armenians, Jews and others — were organised in the millet system, which gave communal leaders substantial autonomy in personal-status and communal affairs under imperial oversight.

Reform and constitutional era

The Tanzimat reforms (1839–1876) created ministries on European lines, secular law codes and courts, provincial councils and a central Council of State. The constitution of 1876 (Kanun-ı Esasi) established an elected Chamber of Deputies, suspended by Abdülhamid II in 1878 and restored by the Young Turk Revolution of 1908. The Committee of Union and Progress dominated government after 1913. The sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922, ending the Ottoman state; the caliphate followed in 1924 under the Republic of Turkey.
