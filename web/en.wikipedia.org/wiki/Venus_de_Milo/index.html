The Venus de Milo (French: Vénus de Milo) is an ancient Greek marble statue of the goddess Aphrodite, created around 150–125 BCE and regarded as one of the most famous works of Hellenistic sculpture. It stands 203 cm tall, is missing both arms, and is displayed in the Louvre Museum in Paris.

Discovery

The statue was unearthed in fragments on 8 April 1820 by a peasant, Yorgos Kentrotas, near the ruins of the ancient city on the island of Milos (Melos) in the Cyclades. After a scramble involving French naval officers and the Ottoman authorities, it was acquired for France and presented to Louis XVIII in 1821, reaching the Louvre the same year. An inscribed plinth naming the sculptor, Alexandros of Antioch, disappeared after the statue's arrival and its association was later disputed, partly because the Louvre initially promoted the work as classical (5th–4th century BCE) rather than Hellenistic.

Description and missing arms

The figure stands in a relaxed contrapposto with drapery slipping from her hips, a type derived from the Aphrodite of Knidos by Praxiteles and the Capitoline Venus. Fragments found nearby included a left hand holding an apple — which would suit Melos (Greek mēlon, apple) — but whether it belongs to the statue remains unresolved. Reconstructions have variously posed her spinning, holding a shield, or grasping her drapery. The statue originally bore painted decoration and metal jewellery.

Fame

Promoted by the Louvre partly to compensate for the return of the Medici Venus to Italy, the Venus de Milo became an icon of classical beauty, praised by artists and endlessly reproduced. During the Second World War it was evacuated to the Château de Valençay for safekeeping.
