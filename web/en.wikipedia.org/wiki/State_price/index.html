State price

State-contingent claims in an Arrow–Debreu economy.

In financial economics, a state price, also called an Arrow–Debreu price, is the price of a security that pays exactly one unit of the numeraire if a particular state of the world occurs at a specified future time, and nothing otherwise. The collection of state prices across all possible states summarizes all information about asset prices in a complete market.

The concept originates in the work of Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu on general equilibrium theory in the 1950s, where securities were defined over "states of nature." State prices are fundamental to the theory of no-arbitrage pricing and the fundamental theorems of asset pricing.

Definition

Suppose there are S possible states of the world at time T. A state security for state s is a contract paying 1 if state s occurs and 0 otherwise. Its price today is the state price, usually denoted πs. Because a payoff of 1 in every state is a risk-free payoff, the sum of all state prices equals the price of a risk-free bond maturing at T:

Σs πs = 1 / (1 + r)

where r is the risk-free rate. Dividing each state price by the bond price gives the corresponding risk-neutral probability of that state.

Use in pricing

Any asset whose payoff in state s is xs can be priced as the sum of its payoffs weighted by the state prices:

price = Σs πs · xs

State prices therefore act as stochastic discount factors resolved state by state. In continuous models, such state price densities underlie the pricing kernel and the change of measure to the risk-neutral probability measure. In practice, state prices can be recovered from option prices: as shown by Breeden and Litzenberger (1978), the state price density is proportional to the second derivative of the call option price with respect to the strike.
