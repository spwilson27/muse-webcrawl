Collision-induced dissociation

Collision-induced dissociation
Synonym: Collisionally activated dissociation (CAD)
Method: Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS)
Collision gases: Argon, nitrogen, helium

Collision-induced dissociation (CID), also known as collisionally activated dissociation, is a mass spectrometry technique in which selected ions are fragmented by collisions with neutral gas molecules. It is the most widely used fragmentation method in tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) for identifying and structurally characterising molecules.

Mechanism

In a typical experiment, a precursor ion is isolated, accelerated to a defined kinetic energy, and passed into a collision cell filled with an inert gas such as argon or nitrogen. Collisions convert part of the ion's translational energy into internal energy, raising it above bond dissociation thresholds so that it breaks into product ions, which are then mass-analysed. In instruments such as ion traps, repeated low-energy collisions accumulate energy gradually, a regime sometimes called resonant excitation.

Applications

CID spectra reveal molecular structure from fragmentation patterns. In proteomics, peptides fragment mainly at amide bonds to give b- and y-type ions, enabling sequence identification by database searching. CID is also routine in small-molecule analysis, metabolomics, forensics, and environmental testing.

Variants

Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD), implemented in Orbitrap instruments, performs CID in a dedicated cell at higher energies and reads out fragments at high mass resolution. Alternative fragmentation methods include electron-transfer and electron-capture dissociation, which complement CID by preserving labile modifications.
