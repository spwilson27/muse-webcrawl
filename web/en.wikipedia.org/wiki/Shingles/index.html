Shingles, medically herpes zoster, is a viral disease characterized by a painful skin rash with blisters in a limited area on one side of the body, often in a stripe. It results from reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (VZV) — the same virus that causes chickenpox — which lies dormant in nerve ganglia after the initial infection.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms may include headache, fever, and malaise, followed by burning, tingling, or pain in the affected area. Within a few days a red rash appears, typically confined to a single dermatome, and develops into fluid-filled blisters that crust over and heal in two to four weeks. The most common serious complication is postherpetic neuralgia, persistent nerve pain lasting months or years after the rash resolves. Involvement of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (herpes zoster ophthalmicus) can threaten vision.

Cause and risk

Only people who have had chickenpox (or, rarely, the chickenpox vaccine) can develop shingles. Reactivation is associated with declining cell-mediated immunity, so risk rises sharply with age; other risk factors include immunosuppressive disease or treatment, and stress. About one in three people develop shingles in their lifetime.

Treatment and prevention

Antiviral drugs such as aciclovir, valaciclovir, and famciclovir, started within 72 hours of the rash appearing, can shorten the duration and severity of the episode and may reduce complications. Pain is managed with analgesics and, for postherpetic neuralgia, agents such as gabapentinoids or tricyclic antidepressants. Vaccination with the recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) is highly effective and is recommended for older and immunocompromised adults in many countries.
