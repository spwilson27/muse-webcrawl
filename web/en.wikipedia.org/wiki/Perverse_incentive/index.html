Perverse incentive

A perverse incentive is an incentive that produces an unintended and undesirable result contrary to the intentions of its designers. The phenomenon is a classic illustration of the law of unintended consequences, and is closely related to Goodhart's law, which observes that a measure used as a target ceases to be a reliable measure.

Examples

The best-known example is the so-called cobra effect: during British rule in India, officials offered a bounty for dead cobras to reduce the snake population of Delhi, but residents began breeding cobras for the reward; when the programme ended, the bred snakes were released, worsening the problem. A similar rat-tail bounty in French colonial Hanoi produced rat farming. Other commonly cited cases include paying surgeons or hospitals by procedure (encouraging overtreatment), the Wells Fargo account scandal, in which aggressive cross-selling targets led employees to open millions of unauthorised accounts, and Soviet factories judged by tonnage producing a few enormous nails instead of many useful small ones.

Analysis

Perverse incentives arise because agents optimise for the measurable reward rather than the underlying goal, and because policy designers cannot always foresee how behaviour will adapt. Mechanism design and careful choice of metrics are proposed remedies.
