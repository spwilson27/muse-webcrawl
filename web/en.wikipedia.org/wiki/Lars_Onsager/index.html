Lars Onsager

Born: November 27, 1903, Kristiania (Oslo), Norway
Died: October 5, 1976, Coral Gables, Florida, U.S.
Known for: Onsager reciprocal relations; exact solution of the 2D Ising model
Awards: Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1968)

Lars Onsager (November 27, 1903 – October 5, 1976) was a Norwegian-born American physical chemist and theoretical physicist. He is best known for the Onsager reciprocal relations — the symmetry of transport coefficients in irreversible thermodynamics — for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1968, and for his exact solution of the two-dimensional Ising model, a landmark of statistical mechanics.

Life and career

Onsager studied chemical engineering at the Norwegian Institute of Technology in Trondheim, graduating in 1925. As a young researcher he corrected Peter Debye's theory of electrolyte solutions, deriving the Onsager limiting law. After time at ETH Zürich and Johns Hopkins University, he joined Yale University in 1928, where he published the reciprocal relations in 1931. The work was so far ahead of its time that it was little appreciated at first; his doctoral dissertation presenting it had earlier been rejected in Norway. Onsager remained at Yale for most of his career, becoming J. Willard Gibbs Professor of Theoretical Chemistry, and also worked on theories of dielectrics, liquid helium, turbulence, and the phase transition of liquid crystals (the Onsager theory of hard rods). He became a U.S. citizen in 1945.

Major contributions

  • Onsager reciprocal relations (1931) — in systems near equilibrium, the matrix of phenomenological transport coefficients (heat conduction, diffusion, electrical conduction, thermoelectricity) is symmetric, a consequence of microscopic reversibility; sometimes called the "fourth law of thermodynamics".
  • Two-dimensional Ising model (1944) — the first exact solution of a model exhibiting a phase transition, showing a logarithmic divergence of the specific heat at the critical temperature.
  • Onsager–Machlup function and foundational work on fluctuation theory in non-equilibrium processes.

Legacy

Onsager's reciprocal relations underpin modern non-equilibrium thermodynamics and earned the 1968 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for the discovery of the reciprocal relations bearing his name, which are fundamental for the thermodynamics of irreversible processes." His Ising solution profoundly influenced the theory of critical phenomena.
