War on drugs

The war on drugs is a global campaign, led by the United States federal government, of drug prohibition, military aid and military intervention, with the aim of reducing the illegal drug trade. The phrase was popularised after a press conference on June 18, 1971, at which President Richard Nixon declared drug abuse to be "public enemy number one".

The campaign includes drug-law enforcement, interdiction of supply, crop eradication overseas, and demand-reduction programmes. It has been credited by supporters with disrupting major trafficking organisations, but critics argue it has produced mass incarceration, racial disparities in enforcement, and high levels of violence in producer and transit countries, while illegal drugs have remained widely available.

History

United States drug prohibition began in the early twentieth century with measures such as the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 and the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, administered by Harry Anslinger's Federal Bureau of Narcotics. The modern campaign dates from the Nixon administration: the Controlled Substances Act of 1970 created the drug schedules, and in 1973 the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) was formed to consolidate federal enforcement.

Under President Ronald Reagan in the 1980s, the campaign escalated sharply. The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 established mandatory minimum sentences, including the controversial sentencing disparity between crack and powder cocaine, and successive administrations expanded interdiction and overseas operations, such as Plan Colombia from 2000. The United States has spent an estimated one trillion dollars or more on drug enforcement since 1971.

Effects and criticism

The policy coincided with a dramatic rise in the United States prison population, with drug offenders a major component. Critics, including the Global Commission on Drug Policy in its 2011 report, have declared the war on drugs a failure and called for decriminalisation and regulation. From the 2010s onward, several U.S. states legalised recreational cannabis, and some countries and cities experimented with decriminalisation or harm-reduction approaches, though federal prohibition remained in place.
