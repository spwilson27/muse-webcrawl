Grammaticalisation

Grammaticalisation (also grammaticalization) is the process of language change by which words with concrete lexical meanings gradually become grammatical markers — auxiliaries, affixes, case markers, articles and the like — typically losing phonological substance and semantic content along the way ("bleaching"). The term was introduced by Antoine Meillet in 1912, and the field was developed by scholars including Jerzy Kuryłowicz, Christian Lehmann, Bernd Heine, Elizabeth Traugott and Paul Hopper. A classic example is the English future marker going to → gonna, from a verb of motion with a purpose clause.

Mechanisms

Hopper identified principles including layering (old and new forms coexist), divergence, specialisation, persistence (traces of the original meaning remain) and decategorialisation (the item loses the morphosyntactic properties of its original category). Lehmann's parameters describe the grammaticalising item's loss of integrity, paradigmaticity and syntagmatic variability. Changes are typically metaphorical and metonymic inferences conventionalised by frequent use: body-part nouns become spatial adpositions, verbs of wanting become future markers (as in English will), and demonstratives become definite articles and complementisers.

Unidirectionality debate

Grammaticalisation is usually held to be largely unidirectional — affixes rarely become free content words again — but claimed cases of "degrammaticalisation" (such as the English suffix -ism becoming a noun) fuel debate about whether the tendency is absolute or statistical. The theory has also been applied beyond language-internal change to the emergence of grammar in creoles and in language evolution.
