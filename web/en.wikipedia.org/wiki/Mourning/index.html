Mourning
Related toGrief, bereavement, funerals
Typical expressionsMourning dress (often black or white), vigils, official mourning periods

Mourning is the outward expression of grief for a death, especially of someone beloved. It encompasses the rituals, clothing, and social conventions through which the bereaved and their community acknowledge the loss. Where grief is the inner emotional response, mourning is its social and cultural performance, and the two do not always coincide.

Customs

Mourning customs vary widely. In much of the West, mourners wear black — an elaborate code of dress, jewellery (jet, hair) and social withdrawal developed in Victorian Britain, partly under the influence of Queen Victoria, who wore mourning for Prince Albert for the rest of her life. In Hindu tradition white is the colour of mourning and a period of ritual observance follows cremation; Chinese mourning historically involved coarse white garments and graded periods of up to three years under Confucian rites; Jewish practice prescribes shiva (seven days of intense mourning at home) followed by shloshim and a year of remembrance. Many cultures include wakes, funeral feasts, keening or professional mourners, and anniversaries of the death.

Official mourning

States proclaim periods of national mourning after the deaths of leaders or mass-casualty disasters: flags fly at half-mast, entertainments cease, and state funerals are held. Public mourning for figures such as Diana, Princess of Wales, or for Queen Elizabeth II drew mass participation far beyond official requirements.

Psychologists distinguish mourning from pathological or "complicated grief" when the normal process becomes prolonged or disabling, and modern scholarship questions rigid stage models, emphasising cultural shaping and continuing bonds with the dead.
