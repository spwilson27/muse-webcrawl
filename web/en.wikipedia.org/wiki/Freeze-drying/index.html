Freeze-drying

Freeze-drying (lyophilisation)
Process typeDehydration / preservation
StagesFreezing; primary drying (sublimation); secondary drying (desorption)
ApplicationsPharmaceuticals, food (instant coffee, astronaut food), biological specimens, document recovery
Key principleSublimation of ice under vacuum below water's triple point

Freeze-drying, also known as lyophilisation, is a low-temperature dehydration process that involves freezing a product, lowering the pressure, and removing the ice by sublimation. It is used to preserve perishable materials and to make them lighter and more convenient for transport and storage.


An industrial freeze-drying unit

Process

The process has three stages. First, the material is frozen, usually well below 0 °C, so that its water becomes ice. In primary drying, the pressure in the chamber is reduced to a few millibars and gentle heat is applied so the ice sublimates directly into vapour, which condenses on a cold trap; this removes about 95% of the water. In secondary drying, slightly higher temperatures drive off water molecules bound to the material by adsorption, leaving a residual moisture content of roughly 1–4%.

Applications

Freeze-drying is the standard way to stabilise many pharmaceuticals and vaccines, blood plasma and biological samples, because it preserves their structure and activity far better than heat drying. In the food industry it produces instant coffee, freeze-dried fruit, camping meals and astronaut food: the products keep their shape, flavour and nutrients and rehydrate quickly. Conservators also use it to salvage water-damaged books and documents.

History

Andean peoples preserved potatoes as chuño by exploiting cold nights and sun — a natural precursor. Modern vacuum freeze-drying was developed in the early 20th century and scaled up during the Second World War for blood plasma and penicillin, before entering large-scale food production from the 1950s and 1960s. The process is slower and more energy-intensive than conventional drying, which is why it is reserved for high-value or sensitive products.
