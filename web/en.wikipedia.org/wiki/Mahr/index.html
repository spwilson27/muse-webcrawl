Mahr

In Islam, a mahr (Arabic: مهر; also spelled mehr, meher, or mahrieh) is the obligation, in the form of money, possessions or a promised sum, paid by the groom to the bride at the time of the marriage contract (nikah). The mahr is the bride's exclusive property and a mandatory part of an Islamic marriage; it is not a bride price paid to her family, as the payment belongs solely to the wife, who may use it as she wishes.

Structure

The mahr is specified in the marriage contract and may be divided into two parts: a prompt portion (muqaddam), payable at the time of marriage, and a deferred portion (mu'akhkhar), payable later — commonly upon dissolution of the marriage by divorce or the husband's death. The amount is agreed by the parties; classical jurists discussed minimum amounts and recommended moderation, and the Qur'an prohibits taking back the mahr once given.

Purpose and legal role

The mahr provides the wife with financial security and a measure of economic independence within marriage, and the deferred portion can act as a deterrent to arbitrary divorce, since it becomes due on dissolution. If no mahr is specified in the contract, classical law generally implies a "proper mahr" (mahr al-mithl) based on what women of similar standing customarily receive. Modern family-law codes in many Muslim-majority countries continue to regulate the mahr, and disputes over unpaid deferred mahr are a common feature of divorce litigation.

Distinctions

The mahr is sometimes confused with dowry, but the two flow in opposite directions: a dowry is property brought by the bride or her family, whereas the mahr is an obligatory payment from the groom to the bride herself, rooted in Qur'anic injunction (Qur'an 4:4).
