The Godolphin Arabian (c. 1724–1753), also known as the Godolphin Barb, was one of three foundation sires from which the modern Thoroughbred horse breed descends, alongside the Darley Arabian and the Byerley Turk. Through his great-grandson Matchem, his male line was for a time the dominant one in racing.

History

The horse was foaled around 1724, probably in Yemen or North Africa. Legend holds that he was given to Louis XV of France by the Bey of Tunis, was neglected and used to pull a cart in Paris, and was eventually bought by the Quaker Edward Coke and brought to England. On Coke's death he passed to Francis Godolphin, 2nd Earl of Godolphin, standing at stud at Gog Magog, Cambridgeshire. A famous story has him used first as a "teaser" for the stallion Hobgoblin, whom he fought and defeated for the mare Roxana; the resulting foal, Lath, became one of the best racehorses of the era.

Legacy

The Godolphin Arabian sired Cade, sire of Matchem (1748), whose male line dominated 18th- and 19th-century racing. Although the Darley Arabian's line (through Eclipse) now accounts for the overwhelming majority of modern Thoroughbreds in the direct male line, the Godolphin Arabian appears in virtually every Thoroughbred pedigree through other branches. He died in 1753 and was buried at Gog Magog, where his grave is still marked. Eugène Sue's novel and the children's book King of the Wind by Marguerite Henry popularised his story.
