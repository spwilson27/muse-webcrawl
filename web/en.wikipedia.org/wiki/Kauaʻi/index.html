Kauaʻi

Kauaʻi (Hawaiian: Kauaʻi, pronounced [kɐwˈwɐʔi]) is the oldest and northernmost of the main Hawaiian Islands and the fourth largest, at about 1,456 square kilometres. Nicknamed the "Garden Island" for its lush, rain-fed landscape, it is known for the Na Pali Coast's fluted sea cliffs, Waimea Canyon — the "Grand Canyon of the Pacific" — and Mount Waiʻaleʻale, one of the wettest places on Earth. Together with Niʻihau it forms Kauai County; its seat is Līhuʻe.

Geography

Kauaʻi is the eroded remnant of a single great shield volcano. Its interior highlands receive extraordinary rainfall — Mount Waiʻaleʻale averages over 11 metres of rain a year — feeding rivers including the Wailua, Hawaii's largest navigable river. The west side is dry by contrast, containing Waimea Canyon, some 16 km long and up to 900 m deep. The north-western Na Pali Coast is roadless, accessible only by trail (the Kalalau Trail), boat or air.

History

Polynesian voyagers settled Kauaʻi by roughly 1000 CE. Captain James Cook made his first Hawaiian landfall at Waimea Bay in January 1778. Kauaʻi and Niʻihau were never conquered by Kamehameha I; their ruler Kaumualiʻi ceded the islands by agreement in 1810 (after the failed Schäffer affair of 1815–17, when a Russian agent briefly built forts including Fort Elizabeth near Waimea). Sugar plantations dominated the economy from the 19th century, succeeded by tourism; the last sugar harvest was in 2009.

Economy and culture

Tourism is now the main industry, and the island's scenery has served as a filming location for dozens of movies, including South Pacific, Raiders of the Lost Ark, Jurassic Park and The Descendants. Kauaʻi is notable for its large population of feral chickens — descended partly from birds freed by Hurricane Iniki in 1992 — and for local resistance to large-scale resort development and genetically modified seed-crop operations. Hurricane Iniki, which struck in September 1992, remains the most powerful hurricane to hit Hawaii in recorded history.
