Quinquereme

A quinquereme (from Latin quīnquerēmis, "five-oared") was a type of war galley used by the Hellenistic fleets and later by the Carthaginians and Romans. It was the standard heavy warship of the Mediterranean powers from the fourth century BC through the Punic Wars and into the early Roman Empire—larger and more powerful than the trireme it succeeded.

Design

The name refers not to five stacked banks of oars but to five rowers per vertical "unit": the oars were grouped so that each oar was pulled by two men, with three oars on one level and two on another (an arrangement of 3+2 per section), or other combinations totalling five rowers per frame. A full quinquereme carried about 300 rowers (150 per side), plus sailors and marines, for a total crew of roughly 400. The ships were typically some 45 metres long.

History and use

Ancient sources credit the quinquereme's invention to Dionysius I of Syracuse around 399 BC, as part of his naval arms race against Carthage. The type became the backbone of the Carthaginian and Roman fleets in the Punic Wars (264–146 BC). Rome, traditionally a land power, famously built its first quinqueremes by copying a wrecked Carthaginian ship and trained crews on land-based rowing frames, according to Polybius.

Quinqueremes fought with the bronze ram at the waterline and with boarding parties; Rome's early success at sea owed much to the corvus, a boarding bridge that let legionaries turn sea battles into infantry fights. At the decisive Battle of Actium (31 BC), Octavian's fleet relied more on lighter ships (liburnians), and heavier polyremes faded in favour of smaller, more versatile vessels in the imperial navy.
