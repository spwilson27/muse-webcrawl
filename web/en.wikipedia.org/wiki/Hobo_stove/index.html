Hobo stove

A hobo stove is a style of improvised heat-producing portable stove used in motion by a transient person, and/or used for camping. It is constructed out of a discarded metal can of any size by removing the top, cutting holes in the upper edge, and cutting a door in the side near the bottom, and filling it with twigs and other small pieces of wood.

Design and use

The can acts as a windbreak and chimney: air enters through the lower door and side holes, fuelling combustion of sticks fed through the door, while the top rim supports a pot or pan. Variants cut a pattern of holes around the top edge so flames can escape beneath a pan placed on top. The design requires no purchased fuel, since it burns small twigs, pine cones and other scavenged material.

History

The stove is named for hobos, itinerant workers who travelled by freight train in the United States in the late 19th and 20th centuries and cooked meals at trackside camps ("jungles"). Instructions for making hobo stoves were popularised by scouting and outdoor literature, and the concept influenced later commercial twig stoves and the modern rocket stove designs used in low-income countries.

Safety

Because the stove uses an open flame in a thin metal container, it becomes extremely hot, can ignite surrounding material, and produces carbon monoxide; it should only be used outdoors. Cut edges of tin cans are sharp, and galvanised coatings can release harmful fumes when heated, so plain steel food cans are preferred.
