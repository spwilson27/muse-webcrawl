Prodigy house

Prodigy houses are large English country houses built by courtiers and nobles during the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras, especially between about 1570 and 1620. The name was coined by the architectural historian Sir John Summerson, who used it to describe houses of exaggerated size and ostentation, built to impress the monarch during royal progresses and to display the owner's wealth and status.

Characteristics

Prodigy houses are characterised by extensive glazing ("more glass than wall", as was said of Hardwick Hall), symmetrical facades, classical detailing drawn from pattern books, long galleries, elaborate state apartments intended for a visiting sovereign, and prominent display of heraldry and the builder's initials. Many were built by officials who had grown rich under Elizabeth I, such as William Cecil, Lord Burghley.

Examples

  • Burghley House, Lincolnshire (William Cecil)
  • Longleat House, Wiltshire (Sir John Thynne)
  • Hardwick Hall, Derbyshire (Elizabeth, Countess of Shrewsbury, "Bess of Hardwick")
  • Wollaton Hall, Nottinghamshire (Sir Francis Willoughby)
  • Theobalds House, Hertfordshire (William Cecil)
  • Holdenby House, Northamptonshire (Sir Christopher Hatton)
  • Hatfield House, Hertfordshire (Robert Cecil, Jacobean)
