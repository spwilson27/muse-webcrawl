Reggio Emilia approach

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Reggio Emilia approach is an educational philosophy and pedagogy focused on preschool and primary education. It is a constructivist, student-centered approach that regards children as capable of constructing their own learning through exploration and relationships with others. The approach originated in the city of Reggio Emilia, Italy, after the Second World War, under the leadership of the teacher and pedagogist Loris Malaguzzi.

History

After the war, parents in the village of Villa Cella built a school for young children with their own hands, using funds from selling a tank, trucks, and horses left behind by the retreating German army. Loris Malaguzzi joined the effort, and in 1963 the first municipal preschool opened in Reggio Emilia. Under Malaguzzi's direction the municipal system of infant-toddler centers and preschools grew and gained international attention; in 1991 a Newsweek panel named the Diana School in Reggio Emilia one of the most advanced early-childhood institutions in the world. Reggio Children, an organization promoting the approach, was founded in 1994, the year of Malaguzzi's death.

Core principles

  • The image of the child: children are seen as rich in potential, strong, powerful, and competent, with an innate curiosity and capacity to learn.
  • The hundred languages of children: Malaguzzi's famous phrase holds that children express understanding in many symbolic forms — drawing, painting, sculpture, drama, music, movement, and words — not language alone.
  • The environment as third teacher: classroom spaces are carefully designed to be beautiful, ordered, and rich in materials that invite exploration and collaboration.
  • Emergent curriculum and projects (progettazione): long-term, in-depth projects arise from children's interests rather than following a fixed syllabus.
  • Documentation: teachers systematically record children's work and words, making learning visible to children, parents, and the teachers themselves for reflection and planning.
  • The role of the teacher: teachers act as co-learners, researchers, and guides rather than transmitters of knowledge.
  • Parental and community participation: families are active partners in the life of the school.

Reggio Emilia schools also employ an atelierista, a teacher with an arts background who runs the atelier (studio) and supports children's expressive work.

Influence

The approach has inspired early-childhood programs worldwide, though its founders emphasize that "Reggio Emilia" names a specific municipal system, and other programs describe themselves as "Reggio-inspired". Its practices are often compared with those of Montessori and Waldorf education.
