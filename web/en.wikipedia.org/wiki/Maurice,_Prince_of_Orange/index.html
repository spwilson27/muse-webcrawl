Maurice of Nassau, Prince of Orange (Dutch: Maurits van Oranje; 14 November 1567 – 23 April 1625) was stadtholder of all the provinces of the Dutch Republic except Friesland from 1585 at the earliest until his death in 1625. Before he became Prince of Orange upon the death of his eldest half-brother Philip William in 1618, he was known as Maurice of Nassau.

Born in Dillenburg, Maurice was the second son of William the Silent and Anna of Saxony. After his father's assassination in 1584, he was elected stadtholder of Holland and Zeeland in 1585 at the age of eighteen, and of Utrecht, Guelders and Overijssel in 1590. Together with his cousin William Louis of Nassau-Dillenburg, he reorganised the Dutch States Army, introducing drill, discipline and linear tactics that became a model for European armies — the so-called "Maurician reforms".

Military career

Maurice studied at Heidelberg and Leiden, where he absorbed classical military theory, including the works of Vegetius and the tactics of the Roman legion, and the use of the countermarch for volley fire. His campaigns of the 1590s — the famous "Ten Glory Years" — recaptured much of the northern and eastern Netherlands from Spain, including Breda (by the celebrated peat-boat stratagem of 1590), Deventer, Zutphen, Nijmegen, Steenwijk and Groningen. In 1600 he defeated a Spanish army at the Battle of Nieuwpoort in Flanders, though the campaign's strategic aims were not achieved. The Siege of Ostend (1601–1604) ended in Spanish victory but at ruinous cost.

Conflict with Oldenbarnevelt

During the Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621), Maurice became embroiled in a bitter political and religious conflict with the Land's Advocate Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, siding with the orthodox Calvinist Counter-Remonstrants against the Remonstrants. The dispute ended with Oldenbarnevelt's arrest and execution in 1619 after the Synod of Dort condemned the Remonstrants. When war resumed in 1621, Maurice's later campaigns were less successful; he died in April 1625 during the siege of Breda, which fell to the Spanish general Spinola weeks later. He was succeeded by his half-brother Frederick Henry.

Legacy

Maurice is regarded as one of the great military reformers of the early modern period. His reforms in drill, engineering, siegecraft and army administration, carried out with Simon Stevin and William Louis, helped transform the Dutch Republic into a major European power. The island nation of Mauritius was named in his honour by Dutch explorers in 1598.
