Constrained writing

Constrained writing is a literary technique in which the writer is bound by some condition that forbids certain things or imposes a pattern. Constraints are adopted voluntarily and may be as simple as form requirements (sonnets, haiku, limericks) or as severe as writing an entire novel without a common letter. They are particularly associated with the French group Oulipo, founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais.

Motivation

Writers have argued that constraints paradoxically liberate creativity: by forbidding the most obvious word or structure, the author is forced into unexpected solutions. Oulipo described such methods as "potential literature" (littérature potentielle) — the systematic exploration of what texts a given constraint can generate.

Forms of constraint

  • Lipogram: writing that avoids a particular letter, most famously Georges Perec's novel La Disparition (1969), written without the letter e, and Ernest Vincent Wright's Gadsby (1939).
  • Pangram: a sentence using every letter of the alphabet.
  • Palindromes and reversals: texts reading the same forwards and backwards.
  • N+7: an Oulipian procedure replacing each noun with the seventh noun following it in a dictionary.
  • Snowball and word-count constraints: sentences or lines whose words grow by one letter or word at a time; also texts in which each word's length follows the digits of pi (Pilish).
  • Univocalism: using only one vowel, as in Perec's Les Revenentes, which uses only e.
  • Fixed forms: the sonnet, sestina, villanelle and haiku constrain line count, metre and rhyme.

Notable works

Queneau's Exercices de style (1947) retells the same anecdote in 99 styles, and his Cent mille milliards de poèmes (1961) offers a book of combinable sonnet lines. Italo Calvino and Harry Mathews also produced major Oulipian works. Constrained writing has ancient roots as well: Greek lipograms attributed to Lasus of Hermione and the pattern poems of the Hellenistic period anticipate the modern practice.
