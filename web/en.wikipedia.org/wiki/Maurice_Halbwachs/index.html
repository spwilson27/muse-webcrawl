Maurice Halbwachs

Maurice Halbwachs (11 March 1877 – 16 March 1945) was a French philosopher and sociologist, a disciple of Émile Durkheim, best known for developing the concept of collective memory. His work argued that individual remembering is structured by the social frameworks of the groups to which one belongs. He died in the Buchenwald concentration camp in 1945.

Life

Born in Reims, Halbwachs studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where he attended the lectures of Henri Bergson, and then turned towards Durkheim's school of sociology, contributing to L'Année sociologique. He taught at the universities of Caen and Strasbourg — where his colleague was the historian Marc Bloch — and from 1935 held a chair at the Sorbonne. In 1944 he was appointed to the Collège de France, but the Gestapo arrested him in July 1944 (his son Pierre had been arrested for Resistance activity) and deported him to Buchenwald, where he died of dysentery in March 1945, weeks before liberation.

Collective memory

In Les cadres sociaux de la mémoire (1925) and the posthumously published La mémoire collective (1950), Halbwachs argued that memory is not a purely private faculty: people remember as members of groups — families, religious communities, classes — whose shared frameworks of language, custom and place organise what can be recalled. He distinguished collective memory, living and plural, from history, which he saw as a single, critical reconstruction of a past no longer lived. These ideas, largely overlooked for decades, were revived from the 1980s onward and became foundational for memory studies, influencing scholars such as Pierre Nora and his "lieux de mémoire" project.

Other work

Halbwachs also wrote on the psychology of social classes, suicide (partially re-examining Durkheim's conclusions in Les Causes du suicide, 1930), urban morphology and population, and the social psychology of the working class standard of living.
