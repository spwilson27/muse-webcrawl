The Timurids were a Turco-Mongol dynasty descended from the conqueror Timur (Tamerlane), who founded the Timurid Empire in 1370. At its greatest extent under Timur, the empire covered all of Persia, Central Asia, Mesopotamia, and parts of Anatolia, the Caucasus, and northern India. After Timur's death in 1405 the empire contracted but endured for another century in reduced form, centered on Herat and Samarkand, and became famous for the Timurid Renaissance in art, architecture, and science.

History

Timur, a member of the Barlas tribe of Transoxiana who claimed inheritance of the empire of Genghis Khan, seized Samarkand in 1370 and spent 35 years in nearly continuous conquest: Persia, the Kipchak steppe (defeating Tokhtamish of the Golden Horde), northern India (sacking Delhi in 1398), Syria, and Anatolia, where he crushed the Ottomans at Ankara in 1402 and captured Sultan Bayezid I. He died in 1405 campaigning toward Ming China.

After a succession struggle, his son Shah Rukh (r. 1409–1447) ruled from Herat over the eastern empire, while Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg governed Transoxiana from Samarkand, where he built his great observatory and compiled influential star tables. The later 15th century saw the empire split between Samarkand and Herat; under Sultan Husayn Bayqara (r. 1469–1506) Herat enjoyed a final flowering with the vizier-poet Ali-Shir Nava'i and the miniaturist Behzad. Uzbek forces under Muhammad Shaybani took Herat in 1507, ending the empire; a Timurid prince, Babur, driven south, went on to found the Mughal Empire in India in 1526.

Culture

Timurid patronage produced some of the masterpieces of Islamic art: the Gur-e Amir mausoleum and Shah-i-Zinda necropolis in Samarkand, the Bibi-Khanym mosque, Herat's illustrated manuscripts, and advances in astronomy and mathematics at Ulugh Beg's observatory. Persian was the language of high culture, while Chagatai Turkic literature reached its classical form in Nava'i's works.
