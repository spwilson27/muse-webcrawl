Savings account

A savings account is a bank account at a retail bank whose purpose is to hold money not intended for daily spending, typically paying interest to the depositor. Unlike a transaction (checking) account, a savings account may limit the number of withdrawals or transfers per month and usually offers no cheque-writing facility, although modern online accounts blur these distinctions.

Savings account
Type: Deposit account
Offered by: Banks, credit unions, building societies
Interest: Variable rate, credited periodically
Insurance: e.g. FDIC/NCUA (US), FSCS (UK)

Features

Savings accounts pay interest calculated on the balance, expressed as an annual percentage yield (APY). Because deposits are a stable funding source, banks can lend the pooled funds while keeping the accounts liquid. In the United States, savings deposits are insured by the FDIC (banks) or NCUA (credit unions) up to $250,000 per depositor; similar schemes exist in most countries. Regulation D formerly limited US savers to six convenient withdrawals per month, a requirement suspended by the Federal Reserve in 2020.

Types

Variants include basic passbook accounts, high-yield savings accounts (often from online banks with lower overheads), money market accounts, children's savings accounts, and goal-based accounts. In the United Kingdom, Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs) shelter interest from tax; in many countries, savings accounts form part of national schemes encouraging household saving. Financial advisers commonly recommend holding an emergency fund of several months' expenses in a savings account because of its safety, liquidity, and protection from market volatility, accepting that returns may not outpace inflation.
