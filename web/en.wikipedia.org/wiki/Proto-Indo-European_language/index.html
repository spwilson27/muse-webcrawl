Proto-Indo-European (PIE) is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family, the most widely spoken language family in the world by number of speakers. PIE is believed to have been spoken as a single language — probably with dialectal variation — from about 4500 to 2500 BCE, after which it diverged into the attested branches. No direct records of the language exist; every feature of it has been reconstructed by applying the comparative method to its descendants, and reconstructed forms are conventionally marked with an asterisk (*).

By the time of the earliest written descendants — Hittite and Mycenaean Greek in the 2nd millennium BCE, and the hymns of the Rigveda, composed in an archaic form of Sanskrit — the branches were already well differentiated. Today, Indo-European languages are spoken natively by roughly 46% of the world's population, including English, Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, Hindi, Bengali, and Persian.

Homeland

The leading theory, the steppe hypothesis (or Kurgan hypothesis, developed by Marija Gimbutas and elaborated by scholars such as J. P. Mallory and David Anthony), places the PIE homeland on the Pontic–Caspian steppe of what is now Ukraine and southern Russia, in the 4th millennium BCE. It links the language's spread to pastoralist cultures that built burial mounds (kurgans), domesticated the horse, and used wheeled vehicles — a technology whose vocabulary (*kʷékʷlos "wheel", *h₂éǵs- "axle") is shared across the family and dates the dispersal to after the wheel's invention around 3500 BCE. Large-scale ancient-DNA studies since 2015 have strongly supported a major migration of steppe pastoralists (the Yamnaya and related groups) into Europe and towards Asia in the 3rd millennium BCE. The main rival, Colin Renfrew's Anatolian hypothesis, associates the dispersal with the spread of farming from Anatolia from around 7000 BCE; it is now held by a minority, though hybrid models positing an origin south of the Caucasus for the earliest stage have gained some support from recent genetic work.

Phonology

Reconstructed PIE had a consonant system traditionally described with three series of stops (voiceless, voiced, and voiced aspirated), a single sibilant *s, nasals, liquids, and glides. Its most debated feature is the set of laryngeals — consonants conventionally written *h₁, *h₂, *h₃ — which Ferdinand de Saussure predicted in 1879 from patterns of vowel alternation (ablaut) and which were spectacularly confirmed when Jerzy Kuryłowicz showed in 1927 that Hittite preserved one of them as ḫ. The laryngeals explain vowel colouring (*h₂ colours an adjacent *e to *a; *h₃ to *o) and vowel lengthening when lost.

The vowel system centred on *e and *o in ablaut alternation (e-grade, o-grade, and zero-grade), with *a less common and long vowels largely secondary. PIE is also reconstructed with a pitch accent, reflected in the accentual systems of Vedic Sanskrit, Ancient Greek, and Balto-Slavic. The centum–satem division — whether the palatovelars merged with plain velars (centum: Greek, Italic, Germanic) or became sibilants (satem: Indo-Iranian, Balto-Slavic) — was once thought to reflect a primary dialect split but is now treated as an areal development.

Morphology

PIE was a highly inflected, fusional language. Nouns, pronouns, and adjectives declined for three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), three numbers (singular, dual, plural), and as many as eight cases (nominative, vocative, accusative, genitive, dative, ablative, instrumental, locative). Verbs conjugated for tense-aspect stems (present, aorist, perfect — originally aspectual rather than purely temporal), mood (indicative, subjunctive, optative, imperative), voice (active and middle), and person and number. Roots combined with suffixes and endings under complex accent and ablaut patterns, which historical linguists classify into paradigms such as acrostatic and hysterokinetic. Later branches simplified this system to very different degrees — English drastically, Lithuanian and Sanskrit only modestly.

Vocabulary and culture

Reconstructed vocabulary offers a window onto the speakers' world: words for the horse (*h₁éḱwos), cattle, sheep and wool, the wheel and wagon, ploughing and grain, bronze (but no common word for iron), snow and winter, and kinship relations that were strongly patrilineal and patrilocal. Divine names such as *Dyḗus Ph₂tḗr, the "Sky Father" — reflected in Greek Zeus, Roman Jupiter, and Vedic Dyaus Pita — anchor the comparative study of Indo-European mythology associated with Georges Dumézil. Personal names followed a poetic two-word compound pattern celebrating fame (*ḱléwos, "glory", is shared by Greek kleos and Sanskrit śrávas).

Development of scholarship

Recognition of the family is traditionally dated to Sir William Jones's 1786 address in Calcutta comparing Sanskrit, Greek, and Latin. Through the 19th century, Rasmus Rask, Jacob Grimm (Grimm's law), Franz Bopp, August Schleicher, and the Neogrammarians built the comparative framework; the 20th century added the decipherment of Hittite and Tocharian — the family's easternmost extinct branches — and refined reconstruction through laryngeal theory. August Schleicher even composed a short fable, "The Sheep and the Horses", in his reconstruction of PIE in 1868; later scholars have repeatedly revised it as reconstruction has improved, a tradition that illustrates both the method's power and its limits.
