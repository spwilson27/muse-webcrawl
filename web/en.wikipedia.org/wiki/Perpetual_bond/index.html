Perpetual bond

Perpetual bond
TypeFixed-income security
MaturityNone (no redemption date)
PaymentsCoupon interest in perpetuity
Notable exampleBritish consols

A perpetual bond, also known irreverently as a perp, is a bond with no maturity date. Therefore, it may be treated as equity, not as debt. Issuers pay coupons on perpetual bonds forever, and they do not have to redeem the principal. Perpetual bond cash flows are, therefore, those of a perpetuity.

History

The most famous perpetual bonds were the British consols, first issued in 1751 by the Bank of England to consolidate government debts. Consols paid a fixed interest (eventually 2.5%) indefinitely. They remained in issue for more than two and a half centuries until the British government redeemed the last of them in 2015. The Dutch water board of Lekdijk Bovendams issued a perpetual bond in 1648 that is still paying interest, making it one of the oldest securities in existence.

Valuation

Because the cash flows continue forever, the price of a perpetual bond is the coupon payment divided by the market yield: P = C / r. Consequently perpetual bonds are extremely sensitive to changes in interest rates (they have very high duration). Most modern perpetual bonds include a call option allowing the issuer to redeem them after a set number of years, usually at par.

Modern issues

Banks issue perpetual subordinated bonds (Additional Tier 1 instruments) to satisfy regulatory capital requirements; such instruments often allow coupons to be skipped and principal to be written down or converted to equity in a crisis. Some corporations have also issued very long-dated or perpetual hybrid securities to obtain equity credit from rating agencies while retaining tax-deductible interest.
