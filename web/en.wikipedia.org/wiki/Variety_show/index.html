Variety show

A variety show, also known as variety arts or variety entertainment, is entertainment made up of a variety of acts including musical performances, sketch comedy, magic, acrobatics, juggling and ventriloquism. It is normally introduced by a compère (master of ceremonies) or host. The variety format made its way from the Victorian stage to radio and then to television.

Variety combined music, comedy and speciality acts in a single bill.

Origins: music hall and vaudeville

The format grew out of British music hall and its American counterpart, vaudeville, in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. A typical bill offered eight to twelve unrelated acts — singers, dancers, comedians, animal acts, strongmen and novelty performers — arranged so that the strongest act closed the show. Circuits such as Keith-Albee in the United States and Moss Empires in Britain employed thousands of performers. Related traditions include the French revue, German Varieté theatre and the minstrel show.

Radio and television

Variety transferred naturally to radio and then to early television, where it dominated the first decades of broadcasting. In the United States, The Ed Sullivan Show (1948–1971) became the definitive platform, famously introducing Elvis Presley and The Beatles to American audiences. Other landmarks include The Milton Berle Show, The Carol Burnett Show, The Dean Martin Show and The Smothers Brothers Comedy Hour. In Britain, programmes such as Sunday Night at the London Palladium and The Morecambe and Wise Show drew enormous audiences.

Decline and legacy

By the late 1970s, rising production costs, changing tastes and the growth of sitcoms and dramas pushed variety out of prime time in most English-speaking markets, though it remained strong in many other countries — notably in East Asia, where Korean and Japanese variety programmes became a dominant television genre. The format survives in talent competitions such as the Got Talent franchise, in late-night talk shows with their mix of interviews, sketches and music, and in live theatrical revues.
