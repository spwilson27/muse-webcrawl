Hunt Brothers

The Hunt brothers were Nelson Bunker Hunt (1926–2014) and William Herbert Hunt (born 1929), sons of Texas oil tycoon H. L. Hunt. They became internationally notorious for their attempt to corner the world silver market in the late 1970s, an episode that culminated in the collapse of silver prices on "Silver Thursday", 27 March 1980, and left the brothers with losses estimated at over a billion dollars.

Background

H. L. Hunt built one of America's largest independent oil fortunes, and his sons inherited substantial interests in oil, real estate, cattle, sugar, and thoroughbred horse racing. Bunker Hunt became one of the world's leading racehorse owners and breeders. Concerned by inflation and the weakening dollar during the 1970s, the brothers — together with investors including members of the Saudi royal family through the International Metals Investment Company — began accumulating silver bullion and futures contracts.

The silver corner

Beginning around 1973, the Hunts bought silver at prices near US$2 per troy ounce. By 1979 their holdings and futures positions were estimated to control a substantial fraction of the world's deliverable supply, and the price of silver rose dramatically, reaching nearly US$50 per ounce in January 1980. In response, the COMEX exchange adopted "Silver Rule 7", imposing liquidation-only trading and sharply raising margin requirements, changes the Hunts denounced as self-dealing by exchange members who were short the metal.

Unable to meet margin calls, the brothers' position unravelled. On 27 March 1980 the silver price fell from about $21.62 to $10.80 in a single day. The Hunts' obligations reportedly threatened several brokerage firms and banks, and a consortium of banks arranged a loan of roughly US$1.1 billion secured by family assets so the brothers could meet their commitments.

Aftermath

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission charged the brothers with manipulating the silver market, and in 1988 a federal jury in a civil case found them liable for conspiring to manipulate prices. Bunker and Herbert Hunt were fined and banned from commodity trading; both had declared bankruptcy in 1988 following years of litigation and tax disputes. The events prompted lasting reforms in futures-market position limits and exchange emergency powers.
