Color model

A color model is an abstract mathematical model describing the way colors can be represented as tuples of numbers, typically as three or four values. When a set of rules for translating between the model and a reference color space is fixed, the result is a definite footprint called a gamut; the mapping, together with the model, defines a color space.

Common models

The RGB color model is additive: red, green, and blue light are combined to reproduce a broad array of colors, matching the trichromatic nature of human vision. It underlies electronic displays such as monitors and televisions. The CMYK color model is subtractive and is used in color printing: cyan, magenta, and yellow pigments subtract wavelengths from white paper, with a fourth black ("key") ink added for density and economy.

The HSV and HSL models rearrange RGB into hue, saturation, and value/lightness, which correspond more intuitively to how people choose colors, and are widely used in color pickers. The CIELAB model (L*a*b*), standardized by the CIE in 1976, was designed so that Euclidean distances approximate perceived color differences, making it approximately perceptually uniform; its companion CIELUV serves display applications. Other influential systems include the CIE XYZ model of 1931 — the foundation of modern colorimetry — the Munsell color system, organized by hue, value, and chroma, and the Pantone Matching System used in industry.

Uses

Color models are selected according to purpose: RGB for screens, CMYK for print, Lab for device-independent interchange and color management via ICC profiles, and YCbCr and related families for compressing video and image data by separating luminance from chrominance.
