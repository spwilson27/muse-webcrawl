Fringing reef

A fringing reef is one of the three main types of coral reef recognised by Charles Darwin, the others being barrier reefs and atolls. A fringing reef grows directly adjacent to the shoreline of an island or continent, extending seaward from the coast with little or no lagoon between the reef and the shore; where a shallow lagoon is present it is typically narrow and partly filled with sediment.

Formation

Fringing reefs form when coral larvae settle on hard substrate along a shoreline in warm, well-lit tropical waters and grow upward toward the surface. Darwin's subsidence theory proposed a sequence: a fringing reef around a volcanic island becomes a barrier reef as the island subsides and a lagoon widens, and ultimately an atoll when the island disappears below the sea. Fringing reefs are thus generally considered the earliest stage of reef development, though many grow simply along coasts that have been stable since sea level rose after the last glacial period. Major examples fringe the coasts of the Red Sea, parts of the Caribbean, and many Pacific islands.

Structure

A typical fringing reef has a reef flat, exposed at low tide, sloping gently from the shore, and a reef crest where wave energy is highest, beyond which the fore-reef slope descends steeply into deeper water. Coral diversity and growth are greatest on the outer slope. Because of their proximity to land, fringing reefs are particularly vulnerable to sedimentation, nutrient runoff and pollution from coastal development.
