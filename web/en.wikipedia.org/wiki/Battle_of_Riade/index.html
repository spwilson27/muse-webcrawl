The Battle of Riade (also called the Battle of Merseburg) was fought on 15 March 933 between the forces of King Henry I of East Francia ("Henry the Fowler") and a Magyar (Hungarian) raiding army, at a place near the Unstrut river in present-day Saxony-Anhalt. It was the first major East Frankish victory over the Magyars, whose mounted raiders had devastated the kingdom for decades, and it vindicated Henry's military reforms.

Background

After a crushing defeat of the Franks in 906 and repeated incursions, Henry concluded a nine-year truce with the Magyars in 924 in exchange for tribute, using the breathing space to build fortified burhs and train a force of armoured cavalry (milites). When the truce expired, Henry refused further tribute, provoking a new invasion in 933.

The battle

Henry split his army into two forces of Thuringians/Saxons and a main body. At Riade, his troops used a small detachment of lightly equipped foot soldiers supported by cavalry to lure and then overwhelm the Magyar horse-archers, who were unaccustomed to facing disciplined heavy cavalry in close combat. The Magyars fled, leaving their camp and captives behind; a second Magyar force was defeated in Bohemia shortly afterwards.

Aftermath

Riade broke the spell of Magyar invincibility, though raids continued until Otto I's decisive victory at the Lechfeld in 955. The battle is known chiefly from the account of the chronicler Widukind of Corvey.
