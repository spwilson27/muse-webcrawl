Archetype (textual criticism)

In textual criticism, an archetype is the earliest surviving manuscript — or, more commonly, the hypothetical lost manuscript — from which all surviving copies of a text descend. The archetype stands between the author's original and the extant witnesses; reconstructing it is a central goal of the stemmatic method.

The stemmatic method

In the method associated with Karl Lachmann, the critic collates surviving manuscripts and identifies shared errors (errores coniunctivi) to group witnesses into families, drawing a family tree or stemma codicum. The point at which all lines of descent converge is the archetype. By eliminating manuscripts demonstrably copied from other surviving manuscripts (eliminatio codicum descriptorum), the critic reduces the evidence to the oldest independent witnesses and infers the archetype's readings.

Archetype and original

The archetype is not necessarily the author's original: errors and alterations may have entered the tradition between the original and the archetype, and where the archetype's reading is evidently corrupt, editors may emend it by conjecture (emendatio ope ingenii). Where a tradition descends from more than one independent source — a "contaminated" or open tradition — a single archetype may not be recoverable, a difficulty that has led some critics, following Joseph Bédier, to prefer editing a single "best text" instead.

Terminology

The archetype is conventionally denoted in stemmata by the Greek letter omega (Ω) or the sign ω. Manuscripts copied directly from the archetype are its apographs in the stemmatic sense, and intermediate lost copies are called hyparchetypes.
