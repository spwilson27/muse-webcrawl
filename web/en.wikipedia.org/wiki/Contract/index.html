Contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties that creates mutual obligations. In common law jurisdictions, the formation of a contract generally requires an offer, acceptance, consideration (something of value exchanged) and mutual intent to be bound; civil law systems structure the analysis differently but reach similar results in most commercial cases. Contracts may be written, oral or implied by conduct, though some (such as land sales) must satisfy formal requirements.

Formation and validity

Key doctrines include capacity (minors and persons lacking mental capacity have limited power to contract), legality (contracts for unlawful purposes are void), mistake, misrepresentation, duress and undue influence (which may make a contract voidable), and interpretation of terms, including implied terms supplied by courts, statute or custom. Consumer-protection and employment law in many countries override freedom of contract to correct imbalances of bargaining power.

Breach and remedies

Failure to perform is a breach, entitling the innocent party to remedies: most commonly damages (money compensation aiming to put the claimant in the position performance would have achieved), and in limited circumstances specific performance or an injunction, rescission, or agreed liquidated damages. Contracts allocate commercial risk worldwide — from sale of goods and construction to finance and software — and international trade is supported by instruments such as the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (1980). Contract theory in economics and philosophy examines why promises bind, how incomplete contracts are governed, and how relational norms supplement formal terms.
