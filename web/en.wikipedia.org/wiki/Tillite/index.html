Tillite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tillite is sedimentary rock formed by the consolidation and lithification of till, the unsorted sediment deposited directly by glacial ice. A tillite consists of clasts of many sizes — from clay and silt up to boulders — embedded without sorting or layering in a fine-grained matrix, the fragments often being a mixture of rock types carried from far away. Individual stones within it may be faceted or striated by their transport in ice.

Significance

Because till is deposited by glaciers, ancient tillites are key evidence of prehistoric ice ages. Widespread late Paleozoic tillites of the Dwyka Group in South Africa and their equivalents in South America, India, Antarctica and Australia were crucial evidence for continental drift: their distribution only makes sense if the southern continents were once joined in the supercontinent Gondwana with ice flowing outward from a shared interior. Much older tillites, such as those of the Huronian Supergroup in Canada and the Sturtian and Marinoan deposits worldwide, record Proterozoic glaciations, including the hypothesized "Snowball Earth" episodes in which ice may have reached the equator.

Identification

Tillite must be distinguished from superficially similar unsorted deposits (diamictites) produced by debris flows or other non-glacial processes; accompanying dropstones in marine sediments, striated pavements beneath the deposit, and its regional extent support a glacial interpretation.
