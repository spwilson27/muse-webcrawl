Holmium

Holmium
Symbol: Ho
Atomic number: 67
Atomic mass: 164.93
Group: Lanthanide
Melting point: 1,474 °C
Boiling point: 2,700 °C
Electron configuration: [Xe] 4f¹¹ 6s²

Holmium is a chemical element with the symbol Ho and atomic number 67. It is a rare-earth element in the lanthanide series, a relatively soft, silvery-white metal that is stable in dry air at room temperature but oxidises readily in moist air. It was discovered spectroscopically in 1878 by Marc Delafontaine and Jacques-Louis Soret, and independently by Per Teodor Cleve, who named it after Stockholm (Holmia in Latin).

Properties

Holmium has the highest magnetic strength of any element and is used as a pole piece in powerful static magnets. It possesses unusual magnetic properties that change with temperature, being antiferromagnetic below about 20 K and ferromagnetic at higher low temperatures. Its only stable isotope is holmium-165.

Applications

Holmium is used in holmium:YAG lasers employed in dentistry and surgery, as a dopant in yttrium iron garnet for microwave equipment, in optical fibre amplifiers, and as a burnable neutron absorber (holmium-165 has a very high neutron cross-section) in nuclear control rods. Holmium oxide is used as a colourant giving yellow or red glass and as a wavelength calibration standard in optical spectrophotometry.

Occurrence

Like other rare earths, holmium occurs in minerals such as monazite and gadolinite, typically making up about 0.3% of monazite sand, and is extracted by ion exchange and solvent extraction, chiefly in China.
