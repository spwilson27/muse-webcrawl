Pratt & Whitney F135

The Pratt & Whitney F135 is an afterburning turbofan developed for the Lockheed Martin F-35 Lightning II single-engine strike fighter. Derived from the F119 engine of the F-22 Raptor, it is the most powerful fighter engine in service, producing more than 40,000 pounds of thrust with afterburner. Three variants power the three F-35 versions, including the F135-PW-600 with a shaft-driven lift fan for the short take-off and vertical landing (STOVL) F-35B.

Development

The F135 grew out of the Joint Strike Fighter programme, combining the core of the proven F119 with a larger low-pressure system for the F-35's greater mass flow requirements. The competing General Electric/Rolls-Royce F136 engine was cancelled in 2011, leaving the F135 as the sole powerplant. Rolls-Royce supplies the LiftSystem — lift fan, roll posts, and three-bearing swivel nozzle — for the STOVL variant.

Design

The engine uses a three-stage fan, six-stage compressor, annular combustor, and single-stage high- and low-pressure turbines, with a full-authority digital engine control (FADEC). Stealth requirements shaped its design: the exhaust incorporates low-observable features, and the F-35B's shaft-driven lift fan provides more than 40,000 lbf of combined vertical lift in STOVL mode.

Operational history and issues

The F135 has accumulated hundreds of thousands of flight hours across the global F-35 fleet. The programme has drawn scrutiny over cost, sustainment, and cooling capacity: modernised F-35 systems demand more bleed-air cooling than originally planned, driving debate about an Engine Core Upgrade versus an entirely new adaptive engine under the AETP programme, which the U.S. Air Force chose not to pursue for the F-35 in 2023.
