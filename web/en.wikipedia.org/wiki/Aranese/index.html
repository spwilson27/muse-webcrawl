Aranese (Aranese: aranés) is a standardized form of the Pyrenean Gascon variety of the Occitan language spoken in the Val d'Aran, in the northwestern corner of Catalonia, Spain, on the border with France. It is the only variety of Occitan with official status in any territory, and it is one of the three official languages of the Val d'Aran, alongside Catalan and Spanish.

Status and use

Aranese was recognized as official in the Val d'Aran by the Statute of Autonomy of Catalonia in 1990, and in 2010 it became co-official throughout Catalonia. It is taught in schools in the valley, used by the local government (the Conselh Generau d'Aran), and heard on local media, including a daily news segment on Catalan television. Surveys suggest that the great majority of the valley's roughly 10,000 inhabitants understand the language, and a majority can speak it, though Spanish remains dominant in many daily contexts.

Linguistic features

As a Gascon variety, Aranese shares characteristic features such as the change of Latin initial F- to h- (e.g., huec "fire", from FOCU), the loss of intervocalic -n-, and a distinctive definite article system (eth, era). Its standardization, overseen by the Institut d'Estudis Aranesi, follows a norm based on the local speech of the valley while remaining compatible with the wider Gascon and Occitan reference norms.

History

The Val d'Aran, though geographically on the northern, French-facing slope of the Pyrenees and drained by the Garonne river, has been politically tied to Catalonia since the Middle Ages, which preserved its Occitan speech within Spain. Written Aranese has a long tradition in local legal and religious documents, and a literary revival since the late 20th century has produced poetry, prose, and translation in the language.
