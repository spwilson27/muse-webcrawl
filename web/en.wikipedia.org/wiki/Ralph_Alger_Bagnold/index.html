Brigadier Ralph Alger Bagnold, FRS, OBE (3 April 1896 – 28 May 1990) was an English explorer, geologist and soldier. He is remembered both as the founder and first commander of the British Army's Long Range Desert Group during the Second World War and as the pioneering scientist of aeolian (wind-driven) sediment transport, whose 1941 book The Physics of Blown Sand and Desert Dunes remains a foundational text in geomorphology and planetary science.

Early life and desert exploration

Bagnold was educated at Malvern College and the Royal Military Academy, Woolwich, and served as a Royal Engineers officer in the First World War. Posted to Egypt in 1926, he spent his leave exploring the Libyan Desert by car with a small group of companions, developing techniques for desert navigation and vehicle travel — including the use of sun compasses of his own design and methods for crossing sand seas. His journeys of 1929–1932 took him to remote regions such as the Gilf Kebir and the Uweinat mountains, and he published the account Libyan Sands (1935).

Physics of blown sand

Between expeditions Bagnold conducted wind-tunnel experiments on how wind moves sand, distinguishing the processes of saltation, surface creep and suspension, and explaining the formation and migration of dunes such as barchans. His book of 1941 founded the quantitative study of aeolian processes; its results were later applied by NASA to the interpretation of dunes on Mars, and the Bagnold number in granular-flow physics is named after him.

Second World War

Recalled to service in 1939, Bagnold proposed and raised the Long Range Desert Group in 1940, a reconnaissance and raiding unit that operated deep behind Axis lines in North Africa using his pre-war desert expertise. He commanded it until August 1941, when he handed command to Guy Prendergast and moved to staff duties in Cairo, retiring as a brigadier.

Later life and honours

Bagnold continued scientific work after the war, including studies of sediment transport in water. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1944, received the Geological Society's Penrose Medal and the National Academy of Sciences' G. K. Warren Prize, and developed the "Bagnold formula" for sediment transport rates. NASA scientists named a field of dunes on Mars the "Bagnold dunes", explored by the Curiosity rover in 2015–2017.
