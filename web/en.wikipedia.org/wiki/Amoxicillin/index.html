Amoxicillin is an antibiotic medication belonging to the penicillin family, used to treat a number of bacterial infections. These include middle ear infections, strep throat, pneumonia, skin infections and urinary tract infections, among others. It is taken orally or, less commonly, by injection. Amoxicillin is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is one of the most commonly prescribed antibiotics worldwide, including for children.

Medical uses

Amoxicillin is effective against a wide range of Gram-positive and some Gram-negative bacteria. It is a first-line treatment for acute otitis media and community-acquired pneumonia in many guidelines. Combined with the β-lactamase inhibitor clavulanic acid (as co-amoxiclav), its spectrum is extended to β-lactamase-producing organisms. Amoxicillin is also part of standard triple therapy for eradicating Helicobacter pylori in peptic ulcer disease.

Mechanism of action

Like other β-lactam antibiotics, amoxicillin binds to penicillin-binding proteins and inhibits the cross-linking of peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall, leading to cell lysis and death. It was developed by scientists at Beecham (now part of GSK) and patented in 1972 as a better-absorbed analogue of ampicillin.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea. A non-allergic rash may occur, notably in patients with infectious mononucleosis. Serious but rarer reactions include Clostridioides difficile colitis and anaphylaxis in patients with true penicillin allergy.
