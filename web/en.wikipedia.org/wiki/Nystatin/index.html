Nystatin

ClassPolyene antifungal
RoutesTopical, oral (not absorbed)
Discovered1950
SourceStreptomyces noursei

Nystatin, sold under the brand name Mycostatin among others, is an antifungal medication. It is used to treat Candida infections of the skin, mouth, oesophagus and vagina. Nystatin is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

Nystatin was discovered in 1950 by Elizabeth Lee Hazen and Rachel Fuller Brown, working for the New York State Department of Health; the drug is named after the state ("NY-stat-in"). It is produced by the bacterium Streptomyces noursei. Hazen and Brown donated the patent royalties to a fund supporting scientific research.

Mechanism and use

Nystatin is a polyene antifungal that binds to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane, forming pores that allow leakage of cellular contents and kill the fungus. It is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and skin, so it is used topically or orally for local effect—for example as a mouthwash ("swish and swallow") for oral thrush—rather than for systemic infections. Side effects are generally mild and include local irritation and, with oral use, nausea.
