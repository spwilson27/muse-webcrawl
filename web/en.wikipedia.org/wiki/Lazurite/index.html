Lazurite is a tectosilicate mineral with sulfate, sulfur and chloride ions, and the principal blue constituent of the metamorphic rock lapis lazuli. Its chemical formula is commonly written (Na,Ca)8[(S,SO4,Cl)2|(AlSiO4)6]. The intense blue colour comes from trisulfur radical anions (S3−) trapped in the aluminosilicate cage structure of the sodalite group.

Lazurite usually occurs as masses rather than well-formed crystals, intergrown with calcite (white), pyrite (brassy yellow) and other minerals that make up lapis lazuli. Well-formed dodecahedral crystals are rare but prized by collectors. It is distinguished from the related mineral haüyne by its deeper colour and from sodalite by the presence of sulfur rather than chlorine as the dominant volatile component.

Occurrence

Lazurite forms by contact metamorphism of limestone and is associated with calcite, pyrite, diopside, humite, forsterite and muscovite. The classic locality is the Sar-i-Sang district of Badakhshan Province, Afghanistan, mined for more than 6,000 years. Other localities include the Baikal region of Siberia, Mount Vesuvius and the Alban Hills in Italy, Mogok in Myanmar, and deposits in Chile and Colorado.

Uses

Ground lazurite — in the form of high-grade lapis lazuli — was the source of the pigment ultramarine, the most expensive blue pigment of the Renaissance, reserved for the most important elements of paintings and illuminated manuscripts. Synthetic ultramarine, chemically identical in colour, has been manufactured since 1828. Lapis lazuli remains widely used in jewellery, inlay, carvings and ornamental stone.
