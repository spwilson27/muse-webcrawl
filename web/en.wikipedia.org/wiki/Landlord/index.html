A landlord is the owner of a house, apartment, condominium, land, or real estate which is rented or leased to an individual or business, who is called a tenant (also a lessee or renter). The term derives from the lord of a manor in the feudal system; its feminine form, landlady, is sometimes used for women, though "landlord" is commonly applied to all genders today.

History

Private landlordism grew with urbanisation during the Industrial Revolution, when factory workers crowded into rented housing owned by small investors and large estates. In Britain, Ireland, and elsewhere, absentee landlordism and high rents became major political grievances — fuelling Ireland's Land War of the 1880s and successive land reform acts. The 20th century saw the rise of public and social housing, then, in many countries from the 1980s onward, a resurgence of private renting, including Britain's "buy-to-let" market.

Rights and obligations

The landlord–tenant relationship is governed by a lease and by statute, which varies widely by jurisdiction. Typical landlord obligations include maintaining the property in habitable condition, complying with health and safety codes (gas, electrical, fire safety), protecting tenant deposits where required, and giving proper notice before entry or eviction. Tenants typically owe rent, care of the property, and notice of defects. Many jurisdictions provide security of tenure, rent regulation, or eviction protections of varying strength.

Types

Landlords range from individuals renting a single property, through portfolio investors, to institutional landlords — corporations and funds owning thousands of homes, whose growth since the 2008 financial crisis has been politically controversial. Commercial landlords lease shops, offices, and farmland under distinct legal regimes.
