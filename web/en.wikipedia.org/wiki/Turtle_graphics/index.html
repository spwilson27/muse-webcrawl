Turtle graphics

Image placeholder: turtle graphics

Turtle graphics are a key feature of the Logo programming language and a popular way to introduce programming to children and beginners. The concept centres on a robotic "turtle" — originally a physical floor robot, later an on-screen cursor — carrying a pen. Given commands such as forward and right, the turtle moves across a plane, drawing lines as it goes when its pen is down.

History

Turtle graphics were developed in the late 1960s by Seymour Papert and colleagues at MIT as part of the Logo language, building on work by Wally Feurzeig and others at Bolt, Beranek and Newman. Physical turtle robots, controlled by a computer and fitted with pens, dragged trails across large sheets of paper. The screen version became famous through school computers of the 1980s, such as the BBC Micro, and Papert's book Mindstorms (1980) set out the educational philosophy behind it.

Method

The turtle has a position, a heading and a pen state. Typical commands are:

forward 100      ; move 100 steps, drawing if pen is down
right 90         ; turn 90 degrees clockwise
penup / pendown  ; lift or lower the pen
repeat 4 [ forward 100 right 90 ]   ; draw a square

Because movement is relative to the turtle's own heading, learners can reason "turtle geometry" by imagining themselves walking the path. Procedures, variables and recursion extend the idea to elaborate drawings: fractals such as the Koch snowflake and Hilbert curve are classic turtle programs.

Modern implementations

Turtle graphics survive in many environments, notably Python's built-in turtle module, which closely follows the Logo model and is widely used in introductory courses. Similar systems appear in KTurtle, Scratch's pen blocks, and numerous web-based teaching tools, keeping the turtle a standard first encounter with code more than fifty years after its invention.
