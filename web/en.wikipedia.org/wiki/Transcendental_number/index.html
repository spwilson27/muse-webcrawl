Transcendental number

TypeReal or complex number
DefinitionNot a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients
Examplesπ, e, Liouville's constant
Contrasted withAlgebraic number

In mathematics, a transcendental number is a real or complex number that is not algebraic — that is, it is not a root of any non-zero polynomial equation with integer (or, equivalently, rational) coefficients. The best-known transcendental numbers are π and e.

Almost all real numbers are transcendental

History

The term "transcendental" was used by Leibniz in 1682 for quantities not expressible by algebraic means. Joseph Liouville proved in 1844 that transcendental numbers exist, constructing explicit examples (the Liouville numbers) that are approximated by rationals too well to be algebraic. Georg Cantor showed in 1874 that the algebraic numbers are countable while the reals are not, proving that "almost all" numbers are transcendental — though identifying any particular number as transcendental is often very difficult.

Charles Hermite proved in 1873 that e is transcendental, and Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 that π is transcendental, settling the ancient problem of squaring the circle: since π is not constructible, the task is impossible with compass and straightedge. In 1934 the Gelfond–Schneider theorem resolved Hilbert's seventh problem, showing that ab is transcendental for algebraic a ≠ 0, 1 and irrational algebraic b.

Open problems

It remains unknown whether many familiar constants are transcendental, including Euler's constant γ, Catalan's constant, and even combinations such as π + e (though at least one of π + e and πe must be transcendental). Schanuel's conjecture, if true, would settle many of these questions.
