Edict of Gülhane

The Edict of Gülhane (Ottoman Turkish: Gülhane Hatt-ı Şerifi, "Noble Edict of the Rose Chamber"), also known as the Tanzimat Fermanı, was a proclamation by Ottoman Sultan Abdülmecid I issued on 3 November 1839 in the Gülhane ("Rose Chamber") park outside the Topkapı Palace in Constantinople. Read publicly by Grand Vizier Mustafa Reşid Pasha, the edict launched the Tanzimat ("Reorganisation") era of reform and modernisation of the Ottoman Empire.

Contents

The edict promised, for all subjects of the empire regardless of religion, guarantees for the security of life, honour and property; a regular system of taxation to replace tax farming, with assessment according to means; regular conscription with fixed terms of military service; public trials and due process in criminal law; and an end to confiscation of the property of executed criminals. It framed these reforms as a return to the principles of Islamic law and justice that, it claimed, had underpinned the empire's former strength, and it sought the support of European powers as well as of the empire's own population.

Background and consequences

The proclamation came at a moment of crisis: the empire had just suffered defeat by the forces of Muhammad Ali of Egypt at the Battle of Nezib (June 1839), and Sultan Mahmud II had died days later. The edict aimed to win British and French support against Muhammad Ali and to strengthen central government control over the provinces. Although implementation was uneven and often resisted, the Gülhane edict set the reform agenda pursued over the following decades — new legal codes, secular schools, reformed ministries and, in 1856, the Reform Edict (Islahat Fermanı), which went further in proclaiming the equality of Muslim and non-Muslim subjects. Historians regard Gülhane as a foundational document of Ottoman modernisation and a landmark in the empire's constitutional development, anticipating the first Ottoman constitution of 1876.
