Edible mushrooms are the fleshy, spore-bearing fruiting bodies of fungi that can be safely eaten by humans. Humans have gathered wild mushrooms since prehistoric times, and mushroom cultivation — begun in China and Japan many centuries ago and industrialised in Europe and North America — now produces tens of millions of tonnes annually; China accounts for the large majority of world production. Edible species number in the hundreds, though only a few dozen are widely cultivated or traded.

Common species

The button mushroom, Agaricus bisporus (sold as white, cremini and portobello mushrooms at different maturities), dominates Western markets. Other major cultivated mushrooms include shiitake (Lentinula edodes), oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus), enoki (Flammulina), king oyster, shimeji and the paddy-straw mushroom. Highly prized wild species that resist cultivation include porcini (Boletus edulis), chanterelles, morels and truffles, which are mycorrhizal partners of trees and are gathered from forests.

Nutrition and cuisine

Mushrooms are low in calories and sodium, contain B vitamins, selenium, potassium and the antioxidant ergothioneine, and are one of the few non-animal sources of vitamin D when exposed to sunlight. Their savoury flavour derives from free glutamates, making them a classic source of umami and a popular meat substitute. They feature in cuisines worldwide, eaten raw, sautéed, grilled, dried (which concentrates flavour, as with dried shiitake and porcini) or in soups, sauces and risottos.

Safety

Many wild fungi are poisonous, and some deadly species — such as the death cap (Amanita phalloides) — resemble edible ones. Foraged mushrooms must be identified with expert certainty; even edible species can cause reactions in some people, and mushrooms bioaccumulate heavy metals from polluted soils. A common rule among mycologists is that no wild mushroom should be eaten unless its identity is beyond doubt.
