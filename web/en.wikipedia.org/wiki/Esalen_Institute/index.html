Esalen Institute

The Esalen Institute is a retreat centre at Big Sur, California, founded in 1962, and a historic centre of the Human Potential Movement.

The Esalen Institute, commonly called Esalen, is a not-for-profit retreat centre and intentional community in Big Sur, California, set on cliffs above the Pacific Ocean around natural hot springs long sacred to the Esselen people, from whom it takes its name. Founded in 1962 by Stanford graduates Michael Murphy and Dick Price, it became the emblematic home of the Human Potential Movement and a laboratory of alternative spirituality, psychology and bodywork.

History

The land, Slates Hot Springs, had been homesteaded by Thomas Slate in the 1880s and run as a rough roadside spa. Murphy, whose family owned the property, had lived at Sri Aurobindo's ashram in India; Price had studied psychology and experienced mental breakdown and institutionalisation, from which he emerged determined to create gentler forms of therapy. After a first seminar by Aldous Huxley on "Human Potentialities", Esalen hosted teachers who shaped the era: Abraham Maslow, Fritz Perls (who developed Gestalt therapy in residence), Ida Rolf, Will Schutz, Alan Watts, Timothy Leary, Joseph Campbell, Gregory Bateson and many others.

Programmes and influence

Esalen pioneered encounter groups, Gestalt practice, massage and bodywork — "Esalen massage" remains a trademarked style developed at its baths — and workshops spanning meditation, yoga, tai chi, psychedelics (in its early years), physics and consciousness (the Fundamental Physics Group that influenced popular books such as The Tao of Physics), and citizen diplomacy with the Soviet Union during the Cold War. Writers including Henry Miller and Hunter S. Thompson, who worked briefly as a caretaker, feature in its lore. Critics have mocked its self-absorption — Tom Wolfe and Joan Didion wrote wryly of it — but its vocabulary of self-actualisation and the encounter group deeply permeated American corporate training and therapeutic culture.

Today

Esalen still operates as a retreat centre offering workshops, personal retreats and scholar residencies, on land partially damaged by landslides and the closure of Highway 1 at various times. It remains a pilgrimage site of the American counterculture.
