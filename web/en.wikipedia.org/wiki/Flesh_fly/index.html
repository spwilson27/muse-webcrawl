Flesh fly

Flesh flies are members of the family Sarcophagidae (from the Greek σαρκο- sarco-, "flesh", and φαγεῖν phagein, "to eat"), a family of true flies in the order Diptera. Members of this family can mostly be recognised by their checkered pattern on the abdomen and stripes on the thorax; many species have bright red eyes.

A flesh fly (Sarcophaga species)

Biology

Unlike most flies, flesh flies are ovoviviparous: the female retains the eggs inside her body until they hatch and then deposits live first-instar larvae (maggots) on the food source. Larvae of many species feed on decaying flesh and carrion, which gives the family its common name, but others feed on dung, decaying plant material, or are parasitoids of other insects and invertebrates. Some species parasitise mammals, including occasionally humans, causing myiasis.

Taxonomy

The family contains around 3,000 described species in three subfamilies: Miltogramminae, Paramacronychiinae, and Sarcophaginae. Members are notoriously difficult to identify to species level by external appearance alone; examination of male genitalia is often required.

Forensic importance

Because flesh flies are among the first insects to colonise carrion, their larvae are used in forensic entomology to help estimate the post-mortem interval of corpses.
