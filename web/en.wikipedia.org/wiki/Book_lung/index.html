A book lung is a type of respiration organ used for atmospheric gas exchange that is present in many arachnids, such as scorpions and spiders. The structure consists of a stack of thin, soft, leaf-like plates (lamellae) rich in haemolymph, arranged like the pages of a book within a cavity of the opisthosoma (abdomen). It is one of the two respiratory systems found in spiders, the other being tracheae.

Structure and function

Each book lung opens to the outside through a slit-like spiracle on the ventral side of the abdomen. Air enters an atrium and diffuses into the air spaces between the lamellae; the lamellae themselves are hollow and filled with haemolymph (blood). Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across the thin walls between the air and the blood, which is kept from collapsing by cuticular struts and is circulated by the heart. Scorpions have four pairs of book lungs; spiders have one or two pairs, located on the second (and third) abdominal segments.

Evolution

Book lungs are modified from the book gills of aquatic chelicerates such as horseshoe crabs, in which the stacked plates are external and used for breathing in water. Internalising the gills produced the book lung as chelicerates colonised land, making the organ one of the earliest adaptations for terrestrial respiration in arthropods. The most primitive spiders, the Mesothelae, retain two pairs of book lungs, while most modern spiders have one pair of book lungs supplemented by tracheae.

Distribution

Book lungs occur in scorpions (four pairs), spiders (one or two pairs), whip scorpions, whip spiders and schizomids. Orders of arachnids that rely entirely on tracheae, such as harvestmen and most mites, lack book lungs. Some very small spiders have neither system well developed and exchange gas partly through the body surface.
