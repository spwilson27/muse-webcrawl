Alvar Aalto

Hugo Alvar Henrik Aalto (pronounced [ˈhuɡo ˈɑlʋɑr ˈhenrik ˈɑːlto]; 3 February 1898 – 11 May 1976) was a Finnish architect and designer. His work includes architecture, furniture, textiles and glassware, as well as sculptures and paintings. He never regarded himself as an artist, seeing painting and sculpture as "branches of the tree whose trunk is architecture." Aalto's early career ran in parallel with the rapid economic and industrial growth of Finland, and his clients included several of the country's leading industrialists.

His work spanned Neoclassicism, functionalist modernism and an organic, materially expressive mature style. He is regarded as one of the masters of modern architecture and a central figure of Scandinavian design; his furniture company Artek and the glassware firm Iittala still produce his designs.

Early life and career

Aalto was born in Kuortane and studied architecture at the Helsinki University of Technology, graduating in 1921. He opened his first office in Jyväskylä in 1923 and married architect Aino Marsio the following year; they collaborated on many works until her death in 1949. Early commissions in a Nordic classical style gave way to functionalism after travels in Europe and contact with the Bauhaus circle.

Major works

Key buildings include the Paimio Sanatorium (1929–33), a landmark of functionalist design conceived as an "instrument for healing"; the Viipuri Library (1927–35); the Villa Mairea (1938–39), a synthesis of modernism and Finnish vernacular materials; the Baker House dormitory at MIT (1947–49), with its undulating brick facade; the Säynätsalo Town Hall; and Finlandia Hall in Helsinki (1962–75). His unrealized and realized urban plans, including the Helsinki city centre plan, also shaped postwar Finnish debate.

Furniture and glassware

With Aino Aalto, Otto Korhonen and Nils Hahl, he co-founded Artek in 1935. His bentwood furniture — notably the Paimio chair, designed for tuberculosis patients, and the L-leg stool — pioneered laminated birch techniques. The Savoy vase (1936), with its free, undulating outline, remains an icon of Finnish glass design.

Legacy

Aalto's humanized modernism — his attention to light, acoustics, material warmth and the user's experience — influenced generations of architects. In Finland he is a national figure: his image appeared on the 50 markkaa banknote, and the Alvar Aalto Medal, established in 1967, is a major international architecture prize.
