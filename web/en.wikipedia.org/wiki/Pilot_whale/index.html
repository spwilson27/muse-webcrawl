Pilot whales are large oceanic dolphins of the genus Globicephala, comprising the long-finned pilot whale (G. melas) of cool temperate waters and the short-finned pilot whale (G. macrorhynchus) of tropical and subtropical waters. Despite their name, they are dolphins, not true whales; the name is thought to derive from the belief that pods follow a leader, or "pilot".

Description

Pilot whales are among the largest oceanic dolphins, with males reaching 6–7.6 m in length. They have rounded, bulbous foreheads (melons), dark grey to black bodies, and a sickle-shaped dorsal fin set forward on the back. They are highly social, living in matrilineal pods of ten to thirty individuals that can aggregate into hundreds.

Behaviour and diet

Pilot whales are deep divers that feed mainly on squid, taken at depths of several hundred metres, as well as on fish such as mackerel and herring. Their strong social bonds are thought to underlie their notorious mass strandings, in which entire pods beach together. They have also been kept in oceanaria and were trained by the US Navy for deep-water retrieval tasks.

Human interactions

Pilot whales have long been hunted in drive fisheries, most famously the grindadráp of the Faroe Islands, as well as historically in Japan, Norway, and Newfoundland. Populations are considered relatively abundant, though the species face threats from bycatch, noise pollution, and accumulation of pollutants.
