Wirtschaftswunder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Wirtschaftswunder (German for "economic miracle"), also known as the Miracle on the Rhine, was the rapid reconstruction and development of the economies of West Germany and Austria after the Second World War, adopting an ordoliberal economic policy based on the concept of the social market economy (soziale Marktwirtschaft). The phrase was first used by The Times in 1950.

Under Chancellor Konrad Adenauer and Economics Minister Ludwig Erhard, West Germany grew from wartime devastation into one of the world's largest economies within a decade: industrial production more than doubled between 1948 and 1957 and unemployment fell from around 10 percent to under 2 percent, transforming everyday life and defining the Federal Republic's identity.

Background

In 1945 German cities lay in ruins, production stood at a fraction of prewar levels, and the black market dominated daily exchange. Yet the industrial plant had suffered less than appearances suggested, the workforce was skilled, and millions of expellees from the east added labour.

Currency reform and market liberalisation

On 20 June 1948 the Deutsche Mark replaced the Reichsmark in the western zones, wiping out monetary overhang. Simultaneously, Erhard — on his own authority — abolished most price controls and rationing, trusting supply to respond. Goods reappeared in shop windows almost overnight; the reform, backed by Marshall Plan aid and the 1953 London Debt Agreement, is conventionally the miracle's starting point.

Causes of the boom

Historians cite the social market economy's competition and hard-currency discipline, export demand (boosted by the Korean War boom), wage restraint by trade unions, co-determination in industry, an undervalued exchange rate within the Bretton Woods system, European integration via the Coal and Steel Community (1951) and the EEC (1957), and the flood of consumer goods — the Volkswagen Beetle above all — symbolising rising living standards.

Social consequences

Real wages rose steadily; housing programmes rebuilt the cities; "guest workers" (Gastarbeiter) were recruited from Italy, then Turkey and elsewhere from 1955 to fill labour shortages. The miracle underwrote the stability of West German democracy and the Adenauer slogan "Keine Experimente" ("No experiments").

End of the miracle

Growth faltered in the recession of 1966–67, and the 1973 oil shock ended the postwar boom era across the West, though the Wirtschaftswunder remains the benchmark of German economic memory.
