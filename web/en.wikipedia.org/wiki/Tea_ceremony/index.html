A tea ceremony is a ritualised form of making and serving tea, practised in several East Asian cultures, most famously in Japan, where it is known as chanoyu ("hot water for tea") or sadō/chadō ("the way of tea"). The Japanese ceremony centres on the preparation and presentation of matcha, powdered green tea, in a highly choreographed sequence of movements performed by a host for one or more guests.

History

Tea was introduced to Japan from China by Buddhist monks in the early medieval period, and powdered tea drinking was cultivated in Zen monasteries as an aid to meditation. In the fifteenth century the shogun's court developed elaborate tea gatherings amid displays of Chinese art objects. Murata Jukō (1423–1502) is credited with founding the wabi style, which emphasised rustic simplicity. His successors Takeno Jōō and, above all, Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591) refined the ceremony into the form still practised today, centred on a small, austere tea room and humble utensils.

The ceremony

A formal gathering (chaji) may last up to four hours and includes a light meal (kaiseki), sweets, thick tea (koicha) and thin tea (usucha). Every element — the scroll in the alcove (tokonoma), the flower arrangement (chabana), the choice of bowl, whisk, tea caddy and ladle — is selected by the host for the season and the guests. Guests purify their hands, admire the utensils, and receive the bowl with both hands, turning it so as not to drink from its decorated front.

Schools

The three principal schools descended from Sen no Rikyū's grandson are Urasenke, Omotesenke and Mushakōjisenke. Urasenke, the largest, is based in Kyoto and has promoted tea culture internationally.

Other traditions

China has its own gongfu tea tradition, emphasising the skill of brewing oolong and other teas in small vessels, and Korea preserves the darye ceremony. Comparable rituals exist in Vietnam and Taiwan.
