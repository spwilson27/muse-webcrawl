Green's function

In mathematics, a Green's function (or fundamental solution) is the impulse response of an inhomogeneous linear differential operator defined on a domain with specified initial or boundary conditions. It is named after the British mathematician George Green, who first developed the concept in the 1820s and 1830s.

Definition

Given a linear differential operator L acting on functions of a variable x, the Green's function G(x, s) satisfies

L G(x, s) = δ(x − s),

where δ is the Dirac delta function. Once G is known, the solution of the inhomogeneous equation L u(x) = f(x) is obtained by convolution:

u(x) = ∫ G(x, s) f(s) ds.

Thus the Green's function encodes the response of the system to a point source, and the response to any distributed source follows by superposition.

Uses

Green's functions are fundamental tools in the theory of ordinary and partial differential equations, boundary-value problems in potential theory and electromagnetism, and quantum field theory, where the propagator plays the role of a Green's function. In physics they answer the question: what field is produced at point x by a unit source at point s?

Example

For the one-dimensional operator L = −d²/dx² on the real line, a Green's function is G(x, s) = −|x − s|/2, whose second derivative is the negative of the delta function. On a bounded interval with Dirichlet boundary conditions the Green's function is piecewise linear, vanishing at both endpoints.

History

Green introduced the idea in an 1828 essay on electricity and magnetism, privately printed in Nottingham. The method lay little noticed until it was taken up by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and subsequently generalised by Bernhard Riemann, who coined the name "Green's function".
