Joseph-Louis Lagrange

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (born Giuseppe Luigi Lagrangia, 25 January 1736 – 10 April 1813) was an Italian-French mathematician, physicist, and astronomer. He made fundamental contributions to analysis, number theory, and both classical and celestial mechanics, and is regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of the eighteenth century.

Life

Lagrange was born in Turin, in the Kingdom of Sardinia. A self-taught mathematician, he was appointed professor at the Royal Artillery School in Turin while still in his teens and co-founded the Turin academy of sciences. His work on the calculus of variations, developed in correspondence with Leonhard Euler, led Frederick the Great to invite him to Berlin, where in 1766 he succeeded Euler as director of mathematics at the Prussian Academy of Sciences. He spent twenty years in Berlin, producing much of his greatest work.

In 1787 Lagrange moved to Paris at the invitation of Louis XVI. He survived the Revolution — during which his friend Lavoisier was executed — and became a leading figure in French science: he helped develop the metric system, taught at the new École Polytechnique, and was made a senator and count by Napoleon. His Mécanique analytique (1788), written in Berlin, recast mechanics as pure analysis, free of diagrams — he boasted that no figures would be found in the work.

Contributions

Lagrange founded the calculus of variations, developed Lagrangian mechanics with its generalised coordinates and the principle of least action, and introduced Lagrangian points in the three-body problem — the equilibrium points in the Sun–Earth system now used for space observatories. In number theory he proved that every natural number is the sum of four squares, and solved the Pell equation. His work on the theory of equations, including Lagrange resolvents, laid groundwork later developed by Galois. He died in Paris in 1813 and is buried in the Panthéon; his name is one of the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
