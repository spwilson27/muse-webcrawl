Sen no Rikyū (千利休, 1522 – April 21, 1591), also known simply as Rikyū, is considered the historical figure with the most profound influence on chanoyu, the Japanese "Way of Tea", particularly the tradition of wabi-cha. He was also the first to emphasize several key aspects of the ceremony, including rustic simplicity, directness of approach and honesty of self. Originating from the Sengoku period and the Azuchi–Momoyama period, these aspects still persist in the tea ceremony.

Rikyū is known by many names; for consistency, he will be referred to as Rikyū in this article. He served as tea master to both Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the two great unifiers of Japan. In 1591, for reasons that remain debated, Hideyoshi ordered Rikyū to commit seppuku.

Early life

Rikyū was born in Sakai in present-day Osaka Prefecture. His father was a warehouse owner named Tanaka Yohei, who later took the family name Sen, and his mother was Gesshin Myōchin. As a youth, Rikyū studied tea under the Sakai townsman Kitamuki Dōchin, and at age nineteen, through Dōchin's introduction, he began to study tea under Takeno Jōō, who is also associated with the development of the wabi aesthetic in tea.

Tea master to the rulers of Japan

In 1579, at the age of 58, Rikyū became the tea master for Oda Nobunaga. Following Nobunaga's death in 1582, Rikyū served Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had him in high regard. Rikyū had a great influence on the development of the tea ceremony during this period, emphasising the rustic, rough-hewn aesthetic that came to be known as wabi. His tea room designs, such as the tiny two-mat Tai-an tea house at Myōki-an temple, embodied this ideal of humble simplicity.

Death

In 1591 relations between Rikyū and Hideyoshi deteriorated. Various theories exist for the rupture, including disagreements over a wooden statue of Rikyū placed at the gate of Daitoku-ji temple, where Hideyoshi would have had to pass beneath it. Hideyoshi ordered Rikyū to commit ritual suicide (seppuku), which he performed on April 21, 1591, after a final tea ceremony.

Legacy

Rikyū's three grandsons founded the three schools of the Japanese tea ceremony that survive today: Omotesenke, Urasenke, and Mushakōjisenke. His teachings, summarised in the concept of ichi-go ichi-e ("one time, one meeting"), continue to shape Japanese aesthetics and culture. He is also credited with designing several tea utensils, including the Raku tea bowls made by the potter Chōjirō.
