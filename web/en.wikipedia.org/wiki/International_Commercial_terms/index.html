The Incoterms (a syllabic abbreviation of International Commercial Terms) are a set of pre-defined commercial terms published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) that are widely used in international commercial transactions. Each three-letter term defines the respective obligations of buyer and seller for the delivery of goods under sales contracts: who arranges and pays for transport, insurance, customs clearance and loading, and at what point risk of loss or damage passes from seller to buyer.

History

The ICC first published the Incoterms rules in 1936, building on a study of common trade terms begun in the 1920s. Revisions followed in 1953, 1967, 1976, 1980, 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2020. The 2010 revision reduced the number of terms from 13 to 11 and introduced DAP in place of several D-terms; the 2020 revision renamed DAT to DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded) and raised the default insurance cover under CIP to Institute Cargo Clauses (A).

Use and legal status

Incoterms are not law; they take effect by incorporation into the contract of sale, and are referenced in the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods practice and trade usage. A complete reference must name the term, the named place or port, and the edition, e.g. "FOB Shanghai, Incoterms 2020". Misuse remains common: FOB and CIF are frequently (and incorrectly) used for containerised cargo delivered to a terminal, for which FCA and CIP are recommended, since risk under FOB passes only when goods are on board the vessel.
