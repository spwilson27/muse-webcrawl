Béla IV (1206 – 3 May 1270) was King of Hungary and Croatia from 1235 to 1270, and Duke of Styria from 1254 to 1258. One of the most famous Hungarian kings, he is remembered above all for rebuilding the kingdom after the catastrophic Mongol invasion of 1241–42, earning him the title "second founder of the homeland" (második honalapító).

Early reign

The eldest son of Andrew II, Béla was crowned junior king in 1214 and governed Transylvania and later Slavonia, where he clashed with his father's lavish grants of royal land. As king he tried to restore royal authority and recover crown estates, and admitted the Cuman refugees fleeing the Mongols in 1239 — a controversial decision that turned tragic when a mob murdered the Cuman khan Köten in 1241 and the Cumans ravaged the country as they departed.

The Mongol invasion

In April 1241 the Mongols under Batu Khan and Subutai destroyed the Hungarian army at the Battle of Mohi. Béla fled through Austria — where Duke Frederick II extorted his treasure — to Croatia, pursued by Kadan's detachment to the Dalmatian coast, where he sheltered in Trogir. Hungary lost perhaps a quarter to half of its population. The Mongols withdrew in 1242 on the death of Ögedei Khan.

Reconstruction

Béla radically reformed defence policy: he granted land on condition of building stone castles (nearly a hundred were raised), founded or refounded some fifty towns with charters for "guest" settlers, and moved Buda to its defensible Castle Hill. He repopulated the country, recalled the Cumans, and settled the Jász (Alans). Abroad, he fought Austria and Bohemia — losing and regaining Styria — and built a web of dynastic alliances, marrying his daughters into the ruling houses of Poland, Rus' and Bavaria. His later years saw conflict with his son, the future Stephen V, who forced a division of the kingdom in 1262. Béla died in 1270 on Rabbits' Island (now Margaret Island, Budapest), named for his daughter St Margaret, who lived in the Dominican convent there.
