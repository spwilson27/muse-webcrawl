The Frankfurt Parliament (German: Frankfurter Nationalversammlung) was the first freely elected parliament for all of Germany, including the German-populated areas of the Austrian Empire. Its sessions were held in St. Paul's Church (the Paulskirche) in Frankfurt am Main from 18 May 1848 to 31 May 1849.


The assembly in the Paulskirche, 1848

Background

The Parliament emerged from the March Revolution of 1848 in the German states. A "pre-parliament" (Vorparlament) arranged elections to a national assembly that would draft a constitution for a united German nation-state and define fundamental citizens' rights.

Work and constitution

The Assembly was dominated by educated liberals — professors, judges and civil servants — and was sometimes mocked as a "parliament of professors." In December 1848 it adopted a catalogue of Basic Rights (Grundrechte) guaranteeing equality before the law, freedom of speech, press and assembly, and the abolition of noble privileges. In March 1849 it completed the Frankfurt Constitution, which envisaged a federal German empire with a hereditary emperor and universal male suffrage for the Reichstag.

The Assembly voted for the "Lesser German" solution (excluding Austria) and offered the imperial crown to King Frederick William IV of Prussia. The king refused it — famously dismissing it as a crown picked up from the gutter — because he would not accept sovereignty from a popular assembly.

Collapse

With the revolution defeated by Prussian and Austrian arms, the larger states ordered their deputies home. A rump parliament moved to Stuttgart in June 1849 and was dispersed by Württemberg troops on 18 June 1849. Although the Parliament failed, its constitution and basic rights became enduring models for later German constitutions, including the Basic Law of 1949.
