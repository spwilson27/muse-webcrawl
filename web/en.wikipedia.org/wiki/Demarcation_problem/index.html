Demarcation problem

Demarcation problem

The demarcation problem in the philosophy of science concerns how to distinguish between science and non-science, including pseudoscience, and where the boundaries of science lie. The problem, debated since antiquity but given its modern form in the twentieth century, asks what criterion — if any — separates genuinely scientific theories and methods from metaphysics, religion, and pseudo-scientific claims such as astrology.

Popper's falsifiability

Karl Popper proposed falsifiability as the criterion of demarcation: a theory is scientific only if it is in principle capable of being refuted by observation. Popper contrasted Einstein's general relativity, which made risky predictions, with psychoanalysis and Marxist history, which he argued could explain any outcome and therefore predicted nothing. Falsifiability became enormously influential among scientists, though philosophers objected that individual theories are never tested in isolation (the Duhem–Quine problem) and that many accepted scientific claims are probabilistic.

Kuhn, Lakatos and Feyerabend

Thomas Kuhn argued that what distinguishes science is not falsification but puzzle-solving within a shared paradigm, and that astrology failed because it never developed a tradition of cumulative puzzle-solving. Imre Lakatos proposed evaluating research programmes over time: a programme is scientific if it is theoretically and empirically "progressive" — predicting novel facts — and degenerating if it only accommodates anomalies after the fact. Paul Feyerabend argued against any fixed methodological rule, quipping that in the history of science "anything goes".

Laudan and later developments

In a widely cited 1983 essay, Larry Laudan declared the demarcation problem a pseudo-problem, arguing that no set of necessary and sufficient conditions had survived scrutiny and that the important question is whether a belief is well supported, not whether it is "scientific". Despite this, demarcation remains practically important: courts, in cases such as McLean v. Arkansas (1982) and Kitzmiller v. Dover (2005), have had to rule on whether creationism and intelligent design count as science, and funding bodies, journals and regulators routinely police the boundary. Contemporary philosophers such as Massimo Pigliucci and Maarten Boudry have revived the problem, proposing cluster or family-resemblance approaches in which pseudoscience is identified by a constellation of features rather than a single criterion.
