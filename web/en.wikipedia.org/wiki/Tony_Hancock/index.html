Anthony John Hancock (12 May 1924 – 25 June 1968) was an English comedian and actor who was one of the most popular performers on British radio and television in the 1950s and early 1960s. He is best known for Hancock's Half Hour, the radio and television situation comedy written by Ray Galton and Alan Simpson, in which he played a pompous, down-at-heel version of himself living at 23 Railway Cuttings, East Cheam, alongside Sid James and, on radio, Bill Kerr, Moira Lister, and Kenneth Williams.

Career

Hancock developed his act in wartime RAF Gang Shows and on BBC radio variety programmes before Hancock's Half Hour began on radio in 1954. The show transferred to television in 1956 and became a landmark of British comedy; celebrated episodes include "The Blood Donor" and "The Radio Ham," and lines such as "A pint? That's very nearly an armful!" entered the national vocabulary.

Anxious to progress, Hancock broke with Galton and Simpson and then with Sid James, believing their material held him back — decisions widely regarded as turning points in his decline. His later television series and films, including The Rebel (1961) and The Punch and Judy Man (1963), never matched his earlier success. Beset by alcoholism and depression, Hancock travelled to Australia in 1968 to make a series for the Seven Network; he died by suicide in Sydney in June of that year.

Legacy

Hancock's lugubrious, failed-aspirant persona influenced generations of British comedy, from Steptoe and Son (also by Galton and Simpson) to characters such as Captain Mainwaring, Basil Fawlty, and Alan Partridge. Many of his radio episodes were wiped, but re-recordings and surviving television episodes remain widely admired.
