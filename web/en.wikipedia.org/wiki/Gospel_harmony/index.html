Gospel harmony

A gospel harmony is an attempt to compile the four canonical gospels of the Christian New Testament into a single account. This may take the form either of a single, merged narrative or a tabular format with one column for each gospel. Two particularly influential early examples are Tatian's Diatessaron in the 2nd century and Augustine's De consensu evangelistarum ("On the Harmony of the Evangelists") in the early 5th century.

Harmonies are constructed by many people, both because they hold the gospels to be without error (inerrant) and because harmonious accounts of the life, death and resurrection of Jesus are judged useful. The synoptic problem — the literary relationship between the gospels of Matthew, Mark and Luke — complicates, but also informs, the work of harmonists, since parallel passages may derive from common sources or independent traditions.

History

The earliest harmony was the Diatessaron ("through [the] four") compiled by the Assyrian theologian Tatian around AD 160–175, combining the four gospels — and possibly additional material — into a single continuous narrative. It became the standard gospel text of the Syriac-speaking church for centuries before being replaced by the separate gospels.

Ammonius of Alexandria produced a synopsis in parallel columns, on which Eusebius of Caesarea based his famous canon tables, an apparatus of cross-references that prefaced gospel manuscripts for over a millennium. Augustine of Hippo defended the consistency of the evangelists in De consensu evangelistarum (c. 400). The Middle Ages produced influential verse and prose harmonies, such as the Old Saxon Heliand and the Vita Jesu Christi of Ludolph of Saxony.

Modern harmonies

Print harmonies proliferated after the Reformation, and in the modern period scholars such as John Calvin (whose Harmony of the Gospels treated the synoptics), and later A. T. Robertson (A Harmony of the Gospels, 1922) and Kurt Aland (Synopsis Quattuor Evangeliorum) produced widely used parallel-column synopses. Some harmonies are contentious when they resolve discrepancies in chronology — for example, the number of times Peter denied Jesus, or the number of Jesus's visits to Jerusalem — by multiplying events or rearranging the narrative.
