Collective security

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collective security is a system of maintaining peace in which states agree that an attack on one is an attack on all, and that aggression will be met by the combined power of the rest. It differs from collective defence (alliances such as NATO directed against an identified external threat) in being universal and inward-looking: the enemy is any aggressor, including a member. The idea was institutionalised in the League of Nations Covenant (Articles 10–16, with sanctions against members resorting to war) and carried into the UN Charter's Chapter VII system, in which the United Nations Security Council may mandate sanctions and force on behalf of the whole membership.

The League's system failed its great tests — Manchuria (1931), Ethiopia (1935–36) — because members would not bear the costs of enforcement against determined aggressors and because key powers were absent; the UN system has been constrained by the great-power veto, its notable enforcement actions (Korea 1950, the Gulf 1990–91) occurring only in the veto's absence or post-Cold War consensus. Theorists note collective security's demanding assumptions: aggression must be identifiable, states must value the system above immediate interests, and the preponderance of the “rest” must be credible.
