Snorri Sturluson

Snorri Sturluson (Old Norse: [ˈsnorːe ˈsturloˌson]; Icelandic: [ˈsnɔrːɪ ˈstʏrtlʏˌsɔːn]; 1179 – 23 September 1241) was an Icelandic historian, poet, and politician. He was elected twice as lawspeaker of the Icelandic parliament, the Althing. He is commonly credited with writing the Prose Edda (also called the Younger Edda or Snorri's Edda) and the Heimskringla, a history of the Norwegian kings that begins with legendary material in Ynglinga saga and moves through to early medieval Scandinavian history. Because of the central importance of these works for Norse mythology and for the preservation of skaldic poetry, Snorri is often considered the most important figure in Old Norse literature.

Life

Snorri was born at Hvammur in western Iceland, son of Sturla Þórðarson, founder of the powerful Sturlung family. At age three he was fostered at Oddi, the great centre of learning in southern Iceland, by Jón Loftsson, the most influential chieftain in the country, whose household gave Snorri his education in law, genealogy, history and poetry. He married into wealth, acquired the estate of Borg and later Reykholt, and became lawspeaker in 1215.

Snorri travelled twice to Norway (1218–1220 and 1237–1239). King Haakon IV and Earl Skúli sought his help in bringing Iceland under Norwegian rule, and Snorri left Norway in 1239 against the king's wishes. In 1241 the king's emissary Gizurr Þorvaldsson led a band of men to Reykholt and murdered Snorri in his cellar; the killing is one of the most famous episodes of the violent Sturlung era that ended with Iceland's submission to Norway in 1262–64.

Works

Prose Edda

Composed around 1220, the Prose Edda is a handbook of Norse mythology and poetics intended to teach young poets the mythological references (kennings and heiti) needed to compose traditional skaldic verse. It consists of a Prologue, Gylfaginning (a dialogue retelling the myths from creation to Ragnarök), Skáldskaparmál (on poetic diction) and Háttatal (a clavis metrica of verse forms). It is the fullest surviving source for Norse mythology.

Heimskringla

The Heimskringla ("circle of the world", named from its opening words) is a collection of sagas about Norwegian kings from the mythical Yngling dynasty to Magnus Erlingsson in 1177. Snorri's preface famously discusses his sources — skaldic poems, oral tradition and earlier written sagas — with a critical spirit unusual for his time. The work includes the long, admired Óláfs saga helga on Saint Olaf.

Snorri has also been proposed as the author of Egils saga, though this is debated.

Legacy

Without Snorri's systematising work, much of Norse myth and skaldic verse would be unintelligible or lost. His writings underpin modern knowledge of pre-Christian Scandinavian religion and inspired later artists and writers from Tolkien onward; the Snorrastofa cultural centre at Reykholt stands on his estate.
