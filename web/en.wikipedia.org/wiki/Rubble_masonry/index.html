Rubble masonry

Rubble masonry
Type: Stone construction technique
Materials: Rough, undressed stone; mortar
Uses: Walls, foundations, vernacular buildings

Rubble masonry is a form of stone construction using rough, undressed or minimally dressed stones set in mortar. It is among the oldest forms of masonry and contrasts with ashlar masonry, in which stones are precisely cut into regular blocks.

Types

Several varieties are recognised. Coursed rubble arranges the stones in rough horizontal layers, while random (uncoursed) rubble uses stones of irregular size and shape fitted together as they come. Dry rubble (dry stone) is laid without mortar. When rubble forms the core of a wall faced with ashlar or brick, it is known as rubble filling or, in Roman work, opus caementicium when set in concrete.

Construction

Rubble walls are generally built thicker than ashlar walls to compensate for the irregular bedding of the stones. Larger "through stones" or bond stones span the wall's thickness to tie the two faces together, and small stone chips (spalls) are used to level and pack the joints. Because the material can be gathered locally — field stone, river cobbles, quarry waste — rubble masonry has been the default walling of vernacular architecture in stony regions for millennia, from megalithic tombs and medieval castles to farm barns and boundary walls.
