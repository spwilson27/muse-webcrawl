Zarzuela

Zarzuela (Spanish: [θaɾˈθwela]) is a Spanish lyric-dramatic genre that alternates between spoken and sung scenes, incorporating operatic and popular song, orchestral music and dance. It emerged at the 17th-century court of Philip IV, taking its name from the royal Palacio de la Zarzuela near Madrid—named for the brambles (zarzas) around it—and flowered in the 19th and early 20th centuries as Spain's national form of music theatre. The genre also spread to Spanish colonies, notably Cuba and the Philippines, where it developed local forms.

History

The earliest zarzuelas, such as El laurel de Apolo (1658) by Calderón de la Barca and Juan de Hidalgo, mixed mythological plots with song for the court at the Zarzuela palace. The form declined under the Bourbon preference for Italian opera, then revived in the 19th century as Romantic zarzuela: Francisco Asenjo Barbieri's El barberillo de Lavapiés (1874) exemplifies the full-length género grande, while the one-act género chico—works like Tomás Bretón's La verbena de la Paloma (1894) and Ruperto Chapí's La revoltosa (1897)—captured Madrid popular life in the sainete tradition.

Characteristics

Zarzuelas combine spoken dialogue with romanzas, duets, choruses and dances drawing on Spanish regional and urban rhythms—jotas, seguidillas, habaneras and the chotis. Plots range from grand historical drama to farcical neighbourhood comedy. The early 20th century added lavish works by Pablo Sorozábal (La tabernera del puerto, 1936) and Federico Moreno Torroba before the Spanish Civil War and competition from film eroded the genre's popularity. Madrid's Teatro de la Zarzuela continues to stage revivals, and singers such as Plácido Domingo, raised in a zarzuela troupe, have championed the repertoire.

Colonial variants

In the Philippines the zarzuela became the sarswela, a vehicle for social commentary under American rule; in Cuba it merged with Afro-Cuban rhythms in the works of Ernesto Lecuona and others.
