Chondrite

A chondrite is a stony (non-metallic) meteorite that has not been modified, by either melting or differentiation, of the parent body. They are formed when various types of dust and small grains in the early Solar System accreted to form primitive asteroids.

Chondrites are the most common type of meteorite that falls to Earth, accounting for roughly 86 percent of all meteorites. Their study provides important clues about the origin and age of the Solar System, the composition of its primordial material, and the origin of organic compounds and water on Earth.

Characteristics

The defining feature of chondrites is the presence of chondrules: small, roughly spherical grains, typically 0.1 to 2 millimetres in diameter, composed largely of silicate minerals such as olivine and pyroxene. Chondrules appear to have formed as molten or partially molten droplets in space before being accreted into their parent asteroids.

Classification

Chondrites are divided into several groups, including ordinary chondrites (H, L, and LL), carbonaceous chondrites (rich in carbon and volatile compounds), enstatite chondrites, and other smaller clans. They are further graded by petrologic type, from 1 (most aqueously altered) to 6 (most thermally metamorphosed).

Composition

Apart from hydrogen and helium, chondrites have elemental abundances remarkably similar to those of the Sun, and they are considered the most primitive material in the Solar System, about 4.56 billion years old.
