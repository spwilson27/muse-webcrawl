Inflammasome

The inflammasome is a multiprotein intracellular complex of the innate immune system that is responsible for the activation of inflammatory responses. Upon sensing pathogens or danger signals, it assembles and activates the protease caspase-1, which cleaves the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) into their mature, secreted forms, and can trigger a form of inflammatory cell death called pyroptosis via gasdermin D.

Structure

A canonical inflammasome consists of a sensor protein (often a nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain-like receptor, NLR), the adaptor protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), and pro-caspase-1. Well-studied sensors include NLRP3, NLRP1, NLRC4, AIM2 (which detects cytosolic double-stranded DNA) and pyrin.

NLRP3

The NLRP3 inflammasome responds to a wide variety of stimuli — ATP, crystalline substances such as uric acid, cholesterol and silica, and many pathogens — usually after a priming step through Toll-like receptors. Dysregulated NLRP3 activation is implicated in gout, atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes, Alzheimer's disease and the cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPS), which are caused by gain-of-function mutations in NLRP3 itself.

History

The term was coined in 2002 by Jürg Tschopp and colleagues, who identified the complex as a caspase-activating platform.
