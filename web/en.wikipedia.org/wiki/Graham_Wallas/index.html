Graham Wallas

Graham Wallas (31 May 1858 – 9 August 1932) was an English socialist, social psychologist, educationalist and a leader of the Fabian Society, along with his friends George Bernard Shaw, Sidney Webb and Beatrice Webb. A co-founder of the London School of Economics (1895) and its first professor of political science, he is best known for his books Human Nature in Politics (1908), a pioneering critique of the assumption that political actors behave rationally, and The Art of Thought (1926), which presented his influential four-stage model of the creative process: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification.

Life

Wallas was born in Sunderland, the son of a clergyman, and educated at Shrewsbury School and Corpus Christi College, Oxford. He lost his religious faith at Oxford and, finding that he could not conscientiously continue as a schoolmaster at Highgate School under the required religious conditions, resigned in 1890 — an episode of conscience that impressed his contemporaries. He had joined the Fabian Society in 1886 and contributed to Fabian Essays in Socialism (1889). Wallas was active in London local government and education reform, serving on the London School Board and the London County Council, and he helped found the London School of Economics in 1895, lecturing there from its opening and becoming its first professor of political science in 1914, succeeding to a chair he held until 1923. He also taught in the United States as a visiting lecturer, influencing American political science. He died at Portloe, Cornwall, in 1932.

Human Nature in Politics

In Human Nature in Politics (1908) Wallas argued that political science had inherited an "intellectualist" fiction: the assumption that voters and politicians form opinions and make decisions by rational calculation. Drawing on contemporary psychology, he insisted that political thought is shaped by non-rational inference, habit, suggestion, party symbols and emotion. The book is considered a founding text of modern political psychology and behavioral political science, foreshadowing later research on heuristics, framing and motivated reasoning. Wallas's aim was reformist rather than cynical: understanding actual human nature, he held, is a precondition for designing democratic institutions that work with it rather than against it.

The Great Society and The Art of Thought

The Great Society (1914), dedicated to Walter Lippmann (who had been his student at Harvard lectures and returned the compliment in his own work), examined the strains placed on human psychology by the scale and complexity of modern industrial society. The Art of Thought (1926) systematized his account of creative thinking in four stages — preparation (conscious investigation of a problem), incubation (a period in which the problem is set aside while unconscious processing continues), illumination (the sudden appearance of the happy idea), and verification (testing and elaboration). The model, building on accounts by Helmholtz and Poincaré, became the standard framework in creativity research and is still widely taught.

Legacy

Wallas helped shape both British social democracy and the modern social sciences. His work at the LSE, his Fabian writings on education and municipal reform, and his psychological turn in political analysis influenced figures including Walter Lippmann and Harold Laski. The four-stage model of creativity is his most enduringly cited contribution across disciplines.
