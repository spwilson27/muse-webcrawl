Just war theory

The just war theory (bellum iustum) is a doctrine, also referred to as a tradition, of military ethics that seeks to ensure that a war is morally justifiable through a series of criteria, all of which must be met for a war to be considered just. It is studied by military leaders, ethicists, policy makers and theologians.

History

Antecedents appear in ancient Egypt, India (the Mahabharata), China (Mozi) and in Cicero's Rome. The theory was systematically developed by Augustine of Hippo, who reconciled Christian teaching with defence of the innocent, and by Thomas Aquinas, whose Summa Theologiae set conditions for a just war. The Spanish scholastics Francisco de Vitoria and Francisco Suárez, and the Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius, carried the tradition into international law. Michael Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars (1977) revived it in contemporary political philosophy.

Criteria

The criteria are traditionally grouped in three parts:

  • Jus ad bellum — justice in resorting to war: just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, proportionality of ends, last resort, and a reasonable prospect of success.
  • Jus in bello — justice in conduct during war: discrimination (distinguishing combatants from non-combatants) and proportionality of means, together with prohibitions such as those on perfidy and weapons malum in se.
  • Jus post bellum — justice after war: fair peace terms, reconstruction, and accountability for crimes.

Influence

The tradition underlies the United Nations Charter's rules on the use of force, the Geneva Conventions, and modern military doctrine, though it is challenged by both pacifists and realists.
