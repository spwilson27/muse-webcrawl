Thermes de Cluny

The Thermes de Cluny are the ruins of Roman public baths in Paris, France, standing at the junction of the boulevards Saint-Michel and Saint-Germain in the 5th arrondissement, on the Left Bank of the Seine. Built in Roman Lutetia around the end of the 2nd or the beginning of the 3rd century AD, the baths are among the best-preserved Roman remains in the city. Their great vaulted frigidarium (cold room) survives almost intact and now forms part of the Musée de Cluny — the national museum of the Middle Ages — which also occupies the adjoining Gothic Hôtel de Cluny. The site's most celebrated exhibit, the Pillar of the Boatmen (Pilier des Nautes), a Gallo-Roman votive column of the 1st century AD, is displayed in the frigidarium.

Description

The baths occupied a large insula of about 65 × 100 metres between the cardo maximus (now the boulevard Saint-Michel) and the decumanus. As in the classical plan, bathers passed through an apodyterium (changing room) to the frigidarium, tepidarium and caldarium, with a palaestra for exercise attached. Surviving above ground are the frigidarium — a vast hall some 21 metres long and 11.5 metres high, roofed with concrete groin vaults among the earliest preserved in Gaul, their springing still showing painted plaster and traces of decoration — and parts of the caldarium and tepidarium, together with subterranean service passages. Water was supplied by the aqueduct of Arcueil.

History

The baths were probably abandoned during the troubled third century and the ruined complex was progressively built over; the frigidarium was used across the Middle Ages as a storehouse and at times a chapel. The abbots of Cluny acquired the site in the fourteenth century and built their Paris hôtel beside it. After the French Revolution the ruins passed through various hands; the city of Paris purchased the baths in 1819 to protect them, and in 1843 the state created the museum in the Hôtel de Cluny, uniting the medieval collections of Alexandre Du Sommerard with the Roman baths. A major renovation of the museum, completed in 2022, re-integrated the frigidarium into the visitor route.

The Pillar of the Boatmen

Found beneath the choir of Notre-Dame in 1710, the Pillar of the Boatmen is a series of sculpted blocks dedicated to Jupiter by the nautae Parisiaci — the boatmen's guild of Lutetia — under the emperor Tiberius. Its reliefs juxtapose Roman gods (Jupiter, Mars, Fortuna) with Gaulish deities (Esus, Tarvos Trigaranus, Cernunnos — the latter's earliest known depiction and inscription), making it a key document of Gallo-Roman religion.
