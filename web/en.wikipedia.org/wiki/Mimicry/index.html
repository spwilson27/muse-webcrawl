Mimicry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In evolutionary biology, mimicry is the evolved resemblance of one organism to another organism or object, conferring a selective advantage on the mimic — typically protection from predators, or access to prey or hosts. It is among the most striking products of natural selection (see adaptation).

Types

Batesian mimicry (described by Henry Walter Bates in the Amazon, 1862): a harmless species copies a harmful or unpalatable one — hoverflies wearing wasp colours. Müllerian mimicry: several genuinely unpalatable species converge on a shared warning pattern, splitting the cost of educating predators. Aggressive mimicry: predators or parasites disguise themselves to approach prey — the anglerfish's lure, or cuckoos' eggs matching their hosts'. Plant examples include orchids mimicking female insects to achieve pollination.

Mimicry systems are dynamic: mimics must stay rarer than their models in Batesian systems, driving frequency-dependent selection and geographic mosaics of patterns. The phenomenon extends to behaviour and sound, and to the human sphere of camouflage and deception — and, in the microbial world, to pathogens mimicking host molecules to evade immunity (see antigen).
