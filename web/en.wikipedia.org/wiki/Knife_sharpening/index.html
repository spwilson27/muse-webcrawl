Knife sharpening

Sharpening a knife on a whetstone removes metal to form a fresh, acute edge.

Knife sharpening is the process of making a knife, or a similar tool, sharp by grinding against a hard, rough surface, typically a stone, or a soft surface with hard particles, such as sandpaper. Additionally, a leather razor strop, or canvas strop, is often used to straighten and polish an edge, though this is properly honing. Sharpening removes material from the blade to form a new edge where two bevels meet at an apex.

Method

Sharpening proceeds from coarse to fine abrasives. A damaged or very dull blade is first ground on a coarse stone (low grit) to establish the bevel angle and remove nicks; progressively finer stones refine the edge and remove the scratches of the previous stage. The blade is held at a consistent angle — commonly 15–20 degrees per side for kitchen knives, with Japanese knives often ground finer and Western knives somewhat coarser — and drawn across the stone in sweeping strokes, alternating sides to keep the edge centred. Grinding raises a burr (a thin foil of metal) along the opposite side, which signals that the apex has been reached; the burr is then removed by finer stones and stropping.

Tools

  • Whetstones — natural or synthetic stones, used with water or oil; Japanese waterstones are popular for fine edges.
  • Sharpening steels — ribbed steel rods that realign (hone) an edge rather than removing much metal.
  • Guided systems and pull-through sharpeners — fix the angle mechanically for consistency.
  • Electric sharpeners — motorised abrasive wheels or belts.
  • Strops — leather or fabric charged with fine compound, used for final polishing and burr removal.

Sharpening vs honing

Sharpening removes metal to create a new edge; honing merely straightens a rolled or bent edge without significantly removing material. Regular honing with a steel delays the need for full sharpening, but cannot restore a worn or chipped edge.

Edge geometry

The choice of edge angle balances sharpness against durability: finer angles (around 10–15° per side) cut more easily but chip and roll more readily, while coarser angles (20–25°) suit heavy-duty tools. Grind profiles — flat, hollow, convex, and compound (double-bevel) — also affect performance and ease of maintenance.
