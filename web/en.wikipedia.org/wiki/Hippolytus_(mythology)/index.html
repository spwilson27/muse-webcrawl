Hippolytus (mythology)

Hippolytus (Greek: Ἱππόλυτος, "unfetterer of horses") is a figure in Greek mythology, the son of Theseus and the Amazon Hippolyta (or Antiope). A devoted worshipper of the virgin goddess Artemis who scorned love and the goddess Aphrodite, he was destroyed by Aphrodite's revenge through the passion of his stepmother Phaedra — a story dramatised by Euripides, Seneca, and Racine.

Myth

In Euripides' tragedy Hippolytus (428 BC), Aphrodite, angered by Hippolytus's contempt for her and his exclusive devotion to Artemis, causes his stepmother Phaedra to fall desperately in love with him. When Phaedra's nurse reveals the passion to Hippolytus, he reacts with horror and revulsion. Phaedra hangs herself, leaving a tablet falsely accusing Hippolytus of rape. Theseus, believing the accusation, curses his son with one of the three curses granted him by Poseidon. A monstrous bull rises from the sea, frightens Hippolytus's horses, and he is dragged to his death in his wrecked chariot. Artemis reveals the truth to Theseus, and father and son are reconciled before Hippolytus dies.

Cult and afterlife

Hippolytus was worshipped at Troezen, where brides offered him a lock of hair before marriage, and at Athens. In some traditions, Asclepius raised him from the dead, and he was identified with the Roman figure Virbius of Aricia in Italy — the version told in Virgil's Aeneid and Ovid's Metamorphoses.

In later literature

The story was retold by Seneca in Phaedra, which heavily influenced Jean Racine's Phèdre (1677), one of the masterpieces of French classical tragedy.
