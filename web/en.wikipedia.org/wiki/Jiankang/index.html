Jiankang

Jiankang (建康), or Jianye (建業) as it was originally called, was the capital city of the Eastern Wu, the Jin dynasty, and the Southern dynasties in early medieval China. It stood at the site of the modern city of Nanjing, on the southern bank of the lower Yangtze River, and was one of the largest and most prosperous cities in the world during the Six Dynasties period.

History

The city was first fortified as Jinling under the kingdom of Chu and later Wu during the Warring States and Han periods. In 229 CE, Sun Quan, founder of Eastern Wu, moved his capital there and renamed it Jianye. After a brief return to a reunified China under the Western Jin, the fall of the north to non-Han regimes drove the Jin court south; in 318 the Eastern Jin re-established the capital at the city, renamed Jiankang to avoid a naming taboo.

For the next two and a half centuries Jiankang served successively as the capital of the Eastern Jin and the four Southern dynasties—Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen. Its population is estimated to have approached or exceeded one million, sustained by the migration of northern aristocratic families and by abundant rice surpluses from the Yangtze delta. The city was a great centre of Buddhism, literature, and court culture; the scholar-official class of the south developed much of classical Chinese letters there.

Decline

Jiankang suffered devastating destruction during the Hou Jing rebellion (548–552) against the Liang dynasty, when the capital was besieged and starved. In 589, the Sui general Han Qinhu crossed the Yangtze and captured the Chen capital, ending the division of China; the Sui ordered the city razed and its site turned to farmland. Nanjing later rose again on the site as the capital of the Southern Tang, the early Ming dynasty, and the Republic of China.
