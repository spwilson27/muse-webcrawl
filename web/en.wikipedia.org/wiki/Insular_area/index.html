Insular area

In United States law, an insular area is a U.S. territory that is not incorporated into a state or the District of Columbia. The term is used by the Department of the Interior's Office of Insular Affairs, which coordinates federal policy for the territories of Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa, as well as the freely associated states (Palau, the Marshall Islands, and the Federated States of Micronesia) that receive U.S. assistance under Compacts of Free Association. Puerto Rico, administered for most purposes by the Interior Department as well, is the largest insular area by population.

Legal status

The constitutional status of the insular areas derives from the Insular Cases, in which the Supreme Court distinguished "incorporated" territories (understood as destined for statehood, such as Alaska and Hawaii at the time) from "unincorporated" territories, where only fundamental provisions of the U.S. Constitution apply of their own force. The populated territories are further classified by whether they are "organized" — having an organic act from Congress creating a local government. Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the Northern Mariana Islands are organized unincorporated territories; American Samoa is unorganized and unincorporated, and its people are U.S. nationals rather than citizens at birth, a status unique among inhabited U.S. jurisdictions.

Minor outlying islands

The United States also administers the U.S. Minor Outlying Islands — mostly uninhabited atolls and islands such as Wake Island, Johnston Atoll, Midway Atoll, Palmyra Atoll, Jarvis Island, Howland Island, Baker Island, Kingman Reef, and Navassa Island — several of which fall under the Office of Insular Affairs for civil administration.

Governance and issues

Residents of the insular areas elect local governments and non-voting delegates (or a resident commissioner, in Puerto Rico's case) to the U.S. House of Representatives, but cannot vote in U.S. presidential elections. Debates over status — statehood, independence, or enhanced commonwealth — and over unequal treatment in federal programs such as Medicaid and Supplemental Security Income remain central political questions in the territories.
