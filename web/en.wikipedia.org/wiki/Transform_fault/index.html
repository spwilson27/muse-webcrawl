Transform fault

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transform fault or transform boundary, also known as a conservative plate boundary, is a fault along a plate boundary where the motion is predominantly horizontal. It ends abruptly where it connects to another plate boundary, either another transform, a spreading ridge, or a subduction zone. The concept was proposed by Canadian geophysicist John Tuzo Wilson in 1965 and was a key insight in the development of plate tectonics.

Oceanic transform faults

Most transform faults are found on the ocean floor, where they offset actively spreading mid-ocean ridges, producing zigzag plate margins and giving rise to fracture zones that extend into inactive crust. Although the ridge segments appear offset, the crust between them is not being pulled apart; the direction of motion on the transform is parallel to the spreading direction. Famous examples include the Romanche Trench offsetting the Mid-Atlantic Ridge near the equator.

Continental transform faults

A smaller number cut through continental crust. The San Andreas Fault in California, the Alpine Fault in New Zealand, the North Anatolian Fault in Turkey, and the Dead Sea Transform in the Levant are major continental transforms that produce large, destructive earthquakes. Continental transforms commonly link segments of subduction zones or continental rifts.

Earthquakes

Transform faults do not offset indefinitely beyond their ends, so strain is concentrated along the active segment between the ridge (or other boundary) ends. Earthquakes along oceanic transforms are usually relatively shallow and moderate, while continental transforms can rupture in magnitude 7–8 events because of their length and the strength of continental crust.
