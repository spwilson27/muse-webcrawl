Castle Bravo

Castle Bravo, detonated on March 1, 1954, was the largest U.S. nuclear test.

Castle Bravo was the code name for the first test of a deployable American hydrogen bomb, detonated on March 1, 1954, at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands as part of Operation Castle. The device, nicknamed “Shrimp,” used lithium deuteride fusion fuel in a dry-fuel design derived from the Teller–Ulam configuration.

The test

Designers predicted a yield of about 6 megatons, but the bomb produced 15 megatons—about two and a half times the expected yield and still the largest explosive ever detonated by the United States. The error arose because lithium-7, assumed largely inert, contributed substantially to the fusion reactions, increasing both yield and fallout.

Fallout

The unexpectedly large explosion vaporised coral and spread radioactive fallout over a wide area. Residents of Rongelap, Rongerik, and Utirik atolls were exposed before evacuation, and the Japanese tuna fishing vessel Daigo Fukuryū Maru (Lucky Dragon No. 5) was contaminated; one crew member later died. The incident became an international controversy and galvanized global opposition to atmospheric nuclear testing.

Legacy

Castle Bravo accelerated research into fallout and contributed to the political momentum that led to the Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty of 1963. Bikini Atoll’s population had been removed before the tests, and resettlement attempts have been limited by persistent contamination.
