Fusuma

In Japanese architecture, fusuma (襖) are vertical rectangular panels that slide from side to side to redefine spaces within a room, or serve as doors. They typically measure roughly 3 feet wide by 6 feet tall (about 0.9 by 1.8 metres), the same dimensions as a tatami mat, with a depth of about an inch (2–3 cm).

Construction

A fusuma consists of a wooden lattice frame covered on both sides with several layers of paper or cloth, finished with a smooth opaque surface and a simple pull handle (hikite). Unlike the translucent shōji, fusuma are opaque and block light entirely. The panels slide in wooden grooves at the top (kamoi) and bottom (shikii) of the opening, and they can be lifted out to merge rooms into a larger space.

Art

Fusuma surfaces in temples, castles, and wealthy homes were frequently decorated with paintings by major artists of the Kanō and Rinpa schools — landscapes, birds and flowers, and scenes from literature in ink, colour, and gold leaf. Famous surviving examples include fusuma paintings at Nijō Castle and in Kyoto temples.

Modern use

Modern fusuma are often covered with durable printed paper or plastic in plain colours or patterns, and remain common in washitsu (Japanese-style rooms) in contemporary houses and inns.
