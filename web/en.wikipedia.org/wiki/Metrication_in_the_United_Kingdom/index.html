Metrication in the United Kingdom

Metrication in the United Kingdom, the process of converting the country's system of measurement from imperial units to the metric system, began in the mid-1960s and remains incomplete. Most of government, industry, and commerce use metric units, but imperial units are officially permitted for certain applications, notably road distances in miles, draught beer and cider in pints, and milk in returnable containers in pints.

History

Parliamentary committees considered the metric system through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In 1965 the government announced a policy of voluntary metrication over ten years, and the Metrication Board was established in 1969 to coordinate the change. Education switched to metric teaching in the early 1970s, and from the 1980s European Community directives required metric units for most regulated trade.

Current status

Since 1 January 2000, goods sold by weight or measure have generally had to be priced in metric units, though imperial equivalents may be shown as supplementary indications. Loose goods controversies, including the prosecution of greengrocer Steve Thoburn — one of the so-called "Metric Martyrs" — drew public attention. Weather forecasts, personal height and weight, and everyday speech still commonly mix the two systems.

Retained imperial uses

Road signs continue to use miles and yards. Draught beer and cider are sold by the pint, and doorstep milk by the pint. Troy ounces remain legal for precious metals.
