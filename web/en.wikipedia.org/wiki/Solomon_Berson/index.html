Solomon Berson

Solomon Aaron Berson (April 22, 1918 – April 11, 1972) was an American physician and scientist whose work, most famously with Rosalyn Sussman Yalow, led to the development of the radioimmunoassay (RIA), a technique that revolutionised medicine by making it possible to measure vanishingly small concentrations of hormones and other substances in blood. Their discovery became one of the most widely used tools in clinical and research laboratories.

Life and career

Berson was born in New York City and graduated from the City College of New York in 1938. After being rejected by many medical schools — a common experience for Jewish applicants under the quotas of the era — he earned a master's degree and taught anatomy before finally being admitted to New York University School of Medicine, receiving his MD in 1945. He served in the US Army, then joined the Bronx Veterans Administration Hospital in 1950, where he built a radioisotope service and began his long partnership with physicist Rosalyn Yalow, whom he hired that year. In 1968 he became chairman of the Department of Medicine at Mount Sinai School of Medicine. He died of a heart attack in Atlantic City in 1972, aged 53.

Radioimmunoassay

Studying how diabetic patients cleared radioactively labelled insulin, Berson and Yalow found that patients who had previously received insulin developed antibodies that bound it — overturning the assumption that a molecule as small as insulin could not be immunogenic. They inverted the finding into a measurement method: unlabelled hormone in a patient's sample competes with labelled hormone for a limited amount of antibody, so the bound radioactivity reveals the hormone's concentration. First applied to insulin (papers from 1956 onward; the full method published in 1960), RIA could detect picogram quantities and was extended to scores of hormones, drugs, viruses, and other analytes.

Berson and Yalow famously refused to patent the technique, making it freely available worldwide. In 1977 Yalow received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the development of radioimmunoassays of peptide hormones. Because Nobel Prizes are not awarded posthumously, Berson — who had died five years earlier — could not share it; Yalow named her laboratory after him, and he has been widely described as a scientist who would certainly have been a co-laureate had he lived.
