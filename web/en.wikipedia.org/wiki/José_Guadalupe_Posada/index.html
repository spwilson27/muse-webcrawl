José Guadalupe Posada

José Guadalupe Posada Aguilar (2 February 1852 - 20 January 1913) was a Mexican political printmaker and engraver whose satirical calaveras (skeletons) - most famously La Catrina - became enduring icons of Mexican popular art and of the Day of the Dead.

Life and work

Posada was born in Aguascalientes and learned lithography and engraving as a young man, working for the newspaper El Jicote. After flooding destroyed his workshop, he moved first to León and then, in 1888, to Mexico City, where he worked for the publisher Antonio Vanegas Arroyo, producing illustrations for penny broadsheets (hojas volantes), sensational crime reports, chapbooks, song sheets and satirical papers.

Calaveras and La Catrina

Posada's zinc etchings of animated skeletons - dancing, drinking, riding bicycles, and mimicking the vanities of the rich and powerful - satirized life under the Porfirio Díaz regime and during the Mexican Revolution. His most famous image, published in 1910 as La Calavera Catrina (originally "La Calavera Catrina," the "dapper skeleton"), mocked the Mexican elite's Francophile affectations; it has since become the emblematic figure of Día de los Muertos. Posada produced an estimated 20,000 images during his career.

Legacy

Posada died poor and largely forgotten in 1913 and was buried in a common grave. In the 1920s the artist Jean Charlot and the muralists, especially Diego Rivera - who popularized the name "Catrina" and depicted Posada in his murals - revived his reputation. He is now celebrated as a forerunner of the Mexican muralist movement and one of the great popular printmakers of the Americas.
