Inuit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Inuit are a group of culturally and linguistically related Indigenous peoples inhabiting the Arctic and subarctic coasts of North America — Greenland (Kalaallit), northern Canada (Inuit Nunangat, "the place where Inuit live"), and Alaska (Iñupiat) — with kin (the Yupik) across the Bering Strait in Siberia. Inuit languages belong to the Inuit–Yupik branch of the Eskaleut family; the older exonym "Eskimo" is now widely avoided in Canada and Greenland. Numbering about 180,000 in total, the Inuit are one people spread across four nation-states, represented internationally since 1977 by the Inuit Circumpolar Council.

Culture and modern history

Inuit traditional life was finely adapted to a land of sea ice: hunting seals, whales, walrus and caribou from kayaks and umiaks, travelling by dogsled, building snow houses (igloos) as temporary shelter alongside sod and stone winter houses, and wearing tailored caribou and sealskin clothing that still sets the standard for polar survival. Art — soapstone carving and the printmaking of Cape Dorset (Kinngait) — became celebrated worldwide after the 1950s. The 20th century brought wrenching change: trading posts, missions, epidemics, residential schools, forced relocations (Canada's High Arctic relocation of 1953–55), and settlement into permanent communities. Political mobilisation reversed the trajectory: Alaska's land-claims settlement (1971), Greenland's home rule (1979) and self-government (2009), and above all Nunavut (1999) — a vast Inuit-majority territory carved from Canada's Northwest Territories, the largest land-claims settlement in Canadian history. Today's agenda — language survival, housing and food insecurity, suicide prevention, and sovereignty over an Arctic being opened by climate change and shipping — is pursued under the principle "nothing about us without us".
