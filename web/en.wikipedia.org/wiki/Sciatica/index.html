The sciatic nerve, the largest nerve in the body

Sciatica is pain going down the leg from the lower back. The pain follows the distribution of the sciatic nerve, typically affecting only one side of the body, and may be accompanied by numbness, tingling, or weakness in parts of the leg and foot. Sciatica is generally a symptom of an underlying condition compressing or irritating one of the spinal nerve roots (L4 to S3) that form the sciatic nerve, rather than a diagnosis in itself; when caused by nerve-root compression it is a form of lumbar radiculopathy.

Causes

The most common cause is a herniated (slipped) intervertebral disc pressing on a lumbar or sacral nerve root. Other causes include lumbar spinal stenosis (narrowing of the spinal canal), degenerative disc disease, spondylolisthesis, bone spurs, and — outside the spine — piriformis syndrome, in which the piriformis muscle irritates the sciatic nerve in the buttock. Rarely, tumours or infections are responsible. Warning "red flag" features such as saddle anaesthesia and bladder or bowel dysfunction suggest cauda equina syndrome, a surgical emergency.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the characteristic radiating pain below the knee and signs such as the straight leg raise test, which reproduces the pain. Imaging with MRI is generally reserved for cases with red flags or those not improving after several weeks, because disc bulges are common in people without symptoms.

Treatment

Most cases resolve within a few weeks with conservative treatment: remaining active, simple analgesics such as NSAIDs, heat or cold, and physical therapy. Evidence for many other treatments, including bed rest, opioids, muscle relaxants, and spinal manipulation, is limited. Epidural corticosteroid injections may give short-term relief. Surgery, such as discectomy, is considered for severe or persistent pain with matching imaging findings, or urgently for cauda equina syndrome; it tends to relieve leg pain faster than conservative care, with similar outcomes after a year or two.

Epidemiology

Depending on definition, between roughly 2% and 40% of people experience sciatica at some point in life. It most often occurs in the fourth and fifth decades, and men and women are affected about equally.
