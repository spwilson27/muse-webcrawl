The Boidae, commonly known as boas or boids, are a family of nonvenomous snakes found in the Americas, Africa, Europe, Asia, and some Pacific islands. They include some of the world's largest snakes, such as the green anaconda. Boas are constrictors: they subdue prey by coiling around it and exerting pressure that kills primarily by stopping circulation. Unlike pythons (family Pythonidae), boas bear live young rather than laying eggs.

Characteristics

Boids are generally heavy-bodied, with broad heads distinct from the neck and, in many species, heat-sensing labial pits used to detect warm-blooded prey. Vestigial pelvic girdles and small claw-like spurs near the cloaca betray their lizard ancestry. Sizes range from the dwarf sand boas of about 50 cm to the green anaconda (Eunectes murinus), which may exceed 5 m and is the heaviest living snake. Most species are nocturnal ambush predators feeding on mammals, birds, and other reptiles.

Distribution and taxonomy

The true boas (subfamily Boinae) are chiefly Neotropical — including Boa (the boa constrictor), the anacondas (Eunectes), tree boas (Corallus), and rainbow boas (Epicrates) — while the sand boas (Erycinae) inhabit arid regions of North America, Africa, and Asia. Pacific boas (Candoia) and the Madagascan boas (Acrantophis, Sanzinia) are sometimes placed in their own subfamilies or families (Candoiidae, Sanziniidae) in modern classifications. Boas historically ranged into Europe, where fossils such as Titanoboa-era giants in South America show the lineage's past size extremes.

Reproduction and conservation

Boas are ovoviviparous, giving birth to live young after internal incubation of eggs. Many species are popular in the pet trade, which, along with habitat loss, has led to CITES listing of the whole family (Appendix II, with a few in Appendix I). The boa constrictor has also become an invasive species in places such as Florida and the island of Cozumel.
