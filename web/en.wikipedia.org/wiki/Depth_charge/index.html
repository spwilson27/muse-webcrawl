A depth charge is an anti-submarine warfare (ASW) weapon intended to destroy or damage a submarine by exploding in the water close to it. Most depth charges use conventional high explosives with a fuze set to detonate at a specific depth, producing a shock wave that can rupture the submarine's pressure hull or damage its systems. They are dropped or projected from surface ships, and historically also from aircraft.

History

The first effective depth charge, the Royal Navy's Type D, entered service in 1916 during the First World War against German U-boats. The principle traces back to experiments with towed explosive sweeps and to the idea, proposed as early as 1911, of a droppable depth-fuzed bomb. In the Second World War depth charges — rolled from stern racks or thrown by launchers such as the "Y-gun" and "K-gun" — were the principal anti-submarine weapon during the early Battle of the Atlantic, usually delivered in patterns bracketing the estimated position and depth of the target located by sonar (ASDIC).

Development

Forward-throwing weapons such as Hedgehog and Squid improved accuracy by attacking ahead of the ship while sonar contact was maintained. After 1945, homing torpedoes and rocket-delivered weapons largely replaced classic depth charges, although nuclear depth bombs were developed during the Cold War, and simple depth charges remain in some inventories.
