Flicker fusion threshold

Also calledCritical flicker frequency (CFF)
Typical human value~60 Hz (varies widely with conditions)
FieldVision science, psychophysics

The flicker fusion threshold (or flicker fusion rate, also called the critical flicker frequency, CFF) is a concept in the psychophysics of vision: it is the frequency at which an intermittent light stimulus appears to be completely steady to the average human observer. Above the threshold, the visual system averages the flashes into a single continuous impression — the phenomenon exploited by cinema, television, and PWM-dimmed lighting.

Determining factors

The threshold is not a single fixed number. It rises with stimulus brightness (the Ferry–Porter law: CFF is proportional to the logarithm of luminance), is higher in peripheral vision than at the fovea, and varies with wavelength, adaptation, fatigue, age, and the size of the stimulated retinal area (the Granit–Harper law). Values of 10–15 Hz suffice for dim stimuli, while bright, large, peripheral targets can require 100 Hz or more before flicker disappears. The Talbot–Plateau law states that the fused brightness equals the time-averaged brightness of the flashes.

Applications

Film projectors historically flashed each frame two or three times to raise the effective flicker rate to 48–72 Hz while keeping film speed at 24 frames per second. Refresh rates of displays, the design of LED lighting (to avoid headaches and stroboscopic effects), and clinical testing all depend on the threshold: CFF measurement is used in ophthalmology, in assessing hepatic encephalopathy, and in studies of fatigue and drug effects. Many animals have much higher thresholds than humans — dogs around 70–80 Hz and some insects well above 200 Hz — which is why fluorescent lighting and old televisions may appear to flicker to them.
