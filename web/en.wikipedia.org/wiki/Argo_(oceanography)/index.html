Argo (oceanography)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An Argo float cycles between the surface and 2,000 m depth, measuring temperature and salinity.

Argo is an international programme that uses profiling floats to observe temperature, salinity, and currents in the upper ocean. It provides real-time data used in climate and oceanographic research, weather forecasting, and seasonal prediction. The programme, which began in 2000, is named after the Greek mythological ship Argo, in recognition of its partnership with the Jason satellite altimeters.

Operation

An Argo float drifts at a parking depth of about 1,000 metres. Every ten days, it descends to 2,000 metres and then slowly rises to the surface, measuring temperature and salinity (from which density is derived) as it ascends. At the surface it transmits its profile and position to satellites before sinking again. Floats are battery powered and typically operate for four to five years, making around 150 profiles over their lifetime.

The array

The programme reached its initial target of 3,000 floats in November 2007 and has since maintained a global array of roughly 4,000 floats spread across the ice-free ocean, deployed and maintained by more than 30 countries. Extensions of the programme include Deep Argo floats, which profile to 4,000–6,000 metres, and Biogeochemical Argo floats, which carry sensors for oxygen, nitrate, chlorophyll, and pH.

Data

Argo data are freely available to anyone, usually within hours of collection, through global data assembly centres. The dataset has transformed knowledge of ocean heat content, sea-level rise, ocean circulation, and the hydrological cycle, and Argo profiles form a central input to ocean reanalyses and climate assessments such as those of the IPCC.

History

The programme was proposed in 1998 by a team of scientists (the Argo Science Team) and endorsed by the Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission and the World Meteorological Organization. The first floats were deployed in 2000. Argo is a major component of the Global Ocean Observing System and the Global Climate Observing System.
