Yoshiwara

Yoshiwara (吉原) was the famous licensed red-light district (yūkaku) of Edo (present-day Tokyo), Japan. Established in 1618 near what is now Nihonbashi and relocated in 1657 to the Asakusa area after the Meireki fire, it operated for over 340 years until prostitution was outlawed in 1958. At its height, several thousand courtesans (yūjo) lived and worked within its moated, walled quarter.

The quarter

Yoshiwara was entered through a single gate, the Ōmon, along a main street, Nakanochō, planted with cherry trees famed for their night-time blossom displays. Courtesans were displayed behind lattice fronts (harimise) in the brothels (ageya and later hikitejaya teahouses arranged introductions). A strict hierarchy existed among the women, headed in the Edo period by the tayū and later by the oiran — highly accomplished courtesans trained in music, calligraphy, tea ceremony, and poetry, whose processions (oiran dōchū) through the quarter, walking in a distinctive slow figure-of-eight step, were celebrated public spectacles.

Life inside

Many girls were sold into the quarter by impoverished families and bound by contracts and debt that few escaped; disease and early death were common, and the Jōkan-ji temple became known as the "throw-away temple" (nagikomi-dera) where the bodies of courtesans who died without family were anonymously buried. Yet Yoshiwara was also a centre of fashion and popular culture: its courtesans set trends in dress and hairstyle, it was celebrated in kabuki plays, popular songs, and the ukiyo-e woodblock prints of artists such as Utamaro and Eisen, and connoisseurship of its etiquette — the ideal of tsū — became a mark of urban sophistication.

Decline and legacy

The district was rebuilt many times after fires — so frequent they were called "the flowers of Edo." After the Second World War it operated under the euphemism of "special restaurants" until the Anti-Prostitution Law of 1956 (enforced 1958) closed it. The area, in Tokyo's Taitō ward, remains a soapland district today, and Yoshiwara's imagery continues to permeate Japanese literature, film, and manga.
