Gender Trouble

Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity — by Judith Butler. Published 1990, Routledge. Subject: feminist theory, gender studies, queer theory.

Gender Trouble: Feminism and the Subversion of Identity is a book by the philosopher Judith Butler, first published in 1990. It is widely regarded as a foundational text of queer theory and of the theory of gender performativity, and it has had a major influence on feminist philosophy and gender studies.

Summary

Butler challenges the assumption that gender is the cultural expression of a natural, pre-existing sex, arguing instead that sex itself is produced through discourse and power. Drawing on figures including Simone de Beauvoir, Sigmund Freud, Jacques Lacan, Michel Foucault, Monique Wittig, and Gayle Rubin, Butler proposes that gender is performative: it is constituted by the repeated, stylized acts, gestures, and speech through which a gendered identity is enacted, rather than expressing an inner essence.

The book critiques the category of "women" as the stable subject of feminism, arguing that this category is itself produced by the structures feminism opposes. Butler uses drag as an example of parodic repetition that can expose the imitative structure of all gender.

Reception and influence

Gender Trouble became one of the most cited works in the humanities; a tenth-anniversary edition appeared in 1999 with a new preface clarifying that performativity is not a matter of voluntary performance. Critics have questioned the book's difficult prose and its political implications, while Butler developed the arguments further in Bodies That Matter (1993).
