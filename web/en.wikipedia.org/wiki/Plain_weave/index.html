Plain weave (also called tabby weave, linen weave or taffeta weave) is the most basic of three fundamental types of textile weaves (along with satin weave and twill). It is strong and hard-wearing, and is used for fashion and furnishing fabrics.

Structure

In plain weave, the weft (filling) yarn passes alternately over and under successive warp threads, and each row is an exact reversal of the one before. This produces a simple criss-cross pattern, identical on both faces of the fabric, with the maximum number of interlacings of any weave — which gives plain-weave cloth its stability, firmness, and resistance to slipping, at the cost of less drape and a rougher hand than looser weaves.

Uses and fabrics

A huge range of everyday fabrics use plain weave, including muslin, cambric, lawn, organdy, percale, seersucker, cheesecloth, and canvas variants. Taffeta and poplin are plain weaves with fine ribs produced by using yarns of different thickness in warp and weft. Balanced plain weaves, with warp and weft of the same size and count, are a traditional ground for embroidery and needlework.

Variants

Variations include basket weave, in which two or more yarns are grouped and woven as one, and ribbed weaves, where alternating thick and thin yarns create a corded effect.
