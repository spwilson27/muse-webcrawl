Analytic language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An analytic language is a type of natural language in which a series of root words is modified with prefixes, suffixes, particles, and word order, rather than by inflection. In linguistic typology, analytic languages are contrasted with synthetic languages, which encode grammatical relations within words through inflection (fusional languages) or by stringing together many morphemes (agglutinative languages). A language that is highly analytic, with a low morpheme-per-word ratio and few or no inflections, is often called an isolating language.

Characteristics

Analytic languages typically:

  • rely heavily on word order to indicate grammatical roles such as subject and object;
  • use separate auxiliary verbs, prepositions, and particles instead of verb conjugations and noun case endings;
  • have words that consist of a single, invariant morpheme, or only a few morphemes.

Examples

Well-known analytic languages include Mandarin Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, and Khmer, which are almost purely isolating. English is moderately analytic: it lost most of the inflections of Old English and now relies on word order and auxiliaries ("will have been seen"), retaining only remnants such as plural -s and past-tense -ed. Afrikaans, Bulgarian, and Macedonian are also notably analytic relative to their language families.

Historical development

Languages can move along the spectrum over time. Old English was a moderately inflected language, but sound changes eroded its endings and Middle and Modern English became progressively more analytic. Linguists describe a long-term cycle in which analytic structures grammaticalise into new inflections, which then erode again.
