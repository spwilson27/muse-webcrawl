Fagus is a genus of deciduous trees in the family Fagaceae, native to temperate Europe, Asia, and North America. Its members are commonly known as beeches. Recent classifications recognise between ten and thirteen species in two distinct subgenera, Englerianae and Fagus. The genus is notable for its smooth grey bark, distinctive elongated buds, and edible nuts known as beechmast.

Description

Beeches are large trees, capable of reaching heights of up to 50 metres. The bark is smooth and light grey, even in old trees. Leaves are alternate, simple, and entire or with small crenations, 5–15 cm long. The trees are monoecious, bearing both male and female flowers on the same plant; the female flowers produce pairs of small triangular nuts enclosed in a spiny cupule that splits into four lobes at maturity.

Species

Well-known species include the European beech (Fagus sylvatica), dominant in much of Europe's temperate forest; the American beech (Fagus grandifolia) of eastern North America; the Japanese beech (Fagus crenata); the Chinese beech (Fagus lucida); and the Oriental beech (Fagus orientalis) of southwestern Asia.

Uses

Beech wood is hard, strong and pale, used for furniture, flooring, tool handles, plywood and charcoal. The timber bends well when steamed, making it suitable for bentwood furniture. Beechmast was traditionally used as fodder for pigs and poultry and is eaten by many wild animals; the nuts are also pressed for an edible oil. Beeches are widely planted as ornamental and hedging trees, including purple-leaved and weeping cultivars of F. sylvatica.

Ecology and threats

Beech forests support rich communities of fungi, invertebrates and birds. In North America, beech bark disease — a complex involving scale insects and Neonectria fungi — has caused widespread mortality in F. grandifolia stands since its introduction in the late 19th century.
