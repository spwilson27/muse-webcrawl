Overview

The Mountain Railways of India are three narrow- and metre-gauge railways built in the mountainous regions of India during the British colonial period, inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage List as outstanding examples of bold, ingenious engineering solutions applied to difficult mountain terrain.

The three railways

The Darjeeling Himalayan Railway, opened in 1881, climbs from New Jalpaiguri at about 100 metres to Darjeeling at about 2,200 metres over 88 kilometres of 610 mm (2 ft) gauge track. It negotiates the ascent with loops and zig-zag reverses, including the famous Batasia Loop. It was the first property inscribed, in 1999.

The Nilgiri Mountain Railway, added in 2005, is a 1,000 mm metre-gauge line in Tamil Nadu climbing from Mettupalayam to Ooty (Udhagamandalam). Its steepest section uses the Abt rack and pinion system — the only rack railway in India — and retains its fleet of Swiss-built X class steam locomotives.

The Kalka–Shimla Railway, added in 2008, is a 762 mm (2 ft 6 in) gauge line in Himachal Pradesh, 96 kilometres long, famous for its 102 tunnels, 864 bridges and spectacular multi-arch gallery viaducts such as the Barog tunnel approach.

Other mountain railways

India has other celebrated hill lines not included in the UNESCO listing, notably the Matheran Hill Railway near Mumbai and the Kangra Valley Railway in Himachal Pradesh. All of the lines remain in regular passenger service and are beloved as "toy trains", though they face continuing challenges from landslides, road competition and the cost of maintaining steam-age infrastructure.
