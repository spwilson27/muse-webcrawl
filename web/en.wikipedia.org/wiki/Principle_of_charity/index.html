Principle of charity

The principle of charity is a methodological principle in philosophy and rhetoric according to which, in interpreting a speaker's arguments, one should assume the best possible interpretation of the other party's statements — the interpretation that renders them most rational, plausible, and well-supported — rather than attributing irrationality, fallacies, or falsehoods where a more charitable reading is available.

Formulations

The term was coined by Neil L. Wilson in 1958–59 in connection with determining the referents of proper names. The idea was taken up by Willard Van Orman Quine and developed extensively by Donald Davidson, for whom charity is not optional but constitutive of interpretation itself: to understand another speaker at all, an interpreter must attribute to them a largely true and coherent set of beliefs, a principle Davidson later reformulated as the "principle of rational accommodation."

In argumentation

In informal logic and debate, the principle underlies the practice of "steelmanning" an opponent's position — reconstructing it in its strongest form before criticizing it — as opposed to the straw man fallacy, which attacks a weakened version. Applying the principle is generally taken to improve the quality of discussion and to reduce misunderstanding, although critics note that excessive charity can lead an interpreter to replace a speaker's actual views with views the speaker would not recognize.
