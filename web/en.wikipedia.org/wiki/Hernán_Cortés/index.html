Hernán Cortés

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A later portrait of Hernán Cortés in armour]

Hernán Cortés (1485 – 2 December 1547) was a Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that conquered the Aztec Empire (1519–1521), bringing central Mexico under Spanish rule and reshaping the history of the Americas. Sailing from Cuba against the wishes of its governor, with some 500 men, he founded Veracruz, burned (or scuttled) his ships, allied with the Aztecs' Tlaxcalan enemies, entered Tenochtitlan as the guest of Montezuma II, survived the disastrous retreat of La Noche Triste, and after a siege destroyed the city in August 1521. Rewarded with a marquisate but never the viceroyalty he craved, he died embittered in Spain in 1547.

The conquest

Cortés' letters to Charles V — self-justifying masterpieces — narrate his march inland, the Cholula massacre, the meeting with Montezuma (whom he seized in his own palace), and the chaos after Montezuma's death during the Spanish evacuation (30 June 1520). Regrouping in Tlaxcala, he built brigantines, blockaded Tenochtitlan by land and lake, and ground down a population ravaged by smallpox — introduced by the expedition — until Cuauhtémoc's surrender on 13 August 1521. Historians now stress that the 'conquest' was largely fought by indigenous armies (perhaps 100,000+ Tlaxcalans and allies) against an unpopular empire, with European steel, horses, disease and diplomacy as multipliers.

Aftermath and reputation

Cortés governed New Spain briefly, mounted further expeditions (Honduras, Baja California), and quarrelled endlessly with the Crown's officials; he died near Seville in 1547. In Mexico his legacy is fiercely contested — long vilified as the destroyer of a civilisation, lately reassessed within a broader story of indigenous agency and catastrophe — and his remains and memory have never rested easily in either country.
