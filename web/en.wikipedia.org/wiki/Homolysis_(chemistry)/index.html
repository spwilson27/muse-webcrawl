Homolysis (chemistry)

In chemistry, homolysis (from Greek homoios, "equal", and lusis, "loosening") or homolytic fission is the dissociation of a chemical bond in a molecule in which each product retains one of the two bonding electrons. The process produces two radicals — highly reactive species each bearing an unpaired electron. In reaction schemes, homolysis is indicated by single-headed "fishhook" arrows showing the movement of single electrons:

A–B → A• + B•

Factors

Homolysis is commonly induced by heat (thermolysis), ultraviolet light (photolysis), or the decomposition of weak-bonded initiators such as peroxides and azo compounds. The energy required is the bond dissociation energy of the bond broken. Bonds break homolytically more readily when the resulting radicals are stabilised — by resonance (as in benzyl or allyl radicals), by adjacent heteroatoms, or by substitution — and weaker bonds such as O–O in peroxides or N–Br in N-bromosuccinimide cleave especially easily. Homolytic cleavage is favoured in non-polar solvents and in the gas phase; in polar solvents heterolytic cleavage, in which both electrons go to one fragment, forming ions, is usually preferred.

Role in reactions

Homolysis is the initiation step of free-radical chain reactions, including radical polymerisation (used to make polystyrene, PVC and many other plastics), the halogenation of alkanes, autoxidation (responsible for the rancidity of fats and the ageing of rubber), and combustion. Radical initiators such as benzoyl peroxide and AIBN work by undergoing facile homolysis to generate radicals that start chains. In biology, homolytic chemistry occurs in some enzyme mechanisms, notably those of radical SAM enzymes and coenzyme B12-dependent rearrangements.
