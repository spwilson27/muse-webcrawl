Fluid

In physics, a fluid is a liquid, gas, or other material that may continuously move and deform (flow) under an applied shear stress, or external force. Unlike a solid, a fluid has zero shear modulus — it cannot resist a shear force while at rest, and it takes the shape of its container. Fluids include the classical states of matter liquid and gas, and also plasmas and, to some extent, certain plastic solids.

A fluid flows and takes the shape of its container.

Properties

Key properties of fluids include density, pressure, temperature, viscosity (resistance to deformation), and surface tension. Fluids are commonly classified as Newtonian, where viscosity is constant and stress is linearly proportional to strain rate — the relation formulated by Isaac Newton — or non-Newtonian, where viscosity varies with the applied stress or strain rate (examples include blood, cornstarch suspensions and most paints).

Fluid mechanics

The study of fluids is fluid mechanics, divided into fluid statics (fluids at rest) and fluid dynamics (fluids in motion). Its governing equations include the continuity equation, the Navier–Stokes equations for momentum, and Bernoulli's principle for energy along a streamline. Concepts such as laminar and turbulent flow, Reynolds number, buoyancy and pressure head arise from these foundations and underpin hydraulics, aerodynamics, meteorology and oceanography.

Modelling

In fluid mechanics, fluids are usually treated as continua: the continuum hypothesis assumes properties such as density can be defined at a point and vary smoothly, ignoring molecular structure. This breaks down only at very small scales or extreme rarefaction, where statistical mechanics is required.
