Stone of Destiny

The Stone of Destiny is a block of red sandstone that was used for centuries in the coronation of the monarchs of Scotland. It is also known as the Stone of Scone (the subject of a separate article), the Coronation Stone, and in Scottish Gaelic as An Lia Fàil, "the speaking stone". Since 2024 it has been on display at Perth Museum in Scotland, and it is brought to Westminster Abbey for British coronations.

History

Tradition holds that the stone was the pillow on which the biblical patriarch Jacob rested his head at Bethel, and that it travelled via Egypt, Spain and Ireland before reaching Scotland. Scottish kings were inaugurated seated above the stone at Scone Abbey in Perthshire; the last Scottish king crowned upon it was John Balliol in 1292.

Removal to England

In 1296, during the First War of Scottish Independence, Edward I of England seized the stone as spoils of war and had it fitted into a wooden chair — the Coronation Chair — at Westminster Abbey in London. For the following 700 years nearly every English and later British monarch was crowned seated above it.

The 1950 theft

On Christmas Day 1950, four Scottish students — Ian Hamilton, Kay Matheson, Gavin Vernon and Alan Stuart — broke into Westminster Abbey and removed the stone, in the process breaking it into two pieces. It was repaired and hidden in Scotland; in April 1951 it was left on the altar of Arbroath Abbey and recovered by police. The stone was returned to Westminster in time for the coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953.

Return to Scotland

On St Andrew's Day, 30 November 1996, the stone was formally returned to Scotland and installed in the Crown Room of Edinburgh Castle alongside the Honours of Scotland. It travelled south again for the coronation of Charles III on 6 May 2023, and in March 2024 it went on permanent display at the new Perth Museum, close to its historic home at Scone.
