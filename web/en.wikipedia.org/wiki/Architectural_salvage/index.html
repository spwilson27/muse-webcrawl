Architectural salvage

Architectural salvage is the practice of reclaiming building materials and architectural elements from structures that are being demolished or renovated, so that they can be reused in other buildings. Salvaged items range from structural timber, brick, and stone to doors, windows, fireplace mantels, staircases, plumbing fixtures, lighting, hardware, and entire facades.

Practice

Reuse of building material is as old as building itself: Roman structures were routinely mined for stone, and medieval and early modern builders incorporated spolia from older monuments. The modern salvage trade grew in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as older buildings were demolished for redevelopment, and developed into a distinct retail sector of salvage yards and architectural antiques dealers.

Today salvage is promoted by the preservation and sustainability movements. Careful deconstruction — dismantling a building piece by piece rather than demolishing it mechanically — preserves the value of materials, diverts waste from landfill, and retains the embodied energy and carbon of manufactured items such as bricks, steel, and old-growth timber, which often exceeds the quality of modern equivalents.

Types of material

Commonly traded items include reclaimed lumber and flooring; bricks and roof tiles; cast-iron radiators, baths, and railings; marble and timber mantelpieces; panelled doors and stained glass; and industrial fittings from factories, schools, and pubs. Elements of historically significant buildings are sometimes removed to museums or incorporated into new buildings on the same site, a compromise that has at times been controversial when facades alone are preserved ("facadism").

Issues

The trade raises questions about the stripping of heritage buildings in poor regions for markets in wealthy ones, the theft of architectural elements, and building-code obstacles to reusing old materials, whose performance must often be re-certified. Nevertheless, reuse is increasingly written into green building rating systems such as LEED and BREEAM.
