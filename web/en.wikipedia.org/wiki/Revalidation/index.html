Revalidation

Revalidation is a process by which licensed medical practitioners in the United Kingdom are required to demonstrate on a regular basis that they are up to date and fit to practise in their chosen field and are able to provide a good level of care. It was introduced for doctors by the General Medical Council (GMC) in December 2012, and subsequently extended to nurses and midwives by the Nursing and Midwifery Council in 2016.

Process for doctors

Under the GMC system, every licensed doctor must revalidate, normally every five years. Revalidation rests on annual appraisals covering the doctor's whole practice, in which the doctor presents supporting information including continuing professional development, quality improvement activity, significant events, feedback from patients and colleagues, and complaints and compliments. A designated "responsible officer" in the doctor's organization makes a recommendation to the GMC about whether the doctor should be revalidated.

The system was developed in response to a series of medical scandals, most prominently the case of Harold Shipman, and to recommendations of public inquiries that doctors should be periodically reassessed rather than licensed for life on the basis of initial qualification alone.

Nurses and midwives

Nurses and midwives revalidate every three years with the Nursing and Midwifery Council, submitting practice hours, continuing professional development, practice-related feedback, reflective accounts, and a reflective discussion and confirmation.
