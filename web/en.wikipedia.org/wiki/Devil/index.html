Devil

In many religions and cultures, the Devil is the personification of evil and the enemy of God and humankind. In Christianity, the Devil is identified with Satan, a fallen angel who rebelled against God and now tempts humans to sin; in Islam, the analogous figure is Iblis, a jinn who refused to bow to Adam. The Devil is traditionally depicted as the ruler of Hell, punishing the damned and seeking the ruin of souls. Belief in the Devil and his role varies widely between and within religions.

Medieval and early modern art commonly pictured the Devil as a horned, cloven-hoofed tempter.

In Judaism and Christianity

In the Hebrew Bible, ha-satan ("the adversary/accuser") appears chiefly in Job and Zechariah as a member of the heavenly court who tests human faithfulness, rather than an independent power of evil. In the New Testament the figure developed into God's cosmic adversary: Satan, the Devil (from Greek diabolos, "slanderer/accuser"), tempter of Jesus in the desert, "the prince of this world", and the dragon of Revelation, destined for defeat at the Last Judgment. Christian theology generally holds that the Devil is a created, fallen angel — powerful but not equal to God — rejecting dualism.

In Islam

Iblis (Shaytan) is a jinn created from fire who refused God's command to bow before Adam and was expelled from Paradise, vowing to lead humans astray until the Day of Judgment. He whispers temptation but has no power over God's faithful servants.

Cultural depictions

The Devil is a central figure in folklore, literature and art: the tempter in Faust legends who buys souls by contract, Dante's frozen Satan at the centre of Hell, Milton's charismatic rebel in Paradise Lost, and countless folk tales, plays, films and songs. Horns, pitchfork, cloven hooves and red skin became standard iconography in the late medieval and early modern periods.
