Close air support

In military tactics, close air support (CAS) is defined as aerial warfare actions—often air-to-ground actions such as strafing or airstrikes—by military aircraft against hostile targets in close proximity to friendly forces. A form of fire support, CAS requires detailed integration of each air mission with fire and movement of all forces involved, and it demands excellent coordination with ground troops, usually directed by a forward air controller (FAC) or joint terminal attack controller (JTAC).

Requirements

CAS missions are distinguished from other air attacks by the nearness of friendly forces, which creates a risk of fratricide and demands precise target identification, marking, and communication. Modern CAS relies on laser designators, GPS-guided munitions such as the Joint Direct Attack Munition (JDAM), and real-time video downlinks from aircraft sensors to controllers on the ground.

History

The concept emerged in the First World War, when aircraft strafed and bombed trenches in support of infantry. It was developed further in the interwar period by the U.S. Marine Corps in the Banana Wars and by the German Luftwaffe, whose Ju 87 Stuka dive-bombers supported blitzkrieg advances. In the Second World War, Allied "cab rank" systems kept fighter-bombers orbiting near the front to strike targets on request.

During the Korean and Vietnam wars, CAS became central to U.S. operations, employing aircraft such as the A-1 Skyraider and OV-10 Bronco, with forward air controllers in light aircraft marking targets with smoke rockets. In the 1970s the U.S. Air Force fielded the A-10 Thunderbolt II, the first aircraft designed specifically for CAS, armed with a 30 mm rotary cannon and built to survive ground fire.

Modern practice

Today CAS is delivered by fixed-wing jets, attack helicopters, gunships such as the AC-130, and increasingly by unmanned aerial vehicles. Debate continues over whether fast jets can fully replace dedicated slow, low-flying CAS platforms, particularly in counter-insurgency campaigns where loiter time and visual identification matter most.
