Subcutaneous injection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A subcutaneous injection is administered into the subcutis, the layer of skin directly below the dermis and epidermis, collectively known as the cutis. Subcutaneous injections are highly effective in administering medications such as insulin, growth hormone, and some analgesics. Because the subcutaneous tissue has few blood vessels, the injected drug is absorbed relatively slowly and steadily, which is desirable for many medicines.

Technique

Subcutaneous injections are typically given with a short, fine needle inserted at an angle of about 45 to 90 degrees into a pinched fold of skin, commonly on the abdomen, the outer upper arm, or the front of the thigh. Volumes are usually limited to about 1–2 millilitres. The technique is simple enough that many patients are trained to self-administer, as with insulin for diabetes mellitus.

Uses

Medications commonly given subcutaneously include insulin, heparin and other anticoagulants such as enoxaparin, certain vaccines, fertility drugs, growth hormone, and some monoclonal antibody therapies. Depot formulations and implants placed under the skin also rely on the slow absorption characteristic of the subcutaneous route.

Comparison with other routes

Compared with intramuscular injection, subcutaneous injection is generally less painful and carries a lower risk of hitting nerves or large blood vessels, but absorbs more slowly and accommodates smaller volumes. Compared with intravenous injection, onset is much slower, but the route can be used outside clinical settings.

Complications

Possible complications include local pain, bruising, infection, and, with repeated insulin injections at the same site, lipodystrophy — changes in the subcutaneous fat that can impair absorption. Rotating injection sites is recommended to prevent this.
