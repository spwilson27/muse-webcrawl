Atmosphères is a piece for orchestra composed by György Ligeti in 1961. It is noted for eschewing conventional melody and meter in favor of densely woven webs of sound — a technique Ligeti called micropolyphony — and it became one of the composer's most famous works after its use in Stanley Kubrick's film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968).

Composition and premiere

Ligeti, who had fled Hungary for Austria in 1956, composed Atmosphères in 1961 on a commission connected with the Donaueschingen Festival in Germany, where it was premiered on 22 October 1961 by the SWF Symphony Orchestra conducted by Hans Rosbaud. The piece is dedicated to Max Deutsch. It was an immediate success and helped establish Ligeti's international reputation.

Music

The score calls for a very large orchestra. Each instrument in the string, wind, and brass sections often plays an entirely separate part, and the individual lines weave together into continuously shifting clusters of sound whose inner movement is perceptible while individual voices disappear into the whole. The piece begins with a huge, quiet chord spanning nearly five octaves that seems to emerge from silence, and textures mutate — through glissandi, overlapping canons, and changing instrumentation — without traditional harmony, theme, or pulse. Ligeti described the result as a music of slowly transforming "atmospheres," in which the form is the process of transformation itself.

In film

Stanley Kubrick used the opening minutes of Atmosphères, together with excerpts from Ligeti's Lux Aeterna and Requiem, in 2001: A Space Odyssey, where the music accompanies the Star Gate sequence and other scenes; the music was used in the film initially without Ligeti's permission, a dispute later settled. Kubrick went on to use Ligeti's music in The Shining and Eyes Wide Shut.
