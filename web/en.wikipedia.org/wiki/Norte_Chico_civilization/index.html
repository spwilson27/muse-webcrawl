Norte Chico civilization

The Norte Chico civilization (also called the Caral–Supe civilization) was a complex pre-Columbian society of the north-central coast of present-day Peru, flourishing roughly between 3500 and 1800 BCE. It is considered the oldest known civilization of the Americas and one of the cradles of civilization where complex society arose independently.

Society

At least 30 major population centers are known, concentrated in the valleys of the Supe, Pativilca, Fortaleza, and Huaura rivers. The largest, Caral in the Supe Valley, contains monumental platform mounds, sunken circular courts, amphitheatres, and residential complexes across some 60 hectares. Remarkably, the civilization built this monumental architecture before the introduction of ceramics or maize agriculture in the region.

The economy appears to have rested on an exchange between coastal fishing communities—rich in anchovies and sardines from the Humboldt Current—and inland farmers who grew cotton (for fishing nets), squash, beans, and other crops using irrigation. Evidence of trade reaches as far as the Amazon rainforest, with finds of achiote and other exotic goods.

Discoveries

Caral was identified early in the 20th century but its antiquity was established only in the 1990s through the excavations of Ruth Shady and subsequent radiocarbon dating, which pushed back the origins of Andean civilization by a millennium. A knotted-cord object resembling the later Inca quipu was found at Caral, suggesting early systems of record-keeping. Caral was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.
