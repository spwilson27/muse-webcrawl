The akonting (also spelled ekonting) is a three-stringed folk lute of the Jola (Diola) people of Senegambia, in West Africa. It consists of a large halved calabash gourd soundbox covered with goat or cow skin, through which passes a long wooden neck, with three strings — traditionally of fishing line or gut — two long melodic strings and one short drone string attached partway up the neck.

Playing style and the banjo connection

The akonting is played with a striking, rhythmic down-stroking technique in which the fingernail or fingertip strikes downward on the strings while the thumb sounds the short drone string. This technique closely resembles the earliest documented styles of banjo playing in the Americas, including the frailing or clawhammer style and the "stroke style" described in 19th-century banjo tutors.

Because of these parallels — a gourd body covered with skin, a short thumb string, and the down-stroke technique — the akonting has been identified by researchers, notably the Swedish musicologist and musician Ulf Jägfors and the American banjo historian and player Daniel Jatta of the Gambia, as the most likely surviving West African ancestor, or closest relative, of the early American banjo, which was created by enslaved Africans in the Caribbean and North America from African lute prototypes.
