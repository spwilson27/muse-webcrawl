Sudeten Germans (German: Sudetendeutsche) were the ethnic Germans living in the borderlands of Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia — collectively called the Sudetenland — whose ancestors had settled there from the Middle Ages onward, often at the invitation of Bohemian kings. By the early twentieth century they numbered over three million and formed one of the largest minorities in Europe.

History

In Czechoslovakia

After the First World War the Sudetenland was incorporated into the new state of Czechoslovakia despite local German demands for union with German Austria. Sudeten Germans initially faced discrimination in land reform and public employment, but participated in democratic politics through parties such as the German Social Democratic Workers' Party and, increasingly in the 1930s, Konrad Henlein's Sudeten German Party, which aligned with Nazi Germany.

Crisis and annexation

Under pressure from Henlein and Hitler, the Munich Agreement of September 1938 transferred the Sudetenland to Germany. Many Czechs fled or were expelled, and the region's Jewish population suffered persecution.

Expulsion

After the Second World War, the Potsdam Conference authorised the transfer of German populations, and under the Beneš decrees roughly three million Sudeten Germans were expelled from Czechoslovakia in 1945–46, many in brutal "wild expulsions" before organised transfers began; tens of thousands died. The expellees resettled mainly in West Germany, where the Sudetendeutsche Landsmannschaft became a significant political organisation advocating recognition of the expulsions as unjust, a question that long complicated Czech–German relations.
