The Foundling Museum is a museum in Brunswick Square, Bloomsbury, London, which tells the story of the Foundling Hospital, Britain's first home for children at risk of abandonment, founded by the philanthropist Thomas Coram in 1739. Opened in 2004 in a building adjacent to the site of the original hospital, the museum holds the Foundling Hospital Collection and the Gerald Coke Handel Collection.

The Foundling Hospital

Coram's hospital received its first children in 1741 and cared for some 25,000 children over its history; mothers leaving a baby left a identifying token — coins, buttons, scraps of fabric — in case they could one day reclaim the child, and thousands of these poignant tokens survive in the museum's collection. The artist William Hogarth, a founding governor, donated works and encouraged fellow artists to contribute, making the hospital Britain's first public art gallery; the composer George Frideric Handel supported it too, conducting benefit performances of Messiah and bequeathing a fair copy of the score. The composer also gave the hospital an organ, and the museum's Handel collection is the world's largest devoted to him.

The museum

The museum's interiors preserve the hospital's original Court Room, with paintings by Hogarth, Gainsborough and Reynolds and plasterwork by Joseph Wilton. The charity's work continues today as the children's charity Coram. Exhibitions explore the history of child welfare, the foundlings' lives, and the links between philanthropy and the arts.
