Involuntary commitment

Involuntary commitment, civil commitment, or compulsory detention is the legal process by which an individual who is deemed by a qualified authority to have symptoms of severe mental disorder is confined in a psychiatric hospital, psychiatric wards, or similar setting against their will. Commitment procedures and criteria vary by jurisdiction, but they generally require evidence that the person poses a danger to themselves or others, or is gravely disabled and unable to care for their own basic needs.

Criteria

In most legal systems, involuntary commitment requires a standard such as imminent dangerousness, grave disability, or the need for treatment. Courts typically require medical certification, often by two physicians, and a judicial hearing at which the individual has legal representation. Many jurisdictions also provide less restrictive alternatives, such as outpatient commitment (assisted outpatient treatment), in which the person lives in the community under a court order to follow treatment.

History

Modern commitment law developed in the nineteenth century alongside the asylum system. In the United States, landmark litigation such as O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975) held that a state cannot confine a non-dangerous individual who is capable of surviving safely in freedom. The mid-20th-century process of deinstitutionalization, enabled by antipsychotic medication and civil-liberties movements, sharply reduced long-term involuntary hospitalization in many countries.

International law and criticism

The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) has been interpreted by some committees as prohibiting detention based on disability, a position that remains contested. Critics argue involuntary commitment can be traumatic, can be applied unevenly across racial and socioeconomic groups, and may deter people from seeking care. Defenders argue it is an essential last-resort intervention that protects life and public safety and can restore decision-making capacity in people with severe psychosis or mania.
