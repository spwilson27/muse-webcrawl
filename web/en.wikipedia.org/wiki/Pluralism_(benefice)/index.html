Pluralism (benefice)

In the context of the Church of England and other Christian churches, pluralism (also called plurality) is the holding of two or more simultaneous ecclesiastical benefices by a single cleric. A clergyman who held several benefices at once was known as a pluralist. The practice was widespread in the medieval and early modern Church, where wealthy clergy might accumulate numerous livings, often performing the duties of most of them through poorly paid curates while retaining the income.

History

Pluralism became common in the medieval Latin Church as a means of rewarding administrators, diplomats, and scholars in the service of kings and bishops, for whom a benefice provided income without necessarily requiring residence. The resulting absenteeism and pastoral neglect drew repeated criticism from reformers. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and later legislation attempted to restrict the holding of incompatible benefices, particularly those involving the cure of souls.

Reform in England

In England, pluralism was attacked during the Reformation and again in the nineteenth century. The Pluralities Act 1838 sharply restricted the practice, limiting the number of benefices that could be held together and imposing conditions on distance and income. Later legislation further tightened the rules, and in the modern Church of England pluralities survive mainly in the form of united benefices held by a single incumbent serving several parishes, a structural arrangement distinct from the older abuse.
