The prefectures of Japan (都道府県, todōfuken) are the country's 47 first-level jurisdictions. They consist of 43 ordinary prefectures (県, ken), two urban prefectures (府, fu: Osaka and Kyoto), one "circuit" or "territory" (道, dō: Hokkaido) and one metropolis (都, to: Tokyo). The Meiji-era Fuhanken sanchisei created the first prefectures (urban fu and rural ken) from 1868, replacing the feudal domains (han), and the system was consolidated after the abolition of the han system in 1871.

Under the Constitution of Japan (Chapter VIII: Local Self-Government) and the Local Autonomy Act of 1947, each prefecture is headed by a directly elected governor (知事, chiji) and a unicameral prefectural assembly (議会, gikai), both serving four-year terms. Prefectures are grouped into eight regions: Hokkaidō, Tōhoku, Kantō, Chūbu, Kansai (Kinki), Chūgoku, Shikoku, and Kyūshū.

History

Before the Meiji Restoration, Japan was divided into provinces (kuni) controlled by feudal lords (daimyo) and by territories administered directly by the Tokugawa shogunate. In June 1868 the Meiji government established the Fuhanken sanchisei, dividing shogunal territory into fu (urban prefectures) and ken (rural prefectures). In 1871, following the abolition of the han system, all former domains became prefectures, and through a series of mergers their number fell from over 300 to 72 by 1872 and to 47 by 1888–1890. The current set of 47 prefectures has remained essentially unchanged since then, aside from boundary adjustments and the reversion of Okinawa Prefecture from United States administration in 1972.

List of prefectures

The traditional order used for listing the prefectures runs from north to south. By population, Tokyo is the largest prefecture (about 14 million residents), while Tottori is the smallest (around 550,000). By area, Hokkaidō is by far the largest, and Kagawa the smallest.

Types of prefecture

To (metropolis)

Tokyo is the only to. After the abolition of Tokyo City in 1943, the metropolitan government combined prefectural and city functions; its 23 special wards (tokubetsu-ku) each have their own elected mayor and assembly, functioning much like cities.

Dō (territory)

Hokkaidō is the only dō. Because of its size it is subdivided into 14 subprefectures. The name dates from its administration as a frontier region by the central government's Hokkaidō Development Commission in the Meiji period.

Fu (urban prefectures)

Osaka and Kyoto are designated fu. Historically the term applied to prefectures containing major cities; in modern law fu and ken have identical powers.

Ken

The remaining 43 prefectures are ken. In Japanese, all four types are collectively referred to by the compound todōfuken.

Government

Each prefecture has a governor elected by popular vote and a unicameral assembly whose members are also elected for four-year terms. Prefectures finance themselves through prefectural taxes supplemented by transfers from the central government, and they administer public schools, police, hospitals, roads, water and other infrastructure. Executive authority over many regulatory matters was historically delegated from central ministries, but decentralisation reforms since the 1990s have expanded local autonomy.
