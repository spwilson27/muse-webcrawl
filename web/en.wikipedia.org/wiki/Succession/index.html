Succession

Succession refers to the orderly transfer of a position, office, title, property or authority from one person or generation to the next. The term is used most prominently for the inheritance of thrones in monarchies, for the passing of estates after death in law, and in ecology for the sequential change of plant and animal communities in an area.

Royal succession

Monarchies regulate succession to the throne through heredity, constitutional law or election. Common hereditary systems include male-preference primogeniture, in which sons rank ahead of daughters, and absolute primogeniture, in which the eldest child succeeds regardless of sex — adopted by Sweden in 1980 and by the United Kingdom for those born after 2011 under the Perth Agreement. A person in line to succeed is an heir apparent if no future birth can displace them, or an heir presumptive if they can. Disputed successions have been a frequent cause of war, from the Wars of the Roses to the War of the Spanish Succession.

Legal succession

In private law, succession governs the transfer of a deceased person's estate. Testate succession follows the terms of a will, while intestate succession follows statutory rules that typically favour a surviving spouse and descendants. Many civil-law jurisdictions impose forced heirship, reserving portions of the estate for close relatives.

Ecological succession

In ecology, succession is the process by which the species composition of an ecosystem changes over time following a disturbance or the creation of new substrate. Primary succession begins on barren surfaces such as lava flows; secondary succession follows disturbances like fire that leave soil intact. Early theories described a deterministic progression toward a stable climax community, while modern ecology treats succession as a more contingent process shaped by facilitation, tolerance and inhibition among species.
