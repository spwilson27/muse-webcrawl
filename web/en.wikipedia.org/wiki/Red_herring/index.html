Red herring

A red herring is something that misleads or distracts from a relevant or important question. It may be either a logical fallacy or a literary device that leads readers or audiences toward a false conclusion. A red herring may be used intentionally, as in mystery fiction or as part of rhetorical strategies (e.g., in politics), or may be used in argumentation inadvertently.

Origin of the idiom

The expression originates from the practice of curing herring: when herring is smoked and brined it turns a deep red colour and acquires a strong smell. A popular account held that the fish were dragged across trails to throw hunting or tracking dogs off a scent. The phrase was popularised in its figurative sense by the journalist William Cobbett in 1807, who recounted using red herrings to deflect hounds from chasing a hare, and then applied the image to the press misleading the public.

As a logical fallacy

As an informal fallacy, the red herring falls into the broad class of fallacies of relevance: an argument is presented that is irrelevant to the issue at hand and serves to divert attention from the original question. Closely related fallacies include the straw man, ad hominem attacks, and "whataboutism".

As a literary device

In fiction — especially detective and mystery stories — a red herring is a clue deliberately planted to mislead the reader and conceal the true solution. Writers such as Agatha Christie made famous use of the device. The term is also used in screenwriting and game design for misleading narrative elements.
