Carrageenan

Carrageenan
TypeSulfated polysaccharide (food additive E407)
SourceRed seaweeds, esp. Chondrus crispus, Eucheuma, Kappaphycus

Main typesKappa, iota, lambda

Carrageenans are a family of natural linear sulfated polysaccharides extracted from red edible seaweeds. They are widely used in the food industry for their gelling, thickening, and stabilising properties, forming the additive designated E407. The name derives from Carragheen (Carraigín) in County Waterford, Ireland, where the seaweed Chondrus crispus (Irish moss) was traditionally used in cooking and medicine.

Types and properties

The three commercially important types are kappa (κ), iota (ι), and lambda (λ) carrageenan, differing in their degree of sulfation and gelling behaviour. Kappa forms strong, rigid gels with potassium ions; iota forms soft, elastic gels with calcium ions; lambda does not gel but is an effective thickener. Carrageenan is used in dairy products, chocolate milk, ice cream, processed meats, plant-based milks, toothpaste, and many other products to control texture and prevent separation.

Production

Most carrageenan today is farmed, principally in the Philippines and Indonesia, from the red algae Kappaphycus alvarezii and Eucheuma denticulatum. The seaweed is washed, treated with hot alkali, filtered, and the carrageenan recovered by precipitation or gel pressing.

Safety debate

Food-grade carrageenan is approved by regulators including the U.S. FDA and EFSA. Degraded carrageenan (poligeenan), a chemically distinct, low-molecular-weight product not used in food, is classified as a possible carcinogen from animal studies; some researchers have raised concerns about whether food-grade carrageenan can degrade in digestion, a claim regulatory reviews have generally not accepted as grounds for prohibition.
