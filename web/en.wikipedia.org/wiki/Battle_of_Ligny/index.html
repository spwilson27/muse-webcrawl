Battle of Ligny

The Battle of Ligny was fought on 16 June 1815 between the French Armée du Nord under Napoleon Bonaparte and the Prussian Army of the Lower Rhine under Field Marshal Gebhard Leberecht von Blücher. It was the last victory of Napoleon's career. Fought two days before the Battle of Waterloo, and on the same day as the Battle of Quatre Bras a few miles to the west, Ligny was a French tactical success that failed in its object: the Prussian army was beaten but not destroyed, retreated in good order toward Wavre — northward, parallel to Wellington's army rather than eastward away from it — and so was able to intervene decisively at Waterloo on 18 June.

Background

After escaping from Elba and resuming power in the Hundred Days, Napoleon faced the Seventh Coalition. Two allied armies were concentrating in Belgium: Wellington's Anglo-Allied army around Brussels and Blücher's Prussians along the Sambre. Napoleon struck first, crossing the Sambre at Charleroi on 15 June to drive a wedge between them and defeat each in detail. For 16 June he planned a double blow: Marshal Michel Ney with the left wing would seize the crossroads of Quatre Bras and hold Wellington, while Napoleon with the right wing and reserve attacked Blücher's army, which had concentrated around Sombreffe and the villages along the Ligny brook.

The battle

The Prussian position ran through the villages of Saint-Amand, Ligny and Saint-Amand-la-Haye on the far bank of the Ligny brook — villages that anchored the line but also swallowed troops into savage house-to-house fighting. Napoleon's attack opened around 14:30. Vandamme's corps fought for Saint-Amand against Steinmetz, Gérard's corps attacked Ligny village itself, and Grouchy's cavalry held the French right against Thielmann.

The battle turned on a misunderstanding. Napoleon had ordered d'Erlon's I Corps, marching between the two battlefields, to fall on the Prussian right and rear and complete the encirclement. D'Erlon was countermanded by Ney, who needed him at Quatre Bras; the corps marched and countermarched between the two fights and engaged at neither — one of the great staff failures of the age. Even so, around 18:00 Napoleon launched his Guard and Milhaud's cuirassiers against the Prussian centre at Ligny. The centre broke. Blücher, leading a cavalry countercharge personally, was unhorsed and ridden over twice, and was rescued by his aide Nostitz, who threw a cloak over the unconscious field marshal in the dark.

Retreat and consequences

Command devolved on Blücher's chief of staff, Gneisenau, who made the decision on which the campaign — and arguably Napoleon's fate — turned. Rather than retreat east on Namur and Liège, along the Prussian line of communications and away from Wellington, Gneisenau ordered the army north to Wavre, staying within supporting distance of the Anglo-Allied army. The retreat was poorly observed by the French, and Grouchy's pursuit on 17 June was slow and misdirected; Napoleon's dispatch of Grouchy with some 33,000 men to "pursue the Prussians" removed a third of the French army from the decisive field. On 18 June, while Grouchy fought Thielmann's rear guard at Wavre, two Prussian corps reached Waterloo and fell on Napoleon's right flank.

Ligny thus illustrates the campaign in miniature: a brilliantly conceived offensive undone by confused staff work, divided command, and an enemy who, though beaten, refused to be destroyed.
