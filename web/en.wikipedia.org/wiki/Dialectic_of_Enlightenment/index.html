Dialectic of Enlightenment (German: Dialektik der Aufklärung) is a work of philosophy and social criticism written by Max Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno of the Frankfurt School, composed in exile in California during the Second World War and published in Amsterdam in 1947. Subtitled "Philosophical Fragments", it is among the most influential works of twentieth-century critical theory.

Thesis

The book asks why humanity, "instead of entering a truly human state, is sinking into a new kind of barbarism". Its central argument is that Enlightenment reason — which promised liberation from myth and fear — reverts into myth itself: reason reduced to instrumental calculation becomes a tool of domination over nature, over other people, and over the inner self. "Myth is already enlightenment, and enlightenment reverts to mythology."

Contents

The opening essay, "The Concept of Enlightenment", reads Odysseus as the prototype of the bourgeois individual who masters nature and himself through cunning and self-denial. Excursuses treat Odysseus, the Marquis de Sade's Juliette, and the limits of enlightenment morality. The famous chapter "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" argues that film, radio, and popular music standardise consciousness and reconcile audiences to the status quo. A final section, "Elements of Anti-Semitism", analyses the pathology of antisemitic projection.

Reception

Largely ignored on publication, the book was rediscovered by the German student movement of the 1960s and became a canonical text of critical theory, influencing debates on mass culture, rationality, and the legacy of the Enlightenment. Jürgen Habermas both criticised its totalising pessimism and developed its project in his theory of communicative action.
