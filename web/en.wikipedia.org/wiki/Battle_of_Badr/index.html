Battle of Badr

The Battle of Badr (Arabic: غَزْوَةُ بَدْرٍ) was fought on 13 March 624 CE (17 Ramadan, 2 AH in the Islamic calendar) between the forces of the Muslim community of Medina, led by the prophet Muhammad, and a force from the Quraysh tribe of Mecca. It took place near the wells of Badr, about 130 km southwest of Medina. Although the Muslims were heavily outnumbered, they won a decisive victory, killing several leading Qurayshi figures, among them Abu Jahl. Badr was the first large-scale battle between the two forces and is regarded as a turning point in Muhammad's struggle with his Meccan opponents; in Islamic tradition it is remembered as a miraculous victory granted by God, and it is mentioned in the Quran.

Background

After the Hijra to Medina in 622, tensions between the Muslim emigrants and the Quraysh escalated into raids on caravans. In early 624 Muhammad led a force out to intercept a wealthy Meccan caravan returning from Syria under Abu Sufyan. Abu Sufyan evaded the ambush by taking a coastal route and called on Mecca for help; a relief army of about a thousand men marched to Badr under the leadership of Abu Jahl and insisted on battle even after learning the caravan was safe.

Battle

The Muslims seized the wells nearest the battlefield and built a cistern, denying water to the Meccans. Following Arab custom, the battle began with single combats between champions — Hamza, Ali and Ubayda ibn al-Harith defeated their Qurayshi opponents. In the ensuing general engagement the smaller Muslim force, tightly disciplined and fighting from a prepared position, broke the Meccan army. Abu Jahl and other senior Quraysh leaders, including Umayya ibn Khalaf, were killed. Islamic tradition holds that angels fought alongside the Muslims, a belief reflected in the Quran (e.g., 3:123–124 and 8:9).

Aftermath

The victory enormously raised Muhammad's prestige in Arabia and strengthened his position in Medina, while Mecca mourned many of its most prominent men; the prisoners were treated unusually well for the time, some being ransomed or freed in exchange for teaching Muslims to read and write. The Quraysh sought revenge at the Battle of Uhud the following year. Badr remains one of the most significant events in Islamic history, commemorated on 17 Ramadan.
