Secularism in Turkey

Secularism in Turkey (Turkish: laiklik) is the principle, embedded in the Turkish constitution, that the state is neutral toward religious beliefs and that religious institutions are subject to state control. It was introduced through the sweeping reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk after the founding of the Republic of Turkey in 1923, and remains one of the most contested principles of Turkish public life.

History

The reform programme of the 1920s and 1930s — the Kemalist reforms — abolished the Caliphate (1924), closed religious schools and Sufi lodges, replaced Islamic law with European-style civil and penal codes, adopted the Latin alphabet (1928), and removed Islam as the state religion from the constitution (1928). In 1937 secularism (laiklik) was written into the constitution as one of the defining characteristics of the republic. Unusually, Turkish secularism does not separate religion from the state entirely: the Directorate of Religious Affairs (Diyanet), founded in 1924, administers mosques and employs imams as civil servants, bringing Sunni Islam under state supervision.

Debates

Secularism has been defended historically by the military, the judiciary and the Republican People's Party (CHP), and challenged by religious-conservative movements. Flashpoints have included the headscarf ban in universities and public institutions — largely dismantled under the governments of the Justice and Development Party (AKP) after 2002 — compulsory religious education, and the expanding budget and role of the Diyanet. Critics describe the Kemalist model as "assertive secularism," in which the state regulates religion rather than merely keeping neutral distance from it.
