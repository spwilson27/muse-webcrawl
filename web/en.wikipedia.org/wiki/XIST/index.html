XIST

XIST (X-inactive specific transcript) is a gene on the X chromosome of placental mammals that produces a long non-coding RNA molecule essential for X-chromosome inactivation, the process by which one of the two X chromosomes in female cells is largely silenced to achieve dosage compensation with males.

Function

Xist RNA is transcribed only from the inactive X chromosome (Xi) and "coats" that chromosome in cis, recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes — including Polycomb repressive complexes — that establish heterochromatin marks such as H3K27me3, leading to stable gene silencing maintained through cell division. In marsupials an unrelated gene, Rsx, performs a similar function.

Regulation

Expression of Xist is controlled by its antisense repressor Tsix and by factors such as the long non-coding RNA Jpx and the protein RNF12/RLIM, forming a network that ensures exactly one X chromosome remains active per diploid cell. Xist was identified in the early 1990s, with the human gene characterised in 1991 and the mouse gene shortly afterwards; transgenic experiments showed Xist is both necessary and, in early development, sufficient to initiate silencing.
