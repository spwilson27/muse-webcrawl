Émile Gallé (4 May 1846 – 23 September 1904) was a French artist and designer who worked in glass and is considered to be one of the major innovators of the French Art Nouveau movement. He founded the École de Nancy (School of Nancy), the alliance of artists and manufacturers that made the city of Nancy a capital of the new style, and his factory produced cameo glass, enamelled glass, and furniture inlaid with naturalistic marquetry that were sold throughout Europe.

Life and work

Gallé was born in Nancy, the son of a dealer in glassware and ceramics. He studied philosophy, botany, and drawing in Weimar and learned glassmaking at Meisenthal in Lorraine before taking over his father's business in 1874. His exhibit at the Paris Exposition Universelle of 1878 established his reputation; at the 1889 exposition his glass won the Grand Prix, and he was made an officer of the Legion of Honour.

A committed naturalist, Gallé translated his botanical studies — insects, orchids, thistles, dragonflies — into designs, often using layered, acid-etched cameo glass and techniques such as marqueterie de verre and metallic foil inclusions. Many pieces carry lines of poetry by Baudelaire, Verlaine, and others, the so-called verreries parlantes ("speaking glassware"). He was also a committed Dreyfusard, and some late works protest the political injustices of the day.

Legacy

In 1901 Gallé founded the École de Nancy, with Louis Majorelle and the Daum brothers among its members. After his death in 1904, his widow continued the factory until 1914, and the Gallé name was produced in later decades in large numbers of signed reproductions. The Musée de l'École de Nancy holds the principal collection of his work.
