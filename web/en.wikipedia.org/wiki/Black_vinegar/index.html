Black vinegar

Aged black vinegar is dark, complex, and mildly sweet

Black vinegar is an aged vinegar made from fermented grains such as rice, wheat, sorghum, or millet. It is a staple ingredient of Chinese cuisine and is also produced in Japan and Korea. Dark in color and rich in flavor, black vinegar develops deep, malty, slightly smoky notes during a lengthy fermentation and aging process, and is generally much less sharp than white vinegar.

China

China's most famous black vinegars include Shanxi aged vinegar (from Shanxi province, made chiefly from sorghum and aged for years, sometimes decades), Zhenjiang aromatic vinegar (from Jiangsu province, made from glutinous rice), Sichuan Baoning vinegar (made from wheat bran and herbs), and Fujian Yongchun aged vinegar. Black vinegar is used as a dipping sauce for dumplings such as xiaolongbao and jiaozi, and in dishes including hot and sour soup, sweet-and-sour preparations, braised meats, and the Shanghai dish smoked fish. Zhenjiang vinegar has been produced for more than 1,400 years, and the craft of Shanxi aged vinegar is recognized as part of China's intangible cultural heritage.

Japan and Korea

In Japan, black vinegar (kurozu) is traditionally made from brown rice, especially in Kagoshima Prefecture, where it is fermented in ceramic pots outdoors for one to five years. In the 1990s and 2000s kurozu became popular in Japan as a health drink, drunk diluted in water. Korean black vinegar (heukcho) is similarly made from brown rice and consumed both in cooking and as a beverage.

Health claims

Black vinegar is rich in amino acids from its grain base and is traditionally believed to aid digestion and circulation. It is marketed in East Asia as a health food, though medical evidence for strong therapeutic effects is limited.
