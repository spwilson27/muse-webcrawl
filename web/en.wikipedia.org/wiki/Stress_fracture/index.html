Stress fracture

A stress fracture is a fatigue-induced fracture of the bone caused by repeated stress over time. Instead of resulting from a single severe impact, stress fractures are the result of accumulated injury from repeated submaximal loading, such as running or jumping. They are common overuse injuries in athletes and military recruits.

Bone scintigraphy showing a tibial stress fracture.

Mechanism and sites

Bone remodels in response to load, but when repetitive stress outpaces remodelling, microdamage accumulates and a crack forms. Common sites include the tibia, metatarsals (the classic "march fracture" of soldiers), fibula, femur, navicular, and pelvis. Risk factors include sudden increases in training volume, low bone mineral density, poor nutrition and energy availability, biomechanical abnormalities, and inappropriate footwear.

Symptoms and diagnosis

The hallmark is localised pain that worsens with activity and improves with rest, with pinpoint tenderness over the bone. Plain X-rays may be normal for the first two to three weeks; MRI is the most sensitive imaging test, with bone scintigraphy as an alternative.

Treatment

Most stress fractures heal with a period of relative rest and modification of activity over six to eight weeks, sometimes with a boot or cast. High-risk sites (such as the femoral neck tension side, anterior tibia, and navicular) may require non-weight-bearing or surgery because of the risk of complete fracture or non-union.
