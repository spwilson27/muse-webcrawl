Hip roof

A hip roof, hip-roof or hipped roof, is a type of roof where all sides slope downwards to the walls, usually with a fairly gentle slope (though a tented roof by definition is a hipped roof with steeply sloping sides rising to a peak). Thus a hipped roof has no gables or other vertical sides to the roof.

A square hip roof is shaped like a pyramid. Hip roofs on rectangular houses will have two triangular sides and two trapezoidal ones. A hip roof has a ridge at the top where the sloping sides meet, or the sloping sides may meet at a single point on a square or irregular plan building.

Construction

Hip roofs are more difficult to construct than gable roofs, requiring more complex systems of rafters or trusses. The sloped end of a hip roof is called a hip end, and the inclined rafters that form the junctions are called hip rafters. Hip roofs can be constructed on a wide variety of plan shapes. Each side's slope is a separate piece of carpentry, and the angles vary depending on the plan geometry.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hip roofs are self-bracing, requiring less diagonal bracing than gable roofs, and are more resistant to wind damage — a significant advantage in hurricane-prone regions. The inward slope of all four sides also helps shed water and snow and leaves no flat ends exposed to high winds. On the other hand, hip roofs are more complex and expensive to build, limit attic space and ventilation, and offer fewer opportunities for daylight through gable windows.

Variants

Variants include the mansard roof (with two slopes on each side), the pavilion roof on square plans, the half-hip or jerkinhead roof (where the gable end is partially sloped), and the Dutch hip roof, which combines a hip roof with small gables at the top of the hips. Hipped roofs are a defining feature of many architectural styles, including Georgian, Federal, Italianate, and the Prairie School houses of Frank Lloyd Wright.
