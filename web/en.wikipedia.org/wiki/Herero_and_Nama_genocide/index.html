Herero and Nama genocide

Date1904–1908
LocationGerman South West Africa (present-day Namibia)
TargetHerero and Nama peoples
Deaths~24,000–100,000 Herero; ~10,000 Nama (estimates vary)
PerpetratorsImperial German Schutztruppe under Lt. Gen. Lothar von Trotha

The Herero and Nama genocide (also called the Herero and Namaqua genocide) was the extermination of the Herero and Nama peoples by the German Empire between 1904 and 1908 in German South West Africa, modern Namibia. It is widely regarded as the first genocide of the twentieth century: up to 80% of the Herero population and half of the Nama population perished through battle, forced flight into the desert, and concentration camps such as Shark Island.

Background

From 1884 Germany colonised South West Africa, progressively seizing Herero and Nama grazing land for German settlers and driving the African population into debt through trader credit. On 12 January 1904 the Herero, led by Samuel Maharero, rose in revolt, killing over a hundred German settlers while generally sparing women, children, and non-Germans. The Nama under Hendrik Witbooi rose later that year.

Extermination campaign

Berlin sent General Lothar von Trotha, whose forces defeated the Herero at the Battle of Waterberg in August 1904. The Herero fled east into the waterless Omaheke desert, whose waterholes Trotha's troops sealed off and poisoned. On 2 October 1904 von Trotha issued his infamous extermination order (Vernichtungsbefehl): "Every Herero, with or without a gun, with or without cattle, will be shot." International outrage and pressure from Chancellor von Bülow forced Berlin to rescind the shooting order, but prisoners were then worked and starved to death in concentration camps — at Windhoek, Swakopmund, and most lethally Shark Island at Lüderitz — while Nama prisoners suffered the same fate after Witbooi's death in 1905. Skulls and body parts of victims were shipped to Germany for racial "research."

Aftermath and recognition

The war destroyed Herero and Nama society and landholding; survivors were stripped of land and subjected to pass laws and forced labour under a codified racial regime. In 1985 the UN Whitaker Report classified the episode as genocide. After decades of campaigns by descendant communities, Germany formally recognised the genocide in 2021 and pledged €1.1 billion in development aid to Namibia, though Herero and Nama representatives criticised the agreement for being negotiated government-to-government without their direct participation, and disputes over reparations continue.
