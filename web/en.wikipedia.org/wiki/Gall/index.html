Gall

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Galls (from the Latin galla, 'oak-apple') or cecidia (from the Greek kēkidion, anything gushing out) are a kind of swelling growth on the external tissues of plants. Plant galls are abnormal outgrowths of plant tissues, similar to benign tumours or warts in animals. They can be caused by various parasites, from viruses, fungi and bacteria, to other plants, insects and mites. Plant galls are often highly organised structures so that the cause of the gall can often be determined without the actual agent being identified. This applies particularly to insect and mite plant galls.

Causes

Insect galls are the highly distinctive plant structures formed by some herbivorous insects as their own microhabitats. The gall is supplied with both nutrition and shelter for the developing larva. Gall wasps (Cynipidae) — especially on oaks, which host famous galls such as oak apples and marble galls — and gall midges (Cecidomyiidae) are among the most prolific gall-formers. Other inducers include aphids, psyllids, eriophyid mites, nematodes, bacteria such as Agrobacterium tumefaciens (crown gall), and many fungi.

Biology

Gall-inducing insects chemically manipulate the host plant, redirecting the growth of plant cells to produce nutritive tissue and a protective shell. The exact mechanism involves effector proteins and plant hormones such as auxins and cytokinins. Despite conspicuous growth, most galls do little serious damage to healthy hosts, though crown gall and phylloxera can be agricultural problems.

Uses

Oak galls rich in tannic acid were historically a principal source of iron gall ink, the standard writing ink in Europe from the early medieval period into the 20th century. Aleppo galls were prized for tanning leather and dyeing. Some galls are used in traditional medicine, and galls serve as food and shelter for a rich community of parasitoids and inquilines studied by ecologists.
