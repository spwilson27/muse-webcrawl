Computer self-efficacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A user's confidence in using a computer influences how readily new technology is adopted

Computer self-efficacy is a person's judgment of their own capability to use a computer to accomplish a task. The concept is an application of Albert Bandura's broader theory of self-efficacy to the field of human–computer interaction and information systems research.

The construct was introduced into the computing literature by Deborah Compeau and Christopher Higgins in 1995, who defined it as an individual's perception of their ability to use a computer in the accomplishment of a task, rather than reflecting simple component skills such as using a specific software feature.

Measurement

Compeau and Higgins developed a widely used ten-item scale in which respondents rate their confidence in completing tasks under varying conditions, such as having only manuals for reference, having someone available to help, or having previously seen someone else perform the task. Scores are typically given on a ten-point scale from "not at all confident" to "totally confident".

Effects

Research has linked computer self-efficacy to computer anxiety (negatively), willingness to adopt new software, workplace technology acceptance, and learning outcomes in computer training. It is a significant variable in extensions of the Technology Acceptance Model. Self-efficacy beliefs are shaped by prior experience, encouragement from others, and vicarious experience of watching peers succeed.
