Zero waste

Zero waste is a set of principles focused on waste prevention that encourages the redesign of resource life cycles so that all products are reused. The goal is for no trash to be sent to landfills, incinerators, or the ocean. The Zero Waste International Alliance defines zero waste as "the conservation of all resources by means of responsible production, consumption, reuse, and recovery of products, packaging, and materials without burning and with no discharges to land, water, or air that threaten the environment or human health."

Principles

  • Redesign products and packaging for durability, repairability and recyclability.
  • Reduce consumption and eliminate toxics.
  • Reuse and repair rather than discard.
  • Recycle and compost remaining materials.
  • Extended producer responsibility, making manufacturers responsible for products at end of life.

History and adoption

The term gained currency in the 1970s through chemist Paul Palmer and the Zero Waste Institute, and spread as a municipal policy goal in the 1990s and 2000s. Cities including Canberra, San Francisco and Kamikatsu in Japan adopted zero-waste goals; San Francisco's 2009 ordinance mandating composting and recycling helped it divert around 80% of waste from landfill. The Japanese town of Kamikatsu became famous for sorting waste into dozens of categories.

Criticism

Critics argue that literal "zero" is an asymptotic goal rather than an achievable state, and that some diversion claims rely on creative accounting, incineration with energy recovery, or exporting waste streams. Supporters respond that the value of the concept lies in shifting attention upstream, from waste management to system redesign and a circular economy.
