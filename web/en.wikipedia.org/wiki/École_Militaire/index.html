The École Militaire ("Military School") is a vast complex of buildings in the 7th arrondissement of Paris, France, housing various military training facilities. Founded in 1750 by King Louis XV with the encouragement of Madame de Pompadour and the financier Joseph Pâris Duverney, it was built to educate impoverished sons of the nobility and of officers as cadets of the French army. It stands at the south-eastern end of the Champ de Mars, opposite the Eiffel Tower.

The central pavilion of the École Militaire, designed by Ange-Jacques Gabriel, viewed from the Champ de Mars.

History

Construction began in 1751 to designs by Ange-Jacques Gabriel, Louis XV's chief architect. The central corps de logis, with its domed pavilion and Corinthian portico, is a landmark of French neoclassical architecture; a chapel, dedicated to Saint Louis, was completed in 1769. Financial difficulties forced the crown to scale back the project and delayed full occupation, and the school was suppressed in 1787 as an economy measure.

The school's most famous cadet was Napoleon Bonaparte, who entered in October 1784 and, by examination, completed the two-year course in a single year, graduating in 1785 as a second lieutenant of artillery. During the Revolution the buildings served various purposes, including as barracks and a depot; under Napoleon the site housed the Imperial Guard's headquarters.

Today

The complex remains in military use, housing the École de guerre (the French war college for senior officers), staff colleges and other defence institutes. It is not generally open to the public except on special occasions such as the Journées du Patrimoine. The nearby Paris metro station École Militaire on Line 8 takes its name from the complex.
