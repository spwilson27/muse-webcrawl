Customer satisfaction

Customer satisfaction (often abbreviated as CSAT) is a term frequently used in marketing to measure how products and services supplied by a company meet or surpass customer expectation. It is defined as "the number of customers, or percentage of total customers, whose reported experience with a firm, its products, or its services exceeds specified satisfaction goals."

Organisations track satisfaction because it is a leading indicator of consumer repurchase intentions and loyalty, and a key differentiator in competitive markets.

Definition

Satisfaction is generally understood through the expectation–disconfirmation model: customers compare perceived performance against their prior expectations. Performance below expectations produces dissatisfaction, performance matching expectations produces satisfaction, and performance exceeding expectations can produce delight. The American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI), founded in 1994, models satisfaction as driven by perceived quality, perceived value, and customer expectations.

Measurement

Satisfaction is typically measured with survey questions on a Likert scale, for example "How satisfied were you with your experience?" on a scale from "very dissatisfied" to "very satisfied". The CSAT score is commonly calculated as the percentage of respondents selecting the top one or two satisfaction categories. Surveys may be transactional (after a specific interaction) or relational (about the overall relationship).

Related metrics include the Net Promoter Score, which measures likelihood to recommend, and the Customer Effort Score. National indices such as the ACSI publish cross-industry benchmarks based on standardised interviews.

Purpose

Businesses use satisfaction data to identify unhappy customers and intervene before they churn, to prioritise product and service improvements, and to benchmark performance against competitors. Research has linked higher satisfaction to stronger customer retention, greater share of wallet, and improved financial performance, though the strength of the link varies by industry.

Criticism

Critics note that satisfaction scores can suffer from response bias, cultural differences in scale use, and survey fatigue. High stated satisfaction does not always translate into loyalty, since satisfied customers may still switch for price or convenience. For this reason satisfaction is usually interpreted alongside behavioural data.
