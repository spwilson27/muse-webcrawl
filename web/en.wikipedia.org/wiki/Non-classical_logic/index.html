Non-classical logic


Non-classical logics depart from or extend the principles of classical logic.
Non-classical logic
FieldMathematical and philosophical logic
ExamplesIntuitionistic, modal, fuzzy, paraconsistent, relevance, linear logic
DeviationsRejects or restricts laws such as excluded middle, non-contradiction, explosion

Non-classical logics (and sometimes alternative logics) are formal systems of logic that differ in some significant way from the standard logical systems, principally classical propositional and predicate logic. They arise either by extending classical logic with new operators (as in modal logic) or by deviating from it by rejecting one or more of its principles (as in intuitionistic or paraconsistent logic).

Classification

The philosopher John P. Burgess and others distinguish extensions of classical logic, which keep all classically valid inferences but add expressive power, from deviant logics, which invalidate some classical theorems. Susan Haack's Deviant Logic (1974) popularised this terminology.

Major families

  • Modal logics — add operators for necessity (□) and possibility (◇); variants include deontic, temporal, and epistemic logic. Formalised semantically by Saul Kripke using possible worlds.
  • Intuitionistic logic — developed by L. E. J. Brouwer and Arend Heyting; rejects the law of the excluded middle and double-negation elimination, tying truth to provability. Central to constructive mathematics.
  • Many-valued and fuzzy logics — allow truth values beyond true and false; Jan Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic and Lotfi Zadeh's fuzzy logic (1965) handle vagueness and degrees of truth.
  • Paraconsistent logic — tolerates contradictions without triviality by rejecting the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet); associated with Graham Priest's dialetheism.
  • Relevance logic — requires premises and conclusion of a valid argument to be relevantly connected.
  • Linear logic — introduced by Jean-Yves Girard (1987), treats propositions as resources that are consumed in inference; influential in computer science.
  • Non-monotonic logic — formalises defeasible reasoning in which conclusions can be withdrawn when new premises arrive; important in artificial intelligence.

Motivations and applications

Non-classical logics are motivated by phenomena classical logic handles awkwardly: vagueness (the sorites paradox), future contingents, inconsistent information in databases, constructive proof, quantum mechanics (quantum logic, proposed by Birkhoff and von Neumann), and the semantics of natural-language conditionals. Many have found practical applications in program verification, type theory, linguistics, and legal reasoning.
