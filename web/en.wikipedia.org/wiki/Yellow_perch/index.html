Yellow perch

Yellow perch
Scientific namePerca flavescens
FamilyPercidae
RangeFresh waters of North America
Typical length10–25 cm (4–10 in)
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The yellow perch (Perca flavescens), sometimes called the American perch, is a freshwater perciform fish native to North America. It is closely related to the European perch (Perca fluviatilis) and is one of the most popular panfish among anglers on the continent, prized for its mild, sweet flesh.

Description

The yellow perch has a laterally compressed, elongate body colored yellow to brassy green, with six to eight dark vertical bars along its sides and a white belly. Its two dorsal fins are separate: the first is spiny and the second soft-rayed. Adults typically measure 10 to 25 cm, though specimens over 30 cm occur in productive waters. Females grow larger than males.

Distribution and habitat

The species is native to lakes, ponds, and slow rivers from the Canadian Arctic drainages south through the Great Lakes and Mississippi basins to the Atlantic and Gulf coastal plain. It has been widely introduced elsewhere in North America. Yellow perch are schooling fish that favor clear water with moderate vegetation, feeding on zooplankton when young and on insect larvae, crustaceans, and small fish as adults.

Reproduction

Spawning occurs in spring when water temperatures reach roughly 7–13 °C. Females lay long, gelatinous ribbons of eggs — sometimes containing tens of thousands — over vegetation or submerged branches; the eggs receive no parental care.

Fishing

Yellow perch support important commercial fisheries, historically centered on Lake Erie, and are a staple of ice fishing in winter. Populations fluctuate cyclically, and overfishing and habitat change have at times sharply reduced catches.
