The Macedonian phalanx (Greek: Μακεδονική φάλαγξ) was an infantry formation developed by Philip II of Macedon and famously used by his son Alexander the Great to conquer the Achaemenid Persian Empire. Formed of professional soldiers called pezhetairoi ("foot companions"), it was armed with the sarissa, a pike roughly 4 to 6 metres (13–20 ft) long — far longer than the spears of the classical Greek hoplite phalanx — and fought in dense blocks typically sixteen men deep.

Organisation and equipment

The basic tactical unit was the syntagma of 256 men: 16 files of 16 men. Six syntagmata formed a taxis of about 1,500 men under a taxiarch. The sarissa required both hands, so the phalangite carried a smaller shield (about 60 cm) slung from the neck and shoulder, a short sword (kopis or xiphos) as a sidearm, a bronze helmet, and greaves; the first five ranks levelled their pikes so that an enemy faced a hedge of five spear-points per file before even reaching the first rank. Philip reportedly drilled his troops relentlessly, with forced marches carrying full kit.

Tactics

Unlike the hoplite phalanx, the Macedonian phalanx was not expected to win battles alone. It served as the immovable anvil of a combined-arms system: it engaged and pinned the enemy front while Alexander's Companion cavalry — the hammer — struck decisive blows at weak points, a system perfected at Chaeronea (338 BC), Issus (333 BC), and Gaugamela (331 BC). Flank protection came from hypaspists and light troops. The formation was formidable on flat ground but vulnerable on broken terrain and from the flanks and rear, as Rome later demonstrated.

Decline

Under the Hellenistic successor kingdoms, the phalanx grew deeper and less flexible, its supporting arms declined, and it proved inferior to the manipular Roman legion in the Macedonian Wars: at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) and decisively at Pydna (168 BC), rough ground opened gaps in the pike line that Roman swordsmen exploited, ending the phalanx's two-century dominance.
