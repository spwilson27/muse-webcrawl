In set theory, a paradoxical set is a set that can be partitioned into two subsets, each of which can be reassembled — using only rigid motions or group actions that intuition says should preserve size — into a copy of the whole original set. Such sets underlie the most counterintuitive theorems of modern mathematics, most famously the Banach–Tarski paradox, which shows that a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls identical to the original.

Definitions

Given a group G acting on a set X, a subset E of X is called G-paradoxical if E can be written as a disjoint union of finitely many pieces that can be moved by elements of G to partition two separate copies of E. A group itself is paradoxical if, acting on itself by multiplication, it can be so decomposed — which holds whenever the group contains a free subgroup on two generators. The free group on two generators is the engine of all such paradoxes: a word in the generators can be split so that multiplying by a single generator transforms part of the group into the whole of it.

Historical development

Felix Hausdorff proved in 1914 that the sphere (minus a countable set of points) admits a paradoxical decomposition under rotations. Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski extended this in 1924 to the full Banach–Tarski theorem. The constructions depend essentially on the axiom of choice: the pieces involved are non-measurable — they cannot be assigned a consistent volume — which is why the paradox does not contradict physical intuition about cutting real objects. Giuseppe Vitali's earlier non-measurable set (1905) is a simpler relative of the same phenomenon.

Consequences and responses

Paradoxical decompositions imply that no finitely additive, motion-invariant measure can be defined for all subsets of three-dimensional space, a foundational fact of measure theory. Conversely, von Neumann showed that the plane admits no such paradox under area-preserving affine motions in the same way, tying the phenomenon to the concept of amenable groups, which he introduced in 1929 precisely to explain where paradoxicality can and cannot occur. The area remains a vivid test case in debates over the axiom of choice and mathematical Platonism.
