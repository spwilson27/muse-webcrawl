Breeder's equation

[Image placeholder: selection response diagram]

The breeder's equation is a fundamental equation in quantitative genetics used to predict the response of a trait to artificial or natural selection. It is written as:

R = h2S

where R is the response to selection (the difference in the mean trait value between the offspring generation and the parental generation before selection), h2 is the narrow-sense heritability of the trait, and S is the selection differential (the difference between the mean trait value of the selected parents and the mean of the whole parental generation).

Interpretation

The equation states that a trait evolves in proportion both to the strength of selection applied to it and to the fraction of its phenotypic variance that is attributable to additive genetic effects. If heritability is zero, no response occurs regardless of selection; if selection is absent, no response occurs regardless of heritability.

Extensions

The multivariate form of the breeder's equation, developed by Russell Lande, is Δz̄ = Gβ, where G is the additive genetic variance–covariance matrix and β is the vector of selection gradients. This form accounts for genetic correlations between traits, which can constrain or facilitate evolution. The breeder's equation is a special case of the more general Price equation.

Applications and limitations

Animal and plant breeders use the equation to forecast genetic gain per generation for traits such as milk yield, growth rate, and crop yield. Its predictions are most accurate over a single generation; over longer periods, selection itself changes allele frequencies and heritability, environmental change and genetic drift intervene, and predicted responses may diverge from observed ones. In studies of natural populations, estimates of heritability and selection differentials in the wild have been used to test whether observed evolutionary change matches predictions.
