Raclette

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A half-wheel of raclette cheese melting by a fire, being scraped onto potatoes]

Raclette is both a Swiss cow's-milk cheese and the dish made from it: the cut face of a half-wheel is held to a fire or heat lamp until it bubbles, then scraped — racler, to scrape, in French — in a molten sheet over boiled potatoes, cornichons, pickled onions and cured meats. It belongs to the canton of Valais, where herdsmen melting cheese by the fire were described in writing as early as the 16th century (the physician Conrad Gessner mentioned the practice in 1574). The cheese itself holds protected status as Raclette du Valais AOP.

Fire and table

The traditional service is slow theatre: one scrape at a time, eaten while the next face melts, with Fendant wine and no hurry. The 1970s tabletop raclette grill — a small electric grill under which individual pans of cheese melt while meats and vegetables cook on top — turned the Alpine ritual into Europe's dinner-party format, especially in France and Germany, where raclette is now a Christmas and New Year institution. Markets across the Alps serve raclette from great wheels onto bread through the winter.

Fondue's sibling

Raclette and fondue share a homeland, a season and a philosophy (melted cheese, starch, pickles, wine, company) but differ in texture and tempo: fondue is a communal pot, raclette a series of individual scrapings — drier, browner, with the prized crisped edge (the religieuse, "the nun", in fondue's pot, has its raclette analogues). Both turned mountain preservation food — hard cheese, potatoes, pickles — into celebration.
