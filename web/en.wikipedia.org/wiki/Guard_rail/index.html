Guard rail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guard rail or guardrail, known in Britain as a crash barrier and in Ireland and some other regions as a safety barrier, is a system designed to keep people or vehicles from straying into dangerous or off-limits areas. In traffic engineering, guard rails are installed along roads — most often on medians, bridges and sharp curves — to prevent errant vehicles from leaving the roadway, striking fixed objects, or crossing into opposing traffic.

Design

The most common highway guard rail is the galvanized steel W-beam, a corrugated strip of steel mounted on wooden or steel posts with offset blocks. When struck, the rail deforms and absorbs energy while redirecting the vehicle along the barrier rather than stopping it abruptly. Other designs include the three-rib thrie-beam, concrete Jersey barriers, and tensioned cable barriers (wire rope), which are cheaper and gentler on impacting vehicles but require more deflection space.

Terminals and safety

The exposed end of a guard rail, called the terminal, can itself be a hazard: early "spear" ends could impale striking vehicles. Modern energy-absorbing terminals crumple or extrude the rail to dissipate impact forces. Standards such as the American AASHTO Manual for Assessing Safety Hardware (MASH) and the European EN 1317 govern crash-test performance and containment levels.

Other uses

The term also covers industrial handrails and barriers that protect pedestrians from machinery and falls, and the safety rails fitted to elevated work platforms and scaffolding.
