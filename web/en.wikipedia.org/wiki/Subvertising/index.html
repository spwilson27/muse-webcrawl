Subvertising

Subvertising is a portmanteau of subvert and advertising. It refers to the practice of making spoofs or parodies of corporate and political advertisements in order to criticize the advertiser, consumerism, or advertising itself. The resulting works — altered billboards, fake magazine ads, remixed commercials, and memes — are a central tactic of culture jamming.

History and practice

Subvertising has roots in the détournement of the Situationist International and in earlier billboard-alteration art. Groups such as the Billboard Liberation Front (founded in San Francisco in 1977) altered outdoor advertisements to reverse their messages, while the Canadian magazine Adbusters popularized glossy "subvertisements" parodying major brands and campaigns. Common targets include tobacco, alcohol, fast food, automobiles, and fashion advertising, as well as political campaign material. Tactics range from small sticker alterations and graffiti to fully reproduced counterfeit ads designed to pass at a glance for the originals.

Digital era

The internet and social media greatly expanded subvertising: parody ads and remixed brand imagery now circulate as memes, and movements such as the UK's Brandalism collective have coordinated large-scale billboard takeovers during events like international climate conferences. Critics and media scholars debate its effectiveness — whether parody ads genuinely shift opinion or mainly preach to the converted — and note that brands sometimes co-opt the style for self-deprecating marketing. Legally, subvertising occupies contested ground between trademark and property law (altering others' billboards is typically unlawful) and free-expression protections for parody.
