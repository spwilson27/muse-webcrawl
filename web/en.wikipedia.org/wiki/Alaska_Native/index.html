Alaska Natives (also known as Native Alaskans) are the Indigenous peoples of Alaska, United States. They comprise a diversity of cultures and language groups, including the Iñupiat of the Arctic coast, the Yup'ik and Cup'ik of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta, the Unangax̂ (Aleut) and Sugpiaq (Alutiiq) of the Aleutian chain and south-central coast, the Athabascan peoples of the interior, and the Tlingit, Haida, Eyak and Tsimshian peoples of the southeastern panhandle.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited Alaska for at least 12,000 years. Contact with Russian fur traders from the eighteenth century, followed by American purchase of Alaska in 1867, brought profound disruption, including epidemics and the suppression of languages through boarding-school policies.

Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act

The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) of 1971 settled aboriginal land claims, conveying about 44 million acres of land and nearly one billion dollars to thirteen Alaska Native regional corporations and more than two hundred village corporations. ANCSA extinguished aboriginal title but made Alaska Natives shareholders rather than members of reservation-based tribes; separately, more than 200 federally recognised tribal governments exist in Alaska, including the Central Council of the Tlingit and Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska.

Culture

Subsistence hunting, fishing and gathering remain central to many rural communities. The annual Alaska Federation of Natives convention is the largest gathering of Indigenous peoples in the state, addressing land, subsistence, health and language revitalisation.
