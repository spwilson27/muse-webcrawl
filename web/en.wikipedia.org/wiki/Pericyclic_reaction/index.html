Pericyclic reaction

A pericyclic reaction is a type of organic reaction in which the transition state has a cyclic geometry and the reaction proceeds in a single concerted step through a cyclic array of atoms, with bonds breaking and forming simultaneously. Because no ionic or radical intermediates are involved, pericyclic reactions are typically insensitive to solvent polarity and proceed with characteristic stereochemistry.

Classes

The main classes of pericyclic reactions are:

  • Cycloadditions — two or more π systems combine to form a ring, as in the Diels–Alder reaction.
  • Electrocyclic reactions — a conjugated π system closes to form a ring with one fewer π bond and one new σ bond, or the reverse ring opening.
  • Sigmatropic rearrangements — a σ bond migrates across a π system, as in the Cope and Claisen rearrangements.
  • Group transfer reactions — a group is transferred between molecules through a cyclic transition state, such as the ene reaction.
  • Cheletropic reactions — two σ bonds are made or broken at the same atom.

Woodward–Hoffmann rules

The feasibility and stereochemical course of pericyclic reactions are governed by the conservation of orbital symmetry, formalised in the 1960s by Robert Burns Woodward and Roald Hoffmann. The Woodward–Hoffmann rules predict, for example, whether an electrocyclic reaction will proceed by conrotatory or disrotatory motion under thermal or photochemical conditions, based on the number of π electrons involved: systems with 4n π electrons react conrotatorily on heating, while systems with 4n+2 π electrons react disrotatorily, with the opposite behaviour under light. Hoffmann shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.
