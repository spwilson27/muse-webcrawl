Aestivation (Latin: aestīvāre, "to pass the summer") is a state of animal dormancy during hot or dry periods, analogous to hibernation in winter. Animals that aestivate reduce their metabolic rate, movement, and physiological activity to conserve energy and water until conditions improve. The term is also used in botany for the arrangement of sepals and petals in a flower bud.

Animal aestivation

Many species aestivate. African and South American lungfish burrow into mud and secrete a mucous cocoon, reducing metabolism to a fraction of normal and subsisting for months or years. Desert frogs and toads burrow underground; snails retreat into shells sealed with an epiphragm. Some tortoises and lizards aestivate during extreme heat, and certain mosquitoes survive the dry season as desiccated eggs. Aestivating animals often tolerate dehydration and elevated urea or other waste products that would be lethal under normal conditions.

Physiology

Aestivation involves profound metabolic depression — in some species metabolism falls to less than 10% of resting rate — along with suppression of digestion, reduced heart rate, and conservation of body water through impermeable cocoons, burrowing, or behavioural retreat. Endogenous antioxidant defences protect tissues against oxidative stress during the prolonged fast.

Aestivation in botany

In plant morphology, aestivation describes the positioning of sepals and petals within a flower bud before it opens — for example, valvate, imbricate, or twisted arrangements. It is distinct from the dormancy sense and belongs to the vocabulary of floral description.
