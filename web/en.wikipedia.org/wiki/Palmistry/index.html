Palmistry

Palmistry, also known as chiromancy or palm reading, is the practice of fortune-telling through the study of the palm: the shape of the hand and fingers and the lines and mounts of the palm are interpreted as revealing character and predicting future events. Palmistry has roots in Indian astrology and Romani fortune-telling and has spread worldwide, but it is regarded by the scientific community as a pseudoscience, with no empirical evidence that palm features predict life outcomes.

The lines

Readers traditionally emphasise three major lines. The heart line, running under the fingers, is said to govern emotional life; the head line, crossing the middle of the palm, intellect and thought; and the life line, curving around the base of the thumb, vitality and major life changes — contrary to popular belief, it is not read as the literal length of one's life. Seven raised areas, the mounts, are named for planets and associated with corresponding traits.

History

Palmistry-like practices appear in ancient India, China, and Egypt, and were studied in classical Greece and Rome. Condemned at times by the Church and banned in England under vagrancy laws, the practice was revived as an occult science in the nineteenth century by figures such as Casimir d'Arpentigny, William John Warner ("Cheiro"), and later popularised by hand-analysis writers.

Criticism

Sceptics note that palm readings rely on cold reading and the Barnum effect, and controlled studies have found no reliable correlation between palm features and personality or lifespan. Dermatoglyphics, the scientific study of fingerprints and palm prints, is a separate field with recognised medical associations.
