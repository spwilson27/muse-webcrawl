Zita of Bourbon-Parma (Zita Maria delle Grazie Adelgonda Micaela Raffaela Gabriella Giuseppina Antonia Luisa Agnese; 9 May 1892 – 14 March 1989) was the wife of Charles I, the last monarch of Austria-Hungary. As such, she was the last Empress of Austria, Queen of Hungary, and Queen of Bohemia, reigning with her husband from 1916 until the dissolution of the empire in 1918.

Early life and marriage

Zita was the seventeenth child of Robert I, the deposed Duke of Parma, and his second wife, Infanta Maria Antónia of Portugal. She grew up in Italy and Austria and was educated by governesses and at convent schools. In 1909 she met Archduke Charles of Austria, a great-nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph I; they were married on 21 October 1911 at Schwarzau Castle. Following the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914, Charles became heir presumptive, and on Franz Joseph's death in November 1916 he succeeded as Emperor Charles I and King Charles IV of Hungary, with Zita as empress and queen.

Empress and exile

During the First World War Zita was politically active; her brothers' service in opposing armies fuelled suspicion, and in 1917 her brother Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma acted as an intermediary in Charles's secret peace feelers with France — the "Sixtus Affair", whose exposure in 1918 damaged the couple's standing. After the collapse of the monarchy in November 1918 the family went into exile in Switzerland. Charles made two unsuccessful attempts to regain the Hungarian throne in 1921 and was then exiled to Madeira, where he died of pneumonia on 1 April 1922, leaving Zita a widow at twenty-nine with seven children and an eighth on the way.

Later life

Zita never remarried, wearing mourning for the rest of her life. She devoted herself to raising her children — her eldest son, Otto von Habsburg, became head of the House of Habsburg — and to charitable and dynastic causes. After stays in Spain and Belgium, the family fled the Second World War to the United States and Canada, where Zita raised funds for war relief. She returned to Europe in 1952, settling eventually in Switzerland. In 1982, after sixty-three years of exile, she was permitted to visit Austria. She died in 1989 at Zizers, aged 96, and was buried in the Imperial Crypt (Kapuzinergruft) in Vienna; her heart was placed with her husband's at Muri Abbey in Switzerland.

Veneration

In 2009 the cause for her beatification was opened by the Catholic Church, and she was declared a Servant of God; her husband Charles had been beatified in 2004.
