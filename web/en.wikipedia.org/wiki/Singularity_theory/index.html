Singularity theory

In mathematics, singularity theory studies spaces that fail to be well-behaved — in particular, the points at which a manifold, function, or algebraic variety fails to be smooth or otherwise "generic". Loosely, it studies the geometry of the "non-generic" cases and how smooth objects degenerate. The subject grew out of the study of critical points of functions by Marston Morse and Hassler Whitney, and was transformed in the 1960s and 1970s by René Thom's catastrophe theory and Vladimir Arnold's classification work.

Basic ideas

A singularity of a smooth function occurs at a critical point where its derivative vanishes. The central problem is classification: to list, up to smooth change of coordinates, all the ways a function or map can degenerate near a point, and to describe how singularities appear and disappear in families (unfoldings and bifurcations). Simple singularities classified by Arnold correspond to the ADE Dynkin diagrams — the Ak, Dk, E6, E7, E8 series — the same diagrams that classify simple Lie algebras, a connection that has proved remarkably fruitful.

Branches and applications

Singularity theory encompasses the differential topology of singular maps (Whitney's work on maps of the plane), the algebraic geometry of singular varieties, and the resolution of singularities pioneered by Heisuke Hironaka. Applications include optics, where singularities of wavefronts produce caustics such as the bright cusp in a coffee cup; robotics and computer vision; bifurcation theory in dynamical systems; and, in theoretical physics, string theory, where ADE singularities are tied to gauge symmetries.

Notable contributors

Key figures include Hassler Whitney, René Thom, John Milnor (whose study of isolated hypersurface singularities and the "Milnor number" is foundational), Vladimir Arnold, Christopher Zeeman, and Egbert Brieskorn.
