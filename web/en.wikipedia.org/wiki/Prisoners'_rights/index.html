Prisoners' rights

The rights of prisoners are the legal and human rights retained by people who are incarcerated. Although imprisonment necessarily restricts liberty, prisoners in most legal systems retain protections against cruel or degrading treatment, rights to adequate food, medical care and sanitation, freedom of religion and expression within limits, and access to courts and legal counsel.

International standards

The United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners, revised in 2015 as the Nelson Mandela Rules, set out baseline standards for prison conditions, healthcare, discipline and contact with the outside world. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights prohibits torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, and the Convention against Torture reinforces that ban.

United States

In the United States, the Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment; courts have held that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs (Estelle v. Gamble, 1976) and to substantial risks of harm (Farmer v. Brennan, 1994) violate it. The First Amendment protects religious exercise and some speech, the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees due process in disciplinary proceedings (Wolff v. McDonnell, 1974), and statutes such as the Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1995 regulate prisoners' civil lawsuits. Litigation over overcrowding led to the Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Plata (2011) upholding a population-reduction order for California prisons.

Topics

Debated issues include solitary confinement, voting rights for prisoners and former prisoners, conjugal visits, education and rehabilitation programmes, healthcare standards, and oversight mechanisms such as prison inspectorates and ombudsmen.
