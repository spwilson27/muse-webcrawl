Radon–Nikodym theorem

In mathematics, the Radon–Nikodym theorem is a fundamental result in measure theory that expresses one measure as an integral with respect to another, provided the first is absolutely continuous with respect to the second. The integrating function is called the Radon–Nikodym derivative, and it generalises the idea of a probability density or a derivative to arbitrary measures. The theorem is named after Johann Radon, who proved a special case in 1913, and Otto Nikodym, who proved the general case in 1930.

Statement

Let (X, Σ) be a measurable space, and let μ and ν be σ-finite measures on it. If ν is absolutely continuous with respect to μ (written ν ≪ μ, meaning μ(A) = 0 implies ν(A) = 0), then there exists a measurable function f : X → [0, ∞) such that for every measurable set A,

ν(A) = ∫A f dμ.

The function f is unique up to equality μ-almost everywhere and is denoted dν/dμ, the Radon–Nikodym derivative of ν with respect to μ.

Applications

The theorem underlies much of modern probability and analysis:

  • Probability densities: a continuous random variable's distribution is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, and its probability density function is precisely the Radon–Nikodym derivative.
  • Conditional expectation: the standard construction of conditional expectation in probability theory is an application of the theorem.
  • Likelihood ratios: in statistics and in the theory of stochastic processes (e.g. Girsanov's theorem), changes of measure are expressed through Radon–Nikodym derivatives.
  • Economics and finance: state-price densities and risk-neutral pricing use the derivative to pass between physical and risk-neutral measures.

Properties

The derivative behaves like an ordinary derivative in several respects: a chain rule holds for successive changes of measure, and when a measure is decomposed via the Lebesgue decomposition theorem into absolutely continuous and singular parts, the Radon–Nikodym theorem describes the absolutely continuous part. A consequence is the characterisation of the dual of L1 spaces as L∞.
