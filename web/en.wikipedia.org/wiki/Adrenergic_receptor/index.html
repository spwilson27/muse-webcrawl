Adrenergic receptor

TypeG protein-coupled receptors
LigandsAdrenaline, noradrenaline
FamiliesAlpha (α1, α2) and beta (β1, β2, β3)

The adrenergic receptors are a class of G protein-coupled receptors that are targets of the catecholamines adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine). They mediate the actions of the sympathetic nervous system throughout the body and are among the most important drug targets in medicine — beta blockers, widely prescribed for cardiovascular disease, act on them.

Classification

Raymond Ahlquist proposed in 1948 that catecholamine effects were mediated by two receptor types, alpha and beta. Subsequent work divided them into subtypes: α1 (coupled to Gq, mediating vasoconstriction and smooth-muscle contraction), α2 (Gi, including presynaptic autoreceptors that inhibit noradrenaline release), β1 (Gs, mainly cardiac — increasing heart rate and contractility), β2 (Gs, mediating bronchodilation and vasodilation), and β3 (involved in lipolysis and bladder relaxation).

Clinical significance

Agonists of β2 receptors (e.g., salbutamol) treat asthma; β1 blockers (e.g., metoprolol) treat hypertension, heart failure, and arrhythmias; α1 blockers (e.g., doxazosin) treat hypertension and benign prostatic hyperplasia; and α2 agonists such as clonidine lower sympathetic outflow. The crystal structure of the β2 receptor, solved in 2007, was a landmark in structural biology and contributed to the 2012 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Robert Lefkowitz and Brian Kobilka.
