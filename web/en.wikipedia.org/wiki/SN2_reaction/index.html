SN2 reaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The SN2 reaction (substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular) is a type of reaction mechanism common in organic chemistry. In this mechanism, one bond is broken and one bond is formed in a concerted step: a nucleophile attacks an electrophilic carbon from the side opposite to the leaving group ("backside attack"), while the leaving group departs simultaneously, passing through a single transition state in which the carbon is partially bonded to both groups.

Kinetics and stereochemistry

The reaction rate depends on the concentrations of both the substrate and the nucleophile, giving second-order (bimolecular) kinetics: rate = k[RX][Nu−]. Because the nucleophile approaches from the back side, the configuration at the carbon is inverted in the product — the so-called Walden inversion. The carbon passes through a planar, sp2-like transition state with five partially bonded groups.

Factors affecting the reaction

SN2 reactions are fastest with methyl and primary substrates and are strongly hindered by steric bulk, so secondary substrates react more slowly and tertiary substrates generally do not react by this pathway, favouring SN1 or elimination instead. Good leaving groups (such as iodide, bromide and tosylate), strong nucleophiles and polar aprotic solvents — which solvate cations but leave anions reactive — accelerate the reaction. A classic example is the Finkelstein reaction.
