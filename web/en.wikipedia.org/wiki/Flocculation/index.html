Flocculation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In colloid and surface chemistry, flocculation is the process by which fine particulates are caused to clump together into a floc. The flocs may then float to the top of the liquid (creaming), settle to the bottom (sedimentation), or be readily filtered from the liquid. Flocculation differs from precipitation in that, prior to flocculation, colloids are merely suspended, under the form of a stable dispersion, in a liquid and are not truly dissolved in solution.

Causes and mechanisms

Colloidal particles typically carry a surface charge that keeps them apart through electrostatic repulsion, described by DLVO theory. Flocculation can be induced by reducing that repulsion — for example by adding electrolytes that compress the electrical double layer — or by adding polymers that adsorb onto several particles at once and bridge them together. In depletion flocculation, non-adsorbing polymers create an osmotic pressure imbalance that pushes particles together.

Applications

Flocculation is a central step in water treatment and wastewater treatment, where coagulants such as aluminium sulfate (alum) and ferric chloride are added so that suspended solids form flocs that can be settled or filtered out. It is also used in papermaking, mining and mineral processing, brewing (where yeast flocculation determines clarity and fermentation behaviour), cheese production, and soil science, where clay flocculation influences soil structure and erosion.

Deflocculation

The reverse process, deflocculation or peptization, redisperses flocculated particles into a stable colloid. Deflocculants such as sodium silicate and sodium hexametaphosphate are used in ceramics to keep clay slips fluid at high solids content.
