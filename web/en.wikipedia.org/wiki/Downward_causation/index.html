Downward causation

Downward causation is a concept in philosophy of science and philosophy of mind according to which higher-level entities, properties or processes can causally influence lower-level ones. It is invoked in debates about emergence, reductionism and mental causation: for example, the claim that an organism's organisation constrains the behaviour of its cells, or that a person's beliefs and intentions cause the physical movements of their body.

Forms

The psychologist Donald T. Campbell introduced the term in 1974, discussing how the structure of a termite's jaw — a product of higher-level evolutionary processes — constrains the physical forces acting on the molecules within it. Later authors distinguish strong downward causation, in which a higher-level property exerts causal power not reducible to its parts, from weak or medium forms in which higher levels act as boundary conditions or constraints on lower-level dynamics.

Criticism

Critics, notably Jaegwon Kim, argue that downward causation threatens the causal closure of the physical world: if every physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, a separate higher-level cause would lead to systematic causal overdetermination. Kim's causal exclusion argument presses emergentists and non-reductive physicalists to explain how higher-level causes can be genuinely efficacious without violating physical completeness. Defenders respond with interventionist and constraint-based accounts of causation, in which higher-level variables are the proportionally appropriate causes of system behaviour.
