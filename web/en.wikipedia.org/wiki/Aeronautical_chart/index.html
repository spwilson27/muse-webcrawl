Aeronautical chart

Aeronautical chart
PurposeAir navigation aid for pilots

An aeronautical chart is a map designed to assist in the navigation of aircraft, much as nautical charts do for watercraft, or a roadmap does for drivers. Using these charts and other tools, pilots are able to determine their position, safe altitude, best route to a destination, navigation aids along the way, alternative landing areas in case of an in-flight emergency, and other useful information such as radio frequencies and airspace boundaries.

There are charts for all land masses on Earth, and long-distance charts for trans-oceanic travel. Specific charts are used for each phase of a flight and may vary from a map of a particular airport facility to an overview of the instrument routes covering a whole continent (e.g., global navigation charts), and many types in between.

Types

Visual flight charts are classified according to their scale. Sectional charts, at a scale of 1:500,000, are the most commonly used charts for visual navigation in the United States. Terminal area charts (1:250,000) provide greater detail around busy Class B airspace. The World Aeronautical Chart (WAC) has a scale of 1:1,000,000 and covers a larger area with less detail.

Instrument flight charts include low- and high-altitude en route charts, standard instrument departures (SIDs), standard terminal arrivals (STARs), and instrument approach procedure charts, which depict the precise courses, altitudes, and minimums for each phase of flight under instrument flight rules.

Conventions

Aeronautical charts show airspace classes, controlled and special-use airspace, airports with their runway layouts and elevations, radio navigation aids such as VOR stations, terrain elevation tints, obstacles, and isogonic lines showing magnetic variation. In the United States, charts are produced by the Federal Aviation Administration's Aeronautical Information Services and are revised on a regular cycle; using an expired chart for navigation is not permitted.

Digital charts

Electronic flight bag (EFB) applications on tablet computers have largely replaced paper charts for many operators, providing moving-map displays with own-ship position from GPS. Nevertheless, the underlying cartographic data and symbology follow the same conventions established for printed charts, and international standards are coordinated by the International Civil Aviation Organization.
