United Duchies of Jülich-Cleves-Berg

The United Duchies of Jülich-Cleves-Berg is a name for a group of territories in the Holy Roman Empire held together in personal union by their dukes during the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. The core lands were the duchies of Jülich, Cleves and Berg, together with the counties of Mark and Ravensberg and other lordships. They formed a strategically important bloc along the Lower Rhine and in Westphalia.

Formation

The combination began when John III, Duke of Cleves, inherited Jülich and Berg in 1521 through his marriage to Maria of Jülich-Berg. The resulting state joined territories on both sides of the Rhine and made their ruler one of the most significant princes in northwestern Germany. The lands were not merged into a single legal state: each retained its estates, laws and administrative traditions, but they were governed by the same duke.

Religious and political position

The duchy's rulers pursued comparatively moderate policies during the Reformation. William, Duke of Jülich-Cleves-Berg, known as William the Rich, sought a middle course between militant Catholic and Protestant positions and resisted imperial pressure. The territories remained religiously mixed. Their position between the Spanish Netherlands, the Dutch Republic and German princes made them an object of intense diplomatic interest.

Succession crisis

When the last duke, John William, died childless in 1609, rival claims led to the War of the Jülich Succession. Brandenburg and Palatinate-Neuburg occupied and disputed the lands. The Treaty of Xanten in 1614 provisionally divided the inheritance: Brandenburg received Cleves, Mark and Ravensberg, while Neuburg received Jülich and Berg. This partition ended the united ducal complex and shaped political geography in the Rhineland and Westphalia for centuries.
