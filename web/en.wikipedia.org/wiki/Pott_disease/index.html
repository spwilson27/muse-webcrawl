Pott disease

Tuberculous spondylitis causing vertebral collapse and kyphosis.

Pott disease, also known as tuberculous spondylitis, is a form of tuberculosis that affects the spine. It is named after the English surgeon Percivall Pott, who described it in 1779. Infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis spreads, usually via the bloodstream from a pulmonary focus, to the vertebral bodies, where it destroys bone and intervertebral discs.

Signs and symptoms

The disease develops slowly, with back pain and stiffness, sometimes accompanied by fever, night sweats and weight loss. Destruction and collapse of the vertebral bodies causes a sharp angular deformity of the spine called a gibbus, a form of kyphosis. A "cold" abscess may form in the paravertebral tissues or track along the psoas muscle into the groin. The most serious complication is compression of the spinal cord, producing weakness or paralysis of the legs — Pott's paraplegia. The lower thoracic and upper lumbar vertebrae are most commonly affected.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on imaging (plain radiographs, CT and MRI), microbiological confirmation from biopsy specimens, and supporting laboratory findings. Treatment is primarily medical, with a prolonged course of the standard antituberculous drugs — typically isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol followed by continuation therapy — lasting at least six to nine months. Surgery is reserved for spinal instability, severe or progressing neurological deficit, large abscesses or failure of medical therapy.

Epidemiology and history

Spinal tuberculosis accounts for a small proportion of all tuberculosis cases but is the commonest form of skeletal tuberculosis; its incidence follows that of TB generally and it remains frequent in regions with high TB and HIV prevalence. Evidence of the disease has been found in ancient Egyptian mummies and pre-Columbian remains, indicating its antiquity.
