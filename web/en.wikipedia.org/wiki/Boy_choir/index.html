VoicesBoy sopranos/trebles and altos, often with adult tenors and basses

A boy choir is a choir primarily made up of choirboys who have yet to begin puberty or are in the early to middle stages of puberty and so retain their more highly pitched childhood voice type. Members are sometimes called boy sopranos or trebles. Boy choirs are most often associated with cathedral and collegiate traditions in Europe, where choir schools train boys from about age seven until their voices change.

History

Boys sang in Christian worship from late antiquity, and the medieval schola cantorum systematised their training. Cathedral and chapel choirs across Europe — the papal choir in Rome, the English Chapel Royal and cathedral choirs, and the court choirs of the German-speaking lands — educated boy choristers who supplied the treble lines, since women did not sing in church choirs. The Thomanerchor of Leipzig, founded in 1212 and later directed by J. S. Bach, and the Vienna Boys' Choir, founded in 1498, are among the oldest surviving institutions.

Training and repertoire

Choristers typically receive a full general education alongside daily vocal training, sight-singing and rehearsals, performing at services and concerts throughout the year. The repertoire spans Gregorian chant and Renaissance polyphony through the masterworks of Bach, Handel, Mozart and Britten — whose A Ceremony of Carols was written for treble voices — to contemporary commissions. Famous individual boy sopranos, such as Ernest Lough, whose 1927 recording of "Hear My Prayer" sold millions of copies, have occasionally become popular recording artists.

Modern developments

Since the late 20th century many formerly all-male cathedral choirs have founded parallel girls' choirs or admitted girls, and independent mixed children's choirs have proliferated. Boys' choirs continue to tour internationally and are a fixture of major festivals and state occasions.
