Abacá (/ɑːbəˈkɑː/ ah-bə-KAH; Filipino: abaka), binomial name Musa textilis, is a species of banana native to the Philippines, grown as a commercial crop in the Philippines, Ecuador and Costa Rica. The plant, also known as Manila hemp, has great economic importance, being harvested for its fibre, which was once used chiefly for maritime cordage and is today used in a range of specialty papers and other products. Despite its common name, it is not a true hemp but a close relative of the banana.

The plant

The abacá plant is a perennial in the banana family that grows to around 4–6 metres tall, forming clumps of fleshy stalks (pseudostems) made of overlapping leaf sheaths. The fibre is extracted from these sheaths: after harvest, the sheaths are stripped (traditionally by hand with a stripping knife, or by machine decortication) to yield long, lustrous fibres of a light buff colour that are then dried in the sun.

Properties and uses

Abacá fibre is among the strongest natural fibres: it is notably resistant to salt water, has high tensile strength and floats on water, which made it the premier material for ships' rigging, fishing nets and hawsers in the age of sail. With the rise of synthetic fibres its cordage market shrank, but abacá pulp is now prized for specialty papers requiring strength and porosity — tea bags, coffee filters, sausage casings, vacuum bags and currency paper (Japanese yen notes have traditionally incorporated abacá). The fibre is also woven into fabrics and handicrafts, used in composites for the automotive industry, and forms the basis of traditional Philippine textiles.

Production

The Philippines supplies the great majority of the world's abacá, with the Bicol region and Eastern Visayas as major producing areas; the island province of Catanduanes is a particularly famous growing region. Abacá was a major Philippine export during the American colonial period, when the United States cultivated it strategically as the main source of naval cordage fibre. Production is threatened by bunchy top virus and other diseases, and by competition from synthetics, but demand for biodegradable fibres has renewed interest in the crop.
