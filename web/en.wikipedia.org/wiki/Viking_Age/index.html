Viking Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Viking longship with striped sail and dragon-headed prow, in a reconstruction]

The Viking Age (c. 793–1066) was the period when Norsemen known as Vikings raided, traded and settled across wide areas of Europe, Asia and the North Atlantic. Traditionally opened by the raid on Lindisfarne monastery in 793 and closed by the Norwegian defeat at Stamford Bridge in 1066, the age saw Scandinavians found Dublin and York, settle Iceland and Greenland, reach North America (Vinland), serve as the Varangian Guard in Constantinople, and plant the Rus' states in eastern Europe. Behind the raids lay superb ships, population pressure, political centralisation at home and rich, weakly defended coasts abroad.

Raids and settlement

From hit-and-run attacks, Vikings moved to wintering, conquest and settlement: the Great Heathen Army (865) conquered half of Anglo-Saxon England (the Danelaw); Rollo's grant in 911 founded Normandy ('land of the Northmen'); Iceland was settled from c. 870 and Greenland from c. 985; Leif Erikson reached Newfoundland around 1000, five centuries before Columbus. In the east, Swedish Vikings (Rus') traded and fought along the Volga and Dnieper to Byzantium and the Caspian.

End of the age

Christianisation and the consolidation of the Scandinavian kingdoms redirected Viking energies into crusades and state warfare. Harald Hardrada's failed invasion of England in 1066 — weeks before Hastings — is the conventional endpoint. The Vikings' legacy includes place-names across Britain and Ireland, the Icelandic sagas, the Norman transformation of England, and a genetic and commercial footprint from Newfoundland to Baghdad.
