Calypso is a moon of Saturn. It was discovered in 1980, from ground-based observations, by Dan Pascu, P. Kenneth Seidelmann, William Baum and Douglas Currie, and given the provisional designation S/1980 S 25. It is named after the nymph Calypso of Greek mythology.

Orbit

Calypso is a Trojan moon of the larger moon Tethys: it orbits Saturn at the same distance as Tethys, trailing about 60 degrees behind it at the trailing Lagrange point (L5) of the Saturn–Tethys system. The moon Telesto occupies the corresponding leading point (L4). Calypso's orbit has a semi-major axis of about 294,700 km.

Physical characteristics

Like many small Saturnian moons, Calypso is irregularly shaped, roughly 30 km long, and thought to be a porous rubble pile of water ice. Images from the Cassini spacecraft show an unusually smooth surface for its size, apparently resurfaced by fine icy particles — likely from Saturn's E ring — that fill in and soften craters, giving the moon a sleek, flowing appearance. Its surface is among the most reflective (highest albedo) in the Solar System, consistent with fresh ice.
