Ozone

Ozone (O₃) is a pale blue, pungent allotrope of oxygen: three oxygen atoms in a bent molecule, a far stronger oxidising agent than O₂. It is continuously formed in the stratosphere by ultraviolet light splitting oxygen, creating the ozone layer that shields life from UV radiation.

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) catalytically destroyed stratospheric ozone, opening the Antarctic 'ozone hole' each spring; the Montreal Protocol (1987) phased CFCs out and the layer is slowly recovering. At ground level, ozone is a harmful pollutant formed from exhaust gases in sunlight, damaging lungs and crops — 'good up high, bad nearby'.

Uses

Ozone disinfects drinking water and swimming pools and bleaches fabrics, generated on-site by corona discharge because it is too unstable to store. Its smell after thunderstorms and near photocopiers is characteristic.

Discovery

Christian Schönbein recognised ozone in 1840 from the smell around electrolysis and lightning, naming it from the Greek ozein, to smell. Its formula O₃ was established by Soret in the 1860s.
