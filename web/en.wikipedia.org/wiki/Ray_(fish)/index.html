Rays are cartilaginous fishes of the superorder Batoidea, closely related to sharks within the subclass Elasmobranchii. They are characterised by greatly enlarged pectoral fins fused to the head, forming a flattened disc, and by gill slits located on the underside of the body (ventrally) rather than on the sides as in sharks. Most rays live on or near the seabed (they are demersal), though the mantas and some eagle rays are open-water swimmers.

Groups

  • Stingrays (Myliobatiformes) — whip-like tails bearing one or more venomous, serrated stings used in defence.
  • Skates (Rajiformes) — fleshy tails without stings; the largest family of rays, laying leathery egg cases ("mermaid's purses").
  • Electric rays (Torpediniformes) — capable of generating electric shocks (up to about 220 volts in the Atlantic torpedo) from specialised organs, used to stun prey and deter predators.
  • Sawfishes (Pristiformes) — shark-like rays with a long, toothed rostrum; all species are critically endangered.
  • Manta and devil rays — giant filter feeders; the giant oceanic manta ray reaches disc widths of up to 7 metres.

Biology

Rays breathe by drawing water in through large spiracles behind the eyes and expelling it through the ventral gill slits, an adaptation to bottom living that keeps sediment out of the gills. Most feed on molluscs, crustaceans and small fish, crushing hard prey with pavement-like teeth; mantas and mobulas filter plankton. Like other elasmobranchs, rays sense prey with electroreception (the ampullae of Lorenzini). Reproduction is mostly internal fertilisation with live birth (ovoviviparity or viviparity), except in skates, which lay eggs.

Conservation

Many rays are threatened by overfishing — targeted fisheries for meat and gill plates (used in traditional medicine) and bycatch in trawls — and by their slow growth and low fecundity. Sawfishes, wedgefishes and giant guitarfishes are among the most endangered marine fishes; manta rays are listed on CITES Appendix II.
