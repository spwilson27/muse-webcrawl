Motor skill

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A motor skill is a learned ability to cause a predetermined movement outcome with maximum certainty. Learning a motor skill involves refining movements through practice so that they become smoother, more accurate, and eventually more automatic. Motor skills are usually divided into gross motor skills, which use large muscle groups and the whole body (walking, running, jumping, swimming), and fine motor skills, which involve small, precise movements of the hands and fingers (writing, threading a needle, playing a musical instrument).

Development

Motor development in infancy follows a broadly predictable sequence: head control, rolling, sitting, crawling, standing, and walking, each new skill building on earlier ones. The sequence is driven by maturation of the nervous system together with practice and environmental opportunity. Fine motor milestones — the pincer grasp, scribbling, using utensils, tying shoelaces — continue to refine through childhood. Significant delays in motor milestones can be an early sign of developmental disorders such as developmental coordination disorder or cerebral palsy.

Stages of learning

Fitts and Posner's classic model describes three stages of motor learning:

  • Cognitive stage — the learner consciously thinks through each step; performance is slow, effortful, and inconsistent.
  • Associative stage — movements are refined through feedback; errors decrease and the learner begins to detect and correct them.
  • Autonomous stage — the skill becomes automatic, requiring little conscious attention, freeing the performer to attend to strategy or style.

Performance improvements typically follow a power law of practice: rapid early gains that taper off, so that expert performance requires large amounts of deliberate practice. Skills are retained well — riding a bicycle is the proverbial example — but degrade without use and must be re-tuned after injury or long breaks.

Classification

Motor skills can also be classified as discrete (a single action with a clear beginning and end, such as a golf swing), serial (a sequence of discrete actions, such as a gymnastics routine), or continuous (repetitive movements without a fixed end, such as running). They are further described as open skills, performed in changing environments (returning a tennis serve), or closed skills, performed in stable conditions (a free throw in basketball).

Neural basis

Motor control involves the motor cortex, the basal ganglia, and the cerebellum, with the cerebellum fine-tuning movement through error correction and the basal ganglia involved in selecting and automating learned sequences. As skills become automatic, control shifts from attention-demanding cortical circuits toward these subcortical systems.
