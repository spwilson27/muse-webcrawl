Cortical spreading depression

Cortical spreading depression (CSD), also called spreading depolarization, is a wave of profound neuronal and glial depolarization that propagates slowly across the cerebral cortex at roughly 2–5 millimetres per minute, followed by a period of suppressed electrical activity. It is accompanied by massive redistribution of ions — potassium released from cells, sodium and calcium entering — and by changes in cerebral blood flow, typically a brief increase followed by prolonged reduction (oligemia).

Discovery

The phenomenon was first described in 1944 by the Brazilian neurophysiologist Aristides Leão, who observed a spreading depression of spontaneous electrical activity in the rabbit cortex while studying experimental epilepsy. Leão himself suggested a connection to migraine.

Role in migraine

CSD is widely regarded as the physiological basis of the migraine aura. The speed of the wave matches the march of visual symptoms across the visual field described by patients, and functional imaging during aura shows waves of altered blood flow spreading over the occipital cortex at a similar rate. Waves of depolarization are thought to activate trigeminal pain pathways, linking aura to the subsequent headache. Mutations causing familial hemiplegic migraine increase susceptibility to CSD in animal models.

In brain injury

Spreading depolarizations also occur in injured human brain — after stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury — where they can enlarge lesions by imposing severe metabolic demands on tissue whose blood supply is already compromised. Monitoring them with electrocorticography has become a research focus in neurocritical care (for example the COSBID consortium). Drugs such as NMDA receptor antagonists (e.g., ketamine) can block the waves in experimental settings.
