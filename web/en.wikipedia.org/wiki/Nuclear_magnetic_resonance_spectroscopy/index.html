Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, most commonly known as NMR spectroscopy or magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), is a spectroscopic technique based on the re-orientation of atomic nuclei with non-zero nuclear spin in an external magnetic field. It is one of the principal methods used to obtain physical, chemical, electronic, and structural information about molecules, and is a cornerstone of modern organic chemistry, biochemistry, and materials science.

Principle

Certain atomic nuclei possess spin and a magnetic moment. In a strong magnetic field B0, the spin states split in energy (the Zeeman effect), and the nuclei can absorb radiofrequency radiation at their characteristic Larmor frequency. The exact resonance frequency depends on the local electronic environment — the chemical shift, reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to a standard such as tetramethylsilane — while spin–spin coupling splits signals into multiplets that reveal neighbouring nuclei.

Techniques

One-dimensional 1H and 13C spectra are routine for structure determination. Two-dimensional experiments such as COSY, HSQC, and HMBC map correlations between nuclei, and the nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) provides through-space distance information used to determine the three-dimensional structures of proteins in solution. Solid-state NMR uses magic-angle spinning to study insoluble materials.

History

NMR was first measured in molecular beams by Isidor Rabi in 1938, and in bulk matter by the groups of Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell in 1946, who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics. Richard Ernst's development of Fourier-transform and multidimensional NMR earned the 1991 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and Kurt Wüthrich shared the 2002 Chemistry prize for protein structure determination by NMR.
