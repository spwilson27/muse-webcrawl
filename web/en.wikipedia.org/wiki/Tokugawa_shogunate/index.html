Tokugawa shogunate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Edo Castle's keep as it appeared before its destruction by fire in 1657]

The Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868), also called the Edo shogunate or bakufu, was the military government of Japan established by Tokugawa Ieyasu after his victory at Sekigahara (1600) and his appointment as shogun in 1603. It ruled Japan for 265 years from Edo (modern Tokyo), the longest span of stable government in Japanese history: daimyo were controlled through the alternate-attendance system (sankin-kōtai), foreign contact was restricted almost to Nagasaki (sakoku), Christianity was suppressed, and a rigid status order underpinned an era of peace, urbanisation and cultural flowering — until Commodore Perry's arrival (1853) triggered the crisis that ended the shogunate in the Meiji Restoration.

The Tokugawa system

Ieyasu and his successors bound the roughly 260 daimyo by law (Buke shohatto), hostage residence in Edo, and transfers at the shogun's pleasure; the bakufu directly ruled the great cities, mines, and a quarter of the land. Society was ordered as samurai, peasants, artisans, merchants (shi-nō-kō-shō) — with merchants formally lowest yet growing rich in castle towns and the metropolises of Edo (a million people by 1700) and Osaka. Christianity, brought by Portuguese traders and Jesuits from 1549, was extirpated by the 1630s after the Shimabara Rebellion.

Isolation, crisis and fall

The sakoku edicts (1630s) confined foreign trade to Dutch and Chinese merchants at Nagasaki and controlled contacts via Tsushima, Satsuma and Matsumae. Two centuries of peace brought literacy, publishing, kabuki and ukiyo-e — and fiscal strain, peasant revolts and samurai indebtedness. Perry's 'black ships' (1853–54) forced opening; unequal treaties discredited the bakufu, and a coalition of Satsuma and Chōshū domains, rallying to the emperor, won the brief Boshin War (1868–69). The last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, surrendered Edo; the Meiji era began.
