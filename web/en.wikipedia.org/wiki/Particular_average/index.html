Particular average

In marine insurance, a particular average is a partial loss of the subject-matter insured, proximately caused by a peril insured against, which is not a general average loss. It falls entirely on the interest suffering the loss and gives no right of contribution from other interests involved in the adventure.

The term "average" in marine insurance means a partial loss (from the Arabic ʿawār, "damage"). Particular average may consist of physical damage to goods (for example seawater wetting of part of a cargo) or a charge such as particular average on the ship (for example the cost of repairing storm damage to the hull borne by the shipowner alone).

Distinction from general average

In a general average loss, sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made for the common safety of the whole adventure — for instance, jettisoning cargo to refloat a stranded ship — and the loss is shared rateably among ship, cargo and freight interests under the York-Antwerp Rules. A particular average loss, by contrast, is accidental and benefits only the owner of the damaged property.

Policy wording

Under the English Marine Insurance Act 1906, "particular average" is defined in section 64. Older cargo clauses often limited recovery through "free of particular average" (FPA) conditions or percentage franchises, under which particular average losses were payable only if they reached an agreed percentage of insured value or resulted from specified casualties such as stranding, sinking, burning or collision. Modern Institute Cargo Clauses allocate these risks among the (A), (B) and (C) levels of cover.
