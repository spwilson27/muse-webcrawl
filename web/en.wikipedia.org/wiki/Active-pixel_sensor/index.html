Active-pixel sensor

An active-pixel sensor (APS) is an image sensor where each pixel sensor unit cell has a photodetector and one or more active transistors. In a metal–oxide–semiconductor (MOS) active-pixel sensor, MOS field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) are used as amplifiers. There are different types of APS, including the early NMOS APS and the now common CMOS APS, also known as the CMOS sensor. CMOS sensors are used in most modern digital cameras, mobile phone cameras, webcams, most modern digital pocket cameras, and many other consumer imaging devices.

History

The concept of an active pixel dates to the 1960s, when Peter Noble proposed imaging arrays with per-pixel amplifiers at Plessey in 1968, though fabrication technology was not yet adequate. The modern CMOS APS was developed by a team led by Eric Fossum at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in the early 1990s, motivated by the need for miniaturised, low-power cameras for interplanetary spacecraft. Fossum co-founded Photobit to commercialise the technology; CMOS sensors subsequently displaced CCDs in most applications during the 2000s thanks to lower power consumption, on-chip integration, and lower manufacturing cost.

Operation

In a typical three- or four-transistor pixel, photons strike a photodiode and generate charge proportional to the incident light. A reset transistor clears accumulated charge, a source-follower transistor converts the charge to a voltage and buffers it within the pixel, and a row-select transistor connects the pixel to a column readout line. Because the amplification happens in-pixel, the signal is less vulnerable to noise picked up during readout than in charge-coupled devices, which must shuttle charge across the array. A fourth transistor enables correlated double sampling and global shutter operation in some designs.

Comparison with CCDs

APS sensors consume far less power than CCDs, can integrate timing, signal processing and analogue-to-digital conversion on the same chip, and permit random access to pixel regions of interest. Early CMOS sensors suffered from higher fixed-pattern noise and lower fill factor, but microlenses, back-side illumination, and improved readout circuitry largely closed the image-quality gap, and CMOS technology now dominates scientific and consumer imaging alike.
