In physics, a shock wave (also spelled shockwave), or shock, is a type of propagating disturbance that moves faster than the local speed of sound in the medium. Like an ordinary wave, a shock carries energy and can propagate through a medium, but it is characterised by an abrupt, nearly discontinuous change in pressure, temperature and density of the medium. Shock waves arise when a pressure wave in a compressible fluid steepens — as around supersonic aircraft, in explosions, lightning and supersonic wind-tunnel flow.

Physics

Across a shock, the flow undergoes a sudden compression: pressure, temperature and density jump upward while flow velocity (in the shock's frame) falls from supersonic to subsonic across a normal shock. The relationships between conditions before and after the shock are given by the Rankine–Hugoniot equations, expressing conservation of mass, momentum and energy. Shock formation is irreversible: entropy increases across a shock, consistent with the second law of thermodynamics. Shocks may be normal (perpendicular to the flow) or oblique (angled, as at the nose or wing edges of a supersonic vehicle), and their strength grows with upstream Mach number. A weak shock propagating into open air is perceived as a sonic boom.

Examples and applications

  • Aerodynamics — shocks on transonic wings cause drag rise and buffeting; the bow shock precedes re-entering spacecraft, converting kinetic energy into extreme heating.
  • Explosions — blast waves cause characteristic overpressure damage; shock physics governs detonation.
  • Astrophysics — supernova blast waves, the solar-wind termination shock, and bow shocks around stars and galaxies.
  • Medicine — extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy fragments kidney stones with focused acoustic shocks.
  • Geophysics — shock metamorphism in meteorite impact craters, including shocked quartz.

Measurement and visualisation

Shocks are visualised by schlieren photography and shadowgraphy, which exploit density gradients' effect on refractive index, and modelled numerically in computational fluid dynamics using shock-capturing methods.
