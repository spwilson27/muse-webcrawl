Synteny

In genetics, synteny originally described the physical co-localisation of genetic loci on the same chromosome within an individual or species. In modern comparative genomics, the term is more loosely used for the preserved order of genes (or other genomic features) on chromosomes of related species, reflecting their descent from a common ancestor. Such conserved blocks are called syntenic blocks.

Usage

When the genomes of two species are compared, stretches of chromosomes containing the same genes in the same order indicate that the region has not been broken up by rearrangement since the species diverged. For example, large parts of the human genome are syntenic with the mouse genome, although the blocks are distributed among different chromosomes in the two species. Synteny analysis helps researchers annotate newly sequenced genomes, infer ancestral chromosome structures, and trace events such as whole-genome duplications.

History

The word was coined in 1971 by John Renwick, from the Greek for "along the same ribbon". The classical meaning — genes physically on the same chromosome — did not require the genes to be linked closely enough for linkage to be detected genetically, a distinction that caused some terminological confusion as the field moved to whole-genome comparisons.
