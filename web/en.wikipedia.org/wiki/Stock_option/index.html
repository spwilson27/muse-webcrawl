Stock option

A stock option is a contract that gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell a specified number of shares of an underlying stock at a predetermined price (the strike price) on or before a specified date. Options are a class of derivative security whose value derives from the price of the underlying shares.

Types

A call option gives the holder the right to buy the underlying stock at the strike price. A put option gives the holder the right to sell. The party who sells (writes) the option receives a payment called the premium and assumes the obligation to fulfil the contract if the holder exercises it.

Options also differ by exercise style. American options may be exercised at any time up to expiration; European options may be exercised only at expiration; Bermudan options may be exercised on specified dates.

Contract specifications

Exchange-traded equity options in the United States are standardized by the Options Clearing Corporation. A standard contract typically covers 100 shares. The quoted price of an option is its intrinsic value — the amount by which it is in the money — plus its time value, which reflects the probability of further favourable movement before expiry and the volatility of the underlying stock.

Valuation

The standard model for valuing European options is the Black–Scholes model, published in 1973, which prices an option from the current stock price, strike price, time to expiration, the risk-free interest rate and the stock's volatility. Sensitivity measures known as "the Greeks" — delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho — describe how an option's value responds to changes in these inputs and passage of time.

Employee stock options

Companies frequently grant stock options to employees as a form of compensation, intended to align employees' incentives with shareholders'. Such grants typically vest over several years and, in the United States, may be structured as incentive stock options or non-qualified stock options with different tax treatment.

Risks

Because options are leveraged instruments, small movements in the underlying stock can produce large percentage gains or losses. Option buyers can lose the entire premium paid; writers of uncovered ("naked") calls face theoretically unlimited risk.
