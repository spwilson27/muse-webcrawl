Tenure

Tenure is a category of academic appointment existing in some countries. A tenured post is an indefinite academic appointment that can be terminated only for cause or under extraordinary circumstances, such as financial exigency or programme discontinuation. Tenure is intended as a protection of academic freedom: it enables scholars to pursue research and express ideas without fear of dismissal for displeasing administrators, donors or political authorities.

University faculty members

History

Modern academic tenure in the United States was codified in the 1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure, issued by the American Association of University Professors and the Association of American Colleges. It prescribed a probationary period of no more than seven years — the "tenure track" — after which a faculty member who is reappointed is granted tenure, typically alongside promotion to associate professor.

The tenure process

Assistant professors on the tenure track are evaluated on research and publication, teaching and service. In the tenure year, the candidate's dossier, including external letters from senior scholars, is reviewed by department, college and university committees. An unsuccessful candidate is normally given a terminal year of employment.

Debate

Supporters argue tenure safeguards intellectual independence and long-term research. Critics contend that it can entrench unproductive staff, raise costs, and that universities increasingly rely on contingent, non-tenure-track faculty — adjuncts — who now teach a majority of courses at many institutions. Several jurisdictions have debated or enacted changes to tenure in public universities and in primary and secondary schools, where the term also applies to job protections for teachers after a probationary period.
