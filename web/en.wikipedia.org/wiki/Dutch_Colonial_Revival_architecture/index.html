Dutch Colonial Revival architecture

Dutch Colonial Revival is a style of domestic architecture in the United States that emulates the houses built by Dutch settlers in the American colonies, primarily in the Hudson River valley and other parts of New Netherland. Popular from the 1890s through the 1930s as part of the wider Colonial Revival movement, the style is instantly recognisable by its gambrel roof, which maximised headroom in the upper storey.

Characteristics

Typical features include a gambrel roof — often sweeping low over a porch — flared eaves, dormers (frequently a long shed dormer across the front), brick, stone, or shingle cladding, Dutch doors divided horizontally, and multi-pane double-hung windows with shutters. Houses are usually one and a half or two and a half storeys. The historical Dutch colonial houses that inspired the style were built of stone or brick in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries in New York and New Jersey, but most Revival examples were wood-framed suburban homes, and the style spread nationwide through pattern books and kit-house catalogues such as those of Sears.

The gambrel roof became so closely associated with the style that many Americans use "Dutch colonial" simply to describe any house with a gambrel roof. The style declined after the 1930s but saw renewed popularity in suburban developments of the later twentieth century.
