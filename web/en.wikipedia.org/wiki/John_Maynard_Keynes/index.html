John Maynard Keynes (5 June 1883 – 21 April 1946) was an English economist whose ideas fundamentally changed the theory and practice of Macroeconomics and government economic policy. His General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), written amid the Great Depression, argued that aggregate demand — especially volatile investment spending — drives output and employment, and that economies can settle into under-employment equilibria that markets will not quickly self-correct.

Ideas and career

Keynes studied mathematics and economics at King's College, Cambridge, worked in the India Office and Treasury, and represented Britain at the Paris Peace Conference, resigning to write The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919), a prophetic attack on reparations. In the General Theory he developed the consumption function, the multiplier, liquidity preference and the decisive role of expectations ("animal spirits"). The policy conclusion — that governments should run deficits in slumps and surpluses in booms — defined post-war Keynesian demand management.

During the Second World War Keynes was central to British war finance and to designing the post-war order: at Bretton Woods in 1944 he helped create the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. He died in 1946, a year before the system he shaped came into full operation. His influence waned in the 1970s stagflation and the rise of Monetarism, then revived sharply after the 2008 financial crisis and again during the COVID-19 pandemic.
