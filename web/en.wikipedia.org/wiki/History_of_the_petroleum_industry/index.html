History of the petroleum industry

The history of the petroleum industry begins with ancient uses of bitumen and oil seeps, but the modern industry dates from the mid-19th century, when drilled wells, refining and global demand for kerosene — and later gasoline — turned oil into the world's most important commodity and a central factor in geopolitics.

The Pennsylvania oil boom of the 1860s launched the modern petroleum industry.

Origins

Petroleum was used in antiquity for waterproofing, construction and medicine; oil was gathered from seeps in Mesopotamia, China and elsewhere, and wells were drilled for brine and oil in China centuries before the modern era. In the 1850s, kerosene distilled from coal and oil created a market for illuminating oil to replace whale oil. Edwin Drake's well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, struck oil on 27 August 1859, sparking a drilling boom. Refineries clustered in Cleveland and Pittsburgh, and John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, founded in 1870, came to dominate American refining and distribution until its breakup by the US Supreme Court in 1911.

Global expansion

Production spread to the Caucasus (Baku, where the Nobel brothers and Rothschilds built major enterprises), Galicia, Romania and the Dutch East Indies, where Royal Dutch Shell emerged. In Persia, the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (later BP) struck oil at Masjed Soleyman in 1908, opening the Middle East. The Spindletop gusher in Texas (1901) and later giant fields in Oklahoma, California, Venezuela and Mexico made oil abundant; the internal combustion engine and the First World War's motorised armies and navies made it strategically vital.

Mid-20th century

Huge discoveries followed in Saudi Arabia (1938), Kuwait and elsewhere in the Gulf, produced under concessions by the "Seven Sisters" — the great Anglo-American majors. Host countries increasingly demanded control: Iran nationalised its industry in 1951, and in 1960 the producing states founded OPEC in Baghdad. The 1973 Arab oil embargo quadrupled prices and reshaped the world economy; a second shock followed the Iranian Revolution in 1979. North Sea and Alaskan production, and later deepwater drilling, diversified supply.

Late 20th century to today

National oil companies came to control most reserves; prices swung through the 1986 collapse, the 1990 Gulf War spike and the 2008 peak near $147 a barrel. In the 2000s–2010s, hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling unlocked US shale oil and gas, making the United States the world's largest producer. The industry now faces the energy transition, climate policy and events such as the 2020 price crash during the COVID-19 pandemic, even as oil remains the largest single source of global energy.
