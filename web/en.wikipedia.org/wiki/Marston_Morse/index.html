Marston Morse

Harold Calvin Marston Morse (March 24, 1892 – June 22, 1977) was an American mathematician best known for his work on the calculus of variations in the large, a subject where he introduced the technique now known as Morse theory, a basic tool of modern differential topology.

Biography

Morse was born in Waterville, Maine. He studied at Colby College and Harvard University, where he received his PhD in 1917 under George David Birkhoff with a thesis on certain types of geodesic motion on a surface of negative curvature. He served in the Ambulance Corps in France during World War I and was awarded the Croix de Guerre.

He taught at Cornell and Brown before returning to Harvard in 1926. In 1935 he moved to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he remained until his retirement in 1962.

Mathematical work

Morse theory relates the topology of a manifold to the critical points of a smooth function defined on it; a function whose critical points are all nondegenerate is called a Morse function. His 1934 book The Calculus of Variations in the Large was a landmark. With John Milnor's later exposition, Morse theory became one of the central techniques of differential topology.

Morse also developed the theory of recurrent geodesics and, with Gustav Hedlund, important work in symbolic dynamics, including the Thue–Morse sequence's role in aperiodic trajectories.

Honours

He was awarded the Bôcher Memorial Prize in 1933 and the National Medal of Science in 1964. He helped found the Institute for Advanced Study's School of Mathematics and was a dominant figure in American mathematics between the wars.
