Hypoventilation

Hypoventilation
DefinitionVentilation insufficient to meet metabolic needs
Blood gas findingsElevated PaCO2 (hypercapnia), respiratory acidosis
SpecialtyPulmonology, critical care medicine
OppositeHyperventilation

Hypoventilation occurs when alveolar ventilation is insufficient to eliminate the carbon dioxide produced by the body, so that the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) rises above the normal range — a state called hypercapnia — and the blood becomes acidotic (respiratory acidosis). It is the opposite of hyperventilation, in which ventilation exceeds metabolic demand and PaCO2 falls.

Causes

Hypoventilation can result from failure at any point in the ventilatory system:

  • Depressed central drive — sedative and opioid drugs, brainstem lesions, congenital central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's curse"), and severe hypothyroidism.
  • Neuromuscular weakness — motor neurone disease (ALS), Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophies and high spinal cord injury.
  • Chest-wall and lung mechanics — severe obesity (obesity hypoventilation syndrome, formerly Pickwick syndrome), kyphoscoliosis, flail chest, and advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).
  • Sleep-related hypoventilation — worsening of any of the above during sleep, often overlapping with obstructive sleep apnoea.

Signs and diagnosis

Symptoms are largely those of hypercapnia: morning headache, daytime sleepiness, fatigue, confusion and, in severe cases, asterixis, papilloedema and coma (CO2 narcosis). Diagnosis is confirmed by arterial blood gas analysis showing PaCO2 above about 45 mmHg with a compensatory rise in bicarbonate when the process is chronic. Overnight oximetry, capnography and polysomnography are used to detect sleep hypoventilation, and pulmonary function tests and imaging help identify the underlying cause.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the cause: reversal of drug effects (for example naloxone for opioid overdose), treatment of neuromuscular or lung disease, and weight loss in obesity hypoventilation. Long-term ventilatory support, most commonly non-invasive ventilation (such as bilevel positive airway pressure) at night, is the mainstay for chronic hypoventilation syndromes and improves survival in conditions such as ALS and obesity hypoventilation. Acute severe hypoventilation with acidosis may require assisted ventilation in an intensive care unit.
