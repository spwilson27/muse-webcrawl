Bab al-Futuh (Arabic: باب الفتوح, "Gate of Conquests") is one of three remaining gates in the walls of the old city of Cairo, Egypt. It was built in 1087 by the Fatimid vizier Badr al-Jamali and marks the northern end of the historic fortified city, at the head of al-Muizz Street.

History

The gate was constructed as part of the stone fortifications raised by Badr al-Jamali when he rebuilt Cairo's defensive walls, replacing earlier mud-brick walls. Tradition holds that armies victorious in battle entered the city through Bab al-Futuh, hence its name, while departing armies passed out through Bab al-Nasr. Together with Bab al-Nasr and Bab Zuweila, it is among the finest surviving examples of medieval Islamic military architecture in Egypt.

Architecture

The gate is flanked by two massive semicircular towers and is built of finely cut stone. Its design, with rounded towers and stone vaulting, shows influences often attributed to craftsmen from northern Mesopotamia or Armenia working in Fatimid service. The gate leads southward into al-Muizz Street, the ceremonial spine of Fatimid Cairo, lined with mosques, madrasas, and mausoleums.
