Posterior cingulate cortex

The posterior cingulate cortex lies on the medial surface of the brain, behind the corpus callosum

Part ofCingulate cortex, limbic lobe
Brodmann areas23, 29, 30, 31
NetworkDefault mode network (hub)

The posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) is the backmost part of the cingulate cortex, lying on the medial surface of the cerebral hemisphere behind the central sulcus and above the corpus callosum. It comprises Brodmann areas 23 and 31 together with the adjacent retrosplenial cortex (areas 29 and 30). The PCC has among the highest resting metabolic rates in the brain and is richly connected with the hippocampal formation, parietal cortex, and prefrontal regions.

Functions

The PCC is a central hub of the default mode network, the set of brain regions most active when a person is awake but not focused on the external environment. It is involved in internally directed cognition, including autobiographical memory retrieval, imagining the future, mind-wandering, and thinking about the thoughts and perspectives of others. Its activity typically decreases during demanding external tasks. The region also plays roles in attention, consciousness, and the regulation of arousal, and helps integrate information about the self and the body's internal state.

Clinical significance

The PCC is one of the earliest regions to show metabolic decline and amyloid deposition in Alzheimer's disease, and its disconnection from the hippocampus is linked to memory impairment. Abnormal PCC activity and connectivity have also been reported in depression, schizophrenia, autism, and disorders of consciousness, making it a frequent target of neuroimaging research and a candidate site for therapeutic neuromodulation.
