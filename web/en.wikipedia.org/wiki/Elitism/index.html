Elitism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elitism is the belief or notion that individuals who form an elite — a select group of people perceived as having inherent quality, high intellect, wealth, power or special skills — are more likely to do what is best for society, and thus that political power should rest largely with them. In political science, elite theory is also a descriptive doctrine, associated with Vilfredo Pareto, Gaetano Mosca and Robert Michels, holding that a small organised minority inevitably rules the unorganised majority in every society — Mosca's “political class”, Pareto's “circulation of elites”, and Michels's iron law of oligarchy.

Elitism is the usual antonym of populism, which morally divides society into a pure people and a corrupt elite and claims politics should express the people's general will. Defenders of elitism in its normative form argue that complex governance requires expertise and that democratic competition among elites (Schumpeter's model) is the realistic core of democracy. Critics argue that elite rule entrenches privilege and that “meritocratic” selection often reproduces existing advantage.
