Transcriptome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The transcriptome is the set of all RNA transcripts, including coding and non-coding RNA, in an individual or population of cells. Unlike the genome, which is roughly fixed for a given cell line (excluding mutations), the transcriptome varies with external environmental conditions and reflects the genes being actively expressed at any given moment.

Composition

The transcriptome includes messenger RNA (mRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), and many classes of non-coding RNA such as microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs. In a typical mammalian cell, rRNA makes up the bulk of RNA by mass, while mRNA accounts for only a few percent but carries the protein-coding information most studies focus on.

Study methods

Transcriptomics, the study of the transcriptome, began with expressed sequence tags and DNA microarrays and is now dominated by RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), in which RNA is converted to cDNA and sequenced at high throughput. RNA-Seq allows quantification of expression levels, discovery of novel transcripts and splice variants, and measurement of allele-specific expression. Single-cell RNA sequencing extends the approach to individual cells, revealing cell-type-specific expression profiles.

Applications

Transcriptome analysis is used to compare gene expression between healthy and diseased tissue, to identify biomarkers and drug targets, to study development, and to annotate genomes by mapping where transcripts are produced, including alternative splicing patterns.
