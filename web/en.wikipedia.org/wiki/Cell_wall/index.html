Cell wall

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cell wall is a structural layer surrounding some types of cells, just outside the Cell membrane. It gives the cell shape, mechanical strength and protection, and filters what reaches the membrane. Cell walls occur in plants, fungi, bacteria, Archaea and some other groups — but not in animal cells.

Composition by group

Plant cell walls are built mainly of cellulose fibres embedded in pectin and hemicellulose; many develop a lignified secondary wall (wood). Fungal walls use chitin. Most bacterial walls contain peptidoglycan — a mesh of sugars and short peptides — whose thickness distinguishes Gram-positive from Gram-negative bacteria; archaeal walls use other polymers such as pseudopeptidoglycan, and some archaea have only a protein S-layer.

Functions

Walls prevent plant and microbial cells from bursting when water enters by osmosis, generating turgor pressure that supports non-woody plants. They mediate cell-to-cell communication (through plasmodesmata in plants), store carbohydrates, and form a first line of defence against pathogens.

Medical relevance

Because human cells have no wall and no peptidoglycan, the bacterial wall is a prime antibiotic target: penicillin and other beta-lactams block peptidoglycan cross-linking, and vancomycin binds wall precursors. The antifungal echinocandins target fungal wall synthesis.
