Idolatry

Idolatry is the worship of a physical image or object — an idol or cult image — as if it were a god, or more broadly the devotion to anything other than God that takes the place of God. In the Abrahamic religions, idolatry is a major sin, prohibited first among the Ten Commandments. The term derives from the Greek eidōlolatreia, "worship of images," and has historically been used both as an internal theological category and as a polemical label for other religions' practices.

In Judaism

The Hebrew Bible condemns idolatry (avodah zarah, "foreign worship") repeatedly: the Second Commandment forbids making graven images for worship, and narratives such as the Golden Calf episode and the prophets' satires of idol-making dramatize the prohibition. Biblical law prescribed severe penalties, and rabbinic Judaism extended the prohibition to benefiting from or even gazing upon idols.

In Christianity

Early Christianity inherited the Jewish rejection of cult images, and the New Testament extends "idolatry" metaphorically to greed and other disordered loves. Disputes over images of Christ and the saints later divided the church: iconoclasm in the Byzantine Empire (8th–9th centuries) destroyed icons before the Second Council of Nicaea (787) affirmed their veneration, distinguishing veneration of images from worship (latreia) due to God alone. During the Reformation, Protestant reformers accused Catholic practice of idolatry and removed or destroyed images in many churches, while Catholic and Orthodox traditions maintained the distinction between veneration and adoration.

In Islam

Islam teaches strict monotheism (tawhid) and regards idolatry (shirk, "association" of partners with God) as the gravest sin. The Qur'an recounts Abraham's rejection of his people's idols, and Islamic tradition holds that Muhammad cleansed the Kaaba of its idols in 630 CE. Islamic religious art consequently avoids depicting God and, in most religious contexts, prophets and animate beings, emphasizing calligraphy and geometric pattern.

Other perspectives

Religions such as Hinduism, Buddhism, and Shinto employ sacred images (for example the Hindu murti) as focal points of devotion; adherents generally reject the label "idolatry," explaining that the deity is invoked into, or represented by, the image rather than identified with the material object. Scholars of religion therefore often treat "idolatry" as a value-laden term reflecting the perspective of the accuser rather than a neutral description.
