DKW was a German car and motorcycle marque founded by Danish engineer Jørgen Skafte Rasmussen. The name derives from Dampf-Kraft-Wagen ("steam-powered car"), reflecting Rasmussen's 1916 experiments with a steam-driven vehicle, though the letters were later also read as Das Kleine Wunder ("the little wonder") for its motorcycles. DKW was one of the four companies that merged in 1932 to form the Auto Union — the four rings of the modern Audi logo represent DKW, Audi, Horch and Wanderer.

A pre-war DKW two-stroke saloon.

History

Rasmussen began building two-stroke engines in Zschopau, Saxony, in 1919; the little engines powered auxiliary bicycle motors, and by the 1920s DKW was the world's largest motorcycle manufacturer. Its first car, the P15, appeared in 1928. In 1929–1931 DKW pioneered front-wheel-drive mass production with the F1 and its successors, combining unitary wooden bodies with two-stroke engines.

After the Second World War, the Saxon plants lay in the Soviet zone, where production continued under the East German IFA badge, while DKW was re-established in the West at Ingolstadt and Düsseldorf. West German DKWs — the 3=6, F11/F12 and F102 — retained the two-stroke formula; the F102's successor, fitted with a four-stroke engine and launched in 1965, was rebadged as the first post-war Audi. Volkswagen, which had acquired Auto Union from Daimler-Benz in 1964–66, retired the DKW brand in 1966.

Motorcycles

DKW motorcycles achieved major racing success in the 1930s, notably with the supercharged split-single two-strokes that dominated the 250 cc and 350 cc classes. After 1945 the ubiquitous RT 125 — a design given away as war reparations — was copied worldwide as the BSA Bantam, Harley-Davidson Hummer and others, becoming one of the most produced motorcycle designs in history.
