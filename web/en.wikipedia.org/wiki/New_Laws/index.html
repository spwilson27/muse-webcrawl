The New Laws (Spanish: Leyes Nuevas), formally the "Laws and ordinances newly made by his Majesty for the government of the Indies and good treatment and preservation of the Indians", were a series of regulations issued on 20 November 1542 by King Charles V of Spain, intended to reform the encomienda system and end the abuse and enslavement of Indigenous peoples in the Spanish colonies of the Americas.

Background

From the beginning of colonisation, the encomienda granted Spanish settlers the right to extract tribute and labour from Indigenous communities, often with brutal consequences compounded by epidemic disease, which devastated native populations. Clerics such as Antonio de Montesinos (whose 1511 sermon at Santo Domingo denounced Spanish cruelty) and above all the Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas — whose Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias (1542) catalogued atrocities — pressed the Crown for reform. Earlier measures, such as the Laws of Burgos (1512), had proved ineffective.

Provisions

The New Laws declared the Indigenous peoples free vassals of the Crown; prohibited their enslavement under any pretext, including war and rebellion; forbade the granting of new encomiendas; ordered existing encomiendas to revert to the Crown on the death of the current holder (ending their inheritance); removed encomiendas from officials and clergy; and banned the use of Indigenous porters (tamemes) and forced labour in the pearl fisheries. Audiencias were charged with enforcement and with investigating abuses.

Resistance and repeal

The laws provoked outrage among settlers. In Peru, enforcement by the first viceroy, Blasco Núñez Vela, triggered the rebellion of Gonzalo Pizarro; the viceroy was defeated and killed at Añaquito in 1546. In New Spain, Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza quietly suspended implementation ("obedezco pero no cumplo" — "I obey but do not comply"). Faced with colonial revolt, Charles V revoked the most explosive provision — the prohibition on inheriting encomiendas — in October 1545. The anti-slavery provisions remained formally in force, though evaded in practice through other coercive labour systems such as repartimiento.

Legacy

Despite partial repeal, the New Laws marked a landmark in the history of human rights law and imperial reform: they affirmed in law the freedom and humanity of Indigenous peoples, accelerated the decline of the encomienda, and framed the great Valladolid debate (1550–1551) between Las Casas and Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda on the legitimacy of conquest.
