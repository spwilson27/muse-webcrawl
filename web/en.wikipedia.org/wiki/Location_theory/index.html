Location theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Location theory is the branch of economic geography and regional science concerned with where economic activities are located and why. It seeks to explain the spatial distribution of farms, factories, shops, and settlements by analysing how firms and households weigh transport costs, land prices, labour, markets, and the pull of other nearby activities (agglomeration).

Classical models

The founder of location theory is generally considered to be the German economist Johann Heinrich von Thünen, whose Der isolierte Staat ("The Isolated State", 1826) modelled an isolated city surrounded by farmland. Because transport costs rise with distance, perishable and heavy products are produced close to the market while extensive activities such as grazing occupy cheaper distant land, producing concentric rings of land use. Von Thünen also showed how land rent declines with distance from the centre—an idea that survives in modern bid-rent theory.

Alfred Weber (1909) extended the analysis to industry, arguing that firms choose the location that minimises the combined cost of transporting raw materials and finished products, modified by cheap labour and the savings of clustering near other firms. Walter Christaller's central place theory (1933) explained the size, number, and spacing of towns as market centres serving surrounding areas with goods of different "threshold" and "range", generating a hierarchy of settlements. August Lösch generalised central place theory, and Harold Hotelling's model of two competing shops on a linear market ("Hotelling's law") introduced strategic interaction between firms.

Modern developments

Twentieth-century location theory was formalised by economists and geographers such as Walter Isard, founder of regional science, and William Alonso, whose bid-rent model applied von Thünen's logic to urban land markets. Since the 1990s, the "new economic geography" associated with Paul Krugman has used increasing returns and transport costs to explain the self-reinforcing growth of industrial clusters and core–periphery patterns. Location theory underpins practical tools such as site selection for retail chains, logistics network design, and the Weber problem of finding the point that minimises total weighted distance to a set of destinations.
