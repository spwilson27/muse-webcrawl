Bootlegger

A bootlegger is a person who makes, transports, or sells alcoholic beverages illegally. The term is most closely associated with the United States during Prohibition (1920–1933), when the Eighteenth Amendment banned the manufacture and sale of intoxicating liquor, but it predates that era: it was in use by the 1880s for sellers of liquor to Native Americans in the West, and is said to derive from the practice of concealing a bottle in the leg of a boot. The word has since been extended to any illegal or unauthorised trade, as in "bootleg" recordings and software.

Prohibition era

When national Prohibition took effect in January 1920, demand for alcohol was met by an illegal industry of home distillers (moonshiners), smugglers bringing liquor across the Canadian and Mexican borders and by sea (rum-running), and organised crime syndicates that controlled distribution to speakeasies. Figures such as Al Capone in Chicago built criminal empires on bootlegging, and the trade was accompanied by widespread corruption of police and officials, violent turf wars, and the production of dangerous adulterated liquor. Repeal came with the Twenty-first Amendment in 1933, though bootlegging persisted afterward in dry counties and to evade taxes.

Etymology and extended uses

The "bootleg" metaphor — goods carried covertly against the leg — passed into general slang. A "bootleg" recording is an unauthorised audio or video recording of a performance; bootleg merchandise is unlicensed goods bearing protected trademarks; and in American football a "bootleg" is a play in which the quarterback runs with the ball concealed against his hip. In economics, the phrase "bootleggers and Baptists" describes coalitions of those who support a regulation for moral reasons and those who quietly profit from it.
