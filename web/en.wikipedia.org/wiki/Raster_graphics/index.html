In computer graphics, raster graphics (also called bitmap graphics) represent an image as a rectangular grid of pixels, each with its own colour value. Raster images are resolution-dependent: they contain a fixed number of pixels, so enlarging them reveals individual pixels and loses apparent sharpness, unlike vector graphics, which are defined by mathematical shapes and scale cleanly. Raster formats are the standard representation for photographs and scanned artwork.

Characteristics

A raster image is characterised by its dimensions in pixels (width × height), its colour depth (bits per pixel) and its colour model, usually RGB with an optional alpha channel for transparency. Display resolution is often expressed in pixels per inch (PPI) and print resolution in dots per inch (DPI). File size grows with the square of linear resolution, so compression is essential: lossless formats such as PNG and GIF preserve every pixel, while lossy formats such as JPEG discard information the eye is less likely to notice, greatly reducing size at some cost in quality.

Formats

Widely used raster file formats include BMP and TIFF (often uncompressed or losslessly compressed), GIF (limited to 256 colours, supports simple animation), PNG (lossless, full alpha), JPEG (lossy, standard for photographs) and newer formats such as WebP and AVIF offering better compression. Raw formats store minimally processed sensor data from digital cameras.

Editing and rendering

Raster editors such as Adobe Photoshop and GIMP manipulate pixels directly and are used for photo retouching and digital painting. Most display hardware is inherently raster-based, so vector graphics and text are rasterised — converted into pixels — before display, a process central to rendering in video games and graphical user interfaces.

Etymology

The term derives from the raster scan pattern of cathode-ray tube displays, in which an electron beam sweeps in horizontal lines; "raster" itself comes from the Latin rastrum, a rake.
