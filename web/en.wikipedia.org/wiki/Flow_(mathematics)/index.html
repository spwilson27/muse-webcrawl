Flow (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, a flow formalises the idea of the motion of points in a space over time, as occurs for the trajectories of particles in a moving fluid. Formally, a flow on a set X is a group action of the additive group of real numbers on X: a family of maps φt : X → X satisfying φ0 = identity and φs+t = φs ∘ φt. The curve t ↦ φt(x) is called the trajectory or orbit through x.

Flows of vector fields

The most important examples arise from autonomous ordinary differential equations. Given a vector field v on a manifold, its flow satisfies d/dt φt(x) = v(φt(x)); existence and uniqueness theorems guarantee a local flow, which is global if the vector field is complete (for instance on a compact manifold). Flows generated this way are diffeomorphisms for each fixed t and underpin the theory of dynamical systems.

Examples and applications

Examples include the rotation flow on a circle, translation flows, the geodesic flow on a Riemannian manifold, the Hamiltonian flow of classical mechanics, and gradient flows used in geometry and optimisation. The study of flows connects differential equations, topology and ergodic theory; classifying their orbits and invariant sets is central to understanding qualitative dynamics, from periodic orbits to chaos.
