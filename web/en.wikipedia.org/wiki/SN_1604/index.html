SN 1604, also known as Kepler's Supernova or Kepler's Star, was a Type Ia supernova that occurred in the Milky Way in the constellation Ophiuchus. Appearing in October 1604, it is the most recent supernova in the Milky Way galaxy to have been unquestionably observed by the naked eye, though many later galactic supernovae have been detected through their remnants.

Observation

The new star was first reported in northern Italy on 9 October 1604 and was studied in detail by Johannes Kepler from Prague; his account, De Stella Nova in Pede Serpentarii (1606), gave the supernova its enduring name, although Kepler had not been its first observer. At its peak it outshone every star in the sky and rivalled Jupiter, remaining visible in daylight for over three weeks and to the naked eye at night for about a year and a half. Because it appeared near a close conjunction of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, it attracted intense astrological as well as astronomical attention.

Remnant

The supernova remnant, a shell of expanding gas some 14 light-years across, is a strong source of radio waves and X-rays and has been studied by the Chandra X-ray Observatory and other telescopes. Its composition and structure confirm a Type Ia origin — the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf — though whether the companion star was a red giant or the product of a double white-dwarf merger remains debated.
