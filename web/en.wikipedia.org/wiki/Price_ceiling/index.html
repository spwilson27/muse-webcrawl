A price ceiling is a government-imposed limit on how high a price can be charged for a product, commodity or service. Price ceilings are typically set below the market equilibrium price and are intended to protect consumers from prices regarded as excessively high, for example for essential goods, housing or utilities.

Effects

In standard supply and demand analysis, a binding price ceiling creates a shortage: at the capped price, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied. The resulting disequilibrium can lead to queues, rationing, black markets, reduced product quality and decreased investment in supply. A non-binding ceiling — one set above the equilibrium price — has no effect on the market outcome.

Examples

Well-known examples include rent control in housing markets, wartime and post-war price controls such as those administered by the US Office of Price Administration during the Second World War, the Nixon administration's wage and price controls of 1971, and caps on interest rates (usury laws). Many jurisdictions cap prices of staple foods or fuel during emergencies, and anti-price-gouging statutes function as temporary ceilings.

Debate

Supporters argue ceilings protect vulnerable consumers and prevent windfall profits in emergencies. Critics respond that shortages and quality deterioration often harm the very consumers the policy aims to help, and that alternatives such as income transfers or supply subsidies achieve distributional goals with fewer distortions. Economists generally regard rent control as a classic case in which a price ceiling reduces the quantity and quality of housing available over the long run.
