Gunboat

A gunboat is a naval watercraft designed for the express purpose of carrying one or more guns to bombard coastal targets, as opposed to ships of war primarily designed for naval combat with other vessels or for troop transport. Gunboats typically have shallow drafts, allowing them to operate in rivers and coastal waters inaccessible to larger warships.

A Second World War-era motor gunboat

History

The first gunboats appeared in the 18th century; the French built small gun-carrying craft during the wars of that era, and the Danish-Norwegian "gunboat war" against Britain (1807–1814) relied on rowed and sailed gunboats. Thomas Jefferson's administration built numerous gunboats for coastal defence of the young United States. During the Crimean War, armoured steam gunboats proved effective against shore fortifications.

In the 19th century, gunboats became symbols of Western imperial power: small shallow-draft vessels such as those of the Royal Navy and the United States Navy patrolled the rivers of China and Africa, a practice that gave rise to the term "gunboat diplomacy"—the use or threat of naval force to achieve political aims. The Yangtze Patrol and vessels such as USS Panay (sunk by Japanese aircraft in 1937) exemplified this era.

World Wars and later use

During the Second World War, motor gunboats and motor torpedo boats of the Royal Navy's Coastal Forces fought in the English Channel and Mediterranean, while German S-boats performed similar roles. Riverine gunboats saw extensive service in the Vietnam War as part of the US "brown-water navy". Modern navies generally use patrol boats and corvettes in place of purpose-built gunboats, though some countries still classify small gun-armed craft as gunboats.
