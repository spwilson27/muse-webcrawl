Law of triviality

The law of triviality is C. Northcote Parkinson's observation that members of an organisation give disproportionate weight to trivial issues. Parkinson dramatised it with a committee that approves plans for a nuclear power plant in minutes — because few members understand the subject — then spends hours debating the design and materials of the staff bicycle shed. The phenomenon is therefore also known as bikeshedding.

The bicycle shed: trivial but easy for everyone to have an opinion about.

Argument

Parkinson set out the law in his 1957 book Parkinson's Law. The time spent on an item in committee, he argued, is inversely proportional to the sum of money involved and to the complexity of the item: large, complex proposals pass quickly because few feel qualified to object, whereas small, simple matters attract lengthy discussion because everyone can grasp them and wishes to contribute.

Usage

In software development communities, "bikeshedding" describes prolonged debate over minor details of a proposal while its major aspects go unexamined. The term "colour of the bikeshed" is a common shorthand for the trivial detail at stake.
