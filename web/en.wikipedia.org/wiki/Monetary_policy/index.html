Monetary policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monetary policy is the policy adopted by the monetary authority of a nation to affect monetary and other financial conditions to accomplish specific goals, such as constraining inflation, maintaining an exchange rate, achieving full employment or economic growth. Traditionally, central banks conduct it through a short-term policy interest rate, influencing money and credit conditions through open-market operations; since the 2008 financial crisis, balance-sheet tools (quantitative easing and tightening), forward guidance and macroprudential measures have joined the kit.

Frameworks and debates

Regimes vary: inflation targeting (pioneered by New Zealand in 1990, now the dominant framework among advanced economies), exchange-rate pegs (currency boards for the hardest versions), monetary-aggregate targeting (abandoned in practice), and the Federal Reserve's dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices. The theoretical spine runs from the quantity theory through Keynesian demand management and Friedman's rules-versus-discretion critique (k-percent rule, long and variable lags) to New Keynesian interest-rate rules (the Taylor rule) and time-inconsistency theory (Barro–Gordon) that justifies central-bank independence. Fault lines persist: how much weight on employment versus inflation; whether asset prices belong in the mandate; fiscal dominance when public debt is high; and the distributional effects of near-zero rates and QE, which pushed monetary policy from technocracy into politics after 2008 and again during the 2021–23 inflation surge.
