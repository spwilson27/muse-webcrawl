Compressive sensing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sparse signal recovered from a small number of random measurements

Compressive sensing (also known as compressed sensing, compressive sampling, or sparse sampling) is a signal processing technique for efficiently acquiring and reconstructing a signal by taking far fewer measurements than required by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem. It works when the signal is sparse — representable with only a few nonzero coefficients in some basis — or compressible. Instead of sampling at a high rate and then compressing, compressive sensing acquires a small number of non-adaptive, generally random, linear measurements from which the signal can be recovered by solving an optimization problem.

Principle

Suppose a signal x of length N is K-sparse in some basis (such as a wavelet basis). Compressive sensing takes M measurements y = Φx, where Φ is an M×N measurement matrix with M much smaller than N but on the order of K log(N/K). Recovery is possible when Φ satisfies conditions such as the restricted isometry property or incoherence with the sparsity basis. The signal is then reconstructed by ℓ₁-minimization (finding, among all signals consistent with the measurements, the one with the smallest ℓ₁ norm), a convex program solvable with standard algorithms, or by greedy methods such as orthogonal matching pursuit.

History

The theory was developed in the mid-2000s through the work of Emmanuel Candès, Justin Romberg, Terence Tao, and David Donoho, whose papers from 2004–2006 established rigorous guarantees for reconstruction. The ideas drew on earlier work in sparse approximation, statistics (such as the LASSO), and approximation theory.

Applications

  • Medical imaging — accelerating MRI scans by acquiring fewer k-space samples, reducing scan time.
  • Single-pixel cameras — imaging with one photodetector and a micromirror array (developed at Rice University).
  • Analog-to-information conversion — sampling sparse radio-frequency signals below the Nyquist rate.
  • Astronomy, radar, seismology, and genomics — recovering sparse scenes or spectra from incomplete data.
