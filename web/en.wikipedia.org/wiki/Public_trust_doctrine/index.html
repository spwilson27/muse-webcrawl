Public trust doctrine

The public trust doctrine is a legal principle holding that certain natural and cultural resources are preserved for public use, and that the government is required to protect and maintain them for the public's reasonable use. It derives from Roman law concepts of res communes — things common to all, such as air, running water, and the sea — transmitted through English common law.

Scope

In its classic form, the doctrine applies to navigable waters, submerged lands, and tidelands, protecting public rights of navigation, commerce, and fishing. The US Supreme Court's decision in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois (1892) held that a state could not abdicate its trust over submerged lands to a private party. Many states have expanded the doctrine to recreational uses, ecological values, and resources such as groundwater and wildlife.

Applications

The doctrine has been invoked in landmark environmental litigation, notably the California Supreme Court's Mono Lake decision (National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, 1983), which required the state to reconsider water diversions harming the lake. It continues to be cited in disputes over water rights, beach access, and climate change, though courts differ on how far it extends.
