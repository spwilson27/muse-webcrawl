Prior art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prior art, in Patent law, is all information made available to the public before a patent application's effective filing date that is relevant to the invention's novelty and inventive step: earlier patents and applications, journal articles, products, public uses and disclosures of any kind, anywhere in the world (the absolute-novelty standard of the European Patent Convention and, since the America Invents Act of 2011, of the United States, which moved from first-to-invent to first-inventor-to-file with a limited one-year grace period for the inventor's own disclosures).

Prior art defeats or narrows claims: a single anticipatory reference destroys novelty; combinations of references support obviousness rejections. Searching it (patent office examiners consult the classified literature of the PCT minimum documentation) is the system's quality filter, supplemented by third-party observations and post-grant challenges. The concept also structures academic priority disputes and defensive publication strategies; and in the age of internet timestamps, the boundary questions — what counts as “publicly available”, and when — continue to generate case law.
