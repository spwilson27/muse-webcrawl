Galantamine

Galantamine is a medication used to treat symptoms of mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease and other dementias. It is an alkaloid originally isolated from the bulbs and flowers of the Caucasian snowdrop (Galanthus woronowii) and other Amaryllidaceae plants, including daffodils. It is sold under brand names including Razadyne (formerly Reminyl).

Galantamine, C17H21NO3.

Medical use

Galantamine is a reversible, competitive acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. By reducing the breakdown of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, and by allosterically modulating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, it modestly improves cognition in Alzheimer's disease. It is taken by mouth as tablets, capsules, or solution.

History

The compound was first isolated in the Soviet Union in the early 1950s from snowdrop bulbs, and was used in Eastern Europe for conditions including myasthenia gravis and poliomyelitis. It was approved for Alzheimer's disease in the United States in 2001. Because extraction from bulbs is impractical at scale, it is now produced synthetically and can also be sourced from daffodil biomass.

Side effects

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, decreased appetite, and dizziness. It is metabolised partly by the liver enzymes CYP2D6 and CYP3A4.
