Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), also known as estrogen receptor agonist/antagonists, are a class of drugs that act on estrogen receptors. A characteristic of these drugs is that their action differs in various tissues, thereby granting the possibility to selectively inhibit or stimulate estrogen-like action in different tissues.

Mechanism

SERMs bind to the estrogen receptors alpha and beta and induce conformational changes that recruit either coactivators or corepressors depending on the tissue. For example, tamoxifen acts as an antagonist in breast tissue (the basis of its use against estrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer) but as a partial agonist in endometrium and bone.

Members and uses

  • Tamoxifen — treatment and risk reduction of ER-positive breast cancer; increases endometrial cancer risk and thromboembolism risk.
  • Raloxifene — prevention and treatment of postmenopausal osteoporosis and reduction of invasive breast-cancer risk, without stimulating the endometrium.
  • Clomifene — ovulation induction in infertility.
  • Bazedoxifene — combined with conjugated estrogens for menopausal symptoms and osteoporosis prevention.
  • Ospemifene — treatment of dyspareunia due to menopausal vulvovaginal atrophy.
  • Toremifene and lasofoxifene — other members used in some countries.

Adverse effects

Class effects include hot flushes and an increased risk of venous thromboembolism. Tamoxifen's endometrial agonism raises the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer, prompting monitoring for abnormal bleeding.
