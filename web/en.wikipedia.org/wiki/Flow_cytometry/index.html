Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry (FC) is a technique used to detect and measure the physical and chemical characteristics of a population of cells or particles. In this process, a sample containing cells or particles is suspended in a fluid and injected into a flow cytometer instrument. The sample is focused so that ideally one cell at a time passes through a laser beam, where the light scattered is characteristic to the cells and their components.

Schematic diagram of a flow cytometer

Principle

Cells are often labelled with fluorescent markers (fluorophores) conjugated to antibodies that bind specific molecules on or in the cell. As each cell passes through the laser beam, detectors measure forward-scattered light (roughly proportional to cell size), side-scattered light (proportional to internal complexity or granularity), and the fluorescence emitted in several colour channels. Thousands of cells per second can be analysed, and populations can be compared using histograms and two-dimensional dot plots.

Cell sorting

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) extends the technique by electrostatically deflecting droplets containing cells of interest into separate collection tubes, allowing purification of live cell populations for further study.

Applications

Flow cytometry is used extensively in immunology — for example, immunophenotyping of leukaemias and lymphomas and monitoring CD4 counts in HIV infection — as well as in molecular biology, pathology, microbiology, plant biology, and marine science.

History

The first fluorescence-based flow cytometer was developed in 1968 by Wolfgang Göhde in Germany. Herzenberg's group at Stanford developed the fluorescence-activated cell sorter in the early 1970s and coined the term FACS.
