Delayed hominization

Early embryonic development, the period at issue in debates over delayed hominization

Delayed hominization is the theory, developed in some strands of Roman Catholic moral theology and natural law philosophy, that the human soul is not infused into the human organism at the moment of conception but at a later stage of development. It is contrasted with immediate hominization, the view that a human person exists from fertilization. The question bears on debates about the moral status of the embryo and abortion.

History

The idea has roots in Aristotelian embryology, in which the developing organism was thought to pass through vegetative and animal stages before receiving a rational soul. Drawing on this framework, Thomas Aquinas held that the human embryo did not receive its rational soul until it was sufficiently organized — a view, sometimes called delayed ensoulment, that reflected medieval understandings of fetal development. In modern discussions, some theologians and philosophers have pointed to phenomena such as monozygotic twinning — the possibility that an embryo may split into two individuals during roughly the first two weeks — as an argument that individuation, and therefore personal identity, cannot be present before that point.

Contemporary discussion

The Catholic Church has not dogmatically defined the moment of ensoulment, though its modern teaching insists that human life from conception must be treated as if it were a person. Advocates of delayed hominization include some Catholic moral theologians, while critics argue that developmental continuity from fertilization supports immediate hominization.
