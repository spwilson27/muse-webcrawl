History of computing

Placeholder image: from the abacus to the modern microcomputer

The history of computing is longer than the history of modern computers: it begins with simple manual aids to calculation such as the abacus and extends through mechanical calculators, electromechanical machines, and the electronic digital computers that transformed society in the 20th and 21st centuries. Computing hardware has passed through several generations, each marked by a fundamental change in technology — relays and vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits, and microprocessors — with each generation making machines smaller, cheaper, faster, and more reliable.

Early devices

Humans have used tools to aid computation for thousands of years. The abacus, used in Mesopotamia, China, and the Roman world, allowed merchants to perform arithmetic quickly. The Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek geared device from roughly the 2nd century BCE, computed astronomical positions. In the 17th century, John Napier's logarithms and the slide rule transformed engineering calculation, while Blaise Pascal (1642) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1673) built mechanical calculators that could add, subtract, multiply, and divide.

The 19th century and the programmable machine

In the 1820s–1830s Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine for computing mathematical tables and then the far more ambitious Analytical Engine, a general-purpose programmable mechanical computer using punched cards adapted from the Jacquard loom. Ada Lovelace, translating and annotating a paper on the engine, described an algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers — often called the first computer program — and grasped that such a machine might manipulate symbols as well as numbers. Herman Hollerith's electromechanical tabulator, used for the 1890 United States census, launched the punched-card data processing industry and the company that would become IBM.

Foundations of theory

In 1936 Alan Turing published his description of a universal computing machine (the Turing machine), founding the theory of computation, while Alonzo Church independently developed the lambda calculus. Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis showed that Boolean algebra could be applied to switching circuits, laying the groundwork for digital circuit design.

Electronic computers

The Second World War spurred rapid development: Konrad Zuse's Z3 (1941) was an early programmable electromechanical computer; the British Colossus machines (1943–44) broke German codes at Bletchley Park; and the American ENIAC (1945) was a general-purpose electronic computer using some 18,000 vacuum tubes. The stored-program concept, articulated in the von Neumann architecture, was realized in machines such as the Manchester Baby (1948) and EDSAC (1949). UNIVAC I (1951) became the first commercial computer in the United States.

Transistors, integrated circuits, and mainframes

The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, began replacing vacuum tubes in the 1950s, yielding smaller and more reliable machines. The integrated circuit (1958–59, by Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce) combined many transistors on one chip. IBM's System/360 (1964) established the idea of a compatible family of mainframes, and minicomputers such as DEC's PDP-8 brought computing to smaller organizations. ARPANET, begun in 1969, pioneered packet-switched networking.

The microprocessor and personal computer

Intel's 4004 (1971) put a complete processor on a single chip. Hobbyist kits such as the Altair 8800 (1975) seeded the personal computer industry; the Apple II, Commodore PET, and TRS-80 (1977) brought ready-made machines to homes and schools, and the IBM PC (1981) standardized business personal computing. Graphical user interfaces, developed at Xerox PARC and popularized by the Apple Macintosh (1984), made computers accessible to non-specialists.

The Internet era and beyond

The World Wide Web, proposed by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989 and released publicly in the early 1990s, turned networked computers into a mass medium. In the 2000s and 2010s, smartphones, cloud computing, and social media made computing ubiquitous, while advances in parallel processors (GPUs) enabled the deep-learning revolution in artificial intelligence. Current research fronts include quantum computing, neuromorphic hardware, and ever denser integration described by the historical trend of Moore's law.
