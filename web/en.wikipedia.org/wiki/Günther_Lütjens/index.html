Johann Günther Lütjens (25 May 1889 – 27 May 1941) was a German naval officer who served in the Imperial German Navy, the Reichsmarine and the Kriegsmarine. A specialist in torpedo craft, he rose to the rank of admiral and became fleet commander. He is best remembered as the task-force commander of the battleship Bismarck and the heavy cruiser Prinz Eugen during Operation Rheinübung in May 1941, in which he was killed when Bismarck was sunk.

Early career

Lütjens joined the Imperial German Navy in 1907, graduating 21st in a class that included Karl Dönitz. He served in torpedo boats during the First World War, commanding a half-flotilla in Flanders and taking part in raids on the English Channel ports. Retained in the small Reichsmarine after the war, he held staff and command appointments, including command of the light cruiser Karlsruhe and chief of the torpedo-boat arm, and was known as a demanding, reserved and professional seaman.

Second World War

As commander of reconnaissance forces at the outbreak of war, Lütjens took part in minelaying operations and in the invasion of Norway (Operation Weserübung), commanding the distant cover forces. After the death of Wilhelm Marschall's relief in June 1940, he became fleet commander. In January–March 1941 he led Operation Berlin, in which the battlecruisers Scharnhorst and Gneisenau sank or captured 22 merchant ships in the Atlantic — the most successful German surface raiding sortie of the war at that time.

Operation Rheinübung and death

In May 1941 Lütjens commanded Bismarck and Prinz Eugen in Operation Rheinübung, a breakout into the Atlantic. On 24 May his force sank the battlecruiser HMS Hood in the Battle of the Denmark Strait, but Bismarck was damaged and later crippled by torpedo bombers from HMS Ark Royal. Lütjens, pessimistic after the damage to his flagship, sent long radio signals that helped the Royal Navy relocate the ship. He went down with Bismarck when she was sunk by British battleships on 27 May 1941. He was posthumously noted for his final signal vowing to fight to the last shell.

Legacy

The West German Navy named the guided-missile destroyer Lütjens (D185), the lead ship of her class launched in 1967, after him. The character of Lütjens featured in the 1960 film Sink the Bismarck!, portrayed as a stereotypical Nazi — a portrayal disputed by historians and by his family, who note that he was a career officer of the old navy and had protested, in an official capacity, against anti-Jewish measures affecting naval personnel after Kristallnacht.
