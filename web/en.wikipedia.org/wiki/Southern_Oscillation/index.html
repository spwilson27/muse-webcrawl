Southern Oscillation

The Southern Oscillation is a seesaw pattern of atmospheric sea-level pressure between the eastern and western tropical Pacific Ocean. It is the atmospheric component of the coupled ocean–atmosphere phenomenon El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). When pressure is high over the eastern Pacific it tends to be low over the Indo-Pacific region, and vice versa. The oscillation was identified statistically in the 1920s by Sir Gilbert Walker, who sought predictors of the Indian monsoon.

Southern Oscillation Index

The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) is computed from the difference in sea-level pressure between Tahiti (eastern Pacific) and Darwin, Australia (western Pacific), normalized by the long-term standard deviation. Sustained negative SOI values accompany El Niño events (warm eastern Pacific waters, weakened trade winds, and a displaced Walker circulation), while sustained positive values accompany La Niña. Typical sustained values beyond about ±8 are indicative of episodes.

Mechanism and effects

The oscillation reflects changes in the Walker circulation, the great east–west atmospheric overturning over the tropical Pacific. During El Niño, convection shifts eastward, bringing drought to Indonesia and Australia and heavy rain to the central and eastern Pacific and parts of South America; during La Niña the pattern intensifies in the opposite sense. Teleconnections from ENSO influence weather worldwide, and Jacob Bjerknes in the 1960s established that the Southern Oscillation and El Niño are two faces of one coupled system.
