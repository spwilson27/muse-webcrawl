Falsetto (Italian: [falˈsetto]; literally "false little voice") is the vocal register occupying the frequency range just above the modal voice register and overlapping with it by approximately one octave. It is produced by the vibration of the ligamentous edges of the vocal cords, in whole or in part. Commonly cited in reference to the voice of men, it is also a term used by voice teachers for the corresponding lighter production in women, although pedagogues disagree about the terminology.

Physiology

In modal voice, the full vocal folds open and close completely with each vibration, producing a rich spectrum of harmonics. In falsetto, the cricothyroid muscles lengthen and thin the folds, and only the edges vibrate; the folds do not close completely, which gives falsetto its characteristic breathy, flute-like timbre and weaker overtones. The change of register occurs at the passaggio, and untrained singers may experience an audible "break" or crack when switching. Falsetto typically extends about an octave above the modal range but is limited in dynamic range, being difficult to sing loudly.

History and usage

Adult male falsetto singing was cultivated in European church music from the medieval period onward, when women were excluded from church choirs; the countertenor voice, and later the castrato, filled alto lines in polyphony. Falsetto technique remains central to choral traditions, barbershop singing (where the tenor sings largely in falsetto), and numerous popular styles: yodelling alternates rapidly between modal voice and falsetto, Hawaiian music features prominent falsetto (leo kiʻekiʻe), and R&B, soul, funk, and pop singers — from Smokey Robinson and the Bee Gees to Prince, Justin Timberlake, and Sam Smith — have made falsetto a signature sound. Heavy metal and glam rock also exploited high falsetto screams, while comedic falsetto voices are a staple of animation and sketch comedy.

Falsetto and head voice

Voice pedagogy distinguishes falsetto from head voice, though usage varies: in many schools "head voice" denotes a full, resonant upper register with complete fold closure that can be blended smoothly with chest voice, while "falsetto" is reserved for the breathier, disconnected production. Trained countertenors generally sing in a reinforced falsetto that projects in concert halls.
