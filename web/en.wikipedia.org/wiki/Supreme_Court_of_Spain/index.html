The Supreme Court of Spain (Spanish: Tribunal Supremo) is the highest court in the Kingdom of Spain for all matters except constitutional issues, which are reserved to the separate Constitutional Court. It sits at the apex of the ordinary judiciary, hears appeals in cassation from the National Court and the superior courts of the autonomous communities, and its judgments unify legal doctrine throughout the country.

Organisation

The Court is organised into five chambers: the First Chamber (Civil), Second Chamber (Criminal), Third Chamber (Contentious-Administrative), Fourth Chamber (Labour/Social) and Fifth Chamber (Military). Its roughly eighty magistrates are senior judges and jurists of recognised standing. The President of the Supreme Court simultaneously presides over the General Council of the Judiciary, the body responsible for judicial governance and appointments — a dual role that has made the office politically prominent and its appointment occasionally contentious between Spain's main parties.

Jurisdiction and notable cases

The Criminal Chamber tries certain high officeholders enjoying procedural privilege (aforados), including deputies, senators and senior officials; it famously tried Baltasar Garzón (2012) and conducted the 2019 trial of Catalan independence leaders arising from the 2017 referendum, a judgment of exceptional public significance. The Court also rules on the legality of political parties in special proceedings, as in the banning of Batasuna in 2003.

History

A supreme tribunal for Spain was first created under the liberal Constitution of Cádiz in 1812, and the institution took durable shape during the nineteenth century, with the Organic Law of the Judiciary of 1870 structuring its chambers. The present court operates under the Spanish Constitution of 1978 and the Organic Law of the Judicial Power of 1985.
