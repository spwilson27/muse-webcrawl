John Bingham

John Bingham (January 21, 1815 – March 19, 1900) was an American Republican Congressman from Ohio, judge, and diplomat. As principal framer of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, he is often called its father; his Section 1 language on citizenship, privileges or immunities, due process, and equal protection reshaped American constitutional law.

Life and career

Born in Mercer, Pennsylvania, Bingham studied law, moved to Cadiz, Ohio, and served as district attorney. Elected to Congress in 1854, he opposed the expansion of slavery. During the Civil War he served as Judge Advocate General and as a special judge advocate in the Lincoln assassination trial.

Fourteenth Amendment

On the Joint Committee on Reconstruction, Bingham drafted Section 1 of the Fourteenth Amendment (ratified 1868), intending to protect civil rights and apply fundamental rights against the states, overruling Barron v. Baltimore. Scholars debate the original scope of the Privileges or Immunities Clause, narrowed in the Slaughter-House Cases.

Later career

Bingham was a House manager in the impeachment of Andrew Johnson, then served as U.S. Minister to Japan (1873–1885), the longest tenure in that post at the time, supporting Japanese sovereignty and treaty revision.
