In geometry and crystallography, a Bravais lattice, named after Auguste Bravais (1850), is an infinite array of discrete points generated by a set of discrete translation operations R = n₁a₁ + n₂a₂ + n₃a₃, where the ai are primitive vectors and the ni are integers. Every lattice point has an identical environment, and any crystal structure can be described as a Bravais lattice plus a basis of atoms attached to each point. In three dimensions there are exactly 14 distinct Bravais lattices (in two dimensions, five).

The 14 lattices

The 14 lattices arise from combining the seven crystal systems with the possible centreings of the unit cell: primitive (P, lattice points at corners only), body-centred (I, an extra point at the cell centre), face-centred (F, points at the centres of all faces), and base-centred (A, B or C, points on one pair of opposite faces). Not every combination is distinct — some can be redrawn as a smaller cell of another type — and the surviving possibilities are: triclinic P; monoclinic P and C; orthorhombic P, C, I and F; tetragonal P and I; trigonal (rhombohedral) P; hexagonal P; and cubic P, I and F.

Examples and applications

Common elemental structures correspond to the cubic lattices: the simple cubic lattice (rare in nature, e.g. polonium), body-centred cubic (iron, tungsten, chromium, alkali metals), and face-centred cubic (copper, aluminium, nickel, gold). The reciprocal lattice of a Bravais lattice is itself a Bravais lattice — the reciprocal of BCC is FCC and vice versa — a duality central to X-ray diffraction and the theory of Brillouin zones. The Wigner–Seitz cell is the primitive cell constructed as the region of space closer to one lattice point than to any other.
