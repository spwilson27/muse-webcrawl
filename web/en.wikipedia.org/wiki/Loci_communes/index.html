The Loci communes rerum theologicarum ("Commonplaces of Theology", 1521) is a work of systematic theology by the German reformer and humanist Philipp Melanchthon, colleague of Martin Luther. It was the first systematic presentation of the theology of the Protestant Reformation and one of the most influential theological textbooks of the sixteenth century, going through numerous editions and substantial revisions during Melanchthon's lifetime (notably 1535, 1543 and the final Loci praecipui theologici of 1559).

Method and content

The title invokes the classical rhetorical concept of loci communes — the "common places" or topical headings under which material is organised for argument, as taught in the humanist dialectic of Rudolph Agricola and Erasmus. Rather than following the scholastic quaestio format, Melanchthon arranged theology as a series of topics drawn from Scripture, especially Paul's Epistle to the Romans: sin, law, gospel, grace, justification by faith, the distinction between law and gospel, the sacraments, repentance, the church, predestination, and Christian freedom. The 1521 edition deliberately omitted speculative scholastic topics (such as detailed treatment of the Trinity and the two natures of Christ), focusing on what Melanchthon called the "benefits of Christ"; later editions restored fuller doctrinal coverage.

Influence

Luther praised the work extravagantly, ranking it worthy of a place beside Scripture in the canon of theological reading. The Loci became the standard dogmatics textbook in Lutheran universities, shaped the structure of Protestant systematic theology (organised by loci) for two centuries, and influenced Reformed works including Calvin's Institutes, which adopted a similar topical arrangement.
