Pregnancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pregnancy is the time during which one or more offspring (embryos, then fetuses) develop inside a woman's uterus. It typically lasts about 40 weeks from the last menstrual period (about 38 weeks from fertilisation) and is divided into three trimesters. Care during pregnancy — antenatal care — monitors the health of mother and baby and prepares for Childbirth.

Development and changes

After fertilisation and implantation, the embryo forms the placenta through which oxygen and nutrients pass. Major organs form in the first trimester; the fetus then grows, moves, and matures — lungs last — reaching viability outside the womb from about 24 weeks with intensive care. The mother's body adapts: blood volume rises by nearly half, hormones transform metabolism and ligaments, and symptoms range from nausea to back pain.

Care and risks

Antenatal care includes ultrasound scans, screening tests for conditions such as Down syndrome, blood tests, vaccinations and folic acid to prevent neural tube defects. Risks include miscarriage (about 1 in 5 recognised pregnancies, mostly early), ectopic pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes and preterm birth. Globally, maternal deaths have fallen greatly but remain high in low-income settings.
