Nymphaea is a genus of hardy and tender aquatic plants in the family Nymphaeaceae, commonly known as water lilies. The genus has a cosmopolitan distribution, with roughly 65 species found in freshwater habitats on every continent except Antarctica. The name derives from the Greek nymphaia and the Latin nymphaea, after the nymphs of Greek mythology.

Description

Water lilies grow from rhizomes anchored in mud at the bottom of ponds and slow streams. Their round, floating leaves — the familiar "lily pads" — are connected to the rhizome by long flexible stalks and are often notched at one side. The flowers, which float at the surface or rise just above it, are radially symmetrical with numerous petals grading into stamens, and come in white, yellow, pink, red, and blue shades. Many tropical species open only at night; some, such as the Egyptian blue water lily (Nymphaea caerulea) and the sacred lotus-like Nymphaea lotus, are strongly fragrant.

Cultivation and significance

Nymphaea species and their many hybrid cultivars are among the most popular of all ornamental pond plants. The French Impressionist painter Claude Monet made his garden pond at Giverny and its water lilies the subject of some 250 paintings, including the monumental Water Lilies murals. Water lilies also held deep symbolic importance in ancient Egyptian religion and art, and several species have been used traditionally as food and medicine. The giant Amazon water lily, once placed in this genus, is now classified as Victoria amazonica in the same family.
