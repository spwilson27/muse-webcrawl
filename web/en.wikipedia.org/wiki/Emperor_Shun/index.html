Emperor Shun (Chinese: 舜; pinyin: Shùn) was a legendary ruler of ancient China, one of the Five Emperors. He is traditionally regarded as having reigned in the third millennium BC and is celebrated in Confucian tradition as a paragon of filial piety and virtuous government.

Legend

According to the traditional accounts, Shun was born to a blind father, Gusou, and endured mistreatment from his father, stepmother and half-brother Xiang, who repeatedly tried to kill him; yet he responded with unfailing filial devotion. Impressed by his virtue, the sage-king Yao tested him, gave him his two daughters Ehuang and Nüying in marriage, and eventually abdicated the throne to him rather than to his own son—the classic exemplar of meritocratic succession by abdication (shanrang).

Rule and legacy

As emperor, Shun is credited with standardising weights, measures and calendars, touring the realm, and appointing capable officials, including Yu the Great, whom he chose as his own successor after Yu controlled the great floods. Shun was said to have died while on a tour of the south and to have been buried at Mount Jiuyi in present-day Hunan; his wives' tears were said to have spotted the bamboo of the region. Mencius and later Confucians upheld Shun as a model sage.
