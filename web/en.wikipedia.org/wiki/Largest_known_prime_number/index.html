The largest known prime number is 2136,279,841 − 1, a number with 41,024,320 decimal digits, found by Luke Durant using the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search (GIMPS) on 12 October 2024. Euclid proved around 300 BC that there are infinitely many primes, so there is no largest prime — only a largest known one. Since 1992, every record holder has been a Mersenne prime, because very fast primality tests (the Lucas–Lehmer test and its probabilistic successors) exist for numbers of that form.

History

Before computers, the record was held for 75 years by 2127 − 1, proved prime by Édouard Lucas in 1876. Electronic computers took over from 1952, and records grew steadily. The distributed-computing project GIMPS, founded in 1996, has found every record prime since, including 282,589,933 − 1 (24,862,048 digits, 2018). The 2024 record was the first found using a probable-prime (Fermat) test on cloud GPUs rather than the classic Lucas–Lehmer test, and it ended the longest gap between records since the 1990s.

Prizes and motivation

The Electronic Frontier Foundation offers cooperative-computing awards for record primes: US$3,000 was awarded for the first ten-million-digit prime (2009), and larger prizes await the first hundred-million- and billion-digit primes. Beyond the records, the search drives development of fast multiplication algorithms (such as FFT-based methods) used throughout computational number theory.
