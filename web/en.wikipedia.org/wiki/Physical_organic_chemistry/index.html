Physical organic chemistry

Physical organic chemistry, a term coined by Louis Hammett in 1940, is the study of the relationship between chemical structure and reactivity in organic molecules, using the tools and theories of physical chemistry. It seeks to understand organic reactions quantitatively in terms of structure, energetics, kinetics, and mechanism.

Scope

Central topics include reaction mechanisms, reactive intermediates such as carbocations, carbanions, radicals and carbenes, linear free-energy relationships (including the Hammett equation), kinetic isotope effects, solvent effects, stereochemistry, and computational modelling of transition states.

History

The discipline developed in the early 20th century through the work of chemists such as Christopher Ingold, Robert Robinson, and Hammett, who applied kinetic and thermodynamic methods to organic reactions. It was codified in Hammett's 1940 book Physical Organic Chemistry and Eric Anslyn and Robert Dougherty's modern textbook Modern Physical Organic Chemistry (2006).

Modern physical organic chemistry extends to supramolecular chemistry, organocatalysis, photochemistry, and the study of enzyme mechanisms.
