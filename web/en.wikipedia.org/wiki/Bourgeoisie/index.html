Bourgeoisie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nineteenth-century bourgeois family in a well-furnished Parisian parlour
A nineteenth-century bourgeois family in a well-furnished Parisian parlour

The bourgeoisie is a social class of business owners, professionals and property holders, originally the inhabitants of a bourg (town). In Marxist theory the term denotes the capitalist class that owns the Means of production and employs wage labour, standing in opposition to the Proletariat; in general usage it describes the middle and upper-middle classes and their values of property, respectability and thrift.

History

Burghers emerged in the walled towns of medieval Europe as merchants and master craftsmen between the nobility and the peasantry. Over the early modern period they accumulated wealth through trade, banking and manufacture while remaining politically subordinate; the French Revolution of 1789 — driven in part by bourgeois demands for legal equality — opened the modern era of bourgeois political ascendancy, consolidated by the July Revolution of 1830 and the liberal reforms of the nineteenth century.

Marxist usage

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels opened The Communist Manifesto (1848) with the bourgeoisie's revolutionary role: it had 'played a most revolutionary part', dissolving feudal ties, creating the world market and modern industry. Yet by concentrating production and socialising labour, it produced its own 'grave-diggers', the proletariat — a contradiction that Marxists predicted would end in revolution.

Culture and criticism

'Bourgeois' became a cultural epithet as much as an economic category: Gustave Flaubert and later the avant-gardes mocked bourgeois taste as philistine ('épater le bourgeois' — to shock the bourgeois). Sociologists such as Pierre Bourdieu analysed how the class reproduces itself through cultural as well as economic capital, while historians distinguish a grande, moyenne and petite bourgeoisie.
