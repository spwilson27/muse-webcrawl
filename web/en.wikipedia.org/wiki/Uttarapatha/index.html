Uttarapatha


The great trade routes of ancient India

Uttarapatha is an ancient term referring both to the "Northern Route" — the great east–west highway of ancient India — and, by extension, to the northern region it traversed, roughly spanning the Indo-Gangetic Plain to the northwest frontiers and Central Asia. Together with the Dakshinapatha (the "Southern Route" into the Deccan), it formed one of the two great axes of overland communication and commerce in early historic South Asia.

The route

The Uttarapatha ran from the Bengal delta and Pataliputra in the east, westward through Prayaga, Mathura and the Punjab to Taxila (Takshashila) in Gandhara, and onward across the Hindu Kush toward Bactria and the Central Asian routes that connected with the Silk Road. Cities along it, such as Varanasi, Kaushambi, Shravasti and Taxila, grew wealthy on the trade in textiles, horses, metals, gems and luxury goods; Taxila in particular became a renowned centre of learning where students from across India studied medicine, law and military science.

Historical references

The term appears in early Sanskrit and Pali literature: the epics, the Jatakas, and the grammatical tradition associated with Panini, himself a native of the northwest. Buddhist texts describe merchants and monks travelling the route, and it remained strategically vital under the Mauryan empire, whose administration maintained royal highways. In later usage "Uttarapatha" could denote the northern division of India generally, in implied contrast to the Deccan and the far south.
