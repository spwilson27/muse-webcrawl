The analysis of competing hypotheses (ACH) is a methodology for evaluating multiple competing hypotheses for observed data. It was developed by Richards (Dick) Heuer, a veteran of the US Central Intelligence Agency, and is described in his book Psychology of Intelligence Analysis (1999). ACH is designed to avoid common cognitive pitfalls in analysis, especially confirmation bias, by focusing on disproving hypotheses rather than confirming a favoured one.

Procedure

The method proceeds in eight steps:

  1. Identify the possible hypotheses to be considered, ideally using input from analysts with different perspectives.
  2. List significant evidence and arguments for and against each hypothesis.
  3. Prepare a matrix with hypotheses across the top and evidence down the side, and analyse the "diagnosticity" of each item — its ability to distinguish between hypotheses.
  4. Refine the matrix; delete evidence that has no diagnostic value and reconsider the hypotheses.
  5. Draw tentative conclusions about the relative likelihood of each hypothesis, proceeding by trying to disprove hypotheses.
  6. Analyse how sensitive the conclusion is to a few critical items of evidence.
  7. Report conclusions, discussing the relative likelihood of all hypotheses.
  8. Identify milestones for future observation that may change the assessment.

Rationale

Cognitive psychology research shows that analysts tend to seek evidence confirming an early-favoured hypothesis and overlook inconsistencies. ACH counters this by making the comparative evaluation systematic and explicit: attention is directed to evidence that is inconsistent with hypotheses, and the hypothesis with the least inconsistent evidence — not the most supporting evidence — is favoured.

Uses and criticism

ACH has been adopted and taught by intelligence agencies in the United States and allied countries, and has been applied in criminal investigation, competitive business intelligence, and medical diagnosis. Critics note that it is time-consuming, that scoring in the matrix can convey a false sense of precision, and that outcomes depend on the quality of the hypotheses and evidence entered.
