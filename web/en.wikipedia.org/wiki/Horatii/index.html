The Horatii were three brothers of the Roman gens Horatia who, according to legend, fought as champions of Rome against the three Curiatii brothers of Alba Longa during the reign of King Tullus Hostilius in the 7th century BC. The combat was to decide a war between the two cities without a full battle between their armies. The story, most fully told by Livy (Ab urbe condita 1.24–26), became a celebrated exemplum of Roman patriotism — devotion to the state placed above family ties.

The legend

To spare the armies, the cities agreed that sets of triplet brothers — the Horatii for Rome and the Curiatii for Alba — would fight, the victors' city to rule the other. The families were tragically intertwined: the Horatii's sister, Horatia, was engaged to one of the Curiatii, while the Curiatii's sister was married to one of the Horatii. In the duel, two Horatii were quickly killed, but the surviving brother, Publius Horatius, pretended to flee and, as the wounded Curiatii pursued at different speeds, turned and killed them one by one.

Returning in triumph, Horatius met his sister, who, seeing her slain fiancé's cloak among the spoils, burst into tears; enraged, Horatius killed her, saying "So perish any Roman woman who mourns an enemy." Tried for murder, he was condemned but acquitted on appeal to the people, after his father argued that the family's loss was enough — though ritual expiations (the "sister's beam", tigillum sororium) were established in memory of the act.

Historicity and interpretation

The duel is generally treated as legend rather than history, though the war and Rome's destruction of Alba Longa under Tullus Hostilius belong to Rome's early traditions. Scholars have compared the triple-champion motif with other Indo-European traditions and studied the legal themes of the trial story, which Livy presents as the first case of provocatio (appeal to the people).

In art and drama

Pierre Corneille's tragedy Horace (1640) dramatised the conflict of duty and love. Jacques-Louis David's painting The Oath of the Horatii (1784) — showing the brothers swearing before their father — became an icon of Neoclassicism and of civic virtue in revolutionary France.
