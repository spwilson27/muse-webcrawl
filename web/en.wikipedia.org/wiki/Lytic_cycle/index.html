Lytic cycle

Lytic cycle
FieldVirology
AgentsVirulent viruses, esp. bacteriophages
OutcomeHost cell lysis; release of progeny virions

The lytic cycle is one of the two cycles of viral reproduction (the other being the lysogenic cycle). It results in the destruction (lysis) of the infected cell and its membrane, releasing new virions. Viruses that reproduce only by the lytic cycle are called virulent; the classic model is bacteriophage T4 infecting Escherichia coli.

Stages

  1. Attachment — the virus binds specific receptors on the host cell surface.
  2. Penetration / entry — the viral genome enters the cell (in phages, by injection through the tail apparatus).
  3. Biosynthesis — the viral genome redirects host machinery to replicate viral DNA and synthesise viral proteins; host DNA is often degraded and recycled.
  4. Maturation / assembly — new capsids are assembled and packaged with viral genomes.
  5. Release (lysis) — viral enzymes such as lysozyme (endolysin) weaken the cell wall; the cell bursts, releasing tens to hundreds of progeny virions that infect neighbouring cells.

Timing and yield

For phage T4 the whole cycle takes roughly 20–30 minutes at 37 °C, releasing about 100–200 new particles per cell (the "burst size"). Growth-curve experiments (the one-step growth experiment of Ellis and Delbrück, 1939) distinguish the eclipse period, when no infectious particles exist inside the cell, from the later rise period.

Animal viruses

Many animal viruses follow a comparable lytic pattern, though enveloped viruses often exit by budding without immediate lysis. Cytopathic lytic replication underlies much of the tissue damage in diseases such as influenza and poliomyelitis.
