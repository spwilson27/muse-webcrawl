Diglyceride

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A diglyceride, or diacylglycerol (DAG), is a glyceride consisting of two fatty acid chains covalently bonded to a glycerol molecule through ester linkages. Two possible forms exist, 1,2-diacylglycerols and 1,3-diacylglycerols. DAGs can act as surfactants and are commonly used as emulsifiers in processed foods. DAG-enriched oil (particularly 1,3-DAG) has been investigated extensively as a fat substitute, as it is less readily stored as body fat than triglycerides.

Biological significance

In cell signaling, diacylglycerol functions as a second messenger. It is produced by the hydrolysis of the phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) by the enzyme phospholipase C, which also produces inositol trisphosphate (IP3). DAG remains in the cell membrane and recruits and activates protein kinase C, while IP3 diffuses into the cytosol and triggers release of calcium ions from intracellular stores.

The effects of DAG can be mimicked by the tumor-promoting phorbol esters, which bind to the same site on protein kinase C. The bacterial enzyme diacylglycerol kinase phosphorylates DAG to produce phosphatidic acid, terminating the signal.

Food use

Mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471) are widely used as emulsifiers in food products such as bread, ice cream, and margarine. Cooking oil enriched in 1,3-diacylglycerol was marketed in Japan and the United States; after ingestion, 1,3-DAG is metabolized differently from triglycerides, with a larger fraction oxidized for energy rather than deposited in adipose tissue.
