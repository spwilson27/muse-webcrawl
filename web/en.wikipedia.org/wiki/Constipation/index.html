Constipation

Constipation is a symptom, rather than a disease, defined by hard, dry bowel movements or passing stool fewer than three times a week. It is one of the most common digestive complaints, affecting a substantial fraction of the general population, and is more frequent in women and older adults. Severe constipation can lead to fecal impaction and, rarely, bowel obstruction.

Signs and symptoms

People with constipation typically strain during bowel movements, pass small hard stools, and feel a sense of incomplete emptying. Associated symptoms include abdominal pain, bloating, and loss of appetite. The Bristol stool scale types 1 and 2 describe stools typical of constipation.

Causes

Common causes include a diet low in fiber, inadequate fluid intake, sedentary lifestyle, and ignoring the urge to defecate. Medications such as opioids, iron supplements, and some antidepressants and calcium-channel blockers can slow colonic transit. Medical conditions including hypothyroidism, diabetes, Parkinson's disease, and disorders of the pelvic floor or colon may also cause constipation. In children, constipation often begins around toilet training.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually based on symptoms. The Rome criteria require a combination of straining, hard stools, sensation of incomplete evacuation or obstruction, and fewer than three defecations per week over several months. Blood tests, imaging, or colonoscopy may be used when warning signs such as rectal bleeding, weight loss, or a family history of colon cancer are present.

Treatment

First-line management is lifestyle change: more dietary fiber, adequate hydration, and physical activity. If these are insufficient, laxatives are used, including bulk-forming agents (psyllium), osmotic agents (polyethylene glycol, lactulose), and stimulant laxatives (senna, bisacodyl) for short-term use. Biofeedback therapy may help when pelvic floor dysfunction is the cause.
