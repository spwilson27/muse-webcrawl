Hypothermia

Hypothermia is defined as a core body temperature below 35.0 °C (95.0 °F) in humans. It occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it, most commonly from exposure to cold weather or immersion in cold water. Symptoms depend on the temperature: shivering and mental confusion characterise mild hypothermia; in severe hypothermia shivering stops, consciousness is lost, and the risk of cardiac arrest rises sharply. Treatment centres on careful rewarming and supportive care.

Classification

Hypothermia is classified by core temperature as mild (32–35 °C), moderate (28–32 °C), and severe (below 28 °C), though clinical staging systems such as the Swiss system grade patients by their signs instead, from a conscious shivering patient (HT I) to an apparently dead patient (HT IV).

Signs and symptoms

In mild hypothermia the person shivers vigorously, feels cold and numb, and may show impaired judgement and fine motor skills — the "umbles": stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, and grumbles. As temperature falls below about 32 °C, shivering ceases, heart rate and breathing slow, and confusion progresses to stupor. The heart becomes irritable and prone to arrhythmias including ventricular fibrillation. Below 28 °C the patient may appear dead, with fixed dilated pupils and no detectable pulse — yet survival with full recovery is possible, giving rise to the maxim that a hypothermic patient "is not dead until warm and dead."

Causes

Primary hypothermia results from environmental exposure in a healthy person, classically in mountaineers, shipwreck survivors, and the homeless. Secondary hypothermia arises from illness impairing thermoregulation, including hypothyroidism, sepsis, stroke, and hypoglycaemia. Alcohol is a major contributing factor: it causes vasodilation, impairs shivering and judgement, and is associated with many urban hypothermia deaths. Infants and the elderly are at particular risk.

Pathophysiology

The hypothalamus regulates temperature near 37 °C. Cold exposure triggers vasoconstriction and shivering thermogenesis. With prolonged exposure, energy stores deplete, metabolic rate falls by roughly 6–7% per degree Celsius, and cold-induced diuresis depletes intravascular volume. During rescue and rewarming, cold peripheral blood returning to the core can cause a further fall in temperature known as afterdrop.

Treatment

Management begins with gentle handling (rough movement can precipitate arrhythmias), removal from the cold, insulation, and removal of wet clothing. Mild cases respond to passive external rewarming and warm drinks if the patient can swallow. Moderate and severe cases require active rewarming: warmed intravenous fluids, heated humidified oxygen, body-cavity lavage, and in cardiac arrest or instability, extracorporeal life support such as cardiopulmonary bypass or ECMO, which offers the best outcomes in hypothermic arrest. Frostbite, a related cold injury, is managed separately.

Paradoxical undressing

A significant minority of fatal hypothermia cases are found partly or fully undressed, a phenomenon called paradoxical undressing, thought to result from cold-induced failure of vasoconstriction producing a sudden sensation of warmth, or from hypothalamic dysfunction. Some victims also exhibit "terminal burrowing" (hide-and-die syndrome), crawling into enclosed spaces before death.

Therapeutic hypothermia

Deliberate cooling — targeted temperature management — has been used to protect the brain after cardiac arrest and in neonatal hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, though modern practice often targets normothermia with strict fever prevention.
