Search and rescue

Search and rescue (SAR) is the search for and provision of aid to people who are in distress or imminent danger. It encompasses many sub-fields, including mountain rescue, ground search and rescue, urban search and rescue in collapsed buildings, combat search and rescue on the battlefield, and air-sea rescue over water. Operations are typically carried out by a combination of professional services — coast guards, military units, police and fire services — and trained volunteers.

Types of search and rescue

Ground search and rescue

Ground SAR concerns people lost or injured on land, often in wilderness areas. Searchers use techniques such as grid searches, tracking, and search dogs, guided by search theory and statistics on lost-person behaviour.

Mountain rescue

Mountain rescue teams operate in steep, high or snowy terrain, using ropes, stretchers and helicopters to reach and evacuate casualties.

Urban search and rescue

Urban search and rescue (USAR) involves locating and extricating victims trapped in collapsed structures, typically after earthquakes or explosions. Heavy USAR task forces deploy search cameras, listening devices, sniffer dogs and engineers.

Air-sea rescue

Air-sea rescue coordinates aircraft and surface vessels to recover survivors from the water, often far from shore. It developed extensively during the Second World War to recover downed aircrew.

Combat search and rescue

Combat search and rescue (CSAR) is the recovery of personnel, especially aircrew, from hostile territory during wartime.

International organisation

Maritime and aeronautical SAR is organised internationally under conventions such as the International Convention on Maritime Search and Rescue (1979) and the Chicago Convention. The world is divided into search and rescue regions, each with a rescue coordination centre (RCC) responsible for coordinating responses. Satellites of the Cospas-Sarsat system detect distress beacons such as EPIRBs and personal locator beacons, greatly improving response times.
