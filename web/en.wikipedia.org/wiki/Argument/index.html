Argument

In logic and philosophy, an argument is a series of statements, called the premises, intended to determine the degree of truth of another statement, the conclusion. The study of arguments belongs to logic, where it is contrasted with mere assertions, explanations, and quarrels; in everyday usage "argument" may also mean a dispute, but its technical sense is a structured attempt at rational persuasion or proof.


An argument moves from premises to a conclusion by an inferential step.

Forms of argument

Deductive arguments aim for validity: if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false, as in a syllogism. Inductive arguments support their conclusion with probability, as in generalizations from samples and inference to the best explanation. Abductive reasoning infers the most plausible cause of an observed effect. Arguments are evaluated by the truth of their premises and the strength of the inference; a deductive argument with true premises and valid form is called sound.

Fallacies

Defective arguments commit fallacies — for example the straw man, ad hominem, appeal to ignorance, begging the question, and false dilemma. Informal logic and argumentation theory, from Aristotle's Rhetoric and Topics to Stephen Toulmin's model of claim, grounds, and warrant, study how real arguments work in discourse.
