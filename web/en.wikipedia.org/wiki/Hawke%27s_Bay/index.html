Hawke's Bay

Hawke's Bay (Māori: Te Matau-a-Māui) is a region on the east coast of New Zealand's North Island, named for the large bay formed by the curve of the coastline, which Captain James Cook named in honour of Admiral Sir Edward Hawke in 1769. The region's main centres are Napier and Hastings, and it is renowned for its wine production, Art Deco architecture and Mediterranean-like climate.

History

The area has a long history of Māori settlement; the bay's Māori name, Te Matau-a-Māui ("the fishhook of Māui"), refers to the shape of the coastline. European settlement began in the 1850s, and the Hawke's Bay Province was established in 1858 before being abolished with the provinces in 1876. On 3 February 1931 the region was devastated by the Hawke's Bay earthquake, which killed 256 people and destroyed much of Napier and Hastings; Napier was rebuilt in the Art Deco style for which it is now famous.

Geography and economy

The region encompasses the fertile Heretaunga and Ruataniwha plains, hill country inland, and the Kaweka and Ruahine ranges to the west. Agriculture, horticulture — especially pipfruit and wine grapes — and food processing dominate the economy. Hawke's Bay is New Zealand's second-largest wine region, noted for red blends and Chardonnay from areas such as the Gimblett Gravels.
