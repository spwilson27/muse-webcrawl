Free software movement

The free software movement is a social movement with the goal of obtaining and guaranteeing certain freedoms for software users, namely the freedom to run, study, modify, and share software. Software that meets these criteria is called free software — "free" as in freedom, not necessarily price. The movement was launched by Richard Stallman in 1983 with the GNU Project and the founding of the Free Software Foundation (FSF) in 1985.

Philosophy

The Free Software Definition lists four essential freedoms: (0) to run the program for any purpose; (1) to study and change it, with access to source code; (2) to redistribute copies; and (3) to distribute modified versions. Stallman's 1985 GNU Manifesto argued that proprietary software is an ethical problem because it divides and renders users helpless, and proposed building a complete free operating system (GNU) compatible with Unix.

Copyleft and licenses

To protect these freedoms in law, Stallman devised copyleft: copyright licensing that requires derivative works to be distributed under the same freedoms. The GNU General Public License (GPL), first released in 1989, became the most widely used free software license, covering the Linux kernel and many other projects. Other licenses include the LGPL, the AGPL, and permissive licenses such as the BSD and Apache licenses.

History and influence

The release of the Linux kernel (1991) completed a widely used free operating system in combination with GNU tools. In 1998, part of the community began promoting the term "open source" to emphasise practical and business advantages rather than ethics; the two movements share most software but differ in philosophy. Free software now underpins most of the Internet's infrastructure, supercomputers, and the Android operating system.
