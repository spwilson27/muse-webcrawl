Invention of tradition

The invention of tradition is a concept popularised by historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger in their 1983 edited volume The Invention of Tradition. An "invented tradition" is a set of practices, normally governed by overtly or tacitly accepted rules and of a ritual or symbolic nature, which seeks to inculcate certain values and norms of behaviour by repetition, and which automatically implies continuity with a suitable historic past — even though that continuity is largely factitious.

The argument

Hobsbawm distinguished invented traditions from genuine "custom", which changes slowly and organically. Invented traditions, by contrast, arise rapidly — often within a few decades — in response to novel situations: nation-building, colonial rule, monarchy's reinvention in an age of mass politics, and the ceremonial needs of new institutions. Examples studied in the volume include the Highland tradition of Scotland (analysed by Hugh Trevor-Roper, who argued that kilts, tartans and clan identities were largely eighteenth- and nineteenth-century constructions), the rituals of the British monarchy, Welsh cultural revivalism, and colonial ceremonies in Africa and India.

Reception

The concept became highly influential across history, anthropology and nationalism studies, and was applied to subjects from national anthems and holidays to martial arts and cuisine. Critics have cautioned that the dichotomy between "genuine" and "invented" traditions can be overdrawn, since all traditions involve transmission and re-creation, and that the term can be misused to delegitimise practices meaningful to their participants.
