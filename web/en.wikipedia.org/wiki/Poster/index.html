Poster

Poster
MediumPrinted paper, usually lithography or screen print
PioneersJules Chéret, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, Alphonse Mucha
UsesAdvertising, propaganda, film, events, protest

A poster is a large printed sheet of paper, typically combining text and graphic imagery, designed to be displayed in a public place to promote an event, product, idea or cause. Posters are among the most visible forms of graphic design, and the poster art of the late nineteenth century — when colour lithography made large runs cheap — is regarded as a significant chapter in the history of art.

Advertising posters on a Morris column in Paris.

History

Printed public notices and playbills existed for centuries, but the modern poster emerged in Paris in the 1870s and 1880s after Jules Chéret developed improved colour lithography, enabling posters with vivid imagery and integrated lettering. Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec's posters for the Moulin Rouge (from 1891) and Alphonse Mucha's Art Nouveau designs, such as Gismonda (1894), made the poster a collectible art form — a phenomenon sometimes called affichomanie, or poster mania.

Twentieth century

Posters became a major instrument of state propaganda in the First World War (Lord Kitchener Wants You; James Montgomery Flagg's Uncle Sam), in revolutionary Russia (constructivist designs by Alexander Rodchenko and El Lissitzky), and in the Second World War. In Switzerland and Germany the Plakatstil ("poster style") and later the International Typographic Style favoured bold, simplified imagery. The psychedelic posters of 1960s San Francisco, Polish film posters, and Shepard Fairey's "Hope" poster for Barack Obama's 2008 campaign are among later landmarks.

Types

  • Advertising posters for products, films and theatre.
  • Propaganda and political posters, including election and protest posters.
  • Educational and public-health posters.
  • Decorative posters and reproductions sold for home display.
  • Academic posters presented at scholarly conferences.

Production

Posters were traditionally produced by lithography and later by screen printing and offset printing; today most are printed digitally. Standard sizes include the French affiche formats, the British quad and double crown, and the one-sheet used for films.
