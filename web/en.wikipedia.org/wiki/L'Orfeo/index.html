L'Orfeo

L'Orfeo (SV 318), sometimes called La favola d'Orfeo ("The Fable of Orpheus"), is a late Renaissance/early Baroque favola in musica, or opera, by Claudio Monteverdi, with a libretto by Alessandro Striggio the Younger. It is based on the Greek legend of Orpheus, telling the story of his descent into Hades and his fruitless attempt to bring his dead bride Eurydice back to the living world.

Title page of Monteverdi's L'Orfeo, published in Venice, 1609

Written in 1607 for a court performance during the annual Carnival at Mantua, L'Orfeo was first performed on 24 February 1607 in the ducal palace before members of the Accademia degli Invaghiti. It is the earliest surviving opera still regularly performed, and one of the earliest works of the genre whose music has come down to us; Monteverdi published the score in Venice in 1609, with a second edition in 1615.

Roles and structure

The work is in a prologue and five acts. The prologue introduces La Musica (Music personified), who sets the scene. Act 1 celebrates the wedding of Orfeo and Euridice; in Act 2 a messenger brings news of Euridice's death from a snakebite; Act 3 sees Orfeo descend to Hades and charm Caronte (Charon) with the aria "Possente spirto"; in Act 4 Proserpina persuades Plutone to release Euridice, but Orfeo violates the condition that he must not look back, and loses her; Act 5 has Orfeo lamenting on Earth before Apollo descends and raises him to the heavens.

Music

Monteverdi's score calls for an unusually large and varied orchestra for its time — some 41 instruments including cornetts, sackbuts, recorders, strings, and a rich continuo group — and specifies much of its instrumentation, an exceptional practice for the period. The famous opening toccata, played three times, became a fanfare of the Gonzaga court. The opera bridges Renaissance polyphony and the new monodic style, and its recitatives, arias, madrigal-style choruses, and instrumental ritornellos establish many conventions of Baroque opera.

Legacy

After its initial performances, L'Orfeo fell into obscurity with the rest of Monteverdi's stage works until a scholarly revival in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It is now celebrated as a masterwork that marks the beginning of the operatic tradition.
