Beatmatching

Beatmatching is a disc jockey technique of pitch shifting or time stretching an upcoming track to match its tempo to that of the currently playing track, and adjusting them so that the beats (and, usually, the bars) are synchronised — that is, the kicks and snares in two house tracks hit at the same time. It is a foundational skill of DJing, allowing seamless transitions between songs in a continuous mix.


A DJ cueing and pitch-adjusting a record to beatmatch

Technique

The tempo of a track is measured in beats per minute (BPM). To beatmatch, the DJ listens in headphones to the incoming track (cueing) while the current track plays through the speakers. Using the turntable's pitch control or a digital jog wheel / sync-free pitch fader, the DJ speeds up or slows down the incoming track until its beats align with those of the outgoing track. Small nudges to the platter or jog wheel correct phase drift.

Once matched, the two tracks can run in parallel for an extended blend, the crossfader or channel faders gradually shifting the mix from one to the other. Classic vinyl beatmatching relies entirely on the ear; modern digital systems display BPM readouts and waveforms, and many controllers offer a "sync" button that performs the tempo and phase alignment automatically — a feature that has prompted ongoing debate about skill and artistry in DJ culture.

History

Beatmatching emerged in the 1970s in the discos of New York, pioneered by DJs such as Francis Grasso, who is often credited with developing the technique on turntables, and was refined through the disco, house and techno eras. The introduction of variable-pitch turntables such as the Technics SL-1200 (with ±8% pitch adjustment) standardised the practice.
