Segregation in materials

In materials science, segregation is the enrichment of a constituent in a specific region of a material system, or the depletion of that constituent in another region. Segregation occurs at surfaces, grain boundaries, interfaces, and during solidification, and it can strongly influence properties such as strength, ductility, corrosion resistance, and electrical behaviour.

Types

Surface segregation is the enrichment of one component at the free surface of an alloy or mixture. Grain boundary segregation is the enrichment of impurities or alloying elements at grain boundaries; it can cause phenomena such as temper embrittlement in steels, where elements like phosphorus and antimony migrate to boundaries. Microsegregation and macrosegregation describe compositional variations arising during casting as solute is rejected into the remaining liquid (described by the Scheil equation), producing "coring" in dendrites and large-scale inhomogeneity in ingots.

Mechanisms

Segregation is driven by thermodynamics: solutes move to sites where they lower the total energy of the system, for example by relieving strain or reducing interfacial energy. Equilibrium segregation is often modelled by the Langmuir–McLean isotherm. In granular materials, segregation instead refers to the separation of particles by size during handling (the "Brazil nut effect").
