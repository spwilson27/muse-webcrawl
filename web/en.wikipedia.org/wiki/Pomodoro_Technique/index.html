The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. It uses a kitchen timer to break work into focused intervals, traditionally 25 minutes long, separated by short breaks. Each interval is a pomodoro, from the Italian word for tomato, after the tomato-shaped timer Cirillo used as a university student.

Procedure

  1. Choose a task to be done.
  2. Set the timer to 25 minutes and work on the task until it rings; interruptions are noted and deferred.
  3. Take a short break (about 5 minutes).
  4. After four pomodoros, take a longer break (15–30 minutes), then repeat.

The method rests on the idea that frequent breaks improve mental agility, and on breaking large tasks into small, committed units to reduce the anxiety of starting and to make time estimation concrete. A pomodoro is indivisible: if it is interrupted beyond a brief moment, it is voided and restarted.

Tools and reception

Originally paper-and-pencil — a mechanical timer, a to-do sheet and an activity inventory — the technique is now supported by numerous apps and is popular among students and software developers, including in pair programming. Critics note that rigid 25-minute blocks can clash with collaborative work, meetings and tasks that benefit from longer flow states; practitioners commonly adapt interval length to taste.
