Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, commonly known as the Non-Proliferation Treaty or NPT, is an international treaty whose objective is to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and weapons technology, to promote cooperation in the peaceful uses of nuclear energy and to further the goal of nuclear Disarmament. Opened for signature in 1968 and in force from 5 March 1970, it has 191 parties — nearly every state — with India, Israel and Pakistan never having joined and North Korea having announced withdrawal in 2003 and developed weapons thereafter.

The treaty's bargain has three pillars: non-nuclear-weapon states renounce nuclear weapons and accept IAEA safeguards (Article II–III); all parties may use nuclear energy peacefully (Article IV); and the five recognised nuclear-weapon states (the US, Russia, the UK, France and China — those that had tested before 1 January 1967) undertake to pursue negotiations toward disarmament (Article VI). Review conferences every five years have repeatedly strained over the disarmament pillar's pace, Middle East zone resolutions, and compliance crises (Iraq in the 1990s, Iran, North Korea). Despite these strains the NPT is credited with holding the number of nuclear-armed states far below the 1960s forecasts of fifteen to twenty-five.
