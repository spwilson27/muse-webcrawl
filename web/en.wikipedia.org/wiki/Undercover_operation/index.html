Undercover operation

Undercover operation
PractitionersPolice, intelligence agencies, regulatory bodies
PurposesEvidence gathering, infiltration, test purchases
Related conceptsSting operation, espionage, entrapment

An undercover operation is an investigation technique in which a law-enforcement officer, agent or cooperator conceals their official identity in order to gather evidence of criminal activity, infiltrate an organisation, or observe conduct that would not occur in front of a uniformed officer. It ranges from brief "test purchases" of contraband to long-term deep-cover infiltration lasting years.

Types

Short-term operations include decoy and buy-bust work against street drug markets and stolen-goods fences, often fronted by undercover storefronts. Long-term infiltration targets organised crime, gangs, trafficking networks and terrorist cells, requiring a fabricated biography (a "legend"), back-stopped documents and handler support. In intelligence, the analogous practice shades into espionage. Regulatory agencies also use undercover inspectors and "mystery shoppers" to test compliance.

Risks and legal issues

Undercover work is dangerous: discovery can be fatal, and prolonged double lives are associated with psychological strain and corruption risk. Operations raise legal and ethical questions about entrapment — inducing a person to commit a crime they were not predisposed to commit — agent provocateur conduct, and the authorisation of officers' own criminality, which many jurisdictions now regulate by statute, such as the UK's "spy cops" legislation following revelations about long-term police infiltration of activist groups.

Notable examples

Famous cases include FBI agent Joseph Pistone's six-year infiltration of the Bonanno crime family as "Donnie Brasco", and the UK undercover policing scandal investigated by the Undercover Policing Inquiry.
