Skid Row, Los Angeles

Encampments on a Skid Row sidewalk

Skid Row is a neighborhood in Downtown Los Angeles. The area, officially known as Central City East, lies roughly between Third and Seventh Streets east of Main Street. It contains one of the largest stable populations of homeless people in the United States, estimated in the thousands, and has been the center of homelessness services and controversy over city policy for decades.

History

The district grew in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries around the rail yards and inexpensive single-room-occupancy hotels serving transient laborers, seasonal agricultural workers, and railroad men. Bars, missions, and cheap lodging houses concentrated services for poor and homeless people in the area; rescue missions such as the Union Rescue Mission (founded 1891) and the Los Angeles Mission became fixtures.

Containment policy

From the 1970s, city officials informally pursued a "containment" policy, concentrating shelters and services in Skid Row rather than dispersing them. The 2006 settlement in Jones v. City of Los Angeles restricted enforcement against sleeping on sidewalks when no shelter beds were available, and litigation over encampment sweeps and property seizures has continued since. Skid Row City Limit, a mural and boundary markers installed in 2014, mark the area claimed by residents as their community.

Services and institutions

The neighborhood hosts missions, clinics, needle exchanges, permanent supportive housing projects, and the Skid Row Housing Trust. It sits adjacent to the gentrifying Arts District, Historic Core, and Little Tokyo, producing ongoing tension between development and the resident population.
