The Ypres Salient was the area around Ypres in Belgium that formed a bulge, or salient, in the British front line on the Western Front during the First World War. Because the German armies held the higher ground of the ridges surrounding the town on three sides, troops in the salient could be fired on from multiple directions, and the position — though tactically disadvantageous — was held throughout the war for strategic, political and symbolic reasons.

Formation and geography

The salient took shape in October–November 1914 during the Race to the Sea and the First Battle of Ypres, when the British Expeditionary Force stopped the German advance toward the Channel ports. It protruded some 6 miles (10 km) into German-held territory and was overlooked by the Messines, Passchendaele and Broodseinde ridges. Ypres itself, a historic cloth town, was shelled to ruins, including its medieval Cloth Hall and cathedral, both later rebuilt.

Major battles

  • First Ypres (Oct–Nov 1914): the BEF held the salient against massed German attacks at Langemarck and Gheluvelt.
  • Second Ypres (Apr–May 1915): Germany released chlorine gas on 22 April 1915, the first successful large-scale gas attack; Canadian troops played a celebrated role in holding the line.
  • Battle of Messines (June 1917): nineteen huge mines were detonated under the Messines ridge in preparation for the summer offensive.
  • Third Ypres / Passchendaele (Jul–Nov 1917): a grinding offensive in unprecedented mud; the village of Passchendaele was finally taken by Canadian forces, at a cost of hundreds of thousands of casualties on all sides. The name became a byword for futile slaughter.
  • Fourth Ypres (Battle of the Lys) (April 1918) and Fifth Ypres (Sep–Oct 1918): the German spring offensive nearly took Ypres before the Allied Hundred Days offensive swept the salient away.

Legacy

The salient is densely covered with Commonwealth War Graves Commission cemeteries — Tyne Cot, the largest Commonwealth war cemetery in the world, lies on the Passchendaele ridge — and memorials such as the Menin Gate in Ypres, where the Last Post has been sounded nightly since 1928 (except during the German occupation in the Second World War). John McCrae wrote "In Flanders Fields", the war's most famous poem, during Second Ypres in May 1915.
