Computability theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A ladder diagram of problem classes from decidable up to uncomputable]

Computability theory, also called recursion theory, is the branch of mathematical logic and computer science that asks which problems can be solved by an algorithm at all, and classifies problems by their degree of unsolvability.

It was created in the 1930s by Alonzo Church, Alan Turing, Kurt Gödel, Emil Post and Stephen Kleene, whose different formalisms — lambda calculus, Turing machines, recursive functions — provably capture the same class of computable functions.

Landmarks

The halting problem is the canonical unsolvable problem; thousands of others (tiling the plane, deciding whether a Diophantine equation has solutions) are shown unsolvable by reduction to it or its kin.

The theory's surprising depths include the arithmetical hierarchy, which stratifies unsolvable problems by logical complexity, and priority arguments, among the most intricate proof techniques in logic.
