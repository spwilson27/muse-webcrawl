Government budget

A government budget is a document prepared by the government and/or other political entities presenting its anticipated tax revenues (inheritance tax, income tax, corporation tax, import taxes) and proposed spending/expenditure (healthcare, education, defence, roads, state benefit) for a financial year. In most parliamentary systems, the budget is presented to the legislature and often requires the approval of the legislature. Through this budget, the government implements its economic policy and realizes its program priorities. Once the budget is approved, the use of funds from individual chapters is in the hands of government ministries and other institutions. Revenues of the state budget consist mainly of taxes, customs duties, fees and other revenues. State budget expenditures cover the activities of the state, which are either provided by law or the constitution.

Elements

A government budget typically contains:

  • Revenue estimates: projected receipts from taxation (income, consumption, corporate, property and excise taxes), customs duties, fees, state-owned enterprises, and borrowing.
  • Expenditure estimates: planned spending, usually divided into current (operating) expenditure — such as public sector wages, transfers and interest payments — and capital expenditure on infrastructure and investment.
  • The budget balance: the difference between revenue and expenditure. A surplus occurs when revenue exceeds spending; a deficit occurs when spending exceeds revenue, and must be financed by borrowing, adding to the government debt.

Budget process

In most countries the budget cycle comprises four phases: formulation by the executive (usually the finance ministry coordinating bids from spending departments), approval by the legislature, execution during the fiscal year, and audit and evaluation afterward by a supreme audit institution. The details vary: in the United States, the President proposes a budget but Congress holds the constitutional "power of the purse" and passes appropriations bills; in parliamentary systems such as the United Kingdom, the government's budget is presented by the Chancellor of the Exchequer and a defeat on the budget is conventionally treated as a loss of confidence.

Purposes

Economists commonly attribute three functions to the budget, following Richard Musgrave's framework: allocation (providing public goods and services), distribution (adjusting income and wealth through taxes and transfers), and stabilization (using deficits and surpluses to smooth the business cycle, as in Keynesian fiscal policy). Budgets also serve political and accountability functions: they translate government priorities into concrete allocations and create a record against which performance can be judged.

Types of budgets

  • Operating and capital budgets: separating day-to-day spending from investment.
  • Line-item, program and performance budgets: different ways of organizing and justifying expenditure.
  • Zero-based budgeting: building each year's budget from zero rather than from the previous year's figures.
  • Participatory budgeting: processes in which citizens directly decide part of public spending, first developed in Porto Alegre, Brazil.

Government budgets around the world

The largest government budgets in absolute terms belong to the United States federal government and the governments of China, Japan and major European states. As a share of GDP, government spending ranges from under 20 percent in some low-tax economies to over 50 percent in several European welfare states. Subnational governments — states, provinces and municipalities — also adopt budgets, often under balanced-budget rules that do not apply to sovereign central governments.
