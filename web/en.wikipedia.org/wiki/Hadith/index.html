Hadith

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hadith is a report of the words, actions or tacit approvals of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, transmitted through a chain of narrators (isnad) with a text (matn). The hadith literature, together with the Quran, is a primary source of Sharia and of Islamic theology, history and ethics. Sunni Islam accords the highest authority to the six canonical collections (Kutub al-Sittah), above all those of Muhammad al-Bukhari (d. 870) and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 875), whose Sahih (“sound”) collections were compiled after critical sifting of the transmitters' reliability ('ilm al-rijal); Shia Islam relies additionally on collections of reports from the Imams, such as al-Kulayni's al-Kafi.

Hadith criticism — grading reports as sound (sahih), good (hasan) or weak (da'if) — was a sophisticated medieval science. Modern academic scholarship (Goldziher, Schacht and successors) questioned the reliability of attributions and dated much material to later generations, a debate continued by revisionist and traditionalist scholars; Muslim scholars generally maintain the traditional critical apparatus. The corpus, numbering in the tens of thousands of reports with repetitions, remains central to Islamic practice and contemporary argument.
