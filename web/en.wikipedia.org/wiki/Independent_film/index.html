Independent film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Independent films are typically produced outside the major studio system on modest budgets.

An independent film, indie film, or indie movie is a feature film or short film that is produced outside the major film studio system, in addition to being produced and distributed by independent entertainment companies (or, in some cases, distributed by major studio subsidiaries). Independent films are sometimes distinguishable by their content and style and by the way the filmmakers' personal artistic vision is realized.

History

The independent impulse is as old as Hollywood itself: United Artists was founded in 1919 by Chaplin, Pickford, Fairbanks, and Griffith to escape studio control. Post-war independents such as John Cassavetes pioneered self-financed, actor-centred filmmaking. The 1990s "Indiewood" boom — driven by the Sundance Film Festival and Miramax — brought works by Quentin Tarantino, Steven Soderbergh, Richard Linklater, and Kevin Smith to wide audiences on small budgets.

Financing and distribution

Indies are financed through combinations of private equity, pre-sales of foreign rights, tax incentives, grants, and increasingly crowdfunding. Film festivals serve as marketplaces where distributors acquire titles; video on demand and streaming platforms have since become major outlets, changing the economics of the sector.

Characteristics

Lower budgets typically mean smaller crews, location shooting, unknown or semi-known casts, and greater creative control for writer-directors. The result is often more idiosyncratic subject matter — though "independent" describes financing and production structure rather than any particular aesthetic.
