Sustainable transport

Sustainable transport, or sustainable mobility, refers to methods of transport that are sustainable in terms of their social, environmental, and climate impacts. It encompasses the shift from private fossil-fueled automobiles toward walking, cycling, public transport, and other low-emission modes, together with land-use planning that shortens trips, vehicle electrification, and the adoption of sustainable fuels for freight, aviation, and shipping.

Rationale

Transport accounts for roughly one fifth of global carbon dioxide emissions from energy, with road vehicles the largest source, and it is a major cause of urban air pollution, noise, road deaths (about 1.19 million per year worldwide), and congestion. Oil dependence and inequitable access — non-drivers, children, the elderly, and low-income households — also motivate change. The European Union and many cities define sustainable mobility policies around avoiding unnecessary travel, shifting trips to efficient modes, and improving vehicle technology (the "avoid–shift–improve" framework).

Modes

Active travel — walking and cycling — produces near-zero emissions and health benefits through physical activity; cities such as Amsterdam and Copenhagen carry very high cycling mode shares. Public transport — buses, bus rapid transit, trams, metros, and regional rail — moves many passengers per unit of energy and space. Shared mobility, including carsharing, ride hailing, bike-share, and demand-responsive services, can reduce vehicle ownership. Intercity rail and high-speed rail substitute for short-haul flights. For freight, rail and shipping are generally more efficient per tonne-kilometre than trucking or air cargo.

Technology

Battery electric vehicles eliminate tailpipe emissions and, on decarbonized grids, sharply reduce lifecycle emissions; hydrogen fuel cells, e-fuels, and sustainable aviation fuels are pursued for aviation, shipping, and heavy trucking. Efficiency standards, feebates, congestion pricing, low-emission zones, and parking reform are common policy instruments.

Planning

Transit-oriented development, mixed-use zoning, the "15-minute city," complete streets, and road space reallocation aim to reduce trip lengths and car dependence. Organizations such as the Institute for Transportation and Development Policy and C40 Cities promote these approaches. Critics raise rebound effects, embodied emissions of infrastructure and batteries, mineral supply concerns, and the risk that electric vehicles without mode shift perpetuate congestion and sprawl.
