History of Liberia

The history of Liberia is shaped by its founding in the 19th century by freed African-American settlers under the American Colonization Society, its long rule by the Americo-Liberian elite, and its recovery from civil wars in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

Early inhabitants and settlement

The region, known to European traders as the Pepper Coast for its melegueta pepper, was home to peoples including the Kpelle, Bassa, Gio and Mano. In 1822 the American Colonization Society established a settlement at Cape Mesurado, named Monrovia after US President James Monroe. Settlers — freed people of colour from the United States and recaptured Africans — formed a society distinct from the indigenous majority.

Independence and Americo-Liberian rule

Liberia declared independence on 26 July 1847, the first African republic to do so. The True Whig Party dominated politics from 1878 to 1980, and Americo-Liberians controlled the state while indigenous Liberians were long excluded from full citizenship. The Firestone rubber concession of 1926 became the backbone of the export economy. President William Tubman (1944–1971) pursued an "Open Door" policy and nominal unification.

Coup and civil wars

In 1980 Master Sergeant Samuel Doe overthrew and killed President William Tolbert, ending Americo-Liberian rule. Doe's regime became increasingly repressive; in 1989 Charles Taylor launched an insurgency that plunged Liberia into the First Liberian Civil War (1989–1997). Taylor won the 1997 election, but renewed conflict (1999–2003) forced his exile. The wars killed an estimated 250,000 people and devastated the economy.

Reconstruction

Ellen Johnson Sirleaf, elected in 2005, became Africa's first elected female head of state and led reconstruction, Ebola-crisis recovery and debt relief. In 2017 George Weah won the presidency, marking Liberia's first democratic transfer of power in decades; Joseph Boakai succeeded him in 2024.
