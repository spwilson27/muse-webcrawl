Serotonin syndrome

Serotonin syndrome results from excess serotonergic activity in the central and peripheral nervous systems.

Serotonin syndrome (SS) is a group of symptoms that may occur with the use of certain serotonergic medications or drugs. The degree of symptoms can range from mild to severe, and the syndrome is potentially life-threatening. Symptoms include high body temperature, agitation, increased reflexes, tremor, sweating, dilated pupils, and diarrhoea; body temperature can increase to greater than 41.1 °C (106.0 °F) in life-threatening cases.

Causes

Serotonin syndrome is typically caused by the use of two or more serotonergic medications or drugs, such as a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor combined with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI), linezolid, tramadol, triptans, certain opioids, St John's wort, or recreational drugs such as MDMA and cocaine. It may also occur after a single serotonergic agent in overdose or in susceptible individuals.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on symptoms and history of serotonergic drug use; the Hunter criteria, centred on clonus (spontaneous, inducible, or ocular) in the presence of a serotonergic agent, are the most accurate diagnostic rules. Treatment consists of discontinuing the offending agents, supportive care, sedation with benzodiazepines, and aggressive cooling in hyperthermia. The serotonin antagonist cyproheptadine may be used as adjunctive therapy.
