Accretion disc

An accretion disc (or accretion disk) is a structure formed by diffuse material in orbital motion around a massive central body — typically a star, white dwarf, neutron star or black hole. Gravity causes the material to spiral inward; friction and compression heat it until it radiates, often brilliantly, across the electromagnetic spectrum.

Physics

Because orbiting gas conserves angular momentum, material falling toward the central object flattens into a disc rather than falling directly in. For the gas to accrete, angular momentum must be transported outward — a process now attributed largely to turbulence driven by the magnetorotational instability. As gas spirals inward it converts gravitational potential energy into heat; in discs around black holes this can radiate with an efficiency exceeding that of nuclear fusion, making quasars and active galactic nuclei among the most luminous objects in the universe.

Types

  • Protoplanetary discs around young stars, from which planets form
  • Discs in X-ray binaries, where a compact object strips gas from a companion star
  • Circumnuclear discs feeding supermassive black holes in galactic centres
  • Debris discs of dust around mature stars

The standard thin-disc model of Shakura and Sunyaev (1973) describes the temperature profile and spectrum of optically thick discs and remains the baseline for interpreting observations.
