Revelation

In religion and theology, revelation is the disclosure of divine knowledge — the revealing or disclosing of some form of truth or knowledge by a supernatural agency to humans. Religious revelation is traditionally regarded as a principal source of knowledge about God and divine things, complementing what can be learned by reason and observation of the natural world (general or natural revelation).

Forms

Theologians commonly distinguish general revelation, available to all people through nature and conscience, from special revelation, given to particular people at particular times — for example through scripture, prophecy, miracles and, in Christianity, the person of Jesus Christ. Many religions are centred on scriptures regarded as revealed: the Torah and the Hebrew Bible in Judaism, the New Testament in Christianity, and the Quran in Islam, which Muslims believe was revealed to Muhammad through the angel Gabriel over about 23 years. Hinduism distinguishes shruti ("that which is heard", revealed scripture) from smriti (remembered tradition).

Revelation and epistemology

Philosophers and theologians debate how revealed claims can be verified, how they relate to church tradition and individual religious experience, and whether ongoing or "continuous" revelation is possible, as affirmed by movements such as the Latter Day Saint movement and the Baháʼí Faith, which teaches progressive revelation through successive messengers of God.
