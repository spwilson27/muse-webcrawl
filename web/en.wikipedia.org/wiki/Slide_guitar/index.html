Slide guitar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guitarist using a glass bottleneck slide.

Slide guitar is a technique for playing the guitar in which a hard, smooth object — the slide, traditionally the neck of a glass bottle ("bottleneck") and today often a machined tube of glass, metal, or ceramic — is pressed lightly against the strings and moved along the neck to vary the pitch continuously, while the strings are plucked with the other hand. The technique produces a smooth glissando and a vocal, singing tone, and is closely associated with blues and country music, as well as Hawaiian music, where the related steel guitar is played with a bar held in the hand.

Technique

The slide is worn on one finger of the fretting hand (commonly the ring or little finger) or held against the strings. Because the slide continuously varies string length, intonation is controlled by ear, and vibrato is produced by oscillating the slide. Players typically use open tunings — such as open E, open G, or open D — so that a straight barre of the slide yields a full chord, and they raise the string action to prevent the slide from striking the frets. Damping strings behind the slide with the other fingers keeps unwanted harmonics from ringing.

History

The technique developed in the early 20th century among African American blues musicians in the Mississippi Delta, influenced by Hawaiian guitar fashions and by single-string instruments such as the diddley bow. Early masters included Blind Willie Johnson, Robert Johnson, Son House, and Charley Patton; electric slide was defined by Elmore James, whose version of "Dust My Broom" opens with one of the most famous slide riffs in blues. Later exponents include Muddy Waters, Hound Dog Taylor, Duane Allman, Ry Cooder, Bonnie Raitt, and Derek Trucks. In country music, the lap steel and pedal steel guitar developed from the same principle using a polished steel bar.
