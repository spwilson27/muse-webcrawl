Jonathan Edwards (October 5, 1703 – March 22, 1758) was an American revivalist preacher, philosopher, and Congregationalist theologian. A leading figure of the First Great Awakening, he is widely regarded as one of America's most important and original philosophical theologians. His sermon "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741) is a classic of early American literature.

Early life and education

Edwards was born in East Windsor, Connecticut, the son of the minister Timothy Edwards. A precocious student, he entered Yale College before age thirteen, graduated in 1720, and wrote youthful essays on natural philosophy and metaphysics, including notes on the soul ("Of Being") and his famous youthful observations of spiders. He was ordained in 1727 as assistant pastor to his grandfather Solomon Stoddard at Northampton, Massachusetts, succeeding him in 1729.

Revival ministry

A revival broke out in Northampton in 1734–1735, which Edwards chronicled in A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God (1737). During the wider Great Awakening he became the movement's chief theological defender, preaching the terrifying and tender sides of Calvinist doctrine. At Enfield, Connecticut, on 8 July 1741, he preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," depicting unconverted sinners dangling over the pit of hell. His Treatise Concerning Religious Affections (1746) sought to distinguish genuine conversion from emotional excess.

Later career

Edwards was dismissed from Northampton in 1750 after a controversy over admission to communion — he rejected his grandfather's open practice — and became a missionary to Native Americans at Stockbridge, Massachusetts, where he wrote his mature philosophical works, Freedom of the Will (1754) and The End for Which God Created the World. In 1758 he became president of the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), but died weeks later, on 22 March 1758, from complications of a smallpox inoculation.

Legacy

Edwards's writings influenced American theology for generations; his descendants included many prominent figures. Yale University's Jonathan Edwards College and the Jonathan Edwards Center at Yale, which publishes his works, bear his name. Scholars such as Perry Miller revived interest in Edwards in the 20th century as a major figure in American intellectual history.
