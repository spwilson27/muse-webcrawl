Bookselling

Bookselling is the commercial trading of books, a trade that is the retail and distribution end of the publishing process. People who engage in bookselling are called booksellers, bookdealers or book merchants. The founding of libraries in c. 300 BC stimulated the energies of the Athenian booksellers.

History

In ancient Rome, books were sold by dealers known as bibliopolae. Medieval stationers at universities such as Paris and Bologna sold and rented texts, and book fairs at Frankfurt and Leipzig became central to the European trade after printing. In Britain, the Stationers' Company (chartered in 1557) long regulated the trade.

Modern trade

The modern book trade distinguishes general bookshops, specialist booksellers (antiquarian, academic, children's), mail-order and online booksellers, and remainder and second-hand dealers. Large chains such as Barnes & Noble, Waterstones and Books-A-Million coexist with independent bookshops; the rise of Amazon and e-books transformed the market from the 1990s onward. Trade associations include the American Booksellers Association and the Booksellers Association of the UK and Ireland, and many countries maintain fixed book price agreements to protect small shops.

Independent bookselling

Independent bookshops are frequently cited as cultural anchors in their communities. In the United States, the number of independent bookstores grew through the 2010s after a long decline, aided by "shop local" movements and events such as Independent Bookstore Day.
