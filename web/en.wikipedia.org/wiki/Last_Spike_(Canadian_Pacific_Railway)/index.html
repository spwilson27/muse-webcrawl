Last Spike (Canadian Pacific Railway)

The Last Spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway (CPR) was driven at Craigellachie, British Columbia, on the morning of 7 November 1885. The iron spike, ceremonially hammered in by CPR director Donald Smith (later Lord Strathcona), marked the completion of Canada's first transcontinental railway, linking the eastern provinces with the Pacific coast. The event is one of the most famous moments in Canadian history, immortalised in the equally famous photograph of Smith driving the spike before a crowd of officials, workers, and onlookers.

The driving of the Last Spike, Craigellachie, 7 November 1885.

Background

When British Columbia joined Canadian Confederation in 1871, one of its conditions was a promise by the federal government to build a railway connecting the Pacific province to eastern Canada within ten years. Construction of the line by the Canadian Pacific Railway, incorporated in 1881, was a vast undertaking across the Canadian Shield, the Prairies, and the mountain ranges of British Columbia. Work was hampered by financial difficulties, difficult terrain, and political scandal, but the railway was pressed to completion in 1885, partly driven by the need to move troops west during the North-West Rebellion of that year.

The ceremony

The final gap in the line was closed at Craigellachie, in Eagle Pass in the Monashee Mountains, west of present-day Revelstoke. On 7 November 1885, Donald Smith, one of the CPR's principal financiers, drove the last — ordinary iron — spike with a sledgehammer. Legend holds that the first spike bent and had to be replaced by a second; the bent spike was retained as a souvenir. Notably absent was the railway's general manager, William Van Horne's more famous counterpart in publicity, Van Horne himself reportedly remarking that "the last spike is only an ordinary iron spike, the 'last spike' will be driven by posterity."

A silver spike had been prepared but was not used; the ordinary iron spike was chosen deliberately. The spike driven at the ceremony was later removed and has been the subject of various claims about its whereabouts; a spike identified with the ceremony is held in Canadian museum collections.

Legacy

The completion of the CPR fulfilled the Confederation promise to British Columbia and physically united the young Dominion from sea to sea, a decade and a half after it was first pledged. The phrase "the Last Spike" entered Canadian folklore, retold in Pierre Berton's bestselling history The Last Spike (1971), the second volume of his railway epic. Craigellachie is today marked by a cairn and is a stop of historical interest on the Trans-Canada Highway.
