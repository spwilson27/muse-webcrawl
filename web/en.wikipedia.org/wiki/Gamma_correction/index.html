Gamma correction

Gamma correction, or simply gamma, is a nonlinear operation used to encode and decode luminance or tristimulus values in video or still image systems. It is defined by a power law of the form:

Vout = A · Vinγ

where γ (gamma) is the exponent. Gamma encoding exploits the fact that human vision perceives light in an approximately logarithmic (or power-law) fashion: the eye is more sensitive to differences between dark tones than between bright ones. Encoding images with gamma therefore allocates more code values to shadows, where viewers notice banding, and fewer to highlights.

Origins in CRT displays

Cathode-ray tube (CRT) displays inherently produced light intensity following a power law of the input voltage with a gamma of roughly 2.5. Early television systems therefore applied inverse gamma correction at the camera so that the combination appeared linear. Modern standards retained the practice: the sRGB color space and the Rec. 709 transfer function both approximate a gamma of about 2.2.

Gamma encoding and decoding

Images stored in standard formats such as JPEG are gamma-encoded ("gamma compressed"). When displayed, the values are effectively decoded by the display's transfer characteristic. Image processing performed in encoded space can produce artifacts, so professional pipelines often convert to linear light ("linear workflow") before compositing or rendering.

Uses

Gamma adjustments are used in photography, computer graphics, video production, and display calibration. In 3D rendering, textures are converted from sRGB to linear space so that lighting calculations are physically correct, then re-encoded for display.
