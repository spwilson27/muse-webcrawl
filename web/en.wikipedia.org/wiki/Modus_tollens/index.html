Modus tollens

TypeRule of inference
FieldPropositional logic
FormIf P then Q; not Q; therefore not P
Also calledModus tollendo tollens; denying the consequent

In propositional logic, modus tollens (Latin for "mode that denies by denying"), also known as modus tollendo tollens and denying the consequent, is a valid rule of inference. It takes the form:

  1. If P, then Q.
  2. Not Q.
  3. Therefore, not P.

Symbolically: P → Q, ¬Q ⊢ ¬P. For example: "If it rained, the street is wet. The street is not wet. Therefore it did not rain." Modus tollens is closely related to modus ponens and can be derived from it together with the contrapositive: "if P then Q" is logically equivalent to "if not Q then not P".

Validity and formal proof

The rule is valid in classical logic, as truth tables confirm: whenever P → Q is true and Q is false, P must also be false, for if P were true the implication would force Q to be true. In natural deduction it follows from implication elimination applied to the contrapositive, and it is the logical backbone of proof by contradiction: assume P, derive Q, but Q is known false, so the assumption P is discharged as false. The rule holds even when the statements are probabilistic in modified form — a strongly unexpected observation counts as evidence against a hypothesis, the logic underlying falsification in the philosophy of science associated with Karl Popper.

Distinction from fallacies

Modus tollens must be distinguished from two invalid forms with which it is often confused. Denying the antecedent (from "if P then Q" and "not P", concluding "not Q") is a fallacy: it did not rain does not entail the street is dry, since a sprinkler could have wet it. Affirming the consequent (from "if P then Q" and "Q", concluding "P") is likewise invalid. Psychological studies, notably the Wason selection task, show that people apply modus tollens far less reliably than modus ponens, even though both are equally valid.
