Adjunct (grammar)

FieldLinguistics, syntax
DefinitionOptional, structurally dispensable constituent
Contrasted withArgument, complement

In linguistics, an adjunct is an optional, or structurally dispensable, part of a sentence, clause, or phrase that, when removed or discarded, does not spoil the grammaticality of what remains. An adjunct can be a single word, a phrase, or a clause, and it typically modifies the meaning of the sentence or one of its constituents, for example by specifying time, place, manner, or reason.

Examples

In the sentence "John helped paint the house yesterday", the word "yesterday" is an adjunct: removing it leaves a complete, grammatical sentence. By contrast, an argument such as a direct object is required by the verb — "John devoured" is incomplete without something being devoured. Adjuncts can often be stacked: "John met Mary in the park on Tuesday by chance" contains three adjuncts.

Distinguishing adjuncts from arguments

Common tests include optionality, iterability (multiple adjuncts of the same type can occur, but not multiple arguments of the same role), and the ability to be omitted without changing the verb's core meaning or subcategorization. Adverbial phrases are prototypical adjuncts, though in phrase-structure grammar adjuncts can attach at various levels of the clause.
