Farrer hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Farrer hypothesis, also known as the Farrer–Goulder hypothesis or the Farrer–Goulder–Goodacre hypothesis, is a proposed solution to the synoptic problem of New Testament scholarship. It holds that the Gospel of Mark was written first, that the Gospel of Matthew was written second, drawing on Mark, and that the Gospel of Luke was written third, drawing on both Mark and Matthew. Crucially, it dispenses with the hypothetical sayings source "Q" that the dominant two-source hypothesis posits.

History

The hypothesis is named after Austin Marsden Farrer, who set it out in his 1955 essay "On Dispensing with Q," published in the Festschrift for D. E. Nineham. Farrer argued that Luke knew Matthew's Gospel, so there was no need to reconstruct a lost collection of sayings to explain the material Matthew and Luke share that is not in Mark. The idea was revived and developed extensively by Michael Goulder, notably in Luke: A New Paradigm (1989), and was later championed by Mark Goodacre of Duke University, whose The Case Against Q (2002) argued that Q is an unnecessary and unsupported hypothesis.

Arguments

Supporters argue that Luke's editorial habits, his handling of Matthean material (including the so-called minor agreements between Matthew and Luke against Mark), and the implausibility of Luke ignoring Matthew if he knew Q material in Matthew's order all favor direct dependence. Critics respond that Luke's rearrangement of Matthew would be puzzling, that the material Luke and Matthew share often differs in style in ways suggesting a common source rather than direct borrowing, and that Q remains the more economical explanation for many scholars. The Farrer hypothesis is a minority position but has gained adherents, particularly in British scholarship.
