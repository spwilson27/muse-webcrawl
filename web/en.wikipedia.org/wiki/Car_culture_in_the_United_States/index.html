Car culture in the United States

The car culture in the United States refers to the central role of the automobile in American society, identity, and popular culture. Since mass motorisation began with the Ford Model T in 1908, the United States has built the world's most car-dependent society: over 91% of households have access to a vehicle, and driving accounts for the overwhelming majority of passenger travel. The car has shaped American suburbanisation, the Interstate Highway System, roadside architecture, youth culture, music, and film.

History

Affordable mass-produced cars in the 1910s–1920s liberated rural and working-class Americans from fixed rail routes. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 created the 46,876-mile Interstate Highway System under President Dwight D. Eisenhower, accelerating trucking, suburban growth, and the decline of passenger rail and streetcars. Post-war prosperity produced the tailfin era, drive-in theatres and restaurants, motels, and the great American road trip along routes such as Route 66.

Subcultures and customisation

Distinct scenes grew around modifying and celebrating cars: hot rods and drag racing in post-war California; lowriders in Chicano communities of the Southwest, with hydraulics and elaborate paintwork; muscle cars of the 1960s–70s; street racing and import tuner culture popularised by the Fast & Furious films; NASCAR and stock-car racing in the South; monster trucks, demolition derbies, off-roading, and car shows and cruise nights nationwide. Cars are also status symbols and collectors' items, from vintage auctions to modern supercars.

The car in media

American songs from "Rocket 88" (1951) to the Beach Boys' car songs, Chuck Berry, Bruce Springsteen, and countless country and hip-hop tracks celebrate driving. Films such as American Graffiti, Bullitt, and the road-movie genre centre on cars, as do television series from Route 66 to The Dukes of Hazzard.

Criticism and change

Car dependence is criticised for traffic deaths (over 40,000 annually in recent years), air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions, sprawl, and the decline of public transport and walkable cities. Since the 2000s, ridesharing, urban cycling, and the transition to electric vehicles have begun to reshape American car culture, though per-capita vehicle ownership and miles travelled remain among the world's highest.
