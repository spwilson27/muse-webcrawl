Annealing (materials science)

In metallurgy and materials science, annealing is a heat treatment that alters the physical and sometimes chemical properties of a material to increase its ductility and reduce its hardness, making it more workable. It involves heating a material above its recrystallization temperature, maintaining a suitable temperature for an appropriate amount of time, and then cooling slowly.

Stages

Annealing proceeds in three stages as temperature rises: recovery, in which internal stresses are relieved; recrystallization, in which new strain-free grains nucleate and grow; and grain growth, in which the microstructure coarsens. In steel, full annealing produces a coarse pearlite microstructure with good ductility, while processes such as normalizing and stress-relief annealing use modified cycles for different results.

Applications

Annealing is applied to steels, copper, aluminium and other alloys to reverse work hardening from cold working, allowing further forming without fracture. Glass is also annealed to relieve internal stresses that would otherwise make it prone to spontaneous breakage. The concept inspired simulated annealing, an optimisation technique in computer science.
