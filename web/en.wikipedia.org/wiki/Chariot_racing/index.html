Chariot racing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chariot racing (Greek: harmatodromia, Latin: ludi circenses) was one of the most popular ancient Greek, Roman, and Byzantine sports. Chariot racing was often dangerous to both driver and horses, as they frequently suffered serious injury and even death, but these dangers added to the excitement and interest for spectators. The sport originated in the ancient Near East and Greece, and reached its greatest popularity in the Roman Empire and its Byzantine successor, where races in Constantinople's Hippodrome continued into the 13th century.

Greek chariot racing

Chariot racing was featured in the ancient Olympic Games from 680 BC, when the four-horse chariot race (tethrippon) was introduced. Races were held in a hippodrome over a course of about twelve laps. Victory brought fame not to the driver, often a slave or hired professional, but to the chariot's owner, who received the olive wreath.

Roman chariot racing

In Rome, races were held in the Circus Maximus, which could seat around 150,000 spectators. The principal teams, or factions, were distinguished by colour: the Reds, Blues, Greens and Whites. Chariots were usually drawn by four horses (quadrigae) and raced seven laps around the central barrier (spina), with violent crashes — naufragia, "shipwrecks" — common at the turning posts. Successful charioteers such as Gaius Appuleius Diocles amassed enormous fortunes and celebrity.

Byzantine era

In Constantinople, racing factions — above all the Blues and the Greens — became powerful political organisations. Rivalry between them erupted in the Nika riots of 532, which nearly toppled Emperor Justinian I and left much of the city in ruins. Races continued, with declining frequency, until the Fourth Crusade sacked the city in 1204.
