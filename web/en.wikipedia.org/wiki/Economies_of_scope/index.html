Economies of scope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economies of scope
FieldMicroeconomics, industrial organisation
Related conceptEconomies of scale
Key ideaCost savings from producing a variety of products together

Economies of scope are efficiencies formed by variety, not volume. They exist when the long-run average and marginal costs of a firm decrease as the firm increases the variety of goods or services it produces. The term was introduced by John C. Panzar and Robert D. Willig in the late 1970s and early 1980s, in work on multiproduct firms and natural monopoly.

Definition

Formally, a cost function exhibits economies of scope at an output combination if the cost of producing two outputs together is less than the cost of producing them separately:

C(q1, q2) < C(q1, 0) + C(0, q2)

The degree of economies of scope can be measured by comparing the standalone costs of separate production with the cost of joint production, expressed as a fraction of the joint cost.

Sources

Economies of scope typically arise from inputs that can be shared across products without being fully used up by any one of them. Common sources include:

  • Shared inputs and facilities — a factory, warehouse network or delivery fleet serving several product lines.
  • Knowledge and technology transfer — research and development, patents and know-how applied to multiple products.
  • Brand and marketing — an established brand name, advertising campaign or customer list extended to new offerings (sometimes called "brand economies").
  • Complementary resources — by-products of one process serving as inputs to another, as when a petroleum refinery produces both fuels and petrochemical feedstocks.

Relation to economies of scale

Economies of scale concern the cost advantages of producing more of a single product, while economies of scope concern the advantages of producing a variety of products from shared resources. The two ideas are distinct but frequently occur together, and both help explain the size and diversification of firms.

Applications

The concept underlies theories of the diversified firm, conglomerates, and corporate strategy debates about focus versus diversification. It is widely applied in banking and financial services (where institutions offer many products through one branch network), media and telecommunications, health care, and platform businesses, where a common technology base supports many services. Critics note that diversification can also create diseconomies through managerial complexity and loss of focus.
