Kimberella

Kimberella
Temporal range: Ediacaran, about 558–555 million years ago
Type species: Kimberella quadrata
Known from: White Sea, Russia; Flinders Ranges, Australia

Kimberella is an extinct genus of bilaterian organism known from the Ediacaran Period, about 555 million years ago. It was first described from the Flinders Ranges of South Australia and has since been found abundantly in the Zimnegory Formation of the White Sea coast of Russia, where exceptionally preserved specimens occur together with the scratch marks of its feeding.

Description

Kimberella was a small oval organism, up to about 15 centimetres long in the largest specimens, with a tough, unmineralised dorsal shell or carapace and a soft, frilled margin. The body shows clear bilateral symmetry and differentiated front and rear ends, features that distinguish it from most other Ediacaran organisms and suggest an active, mobile animal that crept over the sea floor on a muscular foot.

Feeding and affinities

Fossils of Kimberella are commonly associated with fan-shaped sets of scratch marks, named Kimberichnus, interpreted as traces made by a radula-like feeding apparatus raking microbial mats from the sediment—the same mode of feeding used by grazing molluscs. This evidence, together with its bilateral symmetry and apparent differentiation of tissues, has led most researchers to interpret Kimberella as a mollusc-like member of the Lophotrochozoa, and as one of the earliest known convincing bilaterians. It thus suggests that the major animal lineages had begun to diverge well before the Cambrian explosion.

History of study

The genus was named by Martin Glaessner and Mary Wade in 1966 for specimens from South Australia, honouring the late John Kimber. Mikhail Fedonkin's work on the Russian material from the 1990s onward established its significance for understanding the ancestry of molluscs, although alternative interpretations—such as a cnidarian-grade organism—have occasionally been proposed.
