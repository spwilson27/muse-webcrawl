Plesiosauria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plesiosauria (from Greek plesios "near to" and sauros "lizard") is an order of extinct marine reptiles that lived during the Mesozoic Era, first appearing in the Late Triassic, about 203 million years ago, and becoming especially common during the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods until their extinction in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago. Plesiosaurs had a broad, flat body, a short tail, and four large paddle-like flippers, which they moved in an underwater "flying" motion unique among four-limbed swimmers.

Body plans

Two broad body plans occurred. Plesiosauromorphs, such as Plesiosaurus and the giant Elasmosaurus, had small heads on very long necks — in elasmosaurs the neck contained more than 70 vertebrae — and probably fed on small fish and invertebrates. Pliosauromorphs (the pliosaurs), such as Pliosaurus and Kronosaurus, had large heads with powerful jaws and short necks and were apex predators, some exceeding 10 metres in length. The two forms evolved repeatedly within the group.

Discovery and history

The first plesiosaur skeleton was found in 1820–21 by the fossil collector Mary Anning at Lyme Regis, England, and described by William Conybeare; its strange anatomy prompted Georges Cuvier to check that the fossil was genuine. Plesiosaurs became icons of palaeontology and popular culture, and are frequently proposed — without scientific support — as explanations for reports of lake monsters such as the Loch Ness Monster.

Biology

Plesiosaurs breathed air and bore live young, as demonstrated by a fossilised pregnant specimen of Polycotylus. Isotopic evidence suggests they were warm-blooded (endothermic) to a degree, maintaining body temperatures above those of surrounding water. Their dense bones (pachyostosis) acted as ballast in some shallow-water forms.
