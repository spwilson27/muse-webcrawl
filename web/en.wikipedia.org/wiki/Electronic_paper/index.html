Electronic paper

Electronic paper
Also known asE-paper, electronic ink (e-ink)
First developed1970s (Gyricon, Xerox PARC)
Key propertyBistable — maintains image without power
Common usesE-readers, electronic shelf labels, signage

Electronic paper, also known as electronic ink (e-ink) or intelligent paper, is a display device that mimics the appearance of ordinary ink on paper. Unlike conventional flat-panel displays that emit light, electronic paper reflects ambient light like ordinary paper. Many electronic paper technologies hold text and images indefinitely without electricity, drawing power only when the image changes — a property called bistability.

Technology

The most widespread technology is electrophoretic: millions of tiny microcapsules, each containing electrically charged black and white pigment particles suspended in fluid, sit between electrodes. Applying a field moves the desired pigment to the viewing surface. E Ink Corporation, spun out of the MIT Media Lab in 1997, commercialised this approach. Other approaches include electrowetting displays, cholesteric liquid crystals, and the rotating-ball Gyricon display developed at Xerox PARC in the 1970s by Nick Sheridon.

Advantages and limitations

E-paper offers very low power consumption, excellent readability in direct sunlight, wide viewing angles and a paper-like, glare-free reading experience that reduces eye strain. Its chief drawbacks are slow refresh rates (poorly suited to video), limited colour reproduction in commercial products, and ghosting artefacts that require periodic full refreshes.

Applications

Electronic paper is best known from e-readers such as the Amazon Kindle, Kobo and reMarkable devices. It is also used for electronic shelf labels in retail, public-transport timetables, digital signage, smartwatches with memory-in-pixel or e-paper screens, and electronic luggage tags.
