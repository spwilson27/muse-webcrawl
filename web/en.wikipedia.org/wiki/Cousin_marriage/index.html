Cousin marriage

Cousin marriage is the marriage between cousins — people with common grandparents or other close ancestors. It has been practised in many societies throughout history, and marriages between first cousins remain legal in most of the world, including the United Kingdom, most of Europe, the Middle East, South Asia and Japan. It is prohibited or restricted in China, Taiwan, the Koreas, the Philippines, and in a number of U.S. states.

History

Cousin marriage was common in ancient societies and among European royalty and landed families, where it kept property within the kin group. Charles Darwin married his first cousin Emma Wedgwood; Albert Einstein married his cousin Elsa Löwenthal. In many Middle Eastern and South Asian communities, parallel-cousin marriage (especially to a father's brother's daughter) is a preferred and honoured custom.

Biology and health

Children of first cousins face a somewhat elevated risk of autosomal recessive genetic disorders and congenital anomalies compared with children of unrelated parents — commonly estimated as roughly doubling a background risk of about 2–3% to about 4–6% — with risk increasing where cousin marriage is repeated over generations.

Law and attitudes

In the United States, first-cousin marriage is legal in about half the states and banned in the others; the bans largely date from the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The Roman Catholic Church permits first-cousin marriage only with a dispensation, and some other religions regulate the practice by rules of prohibited degrees.
