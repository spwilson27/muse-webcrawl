Radical polymerization

Free-radical polymerization (FRP) is a method of polymerization by which a polymer forms by the successive addition of free-radical building blocks (monomers). Free radicals can be formed by a number of different mechanisms, usually involving separate initiator molecules. Following its generation, the initiating free radical adds (nonradical) monomer units, thereby growing the polymer chain.

Mechanism

The process proceeds in three stages. Initiation: an initiator such as benzoyl peroxide or AIBN decomposes under heat or light into radicals, one of which adds to a monomer to start a chain. Propagation: the chain-end radical adds monomer after monomer, typically thousands of times per second, so the chain grows rapidly to high molecular weight. Termination: two growing chains meet and combine, or disproportionate into two dead chains; chain transfer to monomer, solvent or a transfer agent can also end growth and control molecular weight.

Characteristics

Free-radical polymerization is robust and tolerant of many functional groups and of water, which makes it suitable for bulk, solution, suspension and emulsion processes. It produces major commodity plastics including polystyrene, poly(methyl methacrylate), polyvinyl chloride (via suspension) and low-density polyethylene (made at high pressure). Its main limitation is poor control over molecular weight distribution and architecture; controlled ("living") radical methods such as ATRP, RAFT and nitroxide-mediated polymerization were developed to overcome this and produce block copolymers and narrow distributions.
