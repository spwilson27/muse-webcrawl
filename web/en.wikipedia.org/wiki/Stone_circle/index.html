A stone circle is a ring of standing stones, most commonly found across the British Isles and Brittany, erected in the late Neolithic and Bronze Age (roughly 3200–2000 BC). More than 1,000 stone circles have been recorded in Britain alone. Their purpose remains debated, but they are generally believed to have served ceremonial or religious functions, often with astronomical alignments to solstices and other celestial events.

Construction

Circles vary greatly in size, from a few metres to over 100 metres in diameter; the great henge at Avebury encloses a circle some 330 metres across. Stones were typically undressed or roughly shaped local boulders, set upright in dug sockets. Some sites, such as Stonehenge, feature lintelled trilithons and concentric rings of sarsen and bluestone.

Distribution and types

Regional traditions include the recumbent stone circles of north-east Scotland, in which one large stone lies on its side flanked by two tall flankers, and the axial stone circles of south-west Ireland. Stone circles also occur in Scandinavia, West Africa (the Senegambian stone circles), and elsewhere, though not all are contemporary or related.

Interpretation

Early antiquarians attributed the circles to Druids, an association that persists in popular culture but is chronologically impossible: the monuments predate the Celts by millennia. Modern archaeology links them to ritual landscapes, ancestor veneration, feasting, and the marking of seasonal cycles.
