Day length fluctuations

Day length fluctuations are variations in the length of day (LOD) — the difference between the actual duration of Earth's rotation period and the nominal 86,400 seconds of a mean solar day. They are measured in milliseconds by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) using techniques such as very-long-baseline interferometry, lunar laser ranging and satellite geodesy.

Causes

On the longest timescales, tidal friction from the Moon is slowing Earth's rotation, lengthening the day by roughly 1.7–2.3 milliseconds per century. Superimposed on this trend are fluctuations on many timescales: seasonal exchanges of angular momentum with the atmosphere and oceans produce annual and semiannual variations of a fraction of a millisecond; the El Niño–Southern Oscillation affects the LOD measurably; decadal variations of up to several milliseconds are attributed to interactions between the liquid outer core and the mantle; and large earthquakes can change the day by microseconds by redistributing Earth's mass — the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, for example, is estimated to have shortened the day by about 1.8 microseconds.

Consequences

Because Earth rotation is irregular, the time scale based on it (UT1) drifts against atomic time (UTC), and leap seconds have historically been inserted into UTC to keep the two aligned. Precise knowledge of day-length fluctuations is essential for satellite navigation, deep-space tracking and astronomical observation.
