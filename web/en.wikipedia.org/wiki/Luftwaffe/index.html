The Luftwaffe (German pronunciation: [ˈlʊftvafə], literally "air weapon") was the aerial warfare branch of the Wehrmacht, the unified armed forces of Nazi Germany, during the Second World War. It was officially unveiled by the Nazi government in March 1935, in defiance of the Treaty of Versailles, with Hermann Göring as its commander-in-chief. At its peak it was one of the most powerful, doctrinally advanced and battle-experienced air forces in the world.

History

Origins and rearmament

Germany had been forbidden an air force by the Treaty of Versailles, but military flying continued covertly during the Weimar Republic, including pilot training at the secret Lipetsk air base in the Soviet Union. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, rearmament accelerated, and the Luftwaffe's existence was publicly announced in 1935. The Condor Legion, a Luftwaffe expeditionary force, fought in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), where tactics and aircraft such as the Messerschmitt Bf 109 fighter and Junkers Ju 87 "Stuka" dive bomber were combat-tested; the bombing of Guernica became a notorious symbol of air warfare against civilians.

Second World War

The Luftwaffe was central to the Blitzkrieg campaigns of 1939–1941, providing close air support for the invasions of Poland, Scandinavia, the Low Countries, France and the Balkans, and the early stages of the invasion of the Soviet Union. It failed, however, to defeat the Royal Air Force in the Battle of Britain (1940), its first major defeat, and the night Blitz on British cities did not break British resistance. On the Eastern Front it gradually lost air superiority to the growing Soviet air forces, while from 1942 the combined Allied bomber offensive over Germany forced it into a losing war of attrition in defence of the Reich. Despite fielding pioneering jet and rocket aircraft such as the Messerschmitt Me 262 and Me 163 late in the war, the Luftwaffe was crippled by fuel shortages, pilot losses and Allied numerical superiority, and it was formally dissolved after Germany's surrender in 1945.

Organisation and equipment

The Luftwaffe was organised into air fleets (Luftflotten) and included, unusually among air forces, its own ground combat formations: paratroops (Fallschirmjäger), field divisions and extensive anti-aircraft artillery (Flak) units. Its principal aircraft included the Bf 109 and Focke-Wulf Fw 190 fighters, the Ju 87 dive bomber, the Heinkel He 111 and Junkers Ju 88 medium bombers, and late-war jet types.

Legacy

A new German air force — confusingly also called the Luftwaffe in German — was founded in 1956 as part of the Bundeswehr of the Federal Republic of Germany, and a separate air force existed in East Germany. Neither is a legal continuation of the wartime organisation.
