Overview

The North China Craton (NCC), also called the Sino-Korean Craton, is one of the oldest continental crustal blocks on Earth, underlying much of northern China, the Bohai Sea, and parts of the Korean Peninsula and Inner Mongolia. It contains some of the oldest known rocks, dating to about 3.8 billion years ago.

Geology

The craton is generally divided into Eastern and Western Blocks separated by the Trans-North China Orogen, a mountain belt formed when the blocks collided around 1.85 billion years ago during the assembly of the Columbia supercontinent.

Unusually among cratons, the eastern part of the NCC lost its thick, cold lithospheric root during the Mesozoic era — a process known as craton destruction or decratonisation — which was accompanied by widespread magmatism and the formation of rich gold deposits. Explaining this destruction is a major topic in geodynamics.

Significance

The craton hosts important coal, iron, and rare-earth resources, and its geology provides a long record of early Earth history, including early Archean crust and evidence of the beginnings of plate tectonics.
