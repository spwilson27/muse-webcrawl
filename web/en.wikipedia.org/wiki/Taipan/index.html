Taipan

Taipans are snakes of the genus Oxyuranus in the elapid family. Native to Australia and New Guinea, they are large, fast-moving and highly venomous snakes, considered among the most dangerous in the world. The genus comprises three recognised species: the coastal taipan (O. scutellatus), the inland taipan (O. microlepidotus), and the Central Ranges taipan (O. temporalis).

Species and habitat

The coastal taipan inhabits northern and eastern coastal Australia and southern New Guinea, frequenting cane fields, monsoon forests, and open woodland. The inland taipan, also called the fierce snake, lives in the remote black-soil plains of the Channel Country where Queensland, South Australia, and the Northern Territory meet; its venom is regarded as the most toxic of any land snake, though it is shy and rarely encountered. The Central Ranges taipan was described in 2007 from specimens collected in Western Australia and the Northern Territory.

Venom and behaviour

Taipans feed mainly on mammals such as rats and bandicoots, striking rapidly, often several times, and releasing the prey to avoid injury. Their venom contains potent neurotoxins and procoagulants; untreated bites were historically almost always fatal, but an antivenom developed in 1955 greatly improved survival.
