National debt

[Image: a graph display of government debt rising as a share of GDP over decades]

The national debt (government or public debt) is the total stock of financial liabilities accumulated by a government through borrowing — by issuing bonds and bills to investors, banks and its own central bank — to finance past budget deficits. It is usually expressed relative to GDP: advanced economies commonly carry debts of 60–120% of GDP, Japan well above 200%.

Sustainability and debate

Debt dynamics turn on the difference between the interest rate and the growth rate (r − g), primary budget balances, and market confidence: governments that borrow in their own currency and roll debt over rarely 'run out of money' in a mechanical sense, but can face inflation, currency or political crises — as in the euro-area crisis, where states borrow in a currency they do not issue. Wartime debts (Britain and the US after 1945) were shrunk more by growth and Inflation than by repayment. Economists debate safe levels, the burden on future generations (domestic debt is also their asset), Ricardian equivalence, and the institutional fixes — debt brakes, fiscal rules, independent fiscal councils — surveyed under Public finance. US federal debt held by the public, and the statutory debt ceiling confrontations of 2011 and 2023, keep the topic in the news.
