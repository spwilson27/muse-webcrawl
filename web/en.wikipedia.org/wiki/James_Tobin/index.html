James Tobin

James Tobin (March 5, 1918 – March 11, 2002) was an American economist who served on the Council of Economic Advisers and the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, and taught at Harvard and Yale Universities. He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1981 for his analysis of financial markets and their relations to expenditure decisions, employment, production and prices. A leading Keynesian, Tobin is associated with the concept of "Tobin's q" and with the proposed "Tobin tax" on currency transactions.

Early life and education

Tobin was born in Champaign, Illinois, the son of a social worker and a university publicity director. He entered Harvard University in 1935 on a scholarship, studied under Joseph Schumpeter and Wassily Leontief, and was elected to Phi Beta Kappa. His undergraduate thesis explored the liquidity preference theory of John Maynard Keynes. During the Second World War he served as an officer in the United States Navy, including on the destroyer USS Kearny. He completed his doctorate at Harvard in 1947.

Career

After junior appointments at Harvard, Tobin moved to Yale University in 1950, where he spent the rest of his career and directed the Cowles Foundation for Research in Economics from 1955 to 1961. In 1961–62 he served on President John F. Kennedy's Council of Economic Advisers, helping design the Kennedy administration's economic policy, including the investment tax credit and the 1964 tax cut.

Contributions

Tobin's research developed the portfolio-balance approach to monetary economics, treating households and firms as holding a spectrum of assets whose prices jointly influence spending. His 1958 paper "Liquidity Preference as Behavior Towards Risk" gave a rigorous foundation for portfolio selection and influenced Harry Markowitz's work. Tobin's q — the ratio of the market value of capital to its replacement cost — became a standard indicator linking financial markets to investment. In 1972 he proposed a small uniform tax on foreign-exchange transactions (the "Tobin tax") to discourage destabilising short-term speculation, an idea later taken up by anti-globalisation campaigners, though Tobin distanced himself from some of their aims. With William Nordhaus he also developed the Measure of Economic Welfare, an early attempt to adjust national accounts for social and environmental factors.

Later life

Tobin remained an influential public commentator, criticising monetarism and supply-side economics during the 1980s. He died in New Haven, Connecticut, in 2002, aged 84.
