Lustrum

A lustrum (plural lustra) in ancient Rome was strictly the name of the purification ceremony (lustratio) that concluded the taking of the census. By extension, because the census was in principle held every five years, lustrum came to denote a period of five years itself — a usage that survives in several modern languages for five-year periods and anniversaries.

A Roman lustratio, the purification ceremony concluding the census

The ceremony

The census was conducted by the two censors, who registered citizens and their property for taxation, military service and voting. At its close, one of the censors performed the lustrum on the Campus Martius: a sacrifice known as the suovetaurilia — a pig, a sheep and a bull — was led in procession around the assembled citizen body and offered to Mars, purifying the people. The ceremony concluded the censorial term of 18 months and formally closed the census.

History

Tradition attributed the first census and lustrum to King Servius Tullius. Under the Republic the interval between censuses was nominally five years but was in practice irregular, disrupted by wars and politics. Censuses continued into the Empire under the emperors, with the last classical lustra held in the first century CE (notably under Claudius in 47–48 CE and Vespasian and Titus in 73–74 CE).
