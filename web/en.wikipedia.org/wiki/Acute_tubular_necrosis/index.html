Acute tubular necrosis

Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) is a medical condition involving the death of tubular epithelial cells that form the renal tubules of the kidneys. It is the most common cause of intrinsic acute kidney injury in hospitalized patients.

Causes

ATN results from two main mechanisms:

  • Ischemic injury — prolonged or severe reduction in renal blood flow, as in shock, sepsis, major surgery, or severe volume depletion; often an extension of prerenal azotemia.
  • Nephrotoxic injury — damage from drugs and toxins including aminoglycoside antibiotics, radiographic contrast media, cisplatin, amphotericin B, myoglobin released in rhabdomyolysis, and heme pigments in hemolysis.

Pathophysiology and course

Sloughed tubular cells obstruct the tubules and form "muddy brown" granular casts visible in the urine sediment; tubular damage also causes back-leak of filtrate and impaired sodium reabsorption, so the fractional excretion of sodium is typically elevated (FeNa > 2%). The clinical course classically passes through initiation, maintenance (oliguric), and recovery (diuretic) phases over one to three weeks as tubular epithelium regenerates.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on rising creatinine, characteristic urine sediment and electrolytes, and exclusion of prerenal and postrenal causes. Treatment is supportive: removing the offending insult or drug, careful fluid and electrolyte management, avoiding further nephrotoxins, and dialysis when indicated for refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, or volume overload. Mortality remains substantial, largely reflecting the severity of underlying illness rather than kidney failure itself.
