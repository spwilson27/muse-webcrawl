Tuskegee University

Tuskegee University is a private, historically Black land-grant university in Tuskegee, Alabama, United States. Founded on 4 July 1881 as the Tuskegee Normal School, it was established by the Alabama Legislature with Booker T. Washington as its first teacher and principal.

History

The school was the idea of former slave Lewis Adams and banker George W. Campbell, who secured state funding of $2,000 for teachers' salaries. Washington arrived in 1881 and built the institution literally brick by brick — students made bricks, constructed buildings, and farmed to sustain the school. Washington led Tuskegee until his death in 1915, making it nationally famous through his 1895 Atlanta Exposition address and his autobiography Up from Slavery (1901).

Scientist George Washington Carver joined the faculty in 1896 and developed hundreds of uses for peanuts, sweet potatoes, and other crops to help Southern farmers. During World War II, Tuskegee became home to the Tuskegee Airmen, the first African American military aviators, trained at Moton Field.

Academics and campus

The university offers bachelor's, master's, and doctoral programs through colleges of arts and sciences, business, engineering, agriculture, veterinary medicine, and health sciences. Its College of Veterinary Medicine has trained a large share of the nation's Black veterinarians. The 5,000-acre campus includes the Booker T. Washington National Monument area and the Tuskegee University National Historic District.

Notable people

Alumni and faculty include Booker T. Washington, George Washington Carver, civil rights figure Fred Gray, and singer Lionel Richie. The Tuskegee University Golden Voices Choir is internationally known.
