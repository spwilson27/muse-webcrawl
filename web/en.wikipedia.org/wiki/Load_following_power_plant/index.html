Load following power plant

A load following power plant is a power station that adjusts its electrical output up and down as demand on the grid changes during the day. Load following plants sit between base load plants, which run at constant full output, and peaking plants, which are switched on only for short periods of maximum demand.

Characteristics

Load following units typically operate at between roughly 30% and 100% of rated capacity, ramping in the morning as demand rises and reducing output overnight. Hydroelectric plants and gas-fired plants (both open-cycle and combined-cycle) are well suited to this duty because they can start quickly and change output rapidly. Coal and nuclear plants can load-follow — and routinely do so in countries such as France and Germany — but frequent cycling increases thermal stress, maintenance costs and wear, and reduces the economic advantage of their low fuel costs.

Operation

Ramping capability is measured as a percentage of capacity per minute. Modern combined-cycle gas turbines may ramp at several percent per minute, while large steam plants are slower. As grids add more variable wind and solar generation, the "net load" that dispatchable plants must follow has become steeper, increasing the value of fast, flexible load-following capacity and energy storage.
