The Trappists, officially known as the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance (Latin: Ordo Cisterciensis Strictioris Observantiae, abbreviated OCSO), are a Roman Catholic religious order of cloistered contemplative monastics who follow the Rule of Saint Benedict. The order is a branch of the Cistercians and takes its name from La Trappe Abbey in Normandy, France, where the reform that gave rise to the order began in the 1660s.

Trappists are known for their emphasis on manual labour, silence, prayer, and self-sufficiency. Many Trappist monasteries support themselves by producing and selling foodstuffs and beverages, most famously the Trappist beers brewed by a small number of monasteries in Belgium, the Netherlands, and elsewhere, which bear the "Authentic Trappist Product" label.

History

The reform began at the Abbey of La Trappe under Armand Jean le Bouthillier de Rancé, who became its abbot in 1664 and imposed a return to the strict observance of the Rule of Saint Benedict: rigorous silence, manual work, abstinence from meat, and few comforts. Communities following Rancé's reform spread, and in 1892 the Trappist congregations were separated from the Cistercians of the Common Observance and constituted as an independent order by Pope Leo XIII.

During the French Revolution the monks were expelled, and Dom Augustin de Lestrange preserved the tradition by leading communities into exile in Switzerland, Spain, and elsewhere, from which the order was re-established in the nineteenth century and spread worldwide.

Life

Trappist monks and nuns (Trappistines) rise in the middle of the night for the first of several daily offices of prayer, interspersed with private reading (lectio divina) and manual labour. Although often described as having taken a vow of silence, Trappists do not take such a vow; rather, they keep silence as a monastic discipline, speaking only when necessary. Historically they developed a sign language to communicate at work.

The best-known Trappist in the English-speaking world was Thomas Merton, a monk of the Abbey of Gethsemani in Kentucky, whose autobiography The Seven Storey Mountain (1948) became a bestseller and drew many vocations to the order.
