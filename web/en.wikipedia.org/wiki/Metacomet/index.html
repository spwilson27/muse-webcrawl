Metacomet (c. 1638 – August 12, 1676), also known as Metacom and by the English-adopted name King Philip, was a sachem of the Wampanoag people and the second son of the sachem Massasoit. His older brother Wamsutta (King Alexander) briefly succeeded Massasoit in 1661, and Metacomet succeeded him in 1662 after Wamsutta's sudden death shortly after being summoned and interrogated by Plymouth authorities.

King Philip's War

Decades of English expansion, land purchases of contested legitimacy, and colonial legal pressure on Native communities eroded the alliance Massasoit had maintained with Plymouth Colony since 1621. In June 1675, after Plymouth executed three Wampanoag men for the murder of the Christian Native informer John Sassamon, Metacomet's warriors attacked Swansea, beginning King Philip's War (1675–1678) — proportionally one of the deadliest wars in American history. Metacomet built a broad intertribal coalition including Nipmuc, Narragansett, and Pocumtuc allies, and Native forces attacked dozens of English towns across New England, destroying many.

The tide turned in 1676 as colonial forces, aided by Mohegan and Pequot allies and by "praying Indian" scouts, destroyed Native food supplies and strongholds. Metacomet's wife and young son were captured (and his son later sold into slavery in Bermuda). On August 12, 1676, Metacomet was shot and killed at Miery Swamp in Bristol, Rhode Island, by John Alderman, a Native soldier serving under Captain Benjamin Church. His body was beheaded and quartered; his head was displayed on a pike at Plymouth for over two decades.

Legacy

The war devastated both sides but ended effective Native resistance in southern New England; thousands of Native people were killed, enslaved, or displaced. Metacomet's name is preserved in the Metacomet Trail and Metacomet-Monadnock Trail in New England, and he has been reimagined in literature from Washington Irving's sketch "Philip of Pokanoket" to modern reassessments that view him as a defender of his people's sovereignty.
