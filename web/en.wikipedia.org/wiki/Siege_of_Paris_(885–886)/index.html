Siege of Paris (885–886)

The siege of Paris of 885–886 was a Viking siege of the West Frankish city of Paris, then centred on the Île de la Cité. A large Viking fleet sailed up the Seine and demanded tribute; when the Franks refused, the Vikings besieged the city for more than ten months. The defence, led by Count Odo of Paris and Bishop Gozlin, held out despite famine and disease, and the siege became a landmark in the decline of Carolingian authority and the rise of the Robertian family, from which the Capetian kings descended.

Background and course

Viking raids had struck Paris before, notably in 845, when the city was sacked and ransomed. By the 880s the city was defended by walls on the island and fortified bridges — the Grand Pont and the Petit Pont — whose towers controlled river traffic. In November 885 a fleet which the monk Abbo of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, our main source, reckoned at 700 ships reached Paris. The Vikings demanded payment to pass; the Franks refused, and the assault began.

The Vikings attacked the north-eastern tower of the Grand Pont repeatedly, using siege engines, fire ships and mining, and later dammed and diverted approaches, but the defenders repaired the works nightly. Disease inside the city killed Bishop Gozlin in 886, yet Odo maintained the defence and slipped through the Viking lines to summon help. A relief force fought its way in with supplies during the summer of 886.

Settlement and aftermath

The emperor Charles the Fat eventually arrived with a large army in October 886 but chose not to fight a decisive battle. Instead he paid the Vikings 700 livres (about 257 kg) of silver and granted them free passage to plunder Burgundy, whose inhabitants had defied him. Parisians, who had endured the siege without surrendering, regarded the settlement as a disgrace; Charles was deposed in 887 and died shortly afterwards, while Odo's reputation as the city's defender led the West Frankish magnates to elect him king in 888 — the first non-Carolingian king of the West Franks.

Abbo's verse account, Bella Parisiacae urbis ("The Wars of the City of Paris"), remains the principal narrative source for the siege.
