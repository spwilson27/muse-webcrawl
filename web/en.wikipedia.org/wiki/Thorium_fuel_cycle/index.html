Thorium fuel cycle

The thorium fuel cycle is a nuclear fuel cycle that uses the naturally abundant isotope thorium-232 as fertile material. In a reactor, thorium-232 absorbs a neutron and, through successive beta decays via protactinium-233, transmutes into the fissile isotope uranium-233, which can sustain a chain reaction.

Advantages

Thorium is three to four times more abundant in the Earth's crust than uranium, and nearly all natural thorium is usable fertile material, whereas uranium requires enrichment. Advocates note that a thermal-spectrum thorium breeder, such as a molten-salt reactor, could produce far less long-lived transuranic waste, and that uranium-233 is harder to divert to weapons use because it is typically contaminated with highly radioactive uranium-232, whose decay products emit penetrating gamma rays.

Disadvantages and challenges

The cycle requires an initial supply of fissile material to start, and protactinium separation and remote fuel fabrication pose technical difficulties. Uranium-233 is nevertheless a usable weapons material, so proliferation claims are contested. Because the uranium–plutonium infrastructure was developed first, thorium systems have historically received less investment.

History and programmes

Thorium fuels were tested in several reactors, including the Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment at Oak Ridge in the 1960s. India, holding large thorium reserves, has structured its three-stage nuclear programme around eventual thorium use, with the AHWR design intended to burn thorium-based fuels.
