Pāhoehoe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pāhoehoe (from Hawaiian [pəˈho.eˈho.e], "smooth, unbroken lava") is basaltic lava with a smooth, billowy, undulating or ropy surface. These surface features result from the movement of very fluid lava under a congealing surface crust. The Hawaiian word was introduced as a technical term in geology by Clarence Dutton.

Formation

Pāhoehoe forms from low-viscosity lava, typically at temperatures of about 1,100–1,200 °C. A thin, flexible crust forms on the flow surface and is dragged and folded by the still-moving molten interior, producing rope-like wrinkles. Pāhoehoe flows commonly advance through networks of lava tubes and as thin sheets and toes; with increasing viscosity, distance from the vent, or cooling, a pāhoehoe flow may transform into blocky ʻaʻā.

Textures

Varieties include slabby pāhoehoe, shelly pāhoehoe and "entrail" or ropey forms. Pāhoehoe surfaces are generally much easier to walk on once cooled than jagged ʻaʻā.
