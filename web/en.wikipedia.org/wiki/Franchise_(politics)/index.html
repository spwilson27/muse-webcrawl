Suffrage (franchise)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In politics, the franchise is the right to vote in public elections — suffrage. Its history is the history of democracy's expansion: from the narrow property- and tax-based franchises of the eighteenth century (in Britain after 1832 still only a minority of adult men), through the Reform Acts (1867, 1884) and the organised movements for universal manhood suffrage (Chartism's six points made it a mass demand from 1838), the enfranchisement of women (New Zealand first, 1893; the post-1918 wave; France 1944; Switzerland federally only in 1971), the abolition of racial bars (the US Fifteenth Amendment of 1870, made effective a century later by the Voting Rights Act of 1965; South Africa's universal franchise of 1994), and the lowering of the Voting age from 21 to 18 and, in places, 16.

Residual exclusions remain contested: prisoners (blanket bans held incompatible with the European Convention in Hirst v. United Kingdom, 2005, a judgment the UK long declined to implement in full), non-citizen residents (who vote in local elections in some states and in national elections in a few, New Zealand notably), citizens abroad, and persons under guardianship. The franchise defines the demos itself, which is why its boundaries are never merely technical.
