Presidential Records Act of 1978

Signed byPresident Jimmy Carter
Codification44 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2209

The Presidential Records Act (PRA) of 1978, 44 U.S.C. §§ 2201–2209, is a United States federal law that governs the official records of presidents and vice presidents created or received after 20 January 1981. Enacted in the wake of Watergate and the dispute over Richard Nixon's papers and tapes, the Act changed the legal ownership of presidential records: whereas historically presidents treated their papers as personal property, the PRA declares that the United States retains complete ownership, possession, and control of presidential records.

Provisions

The Act requires the president to ensure that activities and decisions of the administration are adequately documented. At the end of a president's term, custody of the records passes to the Archivist of the United States, who administers them through the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), typically in a presidential library. A departing president may restrict public access to certain categories of records for up to twelve years — including confidential communications, personnel and medical files, and national security material — after which the records become subject to the Freedom of Information Act. The Act distinguishes "presidential records" from "personal records" such as diaries, journals, and purely political materials.

Amendments and executive orders

Executive Order 13233 (2001), issued by President George W. Bush, expanded the ability of incumbent and former presidents (and their heirs) to withhold records; it was revoked by Executive Order 13489 on President Barack Obama's first day in office in 2009. The Presidential and Federal Records Act Amendments of 2014 modernized the law for electronic records, prohibiting officials from conducting official business on personal email accounts unless messages are copied to official accounts within twenty days. In 2022 the FBI's recovery of classified documents from former President Donald Trump's Mar-a-Lago residence, and related litigation, brought the PRA into wide public attention, though the Act carries no criminal penalties of its own; prosecutions in such cases have rested on other statutes such as the Espionage Act.
