Stomach cancer

Stomach cancer, also known as gastric cancer, is a cancer that develops from the lining of the stomach. Most cases are adenocarcinomas arising from glandular cells of the gastric mucosa. It is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer death worldwide, with the highest rates in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of South America. Early disease often has no symptoms, so many cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms are vague — indigestion, loss of appetite, early satiety, and mild nausea — and are easily mistaken for benign conditions. Later features include weight loss, abdominal pain, difficulty swallowing (for tumours near the cardia), vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, and anaemia. Spread to lymph nodes, the liver, and the peritoneum is common; classic physical signs include a palpable left supraclavicular node (Virchow's node).

Causes and risk factors

The dominant risk factor is chronic infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, which drives a cascade of chronic gastritis, atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and carcinoma (the Correa cascade); the WHO classifies H. pylori as a Group 1 carcinogen. Other risk factors include diets high in salt and smoked or pickled foods, low fruit and vegetable intake, smoking, pernicious anaemia, previous partial gastrectomy, and genetic syndromes such as hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (caused by CDH1 mutations, which produce the signet-ring cell type). Epstein–Barr virus is associated with a minority of cases.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy; staging uses CT, endoscopic ultrasound, and sometimes laparoscopy. The Lauren classification divides adenocarcinomas into intestinal and diffuse types. Treatment depends on stage: endoscopic resection can cure some early lesions; otherwise surgical gastrectomy (partial or total) with lymph-node dissection is the mainstay, usually combined with perioperative chemotherapy (for example the FLOT regimen). HER2-positive advanced disease may respond to trastuzumab, and immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors is increasingly used. Prognosis varies enormously by region and stage: five-year survival exceeds 60% in Japan and Korea, where screening endoscopy is routine, but remains under 35% in most Western countries.

Epidemiology

There are roughly one million new cases and about 770,000 deaths per year worldwide. Rates have declined steadily over the past century in the West — once the commonest cancer in the United States — a trend attributed to refrigeration reducing reliance on salted and smoked food preservation, and to falling H. pylori prevalence.
