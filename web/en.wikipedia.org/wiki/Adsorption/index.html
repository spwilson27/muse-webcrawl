Adsorption

Adsorption
TypeSurface phenomenon
PhasesPhysisorption, chemisorption
Key modelsLangmuir isotherm, BET theory
ApplicationsCatalysis, filtration, chromatography

Adsorption is the adhesion of atoms, ions or molecules from a gas, liquid or dissolved solid to a surface. This process creates a film of the adsorbate on the surface of the adsorbent. It differs from absorption, in which a fluid (the absorbate) is dissolved by, or permeates, a liquid or solid (the absorbent). Adsorption is a surface phenomenon, whereas absorption involves the whole volume of the material, although adsorption does often precede absorption.

Mechanisms

Adsorption is generally classified as physisorption (physical adsorption), driven by weak van der Waals forces, or chemisorption (chemical adsorption), in which covalent or ionic bonds form between the adsorbate and the surface. Physisorption is typically reversible and can form multilayers; chemisorption is usually limited to a monolayer and may require an activation energy. The process is usually exothermic.

Isotherms

The amount of adsorbate taken up as a function of pressure or concentration at constant temperature is described by an adsorption isotherm. The Langmuir isotherm models monolayer adsorption onto a surface with a finite number of identical sites. BET theory (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) extends the model to multilayer adsorption and is the standard method for measuring the specific surface area of powders and porous materials from nitrogen adsorption data.

Adsorbents

Important industrial adsorbents include activated carbon, silica gel, alumina and zeolites, which combine very high surface areas (often hundreds of square metres per gram) with pore structures that can be tailored for selectivity.

Applications

Adsorption underlies heterogeneous catalysis, gas masks and air purification, water treatment, pressure-swing adsorption for gas separation (such as oxygen concentrators), adsorption chillers and heat pumps, and chromatographic separations.
