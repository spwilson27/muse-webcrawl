Second Opium War

Date8 October 1856 – 24 October 1860
LocationChina
ResultAnglo-French victory; Treaties of Tientsin and Convention of Peking
BelligerentsUnited Kingdom, French Empire, United States (limited) vs Qing dynasty

The Second Opium War, also known as the Second Anglo-Chinese War or the Arrow War, was a war fought by the United Kingdom and France against the Qing dynasty of China from 1856 to 1860. The conflict arose from Western demands for expanded trade, diplomatic representation in Beijing, and the legalisation of the opium trade, which the Qing government resisted after the First Opium War (1839–1842).

Background

The Treaty of Nanking (1842) had opened five treaty ports to British trade, but foreign merchants remained excluded from much of China, and diplomatic relations with the imperial court stayed limited. British officials sought a revision of the treaties, wider access to Chinese markets, and permission for an ambassador to reside in Beijing. The Qing court refused these demands. American and French envoys pressed similar claims.

The Arrow incident and the war

In October 1856 Chinese officials boarded the Arrow, a Chinese-owned vessel registered in Hong Kong, and arrested its crew on suspicion of piracy. The British consul demanded the crew's release and an apology, claiming the British ensign had been insulted. When the Canton authorities refused, British forces bombarded Canton, opening hostilities. France joined the war after the execution of the French missionary Auguste Chapdelaine in Guangxi.

An Anglo-French expedition under Lord Elgin and Baron Gros captured Canton in January 1858 and then sailed north, taking the Taku Forts and occupying Tianjin. The resulting Treaties of Tientsin (1858) opened more ports and permitted foreign legations in Beijing. When Qing forces repulsed an attempt to force the passage to ratify the treaties in 1859, a larger expedition returned in 1860, defeated Qing armies at Zhangjiawan and Palikao, and entered Beijing. During the advance, the detention and torture of an Anglo-French negotiating party under a flag of truce led Elgin to order the looting and burning of the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan) in October 1860.

Settlement and consequences

The Convention of Peking (1860) ratified the Treaties of Tientsin, ceded the Kowloon Peninsula to Britain, opened Tianjin as a treaty port, and increased indemnities. Russia, acting as intermediary, secured the cession of Outer Manchuria. The war further weakened Qing authority, deepened foreign control of Chinese trade, and coincided with the devastating Taiping Rebellion.
