Samuel Sewall (28 March 1652 – 1 January 1730) was a colonial American judge, merchant, and diarist in Massachusetts Bay Colony. He is remembered as one of the judges of the Salem witch trials who later publicly repented of his role — the only one to do so — and as the author of The Selling of Joseph (1700), the first antislavery tract published in New England. His diary, kept for more than fifty years, is among the most valuable records of daily life in Puritan Boston.

Life

Born in Hampshire, England, Sewall emigrated to Newbury, Massachusetts, in 1661 and graduated from Harvard College in 1671, earning his MA in 1674. Through his marriage to Hannah Hull, daughter of the mintmaster John Hull, he became a wealthy merchant and printer's manager before entering public life as a magistrate and councillor. In 1692 he was appointed to the Court of Oyer and Terminer that condemned twenty people to death for witchcraft. Thereafter he served on the Superior Court of Judicature for 36 years, becoming Chief Justice of Massachusetts in 1718 and retiring in 1728.

Apology and writings

On 14 January 1697, at a colony-wide fast, Sewall stood in Boston's Old South Meeting House while his minister read aloud his statement taking "the Blame and shame" of the witch trials upon himself and begging pardon of God and man — an act dramatised in later literature, including Longfellow's New-England Tragedies. In The Selling of Joseph he argued from Scripture that enslaving Africans was unjustifiable, answering the pro-slavery rebuttal of Judge John Saffin. His works also include Phaenomena quaedam Apocalyptica (1697), a millennial treatise, and his diary (1674–1729), published in the 19th century, which records everything from weather and sermons to his widower courtships and, famously, the gradual thawing of Boston's social order. Sewall died in Boston in 1730 and was buried in the Granary Burying Ground.
