Sheba (Hebrew: שְׁבָא, Šəḇāʾ; Arabic: سبأ, Sabaʾ; Ge'ez: ሳባ, Saba) was an ancient South Arabian kingdom centred in what is now Yemen. The Sabaeans grew wealthy from the trade in frankincense and myrrh, which they carried by caravan to the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia. Sheba is best known in the Hebrew Bible and the Quran for the visit of its queen to King Solomon in Jerusalem.

The kingdom of Saba

The Sabaean kingdom emerged by the early first millennium BC, with its capital at Marib, where a great dam irrigated an oasis that supported intensive agriculture. Sabaean rulers, titled mukarrib and later malik, built temples such as the Awwam temple (Mahram Bilqis) dedicated to the moon god Almaqah, and left thousands of inscriptions in the Ancient South Arabian script. Saba dominated the incense trade for centuries before rival kingdoms — Himyar, Hadhramaut and Qataban — and shifting sea routes reduced its power; it was finally absorbed by Himyar in the late 3rd century AD.

The Queen of Sheba

According to 1 Kings 10 and 2 Chronicles 9, the Queen of Sheba, hearing of Solomon's wisdom, travelled to Jerusalem with a caravan of spices, gold and precious stones, tested him with hard questions, and exchanged gifts. In Ethiopian tradition she is Makeda, and her union with Solomon is said to have produced Menelik I, founder of the Ethiopian Solomonic dynasty. In Islamic tradition she is Bilqis, queen of Saba, whose story is told in Surah An-Naml (27). No contemporary inscription naming her has been found, and her historicity remains debated.

Biblical Sheba

Sheba also appears in genealogies as a descendant of Cush and of Joktan, and in prophetic and poetic texts as a distant source of gold, incense and caravans. Some references may instead concern a North Arabian or African Sheba, and scholars distinguish the South Arabian kingdom from other figures of the same name.
