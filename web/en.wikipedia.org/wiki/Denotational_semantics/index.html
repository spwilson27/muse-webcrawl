Denotational semantics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In computer science, denotational semantics is an approach to formalising the meanings of programming languages by constructing mathematical objects — called denotations — that represent the meaning of phrases and programs. It was developed in the 1960s and 1970s principally by Christopher Strachey and Dana Scott at Oxford, building on earlier work in the semantics of formal languages.

Principles

Denotational semantics is compositional: the denotation of a compound phrase is built from the denotations of its parts. A semantic function maps each syntactic construct to an element of a mathematical domain. For example, expressions denote values, commands denote functions from states to states, and declarations denote functions on environments. Because meaning is given by mathematical objects rather than by an abstract machine, denotational definitions support rigorous proofs about programs, such as proofs of compiler correctness and program equivalence.

Domains and recursion

The central technical problem is giving meaning to loops and recursive definitions, whose denotations must satisfy equations of the form x = f(x). Scott's solution was domain theory: semantic domains are partially ordered sets (complete partial orders) equipped with a notion of approximation, in which recursive definitions denote least fixed points of continuous functions — intuitively, the limit of successively better approximations, where non-terminating computations denote the undefined element ⊥ ("bottom").

Relation to other semantics

Denotational semantics contrasts with operational semantics, which defines meaning through the steps of an abstract machine, and axiomatic semantics, which defines meaning through logical assertions about programs. A denotational model is called fully abstract if it identifies exactly those programs that are observationally equivalent. Denotational techniques strongly influenced functional programming and the design of languages such as Scheme.
