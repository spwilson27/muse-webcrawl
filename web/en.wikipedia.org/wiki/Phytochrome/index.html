Phytochrome

The two interconvertible forms of phytochrome, Pr and Pfr, have different absorption spectra.

Phytochromes are a class of photoreceptor proteins in plants, bacteria and fungi used to detect light. They are sensitive to light in the red and far-red region of the visible spectrum and can be classed as either Type I, which are activated by far-red light, or Type II, which are activated by red light. Phytochrome is the pigment that plants use to detect day length (photoperiodism) and to sense shading by neighbouring plants.

Structure and photoconversion

Phytochrome consists of a protein covalently attached to a light-absorbing pigment, the bilin chromophore phytochromobilin in plants. It exists in two interconvertible forms: Pr, which absorbs red light (around 660 nm), and Pfr, which absorbs far-red light (around 730 nm). Absorption of red light converts Pr to Pfr, generally considered the biologically active form; absorption of far-red light or slow dark reversion converts Pfr back to Pr. This reversible switch allows the plant to measure the ratio of red to far-red light, which changes at dawn and dusk and under a leaf canopy.

Functions

  • Seed germination in light-sensitive seeds
  • Photoperiodic control of flowering
  • The shade-avoidance response (stem elongation when shaded)
  • Chloroplast development and de-etiolation
  • Setting of circadian rhythms

Flowering plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana possess five phytochromes, PHYA through PHYE, with distinct and overlapping roles. Phytochromes were discovered in the 1950s through experiments on seed germination and flowering at the USDA laboratories in Beltsville, Maryland.
