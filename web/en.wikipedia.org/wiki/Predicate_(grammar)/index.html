Predicate (grammar)

The term predicate is used in two ways in linguistics and grammar. In the traditional analysis of a sentence, the predicate is the part of a clause that modifies the subject and describes what the subject is or does: in "The dog barked", "barked" is the predicate. This division of the clause into subject and predicate descends from Aristotle's logic, in which the predicate is what is affirmed or denied of a subject.

In modern syntax

In modern syntactic theories, the predicate is usually understood more narrowly as the main content word — most typically the verb phrase's head — together with an analysis in terms of argument structure: a predicate takes arguments (subject, objects, and other complements) and assigns them semantic roles. On this view, a one-place predicate such as "sleep" takes a single argument, while a two-place predicate such as "devour" takes two. Predicate-argument structure is central to formalisms such as dependency grammar, Lexical Functional Grammar, and semantic theories derived from predicate logic, in which predicates are translated as functions over individuals.

Related notions

A predicative expression is any expression that functions as a predicate, including adjective phrases ("is tall") and noun phrases ("is a doctor") in copular clauses. Some frameworks distinguish the grammatical predicate (a verb phrase) from the logical predicate. In many languages the predicate is the required nucleus of the clause, and some utterances — such as imperatives with understood subjects — consist of a predicate alone.
