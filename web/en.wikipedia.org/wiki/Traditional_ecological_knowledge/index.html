Traditional ecological knowledge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) describes the evolving knowledge acquired by indigenous and local peoples over hundreds or thousands of years through direct contact with the environment. This knowledge includes an intimate and detailed knowledge of plants, animals, natural phenomena, resource management practices, and the interrelationships of biotic and abiotic components of ecosystems. TEK is also called indigenous knowledge, local ecological knowledge, and traditional knowledge, though the terms are not exact synonyms.

Characteristics

Fikret Berkes, a leading scholar in the field, describes traditional knowledge as a "knowledge–practice–belief complex": a body of observations, a set of management practices, and a worldview or cosmology that ties them together. It is transmitted orally and through practice across generations, stored in stories, songs, place names and ceremonies, and continually tested and revised against experience. Unlike scientific knowledge, TEK is typically place-based, qualitative and holistic, embedded in the culture and livelihood of the people who hold it.

Applications

TEK informs many resource management practices, such as the Aboriginal Australian cultural burning that reduces fuel loads and maintains mosaic habitats, Inuit knowledge of sea ice and wildlife used in Arctic monitoring, Pacific Northwest clam gardens, and traditional rotational farming and agroforestry systems. Governments and scientists increasingly draw on TEK in wildlife co-management, environmental impact assessment, climate-change adaptation, and conservation planning; community-based monitoring programmes blend TEK with scientific measurement.

Recognition and policy

The 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity (Article 8(j)) calls for respect for indigenous knowledge, and the 2007 United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples affirms indigenous control of traditional knowledge. The IPBES global assessment of 2019 formally incorporated indigenous and local knowledge into its findings. Institutions such as the Inuit Circumpolar Council have developed protocols for the equitable and ethical engagement of indigenous knowledge holders.

Challenges

TEK is threatened by the loss of indigenous languages, displacement from ancestral lands, and the breakdown of intergenerational transmission. Its use by outsiders raises issues of intellectual property, consent and "biopiracy"; scholars and indigenous organisations argue that TEK must be engaged on the knowledge holders' own terms rather than simply mined for data points that fit scientific frameworks. There are also epistemological debates about how TEK and Western science can be combined without subordinating one to the other — a dialogue sometimes framed in Canada as "Two-Eyed Seeing" (Etuaptmumk), a concept articulated by Mi'kmaq elders Albert and Murdena Marshall.
