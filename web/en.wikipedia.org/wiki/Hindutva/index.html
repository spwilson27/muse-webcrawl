Hindutva

Hindutva (lit. "Hindu-ness") is a political ideology encompassing the cultural justification of Hindu nationalism and the belief in establishing Hindu hegemony within India. The term was formulated by Vinayak Damodar Savarkar in his 1923 pamphlet Hindutva: Who Is a Hindu?, which defined a Hindu in terms of shared nation (rashtra), race (jati) and civilisation (sanskriti), with India as both fatherland and holy land.

Origins and organisations

Savarkar, an independence activist associated with the Hindu Mahasabha, developed Hindutva as a cultural and national rather than narrowly theological identity. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh (RSS), founded in 1925 by K. B. Hedgewar, became the principal organisation of the movement, spawning a family of affiliated bodies (the Sangh Parivar) including the Vishva Hindu Parishad (1964) and, in the political sphere, the Bharatiya Jana Sangh and its successor, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP), founded in 1980.

In Indian politics

The BJP rose to national power in the 1990s, aided by the Ram Rath Yatra campaign and the movement to build a temple at Ayodhya on the site of the Babri Masjid, demolished by activists in 1992. The party has governed India since 2014 under Prime Minister Narendra Modi, and Hindutva themes — including the construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya (consecrated 2024), the revocation of Jammu and Kashmir's special status (2019), and citizenship legislation — have been central to its programme. India's Supreme Court in 1995 famously described Hindutva as a "way of life" in an election-law judgment, a reading critics disputed.

Criticism and debate

Critics, including Indian opposition parties and many scholars, argue that Hindutva is majoritarian and exclusionary toward India's Muslim and Christian minorities, and point to episodes of communal violence. Supporters frame it as cultural nationalism and decolonisation of Indian public life. The ideology is a subject of extensive academic and political debate.
