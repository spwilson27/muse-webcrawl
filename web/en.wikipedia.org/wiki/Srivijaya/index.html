Srivijaya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Srivijayan-era Buddhist statue from Palembang in bronze]

Srivijaya was a Buddhist thalassocratic (sea-based) empire based in Palembang on Sumatra, which from the 7th to the 12th century dominated trade through the Strait of Malacca — the choke-point between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Controlling both shores of the strait and ports in the Malay Peninsula and western Java, Srivijaya grew rich taxing and protecting the shipping of others, and became a famed centre of Buddhist learning where the Chinese pilgrim Yijing studied Sanskrit in 671 on his way to India. Its power faded under Chola raids and Javanese and Malay rivals, leaving few monuments but a lasting mark on Malay maritime culture.

A maritime empire

Inscriptions from 682 onward show Srivijaya's rulers (maharajas) binding riverine and coastal chiefs by oath; the empire was less a territory than a network of ports held by sea power, diplomacy and ritual prestige. Arab and Chinese sources describe its gold, camphor, spices and slaves, and its ability to muster fleets that made the strait safe — or dangerous — for merchants. Nalanda in India received Srivijayan royal endowments, binding the two Buddhist worlds.

Decline

In 1025 the Chola emperor Rajendra I raided Srivijaya's ports in a famous expedition, shattering its prestige. Sanjaya-Mataram and later Kediri and Singhasari in Java, and finally the Melayu kingdom of Jambi, absorbed its role; by the 13th century Srivijaya had dissolved into successor ports, though Palembang's rulers long claimed its mantle. Its very existence was rediscovered only in the 20th century from inscriptions and foreign chronicles.
