Tank car

Tank car
Type: Railroad freight car
Used for: Bulk liquids and gases
Typical capacity: 24,000–30,000 US gal

A tank car (International Union of Railways (UIC): tank wagon) is a type of railroad car (UIC: wagon) designed to transport liquid and gaseous commodities. It is a specialized vehicle in the freight train fleet, consisting essentially of a large cylindrical pressure vessel or atmospheric tank mounted on a rail chassis.

History

Early tank cars of the 1860s were little more than wooden barrels mounted on flatcars, used to carry petroleum from the oil fields of Pennsylvania. By the 1870s, iron cylindrical tanks riveted to wooden frames had become standard, and steel construction followed. The expansion of the oil, chemical and food industries made the tank car one of the most common specialized freight cars in North America.

Types

Tank cars are classified by the commodities they carry. General-purpose cars haul non-hazardous or mildly hazardous liquids such as corn syrup, vegetable oils and fuel oil. Pressure cars, with thicker shells and safety fittings, carry liquefied gases such as propane and chlorine. Cryogenic cars are heavily insulated for liquefied gases at very low temperatures, and lined or coated cars carry corrosive chemicals such as acids. In North America, the common DOT-111 general-purpose car became the subject of safety debate after derailments involving crude oil, leading to the strengthened DOT-117 standard.

Operations

Unlike most freight cars, tank cars are often owned or leased by the shippers whose products they carry rather than by the railroads themselves. Cars are loaded and unloaded through fittings on top or bottom, and hazardous-materials cars must display placards identifying their contents.
