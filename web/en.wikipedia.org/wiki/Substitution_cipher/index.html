Substitution cipher

In cryptography, a substitution cipher is a method of encrypting in which units of plaintext are replaced with ciphertext according to a fixed system. The "units" may be single letters (the most common), pairs of letters, triplets of letters, mixtures of the above, and so forth. The receiver deciphers the text by performing the inverse substitution.

Types

If the cipher operates on single letters, it is termed a simple substitution cipher (or monoalphabetic substitution cipher). The Caesar cipher is the simplest example, shifting each letter by a fixed number of places in the alphabet. The Atbash cipher, used in ancient Hebrew texts, reverses the alphabet. When each letter may map to any other letter arbitrarily, the key space is enormous (26! ≈ 4 × 1026 keys), yet such ciphers remain weak in practice.

Polyalphabetic substitution ciphers, such as the Vigenère cipher, use multiple substitution alphabets selected by a keyword, flattening the letter-frequency profile. Polygraphic systems substitute groups of letters, as in the Playfair and Hill ciphers, and homophonic ciphers map common plaintext letters to several possible ciphertext symbols to frustrate analysis.

Cryptanalysis

Simple substitution ciphers are readily broken by frequency analysis, a technique described by Al-Kindi in the 9th century: because each plaintext letter is always replaced by the same symbol, the ciphertext inherits the characteristic letter frequencies of the language. Newspaper cryptogram puzzles are simple substitution ciphers, and solvers exploit patterns such as one-letter words and repeated-letter words in the same way.

Substitution remains a basic building block of modern ciphers: block ciphers such as AES use substitution boxes (S-boxes) combined with permutation and mixing operations.
