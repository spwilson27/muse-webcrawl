The infrastructure of the London Underground comprises the track, tunnels, stations, power supply, signalling and depots that support the world's first underground railway, opened in 1863. The network combines shallow "sub-surface" lines, built mainly by cut-and-cover, with deep-level tube lines bored through the London Clay.

Track and power supply

Trains run on standard-gauge track but draw power from a distinctive four-rail system: in addition to the two running rails, a centre conductor rail is energised at −210 V DC and an outer rail at +420 V DC, giving a nominal 630 V supply while keeping stray currents away from tunnel linings and utility pipes. Track is maintained largely at night during "engineering hours".

Tunnels

The sub-surface lines (Circle, District, Hammersmith & City and Metropolitan) run in rectangular cut-and-cover tunnels just below street level, while the tube lines use circular bored tunnels of about 3.6–3.8 m diameter, excavated with tunnelling shields developed from Marc Brunel's Thames Tunnel shield. Ventilation of the deep tubes relies mainly on the piston effect of trains, supplemented by fans — heat build-up in the clay is a long-standing engineering concern.

Signalling

Historically the Underground used conventional two-aspect colour-light signalling with train stops; modern lines increasingly use communications-based train control with automatic train operation, as on the Victoria, Jubilee, Central and Northern lines, allowing frequencies above 30 trains per hour.

Stations and depots

Many stations retain heritage tiling and Leslie Green's ox-blood faience façades. Major depots include Neasden, Acton Works (the former central overhaul works), Upminster, Hammersmith and Stratford Market. Step-free access has been progressively added by new lifts and, on newer stations such as those of the Northern line extension, by design.
