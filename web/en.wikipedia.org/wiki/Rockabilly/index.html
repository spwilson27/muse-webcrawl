Rockabilly

Rockabilly is one of the earliest styles of rock and roll music. It dates back to the early 1950s in the United States, especially the South. As a genre it blends the sound of Western musical styles such as country with that of rhythm and blues, leading to what is considered "classic" rock and roll. It has also been described as a blend of country music and rock and roll; the term is a portmanteau of "rock" and "hillbilly".

Origins

Rockabilly emerged at Sun Studio in Memphis, Tennessee, under producer Sam Phillips. Elvis Presley's 1954 recording of "That's All Right" is often cited as a defining early rockabilly record. Other leading artists included Carl Perkins ("Blue Suede Shoes"), Johnny Cash, Jerry Lee Lewis, Wanda Jackson, Buddy Holly, Eddie Cochran, and Gene Vincent.

Sound and style

The characteristic rockabilly combo consisted of electric guitar, upright "slap" bass, and a small drum kit, with vocals often treated with slapback echo. Guitarists such as Scotty Moore pioneered a fingerstyle technique drawing on country and blues.

Legacy and revival

Rockabilly declined in the early 1960s but enjoyed revivals in the late 1970s and 1980s with acts such as the Stray Cats, and it remains a durable subculture with dedicated fashion, car culture, and festival scenes.
