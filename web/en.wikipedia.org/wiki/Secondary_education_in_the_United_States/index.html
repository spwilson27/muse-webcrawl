Secondary education in the United States

Secondary education in the United States comprises the final stage of compulsory schooling for most American students, conventionally spanning grades 6 or 7 through grade 12, and delivered through middle schools (or junior high schools) and high schools. It follows elementary education and precedes post-secondary (tertiary) education. Attendance is compulsory until an age that varies by state (generally 16–18). About 90% of students attend public schools, funded and governed locally through school districts, with the remainder in private schools or homeschooling.

Structure

The most common pattern is elementary school (grades K–5), middle school (grades 6–8) and high school (grades 9–12), whose students are termed freshmen, sophomores, juniors and seniors. High schools offer a curriculum of core subjects — English, mathematics, science, social studies — plus electives, foreign languages, arts, and career and technical education. Advanced students may take Advanced Placement (AP) or International Baccalaureate courses, or dual-enrol in college classes. Completion earns a high school diploma, normally requiring a set number of credits (the Carnegie unit). Students planning for college typically take the SAT or ACT admissions tests in grades 11–12.

History

The first American public secondary school, Boston Latin School (1635), was a classical grammar school. The high school movement of 1910–1940 transformed secondary schooling from an elite institution into a mass one, aided by compulsory attendance laws and the declining demand for child labour. Landmark developments include the comprehensive high school model, the 1954 desegregation ruling in Brown v. Board of Education, federal involvement through the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), A Nation at Risk (1983), the No Child Left Behind Act (2001), and the Common Core standards of the 2010s.

Issues

Recurring concerns include funding disparities between districts (often tied to local property taxes), achievement gaps by race and income, teacher recruitment and pay, school safety, and debates over curriculum content. The high school graduation rate reached about 87% nationally in the early 2020s.
