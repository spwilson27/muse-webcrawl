Shift work sleep disorder (SWSD) is a circadian rhythm sleep disorder characterized by insomnia, excessive sleepiness, or both, affecting people whose work hours overlap with the typical sleep period. Diagnosis requires that symptoms be linked to a work schedule that conflicts with the individual's natural sleep–wake cycle.

Signs and symptoms

The main symptoms are insomnia and excessive sleepiness. People with SWSD may fall asleep at work, experience impaired alertness and performance, headaches, difficulty concentrating, and irritability. Sleep obtained is often shortened and of poor quality; total sleep time is typically reduced by one to four hours per day. Symptoms usually resolve when the person returns to a regular daytime schedule, though older workers may recover more slowly.

Causes

The disorder is caused by a mismatch between the endogenous circadian rhythm and the sleep schedule imposed by shift work. Night and early-morning shifts force wakefulness when the circadian system promotes sleep, and sleep when it promotes wakefulness. Rotating schedules that change frequently are particularly disruptive because the circadian clock cannot adapt.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the clinical history, typically supported by sleep diaries or actigraphy recorded over at least two weeks, showing a disturbed sleep–wake pattern aligned with the work schedule. SWSD is classified in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders and coded in the DSM-5 as a circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorder, shift work type.

Treatment

Management focuses on improving sleep hygiene, strategic exposure to bright light during the shift and darkness during daytime sleep, scheduled napping, and maintaining a consistent schedule even on days off. Wake-promoting agents such as modafinil and armodafinil are approved in some countries for excessive sleepiness associated with SWSD; melatonin may help daytime sleep. Caffeine is widely used but can interfere with subsequent sleep.

Epidemiology

Shift work is common — roughly a fifth of workers in industrialized countries work non-daytime schedules. Estimates suggest around 10–40% of night and rotating shift workers meet criteria for SWSD. Long-term shift work has also been associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and gastrointestinal problems, although separating the effects of the disorder itself from the schedule is difficult.
