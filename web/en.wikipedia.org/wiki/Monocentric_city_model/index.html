Monocentric city model

The monocentric city model is a model of urban land use in urban economics, developed independently by William Alonso (1964), Richard Muth (1969), and Edwin Mills (1967). It explains the spatial structure of a city in which all employment is concentrated in a single central business district (CBD), and households trade off cheaper land and larger dwellings at greater distances against higher commuting costs.

Assumptions

The model assumes a featureless plain on which a city sits, a single employment centre, identical households with the same income and preferences, and transportation that is available everywhere at a cost proportional to distance. Land is allocated to the highest bidder, so at every location the equilibrium land rent equals the bid rent of the user willing to pay the most.

Bid-rent theory

Households maximise utility over housing and a composite good, subject to a budget constraint in which income is divided between consumption, rent, and commuting cost. For a household to be indifferent between locations — a requirement of spatial equilibrium — rent must fall with distance from the CBD at exactly the rate that offsets rising commuting costs. This produces the downward-sloping bid-rent curve and predicts that population density and land prices decline with distance from the centre, that poorer or richer households sort by distance depending on the income elasticity of housing demand, and that the city has a well-defined edge where urban land rent falls to the agricultural rent.

Extensions and criticism

The model has been extended to include durable housing, congestion, multiple income groups, amenities, and local public goods. Critics note that real cities are increasingly polycentric, with suburban employment centres ("edge cities"), so the single-centre assumption fits older, compact cities better than modern dispersed metropolitan areas. Nevertheless, the Alonso–Muth–Mills model remains the workhorse foundation of urban economics.
