Wound healing
PhasesHaemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, maturation (remodelling)
Key cellsPlatelets, neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, keratinocytes
Key factorsPDGF, TGF-β, VEGF, EGF
SpecialtyDermatology, surgery, wound care

Wound healing refers to the complex biological process by which living tissue repairs itself after injury. In undamaged skin the epidermis and dermis form a protective barrier; when that barrier is broken, a set of biochemical events is set in motion to mend the damage. The process is conventionally divided into four overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and maturation.

Phases

Haemostasis

Immediately after injury, blood vessels constrict and platelets aggregate to form a fibrin clot, which seals the wound and provides a provisional matrix for incoming cells.

Inflammation

Neutrophils arrive first to clear debris and bacteria, followed by macrophages, which release growth factors that coordinate the next phase. Redness, heat, swelling and pain are the classic signs of this stage.

Proliferation

Fibroblasts deposit collagen to form granulation tissue, new blood vessels grow by angiogenesis, and keratinocytes migrate across the surface to restore the epidermis (re-epithelialisation). Myofibroblasts contract the wound edges.

Maturation and remodelling

Over weeks to months, collagen is reorganised and cross-linked, increasing tensile strength, though healed skin never fully regains the strength of uninjured tissue.

Healing by intention

Wounds heal by primary intention when edges are closely apposed, as in a sutured surgical incision, and by secondary intention when tissue loss requires the defect to fill in from the base, producing more scar tissue. Chronic wounds, such as diabetic foot ulcers and pressure ulcers, stall most often in the inflammatory phase.
