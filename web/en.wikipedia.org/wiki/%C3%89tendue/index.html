Étendue

Étendue (from French étendue, "extent") is a property of light in geometric optics that quantifies how "spread out" a beam is in terms of both area and angle. For a beam crossing a surface, étendue is the product of the area of the surface and the solid angle subtended by the beam, weighted by the refractive index squared. In an ideal optical system, étendue is conserved: it can stay the same or increase through scattering or aberrations, but it can never decrease.

Definition

In differential form, the étendue of an elementary beam is dG = n² cos θ dA dΩ, where n is the refractive index, dA an element of area, θ the angle between the beam and the surface normal, and dΩ the solid angle. Summed over a finite beam it measures the phase-space volume occupied by the light.

Conservation and consequences

The conservation of étendue follows from Liouville's theorem and the second law of thermodynamics. It implies the conservation of radiance (brightness) along a ray in a lossless system, and sets fundamental limits: for example, a concentrator cannot squeeze light from a large area and wide angle into a smaller area and angle beyond the thermodynamic limit, and no optical system can make an extended source appear brighter than the source itself.

Applications

Étendue is used in illumination design, non-imaging optics (such as solar concentrators and LED optics), fibre-optic coupling, and radiometry, where it is often called throughput. It determines how much light a system can collect and deliver.
