Duverger's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Duverger's law is a principle in political science holding that plurality-rule elections in single-member districts tend to produce two-party systems, while Proportional representation tends toward multiparty systems. It is named for the French sociologist Maurice Duverger, who articulated it in the 1950s from two effects: a mechanical effect — plurality rules award no seats to runners-up, so third parties are systematically under-represented — and a psychological effect — voters and donors abandon trailing parties to avoid wasted votes, and elites decline to found parties that cannot win seats.

The law is probabilistic, not ironclad: Canada and India sustain significant third parties under plurality because their support is regionally concentrated; the United Kingdom, Duverger's own problematic case, retains durable third parties despite long-run pressure. Gary Cox's refinement (the M+1 rule) predicts that the number of viable candidates tends toward district magnitude plus one. Duverger's insight remains the starting point of electoral-systems research and of reform debates in plurality countries.
