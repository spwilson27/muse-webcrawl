Haustorium

In botany and mycology, a haustorium (plural haustoria) is a specialised structure by which a parasitic organism penetrates a host's tissue and absorbs water and nutrients. Derived from the Latin haurire, "to drink," haustoria are produced by parasitic flowering plants and by many parasitic and biotrophic fungi; analogous structures occur in some other parasite lineages.

In parasitic plants

Parasitic plants such as dodder (Cuscuta), mistletoes, broomrapes (Orobanche), and witchweeds (Striga) form haustoria that connect to the host's vascular system. In hemiparasites the haustorium taps the host xylem for water and minerals while the parasite still photosynthesises; in holoparasites, which lack chlorophyll, it also draws sugars from the host phloem. Haustorium formation is triggered by chemical signals from the host root — famously, strigolactones induce germination in Striga — followed by cell-wall-degrading enzymes and mechanical pressure that push intrusive cells into the host.

In fungi

Many biotrophic plant-pathogenic fungi, including rusts, powdery mildews, and downy mildews, develop haustoria that invaginate — but do not rupture — the host cell membrane, creating an intimate interface across which nutrients flow to the fungus and effector proteins are delivered into the host to suppress immunity. Because the host cell remains alive, the haustorium functions like a feeding organ at the heart of the plant–pathogen interaction, and it is a major target of research on disease resistance in crops.

Other occurrences

Haustoria also occur as the nutrient-absorbing tips of the embryo in some seeds, as root-like structures in parasitic algae, and — in a different sense — as the modified roots by which some parasitic crustaceans anchor in their hosts.
