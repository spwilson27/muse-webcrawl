Bakken Formation

The Bakken Formation beneath the Williston Basin

The Bakken Formation is a rock unit from the Late Devonian to Early Mississippian age occupying about 200,000 square miles (520,000 km²) of the subsurface of the Williston Basin, underlying parts of Montana, North Dakota, Saskatchewan and Manitoba. The formation was initially described by geologist J. W. Nordquist in 1953. It is named after Henry Bakken, a farmer in Tioga, North Dakota, who owned the land where the formation was first discovered during oil drilling.

Geology

The formation consists of three members: a lower organic-rich black shale, a middle dolomitic siltstone and sandstone, and an upper organic-rich black shale. The shales are rich source rocks with high total organic carbon, and the middle member is the primary reservoir. The Bakken is a relatively tight formation, with low porosity and permeability, requiring horizontal drilling combined with hydraulic fracturing to produce oil economically.

Oil production

Although oil was discovered in the Bakken in 1951, production remained marginal for decades because of the rock's tightness. The application of horizontal drilling and multi-stage hydraulic fracturing in the 2000s, pioneered in areas such as the Elm Coulee Oil Field in Montana and later around Parshall, North Dakota, triggered a major oil boom. Production from the Bakken surpassed one million barrels per day in the early 2010s, making North Dakota the second-largest oil-producing state in the United States for a time and contributing substantially to the United States tight oil revolution. The United States Geological Survey has estimated billions of barrels of technically recoverable oil in the formation.
