The documentary hypothesis is a model used by biblical scholars to explain the origins and composition of the Torah (Pentateuch). It proposes that the first five books of the Bible were assembled from four originally independent written documents, composed at different times and later combined by redactors.

The four sources

The classical form of the hypothesis, associated with Julius Wellhausen in the 19th century, identifies: the Jahwist (J), using the divine name YHWH and dated to the southern kingdom of Judah; the Elohist (E), using Elohim, from the northern kingdom of Israel; the Deuteronomist (D), responsible for Deuteronomy and linked to the reforms of King Josiah; and the Priestly source (P), concerned with ritual, genealogies and law, from the exilic or post-exilic period.

Evidence and debate

Proponents cite doublets (repeated stories), differences in vocabulary and divine names, and contradictions in narrative and law. Since the late 20th century the classical model has been challenged or modified by supplementary and fragmentary hypotheses, and scholars disagree over the dates and even the existence of some sources, particularly E. Nevertheless, the idea that the Pentateuch is composite remains a mainstream scholarly position.
