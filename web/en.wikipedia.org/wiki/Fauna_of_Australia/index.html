Fauna of Australia

The fauna of Australia consists of a huge variety of animals; some 46% of birds, 69% of mammals, 94% of amphibians, and 93% of reptiles that inhabit the continent are endemic to it. The high level of endemism results from Australia's long geological isolation since the breakup of Gondwana, its stable tectonics, and the effects of an unusual pattern of climate change. Australia is famous for its marsupials, monotremes, venomous species, and diverse marine life.

Mammals

Australia and its surrounding waters host 379 mammal species, including 159 marsupials such as kangaroos, koalas, wombats and the Tasmanian devil, and the world's only egg-laying monotremes, the platypus and echidnas. Placental mammals include bats, rodents, and marine species such as dugongs; the dingo arrived with human seafarers a few thousand years ago.

Birds, reptiles and invertebrates

Some 828 bird species are recorded, including emus, cassowaries, kookaburras and a great diversity of parrots. Australia has more venomous snakes than any other continent, as well as crocodiles, monitors and the greatest diversity of lizards in the world. Its invertebrate fauna is estimated at over 200,000 species, most undescribed.

Conservation

Introduced species such as foxes, cats, rabbits and cane toads, together with land clearing and changed fire regimes, have caused many extinctions since European settlement in 1788; Australia has one of the world's worst mammal extinction records.
