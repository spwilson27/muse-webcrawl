Mohorovičić discontinuity

The Mohorovičić discontinuity (Croatian: [moxorôʋiːtɕitɕ]), usually called the Moho, is the boundary between the Earth's crust and the mantle. It is defined by a sharp change in the velocity of seismic waves, reflecting a change in rock composition from the feldspar-rich rocks of the crust to the denser olivine- and pyroxene-rich peridotite of the upper mantle.

Discovery

The discontinuity was discovered by the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić in 1909, from his study of seismograms of the Kulpa Valley earthquake of 8 October 1909 near Zagreb. He noticed two sets of P-waves and S-waves arriving at stations — one set that had travelled directly through the crust and a faster set refracted along a deeper, denser layer — from which he inferred a boundary a few tens of kilometres below the surface.

Depth and character

The Moho lies about 5–10 km beneath the ocean floor and roughly 20–90 km beneath continents, averaging around 35 km. It is not a perfectly sharp plane everywhere: under some regions it is a transition zone, and in ophiolites — slices of oceanic crust and upper mantle thrust onto continents — the boundary can be studied directly in rock outcrops.

Exploration

Attempts to drill to the Moho include Project Mohole in the 1960s and continuing efforts by the Integrated Ocean Drilling Program / International Ocean Discovery Program, notably the SloMo project aimed at the Atlantis Bank in the Indian Ocean. No borehole has yet reached the mantle, though the deepest, the Kola Superdeep Borehole, penetrated about 12 km of continental crust.
