Religious war

A religious war or a war of religion is a war primarily caused or justified by differences in religion and beliefs. Historical examples include the Crusades, the European wars of religion that followed the Reformation, and conflicts framed in terms of jihad. Scholars debate how far religious motives can be separated from political, dynastic and economic ones; in many conflicts religion supplied identity and legitimacy rather than the sole cause.

Notable examples

The Crusades (1095–1291) were a series of expeditions by Latin Christians, sanctioned by the Church, aimed principally at recovering the Holy Land. In early modern Europe, the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) pitted Catholics against Huguenots, and the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) began as a conflict within the Holy Roman Empire between Protestant and Catholic states before becoming a broader struggle for power among European dynasties.

Concepts

Related ideas include the doctrine of "just war", holy war, and sectarian violence. Modern conflicts sometimes described in religious terms — such as those in Northern Ireland, the former Yugoslavia or the Middle East — typically also involve ethnic and national divisions, leading analysts to caution against purely religious explanations.
