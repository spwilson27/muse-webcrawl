The University of California (UC) is a public land-grant research university system in the U.S. state of California. Established in 1868 in Berkeley as the state's first land-grant university, the system today comprises ten campuses — Berkeley, Davis, Irvine, Los Angeles, Merced, Riverside, San Diego, San Francisco, Santa Barbara and Santa Cruz — along with five medical centers, three affiliated national laboratories, and a statewide Division of Agriculture and Natural Resources.

History

The university was founded by the Organic Act of 1868, merging the private College of California with the state Agricultural, Mining, and Mechanical Arts College. The University of California, Berkeley became the system's founding campus. In the 20th century, under presidents such as Robert Gordon Sproul and Clark Kerr, UC grew into a multi-campus system; Kerr's California Master Plan for Higher Education (1960) assigned UC the state's research mission and the top tier of students. UC faculty and alumni have won more than 70 Nobel Prizes.

Governance and academics

The system is governed by the Regents of the University of California, a 26-member board, and administered by the President of the University of California from the Office of the President in Oakland. UC campuses are consistently ranked among the world's leading public universities, and UC San Francisco is dedicated entirely to graduate health sciences. The university manages or co-manages Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratories for the U.S. Department of Energy.

Admissions

Applications to the system exceed 200,000 annually. Following voter-approved Proposition 209 (1996), UC may not consider race or sex in admissions, and since 2020 it has phased out standardized testing requirements. Tuition for California residents is roughly $14,000 per year.
