Decommunization

Decommunization is the process of dismantling the legacy of communist state structures, culture, and psychology in the post-communist states of Central and Eastern Europe. It includes lustration (the vetting and exclusion of former communist officials and collaborators from public office), the opening of secret-police archives, the removal of communist symbols and monuments, the renaming of streets and places, and legal prohibitions on communist parties or symbols. The term is modeled on denazification after the Second World War.

By country

Germany

After reunification, Germany opened the archives of the East German Stasi through a dedicated federal authority (the "Gauck Authority", later the BStU), allowing citizens to view their files, and prosecuted some former officials, notably border guards involved in shootings at the Berlin Wall.

Poland

Poland established the Institute of National Remembrance (IPN) in 1998 to prosecute crimes against the Polish nation and administer lustration. Lustration laws, expanded controversially in 2006–2007, required many public officials and professionals to declare past collaboration with the security services.

Czech Republic

Czechoslovakia adopted one of the earliest and strictest lustration laws in 1991, screening officials for collaboration with the StB secret police. The Czech Republic also declared the communist regime illegitimate by statute in 1993.

Ukraine

Ukraine passed a package of decommunization laws in 2015, banning communist and Nazi symbols, mandating the removal of Lenin monuments and the renaming of places named for communist figures — a process that had already begun spontaneously during the Euromaidan protests with the "Leninopad" (fall of Lenin statues). After 2022, decommunization merged with broader derussification efforts following Russia's full-scale invasion.

Debates

Decommunization remains contested: supporters argue it is necessary for justice, truth, and democratic consolidation, while critics contend that some measures are retroactive, violate due process, or serve contemporary political purposes. Comparable processes include de-Ba'athification in Iraq after 2003.
