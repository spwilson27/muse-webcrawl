Axoneme

LocationCore of eukaryotic cilia and flagella
StructureNine outer microtubule doublets around a central pair ("9+2"); 9+0 in nodal and many sensory cilia
Motor proteinsAxonemal dyneins (outer and inner arms)

The axoneme is the microtubule-based cytoskeletal core of a eukaryotic cilium or flagellum. In motile cilia and flagella it typically has a "9+2" arrangement: a central pair of singlet microtubules surrounded by nine outer doublet microtubules. Bending of the axoneme, driven by the motor protein dynein, produces the beating or undulating motion that propels cells or moves fluid over epithelial surfaces.

Structure

Each outer doublet consists of a complete A-tubule (13 protofilaments) fused to an incomplete B-tubule (10–11 protofilaments). Attached to the A-tubule are outer and inner dynein arms, whose ATP-driven sliding relative to the neighbouring doublet generates bending because the doublets are anchored at the base and linked by nexin (nexin–dynein regulatory complex) bridges that convert sliding into curvature. Radial spokes project from each doublet toward the central pair and help regulate dynein activity and waveform.

The axoneme grows from a basal body (a centriole-like structure with nine triplet microtubules) at the cell surface, and its components are delivered by intraflagellar transport (IFT) trains moving along the doublets. Motile cilia have the 9+2 pattern; primary (sensory) cilia usually lack the central pair, dynein arms and radial spokes (9+0) and are non-motile, while embryonic nodal cilia are 9+0 yet motile, rotating to generate the leftward flow that establishes body asymmetry.

Clinical significance

Mutations in axonemal components cause primary ciliary dyskinesia — chronic respiratory infections, infertility, and sometimes situs inversus (Kartagener syndrome) — reflecting defective mucociliary clearance and nodal flow. Defects of non-motile ciliary axonemes underlie many ciliopathies affecting the kidney, retina, and skeleton.
