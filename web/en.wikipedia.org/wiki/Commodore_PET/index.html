The Commodore PET (Personal Electronic Transactor) is a line of home and business personal computers produced by Commodore International beginning in 1977. It was one of the three machines dubbed the "1977 Trinity" — alongside the Apple II and the TRS-80 — that launched the personal computer era.

History

Designed under Chuck Peddle after Commodore acquired MOS Technology, the PET 2001 debuted at the Consumer Electronics Show in January 1977. It combined a MOS 6502 processor, Commodore BASIC in ROM, a built-in cassette deck, a calculator-style "chiclet" keyboard, and a 9-inch monochrome monitor in a single steel case. It sold for US$795 with 4 KB of RAM.

Models

Later models replaced the chiclet keyboard with a full-travel one, expanded memory to 8, 16, or 32 KB, and added 80-column business machines (the 8000 series) with separate keyboards and larger screens. The PET found particular success in schools in North America and Europe and in European business markets, and it pioneered Commodore's IEEE-488 peripheral bus used for disk drives and printers.

Legacy

The PET's all-in-one design and built-in BASIC made it influential in education. Its character set, PETSCII, included graphic symbols used for rudimentary drawing and games. The line was eventually superseded by the VIC-20 and Commodore 64 in the home market.
