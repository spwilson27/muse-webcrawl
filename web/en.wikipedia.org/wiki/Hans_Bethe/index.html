Hans Albrecht Bethe (2 July 1906 – 6 March 2005) was a German-American theoretical physicist who made major contributions to nuclear physics, astrophysics, quantum electrodynamics and solid-state physics. He received the 1967 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on the theory of stellar nucleosynthesis — the proton–proton chain and the CNO cycle by which stars shine (worked out in 1938–39).

Bethe led the theory division at Los Alamos during the Manhattan Project; days after the Lamb shift was announced in 1947, on the train home from the Shelter Island Conference, he performed the first (non-relativistic) calculation of it, launching renormalised Quantum electrodynamics. His Cornell laboratory defined postwar nuclear physics (the 'Bethe Bible' reviews of 1936–37), and he remained — into his late nineties — a formidable scientific citizen: arms-control adviser, critic of missile defence, and, at 91, author of a definitive paper on the solar neutrino problem's astrophysics.

How stars shine

Asked by a colleague why the Sun shines, Bethe computed in 1938–39 the two fusion cycles that dominate by stellar mass, deriving the Sun's core temperature (~16 million K) from first principles and closing a question open since Kelvin. The CNO cycle he discovered dominates in hotter stars; his pp-chain accounting later framed the solar Neutrino deficit that led to neutrino oscillation.
