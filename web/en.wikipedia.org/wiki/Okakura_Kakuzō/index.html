Okakura Kakuzō (岡倉 覚三, 14 February 1863 – 2 September 1913), also known as Okakura Tenshin, was a Japanese scholar and art critic who contributed to the development of arts in modern Japan. He is chiefly remembered in the English-speaking world as the author of The Book of Tea (1906), a classic essay linking the Japanese tea ceremony to larger questions of art, philosophy and Asian civilisation.

Life and career

Born in Yokohama to a former samurai family of silk merchants, Okakura learned English as a boy and studied at Tokyo Imperial University under the American philosopher Ernest Fenollosa, with whom he travelled Japan surveying temple treasures. Together they championed traditional Japanese painting (nihonga) against wholesale Westernisation, and in 1889 Okakura became a founding director of the Tokyo School of Fine Arts. Forced out in 1898 amid institutional politics, he founded the Japan Art Institute (Nihon Bijutsuin) with leading nihonga painters.

Works and influence

Writing in fluent English, Okakura addressed Western audiences directly. The Ideals of the East (1903) opens with the famous sentence "Asia is one", arguing for the spiritual unity of Asian art from India to Japan; it was written during a stay in India in the circle of the Tagore family and influenced Indian cultural nationalism. From 1904 he worked in Boston, becoming head of the Asiatic division and then adviser on Chinese and Japanese art at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, building one of the great Asian collections in the West. The Book of Tea presents "teaism" as an aesthetic philosophy and a quiet protest against Western materialism. Okakura died in 1913 at his mountain retreat in Akakura.
