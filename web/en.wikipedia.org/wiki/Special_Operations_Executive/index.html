Special Operations Executive

The Special Operations Executive (SOE) was a British organisation formed in 1940 to conduct espionage, sabotage and reconnaissance in German-occupied Europe (and later Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia) and to aid local resistance movements during the Second World War. Its agents were often referred to as the "Baker Street Irregulars", after Sherlock Holmes's fictional group of spies, because its headquarters were at 64 Baker Street in London.

Formation

SOE was created on the orders of Prime Minister Winston Churchill and Minister of Economic Warfare Hugh Dalton, merging elements of the Secret Intelligence Service (Section D), the War Office (MI R), and the propaganda unit Electra House. Churchill instructed Dalton to "set Europe ablaze"—to create a secret organisation responsible for coordinating all action by way of subversion and sabotage against the enemy overseas.

Operations

SOE operated in nearly every country occupied or attacked by the Axis. Agents were recruited from many nationalities, including exiles and refugees, and trained at country houses across Britain, such as Arisaig in Scotland. Agents parachuted into occupied territory to organise resistance networks, gather intelligence, and conduct sabotage against railways, factories, and communications. Notable operations included support for the assassination of Reinhard Heydrich in Prague (Operation Anthropoid, carried out by Czechoslovak agents), the sabotage of the German heavy water programme in Norway, and arming the French Resistance before D-Day.

Women agents

SOE controversially employed women as couriers and wireless operators in occupied France, reasoning they could move more freely than men. Of the women sent into France, several were captured and executed, including Violette Szabo and Noor Inayat Khan, both posthumously honoured. Vera Atkins, an intelligence officer in SOE's French section, spent years after the war tracing the fate of missing agents.

Legacy

SOE was dissolved in 1946, with many of its personnel and techniques absorbed into the Secret Intelligence Service. Its files were long kept secret, and public recognition of its agents—particularly the women—came only decades later. SOE is credited with pioneering modern special operations and unconventional warfare, and its gadgets, forged documents, and sabotage devices have become iconic of wartime ingenuity.
