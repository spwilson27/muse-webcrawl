Haskalah

The Haskalah (Hebrew: הַשְׂכָּלָה, "enlightenment" or "wisdom"), often called the Jewish Enlightenment, was an intellectual movement among the Jews of Europe from the 1770s to the 1880s. Inspired by the European Enlightenment, its adherents — the maskilim — urged Jews to adopt secular education, European languages and dress, and to integrate into surrounding societies, while reviving Hebrew as a literary language and reforming Jewish religious life.

Origins and programme

The movement crystallised in Berlin around the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, whose German translation of the Pentateuch (printed in Hebrew characters, 1780–1783) was meant to lead Jews toward German culture, and whose associate Naphtali Herz Wessely called for a curriculum combining Torah with secular learning. Journals such as Ha-Me'assef (from 1784) spread the new ideas. Maskilim promoted productivisation — moving Jews into agriculture and crafts — and attacked what they saw as superstition, early marriage and the insularity of traditional education.

Spread and development

From Berlin the Haskalah moved to Galicia and, in the nineteenth century, to the Russian Empire, where maskilim founded modern Hebrew and Yiddish literature: Abraham Mapu wrote the first Hebrew novel (1853), and writers such as Judah Leib Gordon urged Jews to be "a man in the street and a Jew in the home". Conflict with Hasidism sharpened the movement's literary satire. In its later phases the Haskalah fed into Reform Judaism, Jewish nationalism and Zionism, and modern Hebrew culture.

Legacy

Although resisted fiercely by traditionalist rabbis, the Haskalah permanently transformed Jewish life, producing modern Hebrew literature, secular Jewish scholarship (the Wissenschaft des Judentums), and the ideological foundations of much of modern Jewish identity.
