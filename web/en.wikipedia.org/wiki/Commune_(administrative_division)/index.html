Commune (administrative division)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commune is a small administrative division and unit of local government found, under varying names and with varying powers, in many countries. The word derives from the Medieval Latin communia, referring to the sworn associations of townspeople that governed medieval towns. In most systems the commune is the lowest tier of local administration, governed by an elected council and a mayor.

France

The French commune is the classic example: created in 1789 from the parishes and towns of the old regime, France today has roughly 35,000 communes — more than the rest of the European Union combined — ranging from Paris to villages with a handful of inhabitants. Each has a municipal council and mayor responsible for civil registration, local planning, primary schools, and municipal services. Persistent fragmentation has prompted the state to encourage mergers and intercommunal bodies (intercommunalité).

Elsewhere

  • Italy — the comune (about 7,900) is the basic municipal unit, headed by a sindaco.
  • Germany and Austria — the equivalent is the Gemeinde; Swiss cantons use Gemeinde/commune/comune in their respective languages.
  • Belgium — gemeente/commune, 581 since the 1977 fusions.
  • Scandinavia — Swedish kommun and Danish kommune are municipalities with unusually wide responsibilities, including education and social care, though typically much larger in population than French communes.
  • China — the "people's communes" of 1958–1983 were large collective units combining government and farming functions, unrelated in scale to the European model.
  • Vietnam, Cambodia, Chile, Romania, Poland (historical), Algeria, and others also use communes or cognate terms in their territorial organisation.
