Every mass attracts every other mass with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

Newton's law of universal gravitation states that every particle attracts every other particle in the universe with a force that is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers. It was published by Isaac Newton in his Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica of 1687 and was the first theory to unify terrestrial and celestial mechanics — showing that the same force that makes an apple fall also holds the Moon in orbit around the Earth.

F = G · (m1 m2) / r2

where F is the magnitude of the gravitational force between the two point masses m1 and m2, r is the distance between their centers, and G is the gravitational constant, first measured by Henry Cavendish in 1797–98.

Applications and success

The law accounts for Kepler's laws of planetary motion, the tides, the orbits of comets such as Halley's, and the precession of the equinoxes. It was used to predict the existence and position of Neptune, discovered in 1846 from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus. In modern form — Newton's law applied with vector notation and extended to continuous mass distributions — it remains accurate enough for most practical purposes, including spacecraft navigation within the Solar System.

Limitations

Newton's theory could not explain the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion (43 arcseconds per century), nor the bending of light by the Sun. Einstein's theory of general relativity (1915) replaced the notion of gravity as a force with the curvature of spacetime, reproducing Newton's law as a limiting case for weak fields and slow speeds while correctly predicting these effects. Newton's law is thus regarded as an excellent approximation rather than an exact description of nature.
