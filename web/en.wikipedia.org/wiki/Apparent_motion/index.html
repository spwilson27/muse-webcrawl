Apparent motion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Apparent motion is the motion of an object as perceived by an observer, which may differ from the object's actual motion because of the observer's own movement or frame of reference. The term is used in astronomy for the observed movement of celestial bodies across the sky, and in psychology for illusions in which stationary objects appear to move.

In astronomy

The most familiar example is the daily (diurnal) apparent motion of the Sun, Moon, stars, and planets from east to west across the sky. This motion is apparent rather than real: it results from the Earth's rotation on its axis from west to east. Similarly, the annual apparent path of the Sun along the ecliptic, and its seasonal migration between the tropics, result from the Earth's revolution around the Sun combined with the tilt of its axis.

Astronomers distinguish several other kinds of apparent motion:

  • Retrograde motion — the temporary apparent reversal of a planet's eastward drift against the stars, caused by the Earth overtaking an outer planet (or being overtaken by an inner one). In the geocentric astronomy of antiquity this required explanation by epicycles; in the heliocentric model it follows naturally from relative orbital motion.
  • Proper motion — the genuine angular motion of a star across the celestial sphere, measured relative to more distant background stars; it is a real change in position projected onto the sky.
  • Parallactic motion — the apparent annual displacement of nearby stars caused by the Earth's changing viewpoint over its orbit, the basis of stellar parallax distance measurements.
  • Apparent superluminal motion — the illusion, seen in some astrophysical jets such as those of quasars, that matter is moving faster than light, an effect of relativistic beaming and projection geometry.

In perception

In perceptual psychology, apparent motion refers to the sensation of movement produced by stimuli that are not actually moving. The best known is the phi phenomenon, described by Max Wertheimer in 1912, in which two stationary lights flashing in alternation at an appropriate interval are seen as a single light moving back and forth. Related effects include beta movement, the basis of motion perception in cinema and animation (where a rapid sequence of still frames appears as continuous motion), and induced motion, in which a stationary object appears to move because its surroundings move — as when a stationary train seems to roll backward when the adjacent train departs. The autokinetic effect, in which a fixed point of light in darkness appears to wander, is another classic example.
