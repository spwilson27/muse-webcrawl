Phraseology is the branch of linguistics that studies the structure, meaning and use of fixed multi-word expressions — the set phrases, idioms and word combinations of a language whose meaning or form is not fully predictable from their individual words. The term also refers to the stock of such expressions in a given language, register or specialised field (for example, legal or aviation phraseology).

Subject matter

Phraseological units occupy a spectrum of fixedness. At one end are fully frozen, semantically opaque idioms such as kick the bucket ("to die") or spill the beans; in the middle are collocations — habitual but partly transparent combinations such as heavy rain, make a decision or deeply concerned; and at the freer end are sentence stems, proverbs, slogans and routine formulae (How do you do?, once upon a time). Phrasal verbs (put off, look into) are a major phraseological category in English.

History and applications

The discipline originated with the Swiss linguist Charles Bally, a student of Saussure, and was developed extensively by Soviet and Russian linguists (notably Viktor Vinogradov) and by German scholarship. In Britain, John Sinclair's corpus linguistics demonstrated that language use is governed largely by the "idiom principle" — text is assembled from semi-prefabricated phrases — with major consequences for lexicography, language teaching and natural language processing. Phraseology is applied in compiling dictionaries of idioms and collocations, in foreign-language teaching (where formulaic language is a key component of fluency), in translation, and in standardised professional communication such as radiotelephony phraseology in aviation and maritime contexts.
