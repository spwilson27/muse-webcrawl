Honorifics

An honorific is a form of language that conveys respect, deference, or social distance toward the person being spoken to or spoken about. Honorifics may be words (such as titles like "Sir," "Madam," "Dr.," or "Your Majesty"), affixes added to names, or—in languages with grammaticalized politeness—entire systems of special vocabulary, verb conjugations, and pronouns.

In different languages

Japanese keigo is among the most elaborate systems: it distinguishes polite speech (teineigo), respectful speech that elevates the listener or subject (sonkeigo), and humble speech that lowers the speaker (kenjōgo), with distinct verb forms and name suffixes such as -san, -sama, -kun, and -chan. Korean has a comparable system of speech levels and honorific suffixes such as -nim. Javanese and Thai likewise stratify vocabulary by formality and rank.

In European languages, the most familiar honorific device is the T–V distinction: using a plural or formal pronoun (French vous, German Sie, Spanish usted) to address a single person respectfully, versus the informal pronoun (tu, du, tú). English lost its own thou/you distinction but retains titles and styles such as Mr., Ms., Dr., Professor, Reverend, and "the Honourable."

Functions and change

Honorifics encode social information: relative age, rank, intimacy, and the formality of the setting. Choosing the wrong level can give serious offence or sound comic. Honorific systems change with society—increasingly egalitarian cultures often simplify them, workplaces flatten title use, and some speakers deliberately adjust honorifics to claim closeness or assert status. Sign languages, including Japanese Sign Language and ASL, also have honorific registers expressed through modified signing space and non-manual markers.
