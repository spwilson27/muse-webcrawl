Archaic Greek sculpture is the freestanding and relief sculpture of Greece during the Archaic period, roughly from the mid-7th century BCE to the Persian Wars (480 BCE). It marks the revival of large-scale stone carving in the Greek world after the Bronze Age collapse, strongly influenced by Egyptian and Near Eastern art, and it developed steadily toward the naturalism of the Classical period.

Types

Kouros

The kouros (plural kouroi) is a standing nude male youth, arms at his sides, fists clenched, left foot advanced, carved with strict frontal symmetry on the Egyptian model (as famously in the New York kouros, c. 590–580 BCE). Kouroi served as grave markers and as votive offerings in sanctuaries, and are often identified with Apollo.

Kore

The kore (plural korai) is the female counterpart: a standing, fully dressed maiden wearing a chiton and often a himation, with elaborately patterned drapery and hair. The korai dedicated on the Athenian Acropolis — many buried after the Persian sack of 480 BCE and excavated in the 1880s — preserve traces of their original bright paint.

Characteristics and development

Early works follow the block-like "Daedalic" style with wig-like hair and flat patterning. Archaic figures famously wear the enigmatic Archaic smile, perhaps a convention signifying vitality rather than emotion. Over the 6th century BCE anatomy became progressively more observed — muscle groups, kneecaps, and the fall of drapery were rendered with growing confidence, culminating in late Archaic works such as the Kritios Boy (c. 480 BCE), which bridges into the Classical.

Pedimental and architectural sculpture

Temples carried carved pediments and friezes: notable examples include the gorgon pediment of the Temple of Artemis at Corfu (c. 580 BCE) and the Siphnian Treasury frieze at Delphi (c. 525 BCE), whose Gigantomachy shows Archaic narrative carving at its most refined.
