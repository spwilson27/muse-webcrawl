Psychostasis

Psychostasis (from Greek psyche, "soul", and stasis, "standing/weighing") is the weighing of the soul — or of the heart or deeds — of a deceased person to determine their fate in the afterlife. The motif appears earliest and most fully in ancient Egyptian religion and was inherited, in altered forms, by Greek, Jewish and Christian traditions of the Last Judgement.

Egyptian psychostasis

In the Egyptian Book of the Dead (spell 125), the heart of the deceased is weighed on a balance against the feather of Ma'at, goddess of truth and cosmic order, in the Hall of the Two Truths. Anubis adjusts the scales and Thoth records the result while the deceased recites the "negative confession". If the heart is heavy with sin, it is devoured by the monster Ammit and the soul is annihilated; if it balances, the deceased is declared "true of voice" and admitted to the Field of Reeds.

Later traditions

Classical Greek literature knew a weighing of fates (the kerostasia of Zeus in the Iliad). In medieval Christian art the psychostasis becomes the weighing of souls by the Archangel Michael, whose scales — often with a devil pulling at one pan — appear in Romanesque tympana (as at Autun) and in countless Last Judgement frescoes. Related weighing imagery occurs in Zoroastrian eschatology, where deeds are weighed at the Chinvat Bridge.
