Unreliable narrator


An unreliable narrator's account of events cannot be taken at face value.

An unreliable narrator is a narrator whose credibility is compromised in the eyes of the reader. The term was coined by literary critic Wayne C. Booth in his 1961 work The Rhetoric of Fiction, where he defined a narrator as reliable when he speaks for or acts in accordance with the norms of the work (i.e., the implied author's norms), and unreliable when he does not. Unreliable narrators may lie deliberately, be deceived themselves, be mentally unstable, be too young or naïve to understand events, or hold prejudices that distort their account.

Types

Literary scholars have proposed various typologies. James Phelan distinguishes misreporting (errors about facts and events), misreading (faulty interpretation or perception), and misevaluating (flawed moral judgment). Common categories of unreliable narrators include:

  • The liar or pícaro — deliberately deceives, as in many trickster tales.
  • The madman — whose grip on reality is fractured, such as the narrator of Gogol's Diary of a Madman or Poe's The Tell-Tale Heart.
  • The naïf — too young or inexperienced to comprehend events, like Huckleberry Finn or Scout in To Kill a Mockingbird.
  • The biased or self-deceived — such as Stevens, the butler in Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day.

Examples

Famous examples include Humbert Humbert in Nabokov's Lolita, who seduces the reader into sympathizing with a criminal; the governess in Henry James's The Turn of the Screw, whose possible hallucination of ghosts has fueled a century of critical debate; Holden Caulfield in The Catcher in the Rye; and the narrator of Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, whose concealment is a celebrated fair-play twist. In film, the device structures works such as Rashomon (multiple conflicting accounts), Fight Club, and The Usual Suspects.
