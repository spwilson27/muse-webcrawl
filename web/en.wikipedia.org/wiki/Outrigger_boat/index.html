Outrigger boat

An outrigger boat is a watercraft fitted with one or more lateral support floats, called outriggers (in Pacific languages, ama), connected to the main hull by crossbeams (aka). The outrigger provides great stability for a narrow hull, allowing slender, fast canoes to sail open ocean without ballast. Outrigger canoes were the characteristic vessels of the Austronesian peoples, whose voyages settled Madagascar, island Southeast Asia and the whole Pacific as far as Hawaii, Easter Island and New Zealand.

Types

Single-outrigger canoes carry one ama, held to windward or leeward depending on regional practice — Pacific proas keep the outrigger to windward and "shunt" rather than tack. Double-outrigger boats, common in island Southeast Asia and around the Indian Ocean (for example Tamil and Javanese craft), carry floats on both sides. The configuration also appears on modern craft: outrigger racing canoes (OC-1, OC-6) used in the sport of outrigger canoeing, and trimarans, which are in effect double-outrigger yachts.

History and spread

Outriggers arose among Austronesian-speaking peoples several thousand years ago. Lashed-lug construction and crab-claw sails made their canoes capable of long blue-water passages, carrying settlers, crops and animals across the Pacific in one of the great maritime expansions of prehistory. Today outrigger canoes remain everyday fishing and transport craft across Oceania and Southeast Asia, and six-person outrigger racing is a signature sport of Hawaii and Tahiti.
