Panthera is a genus within the family Felidae whose members — the lion, tiger, leopard, jaguar and snow leopard — are commonly known as the "big cats". The genus was named by Lorenz Oken in 1816. Four of the five species are capable of roaring, owing to distinctive anatomy of the larynx and hyoid apparatus; the snow leopard, long placed in its own genus Uncia, cannot roar but is grouped within Panthera on genetic evidence.

Evolution

The Pantherinae lineage diverged from other cats roughly 11 million years ago; the genus Panthera itself arose in Asia, with the oldest known fossil species, Panthera blytheae from Tibet (about 4–6 million years old), resembling the snow leopard. Tigers and snow leopards form one lineage, lions, leopards and jaguars another; jaguars reached the Americas via Beringia. Extinct members include the American lion (P. atrox), the cave lion (P. spelaea) and the European jaguar.

Characteristics

Panthera species are large, muscular apex predators with proportionally short faces, retractile claws and (in the four roaring species) an incompletely ossified hyoid suspending a modified larynx. Coat patterns differ: lions are largely plain (males maned), tigers striped, leopards spotted, jaguars rosetted with central dots, and snow leopards smoky-grey with pale rosettes. Melanistic leopards and jaguars are the animals commonly called "black panthers".

Distribution and conservation

The genus historically ranged across Africa, Asia, Europe and the Americas. All five species are now threatened to some degree — the tiger, lion, leopard and snow leopard are listed as Endangered or Vulnerable by the IUCN, and the jaguar as Near Threatened — primarily through habitat loss, poaching (including for body parts used in traditional medicine) and conflict with humans over livestock.

Hybrids

In captivity the species hybridise: lion × tiger crosses produce ligers and tigons; leopard × lion and jaguar × leopard crosses are also documented, though such hybrids are generally sterile or reduced in fertility.
