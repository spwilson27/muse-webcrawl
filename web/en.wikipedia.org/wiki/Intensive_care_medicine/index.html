Intensive care medicine

Intensive care medicine, also called critical care medicine, is the medical specialty concerned with the diagnosis and management of life-threatening conditions requiring sophisticated organ support and invasive monitoring. It is practised in intensive care units (ICUs) by multidisciplinary teams led by physicians called intensivists, together with critical care nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, physiotherapists and dietitians. Patients typically have, or are at risk of, failure of one or more organ systems — respiratory, cardiovascular, renal, hepatic or neurological.

History

The specialty traces its origins to Florence Nightingale's practice of grouping the most seriously wounded soldiers near the nursing station during the Crimean War, and to the recovery rooms of the early 20th century. Modern intensive care is usually dated to the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic, when anaesthetist Bjørn Ibsen organised manual positive-pressure ventilation of patients with respiratory paralysis by relays of medical students, dramatically reducing mortality and leading to the establishment of the first multidisciplinary ICU in Copenhagen in 1953. Dedicated units spread through the 1960s with advances in mechanical ventilation, haemodialysis, defibrillation and continuous monitoring.

Scope of practice

Core interventions include airway management and mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs and advanced haemodynamic monitoring, renal replacement therapy, sedation and analgesia, management of sepsis and septic shock, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multi-organ failure, major trauma, poisoning and post-cardiac-arrest care. Intensivists coordinate care for patients with complex interactions between chronic diseases and acute illness, lead decisions about the appropriateness and limits of life-sustaining treatment, and manage end-of-life care. Scoring systems such as APACHE and SOFA are used for prognostication and benchmarking.

Training and organisation

Training pathways vary by country: in the United States critical care is a fellowship subspecialty of internal medicine, anaesthesiology, surgery or paediatrics; in the United Kingdom intensive care medicine became a standalone specialty in 2010 with its own Faculty. The COVID-19 pandemic placed unprecedented strain on ICU capacity worldwide and highlighted issues of triage, staffing and long-term outcomes for survivors, including post-intensive care syndrome.
