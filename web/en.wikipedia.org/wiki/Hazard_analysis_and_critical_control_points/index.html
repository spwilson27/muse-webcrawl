Hazard analysis and critical control points

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hazard analysis and critical control points, or HACCP, is a systematic preventive approach to food safety from biological, chemical and physical hazards in production processes that can cause the finished product to be unsafe. It designs measures to reduce these risks to a safe level. Rather than relying on inspection of finished products, HACCP identifies the specific points in a process — the critical control points — where hazards can be prevented, eliminated or reduced, and monitors them continuously.

History

HACCP emerged in the 1960s from a collaboration between the Pillsbury Company, the United States Army Laboratories and NASA, which needed food for astronauts that could be guaranteed safe; the approach adapted engineering reliability methods (failure mode and effects analysis) to food production. Pillsbury presented the system publicly in 1971, and it was progressively adopted by regulators: the US Food and Drug Administration built HACCP rules for seafood (1995), meat and poultry (through the USDA, 1996) and juice (2001), and the Codex Alimentarius incorporated HACCP into its General Principles of Food Hygiene. The EU requires food businesses to maintain HACCP-based procedures.

The seven principles

  1. Conduct a hazard analysis — identify biological, chemical and physical hazards at each process step.
  2. Determine the critical control points (CCPs) — points where control is essential to prevent or eliminate a hazard.
  3. Establish critical limits — maximum/minimum values (e.g., temperature, time, pH) for each CCP.
  4. Establish monitoring procedures — planned measurements or observations of each CCP.
  5. Establish corrective actions — actions to take when monitoring shows a loss of control.
  6. Establish verification procedures — confirm the system works effectively.
  7. Establish record-keeping and documentation — maintain records of the HACCP plan and its operation.

Application

HACCP is applied throughout the food chain — primary production, manufacturing, distribution, catering and retail — and its logic has been adapted to pharmaceuticals, cosmetics and water safety planning (where the WHO promotes analogous Water Safety Plans). Certification schemes such as ISO 22000 build on HACCP.
