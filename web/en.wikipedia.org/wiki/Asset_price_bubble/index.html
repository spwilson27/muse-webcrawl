Asset price bubble

An asset price bubble, or economic bubble, is a situation in which asset prices appear to be based on implausible or inconsistent views about the future, rising far above the assets' intrinsic value. Bubbles occur in markets for equities, real estate, commodities and other assets, and are typically followed by a crash in which prices fall sharply, often causing wider economic damage.

Characteristics

Economist Charles Kindleberger, following Hyman Minsky, described a typical pattern: a displacement (such as a new technology or financial innovation) creates new profit opportunities; credit expansion fuels a boom; euphoria sets in as prices rise and attract widespread speculation; profit-taking by insiders begins; and finally panic selling produces a crash. During the mania phase, normal standards of valuation are often dismissed with claims that "this time is different".

Historical examples

Famous bubbles include the Dutch tulip mania of the 1630s, the South Sea Bubble of 1720, the railway manias of the 1840s, the Florida land boom and stock market bubble of the 1920s, the Japanese asset price bubble of the late 1980s, the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, and the United States housing bubble whose collapse precipitated the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Causes and debate

Proposed causes include excessive monetary liquidity and low interest rates, herd behaviour and momentum trading, moral hazard created by implicit guarantees, and financial liberalisation. Some economists, following the efficient-market hypothesis, dispute whether bubbles can be identified in advance, since prices may reflect genuine expectations about future earnings. Others argue that experimental markets and repeated historical episodes demonstrate systematic mispricing driven by speculation.

Impact

Bursting bubbles can destroy household wealth, trigger banking crises and recessions, and leave lasting debts, as after the Japanese bubble's collapse, which was followed by the "Lost Decades" of slow growth. Central banks debate whether to "lean against" bubbles with tighter policy or merely clean up after they burst.
