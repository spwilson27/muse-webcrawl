A weasel is a mammal of the genus Mustela of the family Mustelidae. The term is applied loosely to several small, slender carnivores with long bodies and short legs, but in its strictest sense refers to the least weasel (Mustela nivalis), the smallest living carnivoran, and to the closely related long-tailed weasel (Mustela frenata) of the Americas.

Description and behaviour

Weasels are small predators with elongated bodies, short legs, and sharp senses, adapted to hunting small mammals — particularly voles, mice, and rats — in burrows too narrow for larger predators. The least weasel weighs as little as 25–45 grams and measures 11–17 cm in body length. In northern populations, the coat turns white in winter (in that state the animal is often called an "ermine" or "stoat," terms that more strictly refer to Mustela erminea).

Weasels are solitary, highly active hunters with fast metabolisms; they may kill prey several times their own body weight and cache surplus kills for later. Females may produce two litters a year. Their short lifespans (typically one to three years in the wild) and high reproductive rate let populations respond quickly to rodent abundance.

Distribution and cultural history

Weasels occur throughout much of the Northern Hemisphere. They have historically been both valued as rodent controllers and persecuted as poultry thieves, and in the folklore of many cultures they carry a reputation for cunning. The word "weasel" has entered English idioms ("to weasel out of something") for evasion and sneakiness, and the animals appear in folklore and literature from ancient Greek fables to modern children's stories.
