In Hinduism, Manu (Sanskrit: मनु) is the title given to the progenitor of humanity in each cosmic cycle (manvantara). The first Manu, Svayambhuva Manu, is regarded as the legendary author of the Manusmriti (the Laws of Manu), the most famous of the Hindu law books. The word is related to the Sanskrit root man-, "to think", and Manu is conceptually linked to the English word "man" through a shared Indo-European origin.

The fourteen Manus

Hindu cosmology divides a kalpa (a day of Brahma) into fourteen manvantaras, each presided over by its own Manu. The Manu of the present age is Vaivasvata Manu, the seventh. He is the son of the sun god Surya (Vivasvan) and is best known for the flood myth: warned by the god Vishnu, who took the form of a fish (Matsya), Vaivasvata built a boat and survived a great deluge, afterwards repopulating the earth — a story parallel to those of Noah and other flood heroes.

Manu in the Vedas

Manu appears in the Rigveda as the first sacrificer and the father of mankind. The Brahmanas and later texts expand his role as the archetypal king, priest, and lawgiver from whom social order (dharma) derives.

The Manusmriti

The Manusmriti, composed roughly between the 2nd century BCE and the 2nd century CE, presents itself as the teaching of Svayambhuva Manu, transmitted through the sage Bhrigu. It codifies rules of caste, family life, kingship, and ritual. British colonial administrators used it as a principal source of "Hindu law," giving it an authority it had not always held, and the text remains controversial for its prescriptions about caste and women.
