Chavín culture

The Chavín culture was an early civilisation of the central Andes, flourishing roughly between 900 and 200 BCE in what is now Peru. Named after the site of Chavín de Huántar, it is regarded as one of the earliest widespread cultural horizons of the Andean region, uniting the highlands and coast through shared religious art and iconography.

Chavín de Huántar

The ceremonial centre of Chavín de Huántar, in the Mosna valley highlands, consists of massive temple platforms built of stone, with interior galleries and ventilation and drainage channels. In one cruciform gallery stands the Lanzón, a 4.5-metre granite monolith carved with a fanged deity, one hand raised to heaven and the other lowered to earth. Other celebrated carvings include the Raimondi Stela and the Tello Obelisk, covered in complex imagery of caymans, jaguars, eagles and plants.

Art and religion

The Chavín style spread widely across northern and central Peru, appearing in pottery, textiles, goldwork and stone carving. Its art uses a technique of competing lines and reversible images, and depicts shamanic transformation between human and animal forms — jaguars, harpy eagles, caimans and snakes — possibly connected with the use of hallucinogenic plants such as the San Pedro cactus. The culture appears to have spread through a religious cult rather than military conquest.
