Deflection (engineering)

In structural engineering, deflection is the degree to which a part of a structural element is displaced under a load (because it bends). It may refer to an angle or a distance. The distance of deflection of a member under a load can be calculated by integrating the function that mathematically describes the slope of the deflected shape of the member under that load.

Beam deflection

Standard formulas exist for the deflection of common beam configurations and load cases. For a cantilever beam of length L with a point load P at the free end, the maximum deflection is δ = PL³/(3EI), where E is the Young's modulus and I is the second moment of area of the cross-section. For a simply supported beam with a uniformly distributed load w, the maximum deflection at midspan is δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI). More complex cases are handled by the methods of Euler–Bernoulli beam theory — such as integration of the moment–curvature equation, moment-area theorems, virtual work, or superposition — or by finite element analysis.

Design limits

Building codes limit deflection for serviceability rather than strength: excessive deflection cracks finishes and partitions, causes ponding on roofs, misaligns doors and glazing, and feels unsafe to occupants even when the structure is in no danger of collapse. Common limits are expressed as span ratios — for example L/360 for floor live-load deflection or L/240 for roof deflection under some codes — with stricter limits for members supporting brittle finishes. Deflection is proportional to the cube (cantilever) or fourth power (simply supported, distributed load) of span, so length is usually the dominant design variable.
