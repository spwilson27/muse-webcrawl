Workplace harassment

Workplace harassment is belittling, threatening or otherwise offensive behaviour directed at an individual worker or group of workers, which may be verbal, physical, psychological or digital. It ranges from incivility and bullying to discriminatory and sexual harassment. Unlike a one-off disagreement, harassment typically involves a pattern of conduct that creates an intimidating, hostile or humiliating working environment, and employers in many jurisdictions bear legal responsibility for preventing it and remedying complaints.

Forms

  • Sexual harassment — unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favours and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature, divided in US law into quid pro quo harassment and hostile-work-environment harassment.
  • Discriminatory harassment — conduct targeting protected characteristics such as race, sex, religion, disability, age or sexual orientation.
  • Workplace bullying and mobbing — persistent negative acts such as exclusion, humiliation, excessive criticism and sabotage; Heinz Leymann's research in Sweden in the 1980s established "mobbing" as an occupational-health problem.
  • Psychological and third-party harassment — including abuse by customers, patients or clients, and online ("cyber") harassment.

Legal frameworks

In the United States, harassment based on protected characteristics violates Title VII of the Civil Rights Act 1964 and related statutes; the Supreme Court recognised sexual harassment claims in Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson (1986) and set employer-liability standards in the Faragher and Ellerth decisions (1998). The EU's equality directives and the UK Equality Act 2010 similarly prohibit harassment related to protected characteristics, and France criminalises harcèlement moral (moral harassment) — a framework tested in the France Télécom case, in which executives were convicted in 2019 over a restructuring linked to a wave of employee suicides. The ILO's Violence and Harassment Convention No. 190 (2019) is the first international treaty recognising a universal right to a world of work free from violence and harassment.

Effects and prevalence

Surveys commonly find that a substantial minority of workers experience bullying or harassment in a given period. Consequences documented in occupational-health research include anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress symptoms, cardiovascular disease, sickness absence and high staff turnover; the US EEOC's 2016 task force report emphasised that training alone is ineffective without leadership accountability and accessible complaint systems. The #MeToo movement from 2017 onwards dramatically raised reporting of sexual harassment and prompted legislative reforms, including limits on non-disclosure agreements, in many countries.
