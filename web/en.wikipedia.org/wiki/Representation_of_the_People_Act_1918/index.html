Representation of the People Act 1918

The Representation of the People Act 1918 (7 & 8 Geo. 5. c. 64) was an act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that fundamentally reformed the electoral system in Great Britain and Ireland. It is sometimes known as the Fourth Reform Act. The act extended the franchise in parliamentary elections to all men aged 21 or over, whether or not they owned property, and to women aged 30 or over who met minimum property qualifications. It was the first act to include women in the parliamentary franchise, though on unequal terms with men.

Background

Before 1918 the franchise was restricted by property qualifications, and around 40 per cent of adult men were excluded from voting, including most soldiers serving in the First World War. The anomaly that men fighting for their country could not vote, together with the long campaign of the women's suffrage movement, made reform politically unavoidable. A Speaker's Conference in 1916–17 reached cross-party agreement on the main lines of reform.

Provisions

The act abolished almost all property qualifications for men, enfranchising all men aged 21 and servicemen aged 19. Women over 30 who were householders, wives of householders, occupiers of property worth £5 or more, or graduates of British universities gained the vote. The act also introduced a system of registration, set a single qualifying date, provided for absent voting by post for servicemen, and redistributed seats to reflect population changes, increasing the House of Commons to 707 members.

Effects

The electorate roughly tripled, from 7.7 million in 1912 to 21.4 million by the end of 1918, with women making up about 40 per cent of the new electorate. The first election held under the act was the 1918 general election. Full equal franchise for women at age 21 was not achieved until the Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928.
