Mainstreaming (education)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mainstreaming, in the context of special education, is the practice of placing students with special educational needs in general education classrooms during specific times of the school day, usually for subjects in which the student can participate successfully with appropriate supports. It rests on the philosophy that students with disabilities benefit academically and socially from learning alongside their non-disabled peers whenever possible.

Legal basis

In the United States, mainstreaming grew out of the Education for All Handicapped Children Act of 1975 (now the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, IDEA), which requires that children with disabilities be educated in the least restrictive environment — that is, with non-disabled peers to the maximum extent appropriate. Each student's Individualized Education Program (IEP) determines the balance between general and special education settings.

Mainstreaming versus inclusion

Mainstreaming is often contrasted with inclusion (or full inclusion): under mainstreaming, a student primarily belongs to a special education class and joins general classes for selected activities, whereas inclusion places the student in the general classroom as the primary placement, with support services brought to the student. Critics of mainstreaming argue it can fail students placed without adequate support; supporters counter that it balances social participation with individualised instruction.
