The AFL–NFL merger was the merger of the established National Football League (NFL) and the rival American Football League (AFL), agreed in 1966 and fully implemented in 1970. The agreement ended the expensive bidding war for players between the two leagues, created a common draft, and established an annual championship game between the leagues' champions — the AFL–NFL World Championship Game, soon renamed the Super Bowl.

Background

Founded in 1960, the AFL survived its early years and prospered with lucrative television contracts, signing star college players such as Joe Namath for record sums. By the mid-1960s, escalating salaries and competition for players and television revenue pressured both leagues toward an accommodation. Secret negotiations, led on the NFL side by Dallas Cowboys executive Tex Schramm and on the AFL side by Kansas City Chiefs owner Lamar Hunt, produced the deal, which required — and received — an antitrust exemption from the United States Congress.

Terms and implementation

Under the merger, all ten AFL franchises were retained. To balance the two resulting conferences at thirteen teams each, three NFL clubs — the Baltimore Colts, Cleveland Browns, and Pittsburgh Steelers — agreed to join the former AFL teams in the new American Football Conference, while the remaining NFL teams formed the National Football Conference. A single league office under NFL commissioner Pete Rozelle, a common schedule (from 1970), and a combined draft followed. The first Super Bowl was played in January 1967; the AFL's legitimacy was underlined when its champions, the New York Jets and Kansas City Chiefs, won Super Bowls III and IV.
