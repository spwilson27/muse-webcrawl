Royal forest

A royal forest, in medieval England and parts of continental Europe, was an area of land — not necessarily wooded — reserved for the king's hunting and placed under forest law, a separate and often harsh legal regime protecting the game (the "venison") and its habitat (the "vert"). At their greatest extent in the twelfth century, royal forests covered perhaps a third of England.

Forest law

The institution was introduced to England by the Norman kings after 1066, above all William the Conqueror, who created the New Forest in Hampshire around 1079. Within a forest, special courts — attachment, swainmote, and eyre courts — enforced rules that forbade hunting deer and boar, clearing woodland ("assarting"), enclosing land, or letting dogs run unmuzzled; dogs kept within forests could be "lawed", having claws removed, to prevent them chasing game. Offences against the venison were originally punished as severely as homicide under the Normans — with blinding or death — though penalties moderated to fines over time.

Officials such as wardens, foresters, verderers, agisters, and regarders administered the forest. Local inhabitants retained limited common rights — grazing (pannage for pigs feeding on acorns and beechmast, and pasturage), gathering wood (estovers) — under regulation.

Extent and grievance

Because "forest" was a legal status, royal forests included villages, farmland, heath, and moor as well as woodland. Kings extended forests opportunistically, and the afforestation of whole counties under Henry II and his sons made forest law a major political grievance. The Charter of the Forest (1217), issued alongside Magna Carta, disafforested lands wrongly taken, curtailed the severest punishments, and defined commoners' rights; it remained an important statement of liberties for centuries.

Decline

From the later Middle Ages the forests' legal machinery decayed as kings sold disafforestation rights and timber, and as deer hunting became less central to kingship. Charles I briefly revived forest eyres in the 1630s as a revenue device, provoking resistance. Most remaining forests were formally disafforested in the nineteenth century, though the New Forest, the Forest of Dean, and Epping Forest survive as protected landscapes, and hereditary verderers and agisters still operate in the New Forest under its own Acts of Parliament. The modern Forestry Commission descends in part from the administration of the former royal forests.

Similar reserved hunting forests existed in medieval France, Scotland, and Germany, and the concept influenced colonial forest reserves and, arguably, modern protected-area law.
