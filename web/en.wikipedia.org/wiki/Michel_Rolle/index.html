Michel Rolle

Born: 21 April 1652, Ambert, France
Died: 8 November 1719, Paris, France
Known for: Rolle's theorem; Gaussian elimination in Europe

Michel Rolle (21 April 1652 – 8 November 1719) was a French mathematician, best known for Rolle's theorem (1691), a fundamental result in differential calculus stating that between any two roots of a differentiable function there is at least one point where the derivative is zero.

Life and work

Rolle was largely self-educated. Born at Ambert in the Auvergne, he worked as a scribe and attorney's clerk before moving to Paris in 1675, where his skill in solving mathematical problems — including a challenge problem posed by Jacques Ozanam — brought him patrons and, in 1685, election to the Académie Royale des Sciences. His Traité d'algèbre (1690) gave an early European account of what is now Gaussian elimination and introduced the notation for the nth root, √[n]{x}, still in use.

Rolle's theorem

In his Méthode pour résoudre les égalités (1691) Rolle proved, for polynomials, the result now bearing his name, using his "method of cascades". The general form used today: if a real-valued function f is continuous on [a, b], differentiable on (a, b), and f(a) = f(b), then there exists c in (a, b) with f′(c) = 0. The mean value theorem of Lagrange is a direct generalisation.

Opposition to calculus

Ironically, Rolle became one of the fiercest critics of the new infinitesimal calculus of Leibniz, attacking its logical foundations in the Académie around 1700–1701 in controversy with Pierre Varignon and Joseph Saurin; he later reconciled with the calculus after realising his criticisms rested on misunderstandings.
