Globalization of wine

The globalization of wine is the expansion of wine production and consumption beyond its traditional European heartlands, and the convergence of styles, grape varieties and winemaking techniques that accompanied it in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Wine historians and journalists often divide producers into the "Old World" (France, Italy, Spain, Germany and other historic European regions) and the "New World" (the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and, increasingly, China and other parts of Asia).

History

Vines spread with European colonisation from the 16th century onward, establishing industries in South America, California, Australia and South Africa. The modern phase of globalization accelerated after the Judgment of Paris in 1976, when Californian wines outscored French classics in a blind tasting, demonstrating that fine wine could be made outside Europe's classic regions. New World producers, unconstrained by appellation rules, adopted scientific viticulture, stainless steel, temperature control and marketing by grape variety, and their fruit-forward styles influenced Old World producers in turn.

Convergence and "international style"

A small set of "international varieties" — Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot noir, Syrah, Chardonnay and Sauvignon blanc — came to dominate plantings worldwide, while flying winemakers and consultants spread shared techniques across hemispheres. Critics coined terms such as "Parkerization" (after critic Robert Parker) for the trend toward ripe, oaky, high-alcohol wines suited to international palates, and warned of a loss of regional distinctiveness and terroir. Others pointed to a counter-trend of revived local varieties and traditional methods, and to the growing importance of new markets — above all China, which became a major producer and consumer in the 2000s and 2010s.
