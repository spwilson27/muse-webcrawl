Eigengrau

Literal meaning: "Intrinsic grey" (German)

Also called: Eigenlicht ("intrinsic light"), visual noise, dark light

Perceived colour: Dark grey, lighter than true black

Eigengrau (German for "intrinsic grey"; pronounced [ˈaɪ̯ɡŋ̍ˌɡʁaʊ̯]), also called Eigenlicht ("intrinsic light"), dark light or brain grey, is the uniform dark grey background colour that many people report seeing in the absence of light. The term dates to the nineteenth century, but recent research in visual science has connected the phenomenon to spontaneous activity and noise in the visual system.

Perception

Even in total darkness, the visual field is not experienced as pure black but as a dark grey, flecked with shifting noise. The owner of the experience sees eigengrau as lighter than a black object at normal lighting conditions, because contrast is more important to the visual system than absolute brightness. A truly black surface in a lit room therefore looks blacker than eigengrau does in a dark one.

Causes

The phenomenon arises from intrinsic noise in the retina and visual pathways: photoreceptor cells and retinal ganglion cells fire spontaneously at a low rate, and the thermal isomerisation of rhodopsin in rod cells produces events indistinguishable from the absorption of real photons. The brain interprets this baseline activity as a dim, uniform grey. Phosphenes — seen when pressure is applied to the eye or the retina is stimulated — are related intrinsic visual phenomena.

History

The term was introduced by Gustav Theodor Fechner in the nineteenth century in the context of psychophysics and threshold perception, and the concept was developed by later researchers studying dark adaptation and the absolute threshold of vision.
