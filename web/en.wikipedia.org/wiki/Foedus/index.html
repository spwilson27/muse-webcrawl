Foedus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In ancient Rome, a foedus (plural foedera) was a formal treaty or league concluded between Rome and another people or state. The word derives from Latin foedus, "agreement" or "compact". Treaties were ratified with religious ceremony conducted by the fetial priests (fetiales), involving oaths and sacrifice, and the texts were preserved on bronze tablets.

Types

Roman lawyers distinguished the foedus aequum, an equal treaty between independent partners, from the foedus iniquum, an unequal treaty in which the allied people were bound to "uphold the majesty of the Roman people" — in practice a mark of subordination. Communities bound by a foedus were civitates foederatae; their troops served Rome as socii (allies), and the terms governed matters such as military support, territory, and legal relations.

Late antiquity

In the later Roman Empire, foedera were concluded with Germanic and other peoples — Goths, Vandals, Franks and others — who were settled within imperial territory as foederati in return for military service. The Gothic settlement of 382 under Theodosius I is a famous example. The arrangements shaped the transformation of the western empire into successor kingdoms in the 5th century.
