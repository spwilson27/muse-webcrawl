ed is a line editor for the Unix operating system and the standard text editor defined by the POSIX standard. It was one of the first components of Unix, written by Ken Thompson at Bell Laboratories around 1969–1971, and was influenced by the QED editor Thompson had worked on earlier.

Features

Unlike a screen editor, ed works one line at a time: the user issues terse commands to print, insert, delete, substitute and move lines, addressed by line number or by regular expression patterns. Interaction is minimal by design — ed's only error message is a bare ? — reflecting its origins in an era of slow teletypes. A typical session:

$ ed notes.txt
?

Actually entering the file correctly produces a byte count rather than an error. The editor supports global commands (g/re/p) that are the ancestor of the name of the grep utility.

Influence

ed's command language shaped much of Unix text processing: the sed stream editor is essentially a scriptable descendant of ed's substitution commands; the interactive editors ex and vi grew out of it at Berkeley; and its regular-expression syntax became the basis of "basic regular expressions" throughout the Unix toolset. Because POSIX requires ed, it remains present on virtually every Unix-like system, where it is still used in shell scripts and as an editor of last resort.

Reputation

ed is the subject of affectionate humour in Unix culture. The humorous essay "Ed, man! !man ed" and the claim that "ed is the standard text editor" poke fun at its austerity in comparison with modern editors — yet its defenders note that it starts instantly, works on any terminal, and has changed little in half a century.
