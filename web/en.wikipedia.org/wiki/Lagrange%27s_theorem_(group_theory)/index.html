Lagrange's theorem (group theory)

In group theory, Lagrange's theorem states that for any finite group G, the order (number of elements) of every subgroup H of G divides the order of G. The theorem is named after Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who published a related result in 1771.

Proof

The left cosets of H in G partition G, and every coset has exactly as many elements as H (multiplication by a fixed group element is a bijection). Hence |G| = [G : H] · |H|, where [G : H] is the index of H in G, so |H| divides |G|.

Corollaries

An immediate consequence is that the order of any element of a finite group divides the order of the group, and hence a|G| = e for every a in G. A group of prime order is necessarily cyclic and has no nontrivial subgroups. The theorem is also a step toward the Sylow theorems.

Converse

The converse of Lagrange's theorem is false in general: a group may have no subgroup of some order dividing its own order. It holds for certain classes of groups, such as finite abelian groups and, by the Sylow theorems, for prime-power divisors.
