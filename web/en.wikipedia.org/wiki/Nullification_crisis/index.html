Nullification crisis

The nullification crisis was a United States political crisis of 1832–1833 in which the state of South Carolina declared that the federal tariffs of 1828 and 1832 were unconstitutional and therefore null and void within its boundaries. It was the most serious test of federal authority before the Civil War, pitting President Andrew Jackson against the nullification doctrine championed most prominently by his own former vice president, John C. Calhoun.

Background

Southern planters blamed the protective Tariff of 1828 — denounced in the South as the "Tariff of Abominations" — for raising the price of manufactured goods while depressing the market for cotton. Calhoun, anonymously authoring the South Carolina Exposition and Protest (1828), argued that a state, as a party to the constitutional compact, could nullify a federal law it judged unconstitutional. The debate became entangled with the politics of slavery and states' rights. The Tariff of 1832 modestly reduced duties but did not satisfy South Carolina, where a state convention adopted an Ordinance of Nullification on November 24, 1832, threatening secession if the federal government attempted to collect the duties by force.

Confrontation and compromise

Jackson responded with a ringing Proclamation to the People of South Carolina (December 10, 1832), denying any right of nullification or secession, and asked Congress for a "Force Bill" authorising military enforcement. He privately threatened to hang Calhoun and reinforced federal forts in Charleston Harbor. At the same time Henry Clay brokered a compromise: the Compromise Tariff of 1833 gradually reduced duties over nearly a decade to 1816 levels. South Carolina's convention repealed its nullification of the tariffs in March 1833 — while symbolically nullifying the Force Bill — allowing both sides to claim vindication.

Significance

The crisis was defused, but the constitutional arguments it aired — compact theory, nullification, and secession — remained central to American politics and returned with far greater force in 1860–61. It also marked the final rupture between Jackson and Calhoun and helped define the emerging second party system.
