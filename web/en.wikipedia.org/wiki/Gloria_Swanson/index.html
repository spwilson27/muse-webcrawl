Gloria Swanson (27 March 1899 – 4 April 1983) was an American actress, one of the defining stars of the silent-film era and among the highest-paid performers of the 1920s. After her career faded with the coming of sound, she achieved an immortal comeback as the reclusive former silent star Norma Desmond in Billy Wilder's Sunset Boulevard (1950), delivering the famous line "I'm ready for my close-up, Mr. DeMille."

Silent stardom

Born in Chicago, Swanson began as an extra at Essanay Studios in 1914 and appeared in small roles, including brief work with Charlie Chaplin, before joining Mack Sennett's Keystone comedies in 1916. Her breakthrough came at Paramount under director Cecil B. DeMille, whose sophisticated marital dramas — Don't Change Your Husband (1919), Male and Female (1919), Why Change Your Wife? (1920) and The Affairs of Anatol (1921) — made her the screen's emblem of glamour. By the mid-1920s she was earning enormous salaries, was voted the world's most popular actress, and formed her own production company, releasing films through United Artists.

Queen Kelly and the sound era

Backed by financier Joseph P. Kennedy, Swanson produced Queen Kelly (1928–29), directed by Erich von Stroheim; the production collapsed amid censorship problems and Stroheim's dismissal, and the film went unreleased in the United States for decades — a legendary fiasco later quoted in Sunset Boulevard itself. She earned Academy Award nominations for Sadie Thompson (1928) and The Trespasser (1929), her talkie debut, but her popularity waned in the 1930s and she largely withdrew from films, pursuing business ventures including a patents and inventions company that helped rescue Jewish scientists from Nazi Europe.

Sunset Boulevard and later life

Wilder cast Swanson — after several other former stars declined — as Norma Desmond, a wealthy, delusional silent-film queen planning a return to the screen. Released in 1950, Sunset Boulevard won her a Golden Globe and a third Oscar nomination and is now routinely ranked among the greatest films ever made. Swanson thereafter worked selectively: Broadway and television appearances, a late film role in Airport 1975, and a bestselling autobiography, Swanson on Swanson (1980). A lifelong health-food enthusiast, she was also a prominent advocate of yoga and macrobiotic diets. She died in New York in 1983.
