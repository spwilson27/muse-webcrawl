Quadriga

A quadriga (Latin quadriuga, from quattuor, "four", and iugum, "yoke") is a chariot drawn by four horses abreast, used for racing and for triumphal processions in ancient Greece and Rome. Chariot racing with four-horse teams, the tethrippon, was added to the Olympic Games in 680 BC, and quadriga races were the highlight of the Roman circus games.

In art and architecture

The quadriga became a standard emblem of triumph and divine power, driven in art by Apollo, Helios, Nike and emperors. The most famous surviving ancient example is the set of four gilded bronze horses now in St Mark's Basilica, Venice, looted from the Hippodrome of Constantinople in 1204. Neoclassical triumphal arches revived the motif: the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin is crowned by a quadriga driven by Victoria (1793), as are the Wellington Arch in London, the Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel in Paris and the Soldiers' and Sailors' Arch in Brooklyn.

Racing

In Rome the quadriga was the premier racing chariot of the Circus Maximus, driven by a quadrigarius for one of the four factions — Reds, Blues, Greens and Whites. Victory brought enormous fame and prize money; the charioteer Gaius Appuleius Diocles won 1,462 races and retired one of the richest athletes of antiquity.
