South Asian Monsoon

The South Asian Monsoon, also known as the Indian monsoon, is a seasonal reversing wind system accompanied by large changes in precipitation that dominates the climate of South Asia. It is one of the world's most consequential weather systems, delivering roughly 70–80 percent of the annual rainfall of India and neighbouring countries and underpinning the agriculture on which more than a billion people depend.

Mechanism

The monsoon arises from the differential heating of land and ocean. In summer the Asian landmass — especially the Tibetan Plateau — heats strongly, generating low pressure that draws moist air inland from the Indian Ocean as the southwest monsoon. The Intertropical Convergence Zone shifts northward, and cross-equatorial flow from the Southern Hemisphere turns to become southwesterly winds over the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. In winter the circulation reverses: cool, dry air flows seaward as the northeast monsoon, which nonetheless brings significant rain to southeastern India and Sri Lanka.

Onset and progression

The southwest monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and advances north and west, covering India by mid-July. It withdraws from September, retreating southward by October. Two branches — the Arabian Sea branch and the Bay of Bengal branch — bring rain in a staggered pattern, interrupted by "active" and "break" spells. The Western Ghats force orographic rainfall on the windward coast, while the rain shadow east of the range is dry.

Impacts

Monsoon rainfall determines harvests, reservoir levels, and groundwater recharge across the subcontinent; both failure (drought) and excess (flooding, as in the annual floods of Assam and Bangladesh) have severe consequences. The system's variability is modulated by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (see Southern Oscillation), the Indian Ocean Dipole, and intraseasonal oscillations. Long-term climate change is projected to intensify monsoon precipitation while increasing its variability.
