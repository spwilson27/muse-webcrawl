Attention

Attention
FieldCognitive psychology, neuroscience
TypesSelective, divided, sustained, executive, overt and covert
Key phenomenaInattentional blindness, cocktail party effect, attentional blink

Attention is the cognitive process of selectively concentrating on one aspect of the environment while ignoring other things. William James famously wrote that "everyone knows what attention is": the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought. Because sensory input vastly exceeds processing capacity, attention acts as a filter and amplifier that prioritises information relevant to current goals.

Types of attention

Psychologists distinguish several varieties. Selective attention focuses on one input at the expense of others, as in listening to a single voice at a noisy party (the "cocktail party effect"). Divided attention concerns multitasking and the costs of splitting resources. Sustained attention (vigilance) is maintaining focus over long periods, and executive attention resolves conflicts between competing responses. Attention may be overt (accompanied by eye movements) or covert, and driven by goals (endogenous) or captured by salient events (exogenous).

History of research

After early introspectionist work, the field was revived in the 1950s by Colin Cherry's dichotic listening experiments and Donald Broadbent's filter model (1958), which proposed an early selection bottleneck. Anne Treisman's attenuation model and feature integration theory, Daniel Kahneman's capacity model, and Michael Posner's work on attentional networks — alerting, orienting and executive control — shaped the modern view. Striking demonstrations such as inattentional blindness (missing a clearly visible unexpected object when attention is engaged) show how selective attention both enables and limits perception.

Neural basis and disorders

Attention involves distributed frontoparietal networks that bias activity in sensory cortex, modulated by neurotransmitter systems including acetylcholine and noradrenaline. Deficits appear in ADHD, in hemispatial neglect after parietal stroke, and in various psychiatric and neurological conditions. Applied interest in attention spans media use, education, human–computer interaction and the "attention economy".
