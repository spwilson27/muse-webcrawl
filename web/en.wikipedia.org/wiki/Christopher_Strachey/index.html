Christopher Strachey (16 November 1916 – 18 May 1975) was a British computer scientist, one of the founders of denotational semantics and a major figure in the design of programming languages. He was a member of the prominent Strachey family and professor of computation at the University of Oxford.

Early work

Strachey was educated at King's College, Cambridge. After wartime work on radar, he became a schoolmaster at Harrow, where in 1951 he wrote a draughts (checkers) program — the first video game — which eventually ran on the Ferranti Mark 1 at Manchester, the machine for which he also produced the famous "Love Letter" algorithm, an early work of generative text art.

Time-sharing and CPL

In 1959 Strachey presented the influential paper "Time Sharing in Large Fast Computers," advocating what became time-sharing systems, and filed a patent for the concept. In the early 1960s he led the design of CPL (Combined Programming Language), an ambitious language that, though never fully implemented, strongly influenced BCPL and hence the C programming language.

Semantics and Oxford

From 1965 Strachey directed the Programming Research Group at Oxford. With Dana Scott he developed denotational semantics — a mathematical approach to defining the meaning of programs — and introduced influential concepts such as the distinction between L-values and R-values, parametric and ad hoc polymorphism, and continuations. His lecture notes Fundamental Concepts in Programming Languages circulated widely and shaped the field.

Legacy

Strachey helped establish programming-language theory as a rigorous discipline. The annual Strachey Lectures at Oxford are named in his memory, and his work on semantics underlies modern research in programming languages and formal verification.
