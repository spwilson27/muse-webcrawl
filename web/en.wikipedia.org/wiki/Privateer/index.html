Privateer

A privateer is a private person or vessel authorised by a government by letters of marque to attack foreign vessels during wartime, and take them as prizes. Privateering allowed a state to project naval power and disrupt enemy commerce without the expense of maintaining a large navy; captured ships and cargo were sold, with the proceeds shared among the privateer's owners, captain, and crew.


Privateers operated under letters of marque issued by a government.

History

Privateering flourished from the 16th to the 19th centuries. Famous privateers include Francis Drake, whose raids on Spanish shipping were backed by Elizabeth I of England, and the corsairs of the Barbary Coast. During the American Revolutionary War and the War of 1812, American privateers captured hundreds of British merchant ships. Privateers were distinguished from pirates because they operated under legal authority; if captured, they were entitled to be treated as prisoners of war rather than hanged as criminals.

Abolition

The Declaration of Paris of 1856, signed by most major maritime powers after the Crimean War, abolished privateering among its signatories. The United States did not sign but generally observed its terms. Later agreements, including the Hague Conventions of 1907, further regulated the conversion of merchant ships into warships, effectively ending the practice.
