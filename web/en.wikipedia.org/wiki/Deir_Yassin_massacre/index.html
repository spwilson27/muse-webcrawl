Deir Yassin massacre

Deir Yassin massacre

Date9 April 1948
LocationDeir Yassin, Mandatory Palestine (near Jerusalem)
PerpetratorsIrgun and Lehi paramilitary groups, with Haganah support

Context1947–48 Civil War in Mandatory Palestine

The Deir Yassin massacre took place on 9 April 1948, when Zionist paramilitary groups of the Irgun (Etzel) and Lehi (Stern Gang) attacked Deir Yassin, a Palestinian Arab village of roughly 600 people on the western outskirts of Jerusalem. At least 107 villagers, including women and children, were killed. The attack occurred during the civil war phase of the 1948 Palestine war, despite a non-aggression pact between the village and the neighbouring Jewish community of Givat Shaul.

Background

Deir Yassin was a prosperous village known for its limestone quarries. Its residents had sought to stay out of the fighting and had reached an agreement with the Haganah-run Jewish neighbourhood of Givat Shaul not to allow Arab irregulars to use the village as a base, in exchange for safe passage. Nevertheless, the Irgun and Lehi, acting as part of Operation Nachshon to open the road to besieged Jewish Jerusalem, decided to capture the village. The Haganah command in Jerusalem approved the operation and provided some support, including covering fire.

The attack

The assault began before dawn on 9 April 1948. The attackers met stiffer resistance than expected and suffered casualties; fighting went house to house. During and after the fighting, villagers were killed in their homes and, according to survivors and later investigations, some prisoners were executed after capture. A number of survivors were paraded through West Jerusalem before being released. Estimates of the death toll were initially inflated — figures of 254 were widely reported at the time — while later scholarly research, including a Birzeit University study based on survivor testimony, put the number killed at around 107 to 120.

Aftermath and significance

The massacre was condemned by the Jewish Agency and the Haganah, and David Ben-Gurion expressed disapproval; the Irgun and Lehi disputed some of the accounts. News of the killings spread rapidly and is widely regarded by historians as a major accelerant of the flight and expulsion of Palestinian Arabs during the 1948 war — the events Palestinians call the Nakba. Menachem Begin, the Irgun commander, later argued that exaggerated reports of the massacre helped demoralise Arab forces, though they also provoked retaliatory attacks, including the Hadassah medical convoy massacre four days later.

The site of the village was later incorporated into West Jerusalem; the Kfar Shaul Mental Health Center was built on part of the land, and some village buildings survive. Deir Yassin remains one of the most symbolically charged episodes of the 1948 war, commemorated annually by Palestinians and debated by historians.
