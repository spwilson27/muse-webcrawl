Tynwald

Tynwald (Manx: Tinvaal), the parliament of the Isle of Man, is claimed to be the oldest continuous parliamentary body in the world, with a tradition dating to 979 AD or earlier. Its name derives from the Old Norse Þingvǫllr, "assembly field", reflecting the island's Norse heritage.

Structure

Tynwald consists of two branches that vote separately in most matters but sit together as Tynwald Court: the popularly elected House of Keys (24 members) and the Legislative Council, which includes members elected by the Keys, the Bishop of Sodor and Man, and the Attorney General. The Lord of Mann (the British monarch) is head of state, represented on the island by the Lieutenant Governor.

Tynwald Day

Each year on 5 July (Old Midsummer's Day), Tynwald meets in the open air on Tynwald Hill at St John's. In a ceremony in English and Manx Gaelic, new laws are promulgated — an Act does not take effect unless promulgated on the hill within eighteen months — and petitions for redress of grievance may be presented by any person. The hill's four tiers are said to incorporate soil from all seventeen historic parishes of the island.

History

Assemblies of Norse settlers met at the site for the proclamation of laws and settlement of disputes. Over the centuries the Keys evolved from a judicial into a legislative body, gaining direct election in 1866 and becoming the dominant branch. Women first gained the vote on the island in 1881, ahead of most of the world.
