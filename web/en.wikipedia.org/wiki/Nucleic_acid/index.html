Nucleic acid

Nucleic acids are large biomolecules that store and transmit genetic information in all known forms of life and many viruses. The two main types are deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA). Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides, each consisting of a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base.

Structure

Nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 3′ carbon of one sugar and the 5′ carbon of the next, giving the chain a direction. DNA normally forms a double helix in which two antiparallel strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases — adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine — as established by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953 using data from Rosalind Franklin and others. RNA is usually single-stranded, uses ribose instead of deoxyribose and uracil instead of thymine, and can fold into complex catalytic and regulatory structures.

Function

DNA stores the genome; its sequence of bases encodes the instructions for building proteins. RNA carries these instructions to the ribosome (messenger RNA), forms part of the ribosome itself (ribosomal RNA), brings amino acids to the growing chain (transfer RNA), and performs many regulatory roles. The flow of information from DNA to RNA to protein is known as the central dogma of molecular biology.

History

Nucleic acid was first isolated in 1869 by Swiss physician Friedrich Miescher, who called the phosphorus-rich substance from cell nuclei "nuclein". The Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment (1944) and the Hershey–Chase experiment (1952) established DNA as the hereditary material, paving the way for the double helix model.
