Thach Weave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Thach Weave (also known as a Beam Defense Position) is an aerial combat tactic developed by United States Navy fighter pilot John S. Thach and named by James H. Flatley. First used in combat during the Battle of Midway in June 1942, it enabled the comparatively slow and less maneuverable American F4F Wildcat fighters to defend themselves against the superior Japanese Mitsubishi A6M Zero.

Description

Two fighters (or pairs) fly in line abreast formation, spaced far enough apart that each can observe the other's rear. When an enemy fighter attacks one of the pair from behind, both fighters turn toward each other. Their crossing flight paths bring the attacker into the gunsight of the defender's wingman, forcing the attacker to break off or be shot down. The weave is repeated as needed, generating a defensive, interlocking maneuver that negates individual aircraft performance advantages.

History

Thach developed the tactic in 1941 while commanding Fighting Squadron Three (VF-3), testing it with matchsticks on his kitchen table after receiving intelligence reports on the Zero's performance. At Midway, his division used it successfully. The tactic was widely adopted by U.S. Navy and Allied air units for the rest of World War II and remains a foundational concept in air combat maneuvering.
