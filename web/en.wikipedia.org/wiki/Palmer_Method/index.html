The Palmer Method of penmanship instruction was developed and promoted by Austin Norman Palmer in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It was intended as a simpler, faster method of writing than the ornamental Spencerian script that dominated American handwriting instruction after the Civil War.

History

Austin Palmer, a penmanship teacher in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, began developing his system in the 1880s. Through his company and its publications, including Palmer's Guide to Business Writing (1894) and the widely used Palmer Method of Business Writing (1901), the method spread to schools across the United States. By the early 20th century it had largely displaced Spencerian copybooks in American classrooms, and it remained a standard of elementary instruction into the mid-20th century.

Technique

The Palmer Method emphasized "muscular movement" — writing driven by the muscles of the forearm rather than the fingers. Students performed repetitive drills of ovals and push-pull strokes to build rhythm and speed, and letters were formed as a simplified, slanting cursive with minimal ornamentation, loops, and shading. The goal was a legible, rapid hand suitable for business correspondence, taught through whole-arm movement, correct posture, and graded copybook exercises.

Influence and decline

The method's dominance waned after the Second World War as schools adopted manuscript (print) writing for young children, typewriters and later computers reduced the emphasis on penmanship, and competing systems such as the Zaner-Bloser method gained ground. The Palmer Method remains a reference point in the history of American handwriting education and is still taught in some schools and by handwriting revivalists.
