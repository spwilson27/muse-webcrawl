Rock art at Alta

Rock art at Alta
Location: Alta, Finnmark, Norway
UNESCO World Heritage Site: inscribed 1985
Age: c. 4200 BC – 500 BC

The rock art at Alta (Norwegian: Helleristningene i Alta) is a collection of prehistoric petroglyphs in Alta Municipality in Finnmark county, northern Norway. The carvings, some of the largest concentrations of hunter-gatherer rock art in Northern Europe, were inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1985. More than 6,000 figures have been documented at five main areas around the head of the Altafjord, the largest being Jiepmaluokta (Hjemmeluft).

Discovery and dating

The first carvings were discovered in 1973. The figures were carved into sloping bedrock slabs that at the time stood at the shoreline; because of post-glacial isostatic rebound, panels at different heights can be dated by their elevation above present sea level. The art spans roughly from 4200 BC to 500 BC, divided into phases, with the oldest panels highest (about 26 metres above today's sea level) and the youngest near the current shore.

Imagery

The petroglyphs depict reindeer (often in corrals or hunting scenes), bears, moose (elk), whales, seals, waterfowl, fish such as halibut, boats, and human figures engaged in hunting, fishing, dancing and ritual. Many figures have been painted red ochre to make them visible in modern times, following the Scandinavian tradition — a practice now debated by conservators. Scenes of reindeer fences and bear hunts with tracks ending in dens are among the most distinctive, and are interpreted as reflections of both subsistence practice and ritual or shamanistic belief.

World Heritage Centre

The Alta Museum — World Heritage Rock Art Centre at Hjemmeluft presents the carvings with boardwalk access to the panels, and interprets the art and the Stone Age settlement of the region.
