Mummy (undead)

Mummies are commonly featured as undead monsters in works of fiction, particularly horror films and games. Drawing on the ancient Egyptian practice of mummification and the Western fascination with Egypt that followed the opening of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922, the fictional mummy is typically a preserved corpse — often a priest or pharaoh — reanimated by a curse or ritual, wrapped in linen bandages and seeking vengeance, a lost relic, or a reincarnated lover.

History in fiction

Early mummy fiction appeared in the 19th century, including stories by Louisa May Alcott and Arthur Conan Doyle. The modern archetype was established by Universal Pictures' The Mummy (1932), starring Boris Karloff as Imhotep, which inspired sequels and the later Hammer horror films. The character was revived for a wider audience in the adventure film The Mummy (1999) and its sequels, which recast the monster in an action-adventure register.

Characteristics

The classic fictional mummy shambles slowly in decaying bandages, possesses great strength, and is often bound to a curse protecting a tomb. In games and role-playing systems, mummies are frequently given powers such as spreading disease ("mummy rot"), immunity to ordinary weapons, or vulnerability to fire.

In popular culture

Mummies appear throughout film, television, literature, comics, and video games, and the bandaged figure is a staple Halloween costume. The trope reflects both genuine historical practices and the "curse of the pharaohs" legend amplified by popular reporting after 1922.
