The wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is a type of apparent temperature used to estimate the effect of temperature, humidity, wind speed, sun angle and solar radiation on humans. Industrial hygienists, athletes and the military use it to determine appropriate exposure levels to high temperatures, and it is the basis of the international standard ISO 7243 for heat-stress assessment.

Measurement and formula

The WBGT combines three measurements: the natural wet-bulb temperature (Tw), the black-globe temperature (Tg), and the (shaded) dry-bulb air temperature (Ta). In direct sunlight the index is calculated as:

WBGT = 0.7·Tw + 0.2·Tg + 0.1·Ta

In shade (or at night), where solar load is absent, the formula is WBGT = 0.7·Tw + 0.3·Tg. The heavy weighting on the wet-bulb temperature reflects the dominant role of humidity in limiting evaporative cooling of the body.

History and use

The index was developed in the 1950s at the U.S. Marine Corps Recruit Depot Parris Island after a series of heat casualties among recruits; Yaglou and Minard published the method in 1957. Flag conditions derived from WBGT readings still govern physical training at U.S. military bases, and the index is used by occupational-safety bodies (NIOSH, ACGIH), sports federations, and event organisers to set work–rest cycles, hydration requirements, and cancellation thresholds.

WBGT should not be confused with the wet-bulb temperature alone, nor with the heat index: WBGT additionally accounts for solar radiation and is measured outdoors in the sun. Sustained WBGT values above about 32 °C are considered dangerous for unacclimatised people performing moderate work. Climate scientists have increasingly used WBGT projections to estimate how rising temperatures and humidity will restrict outdoor labour in tropical and subtropical regions.
