Antinomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An antinomy (Greek ἀντί, anti, "against", and νόμος, nomos, "law") is a pair of mutually contradictory but apparently equally well-proved statements or conclusions. The term is most famous from Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781), where the "antinomies of pure reason" are presented as evidence that reason falls into contradiction when it tries to apply its categories beyond possible experience, to the world as a whole.

Kant's four antinomies

  • First antinomy — Thesis: the world has a beginning in time and a limit in space. Antithesis: the world is infinite in time and space.
  • Second antinomy — Everything consists of indivisible simples; versus: nothing simple exists, and matter is infinitely divisible.
  • Third antinomy — There is free causality; versus: everything happens according to natural necessity.
  • Fourth antinomy — A necessary being exists; versus: there is no necessary being.

Kant resolved the first two (mathematical) antinomies by holding both sides false — the world as a thing in itself is neither finite nor infinite — and the last two (dynamical) by allowing both to be true in different standpoints: phenomena are determined, while the noumenal self may be free.

Other uses

In logic and law the term also denotes a real or apparent contradiction between two valid laws or principles. Later philosophers and logicians have applied it to paradoxes such as Russell's paradox, and it appears in critical theory as a name for deep structural tensions, for example within modernity or capital.
