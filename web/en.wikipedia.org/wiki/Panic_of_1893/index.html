The Panic of 1893 was a severe economic depression in the United States that began in 1893 and ended in 1897. Like the Panic of 1873, it was marked by the collapse of railroad overbuilding and shaky railroad financing, which set off a series of bank failures. It remains one of the worst depressions in American history before the Great Depression.

Causes

Decades of railroad expansion had been financed with heavy borrowing, much of it from European investors. When the British bank Barings crisis of 1890 (triggered by Argentine defaults) shook investor confidence, foreign capital withdrew. At home, the Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890 required the Treasury to buy silver with notes redeemable in gold, steadily draining federal gold reserves and raising fears that the United States would abandon the gold standard. The failure of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad in February 1893 and of the National Cordage Company in May triggered runs on banks and a stock market collapse.

Course and effects

Some 500 banks failed, as did roughly 15,000 businesses; major railroads including the Northern Pacific, Union Pacific, and Santa Fe went into receivership. Unemployment is estimated to have reached 17–19% at its peak, accompanied by farm distress, Coxey's Army of unemployed marchers on Washington (1894), and the bitter Pullman Strike of 1894. President Grover Cleveland, a gold-standard Democrat, secured repeal of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act in 1893 and, in 1895, arranged a controversial gold loan through a syndicate led by J. P. Morgan to restore Treasury reserves.

Political consequences

The depression shattered the Democratic Party and powered the free silver movement, culminating in William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech and the election of 1896, won by Republican William McKinley. The realignment of 1896 inaugurated a generation of Republican dominance in national politics.
