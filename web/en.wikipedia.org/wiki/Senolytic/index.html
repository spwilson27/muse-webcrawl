Senolytics are a class of small molecules under basic and clinical research to determine whether they can selectively induce death of senescent cells and improve health in humans. A senolytic agent targets the pro-survival pathways that senescent cells use to resist apoptosis, so that these accumulated, damaged cells can be cleared while healthy cells are spared.

Background

Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible cell-cycle arrest; senescent cells accumulate with age and secrete a pro-inflammatory cocktail of factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP) that damages neighbouring tissue and is implicated in many age-related diseases. In 2015, researchers at the Mayo Clinic and Scripps Research identified the first senolytics, the combination of the tyrosine kinase inhibitor dasatinib with the flavonoid quercetin, showing that clearing senescent cells in aged mice improved physical function and extended healthspan.

Candidates and trials

Other candidate senolytics include the BCL-2 family inhibitor navitoclax (ABT-263) and the natural flavonoid fisetin. Early human pilot trials — for example of dasatinib plus quercetin in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and diabetic kidney disease — have reported reductions in senescent-cell burden and some functional improvements, but large randomized trials are still lacking. Agents that suppress the SASP without killing cells are termed senomorphics.
