Barnstorming

Era1920s (heyday)
ActivityStunt flying, air shows, joyrides
Typical aircraftCurtiss JN-4 "Jenny", Standard J-1
Notable barnstormersBessie Coleman, Clyde Pangborn, Charles Lindbergh (early career)

Barnstorming was a form of entertainment in the 1920s in which stunt pilots performed tricks individually or in groups called "flying circuses". The term also came to describe pilots touring the country giving airplane rides and performing stunts, and in other contexts — notably American politics and sport — it later came to mean touring rural areas on a campaign or exhibition.

Origins

After the First World War, the United States government sold surplus military aircraft, especially Curtiss JN-4 "Jenny" trainers, at very low prices. Demobilised pilots bought them cheaply and earned a living travelling between small towns, landing in farmers' fields or pastures — often beside a barn, hence the name — and charging for short rides and aerobatic displays.

The flying circuses

Troupes such as the Gates Flying Circus performed coordinated routines: loop-the-loops, barrel rolls, stall turns, and the most crowd-pleasing feat, wing walking, in which a performer walked along the wings of a plane in flight or transferred between aircraft. Parachute jumps and aerial "weddings" were also popular attractions.

Significance

Barnstormers popularised aviation for a public most of whom had never seen an aeroplane, and helped create demand for commercial air travel and airmail. Many notable aviators, including Charles Lindbergh, began their careers barnstorming. Increasing federal regulation of civil aviation in the late 1920s, and the onset of the Great Depression, brought the barnstorming era largely to an end.
