Khudai Khidmatgar

Khudai Khidmatgar (Pashto: خدايي خدمتگار, literally "Servants of God") was a predominantly Pashtun nonviolent resistance movement in the North-West Frontier Province of British India, led by Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a close associate of Mahatma Gandhi. Because its members wore red-dyed uniforms, the movement was also known as the Red Shirts (Surkh Posh).

History

Founded in November 1929, the Khudai Khidmatgar began as a social reform organisation devoted to education, sanitation and the elimination of blood feuds among Pashtuns. Under Ghaffar Khan—known as Bacha Khan or the "Frontier Gandhi"—it adopted Gandhi's discipline of nonviolence and allied with the Indian National Congress in the struggle for Indian independence. Members took an oath of nonviolence and service, and at its height the movement claimed around 100,000 members.

The movement was repeatedly repressed by the colonial authorities, most infamously in the Qissa Khwani Bazaar massacre of April 1930, when British Indian Army troops fired on unarmed Khudai Khidmatgar protesters in Peshawar, killing hundreds; a platoon of Royal Garhwal Rifles famously refused the order to fire.

Opposition to partition

The Khudai Khidmatgar opposed the partition of India and, when the 1947 referendum confined the Frontier's choice to India or Pakistan, they boycotted the vote and demanded an independent Pashtunistan. After independence, the movement was banned in Pakistan and its leaders imprisoned, and Ghaffar Khan spent many years in jail or exile. Its political legacy continued through the National Awami Party and later the Awami National Party.
