Compacts of Basel

[Image: The Council of Basel, 1430s]

The Compacts of Basel, also known as the Basel Compacts or Compactata, were agreements concluded in 1436 between the Council of Basel and the moderate Hussites (the Utraquists or Calixtines) of Bohemia. They ended the religious conflict of the Hussite Wars by conceding, in limited form, the chief demand of the Hussite reformers.

Background

The Hussite movement, inspired by the teachings of Jan Hus, who was burned at the stake at the Council of Constance in 1415, had resisted five crusades launched against it between 1420 and 1431. The Hussites' programme was summarised in the Four Articles of Prague (1420), demanding free preaching of the word of God, communion in both kinds (bread and wine) for the laity, the exclusion of clergy from secular lordship and property, and the punishment of public mortal sins.

Terms

After negotiations begun at Cheb in 1432, where the Hussites won the remarkable concession that Scripture, rather than the Church, would be the judge of disputed questions, Hussite delegates attended the Council of Basel in 1433. The resulting compacts, ratified at Jihlava in July 1436, granted communion in both kinds to the laity of Bohemia and Moravia who desired it, while interpreting the other three articles in a restrictive sense acceptable to the council.

Aftermath

The settlement divided the Hussites: the radical Taborites rejected it, and were defeated by the Utraquist nobility and their Catholic allies at the Battle of Lipany in 1434, before the formal ratification. The compacts became the legal basis of the Utraquist Church in Bohemia, and in 1436 Sigismund of Luxembourg was finally accepted as King of Bohemia. Pope Pius II repudiated the compacts in 1462, but they remained in force in practice until the aftermath of the Battle of White Mountain in 1620.
