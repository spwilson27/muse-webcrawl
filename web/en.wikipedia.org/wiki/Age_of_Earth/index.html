Age of Earth

The age of Earth is estimated to be 4.54 ± 0.04 billion years (4.54 × 109 years). This estimate is based on evidence from radiometric age-dating of meteorite material, which is consistent with the radiometric ages of the oldest-known terrestrial material — zircon crystals from Western Australia dated to about 4.4 billion years, and calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions in meteorites dated to 4.567 billion years, marking the start of the Solar System's formation.

History of the estimate

Biblical chronologies of the 17th century, such as Archbishop James Ussher's, placed creation around 4004 BC. In the 18th and 19th centuries, naturalists argued for far greater antiquity: Buffon computed ~75,000 years from cooling experiments, and uniformitarian geologists like Charles Lyell demanded immense time. Lord Kelvin calculated 20–100 million years from Earth's cooling, but assumed no internal heat source. The discovery of radioactivity (1896) and radiometric dating followed: Arthur Holmes championed ages of billions of years, and in 1956 Clair Patterson, using lead isotope measurements on the Canyon Diablo meteorite, obtained 4.55 billion years — essentially the modern value.

Radiometric dating

Earth's own oldest rocks have been recycled by plate tectonics, so scientists date meteorites, which formed from the same solar nebula. Lead–lead isochron dating of iron meteorites, assumed to represent the primordial lead of the Solar System, gives the age of the Earth and the Solar System. Lunar samples returned by Apollo missions corroborate ages near 4.5 billion years.
