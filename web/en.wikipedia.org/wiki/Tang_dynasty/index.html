Tang dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Tang dynasty court ladies in a mural painting from a tomb near Xi'an]

The Tang dynasty (618–907) was an imperial dynasty of China widely regarded as a high point of Chinese civilisation — a cosmopolitan golden age of poetry, art and international influence, when the capital Chang'an was the largest city in the world. Founded by the Li family after the collapse of the Sui, the Tang ruled a vast empire whose laws, writing system and Buddhism shaped the cultures of Korea, Japan and Vietnam, while its armies and traders reached deep into Central Asia along the Silk Road.

Golden age

Under Taizong (r. 626–649) and the long reign of Xuanzong (712–756), the Tang perfected the civil-service examination system, codified law, and patronised the arts: Li Bai and Du Fu, China's most celebrated poets, wrote in this era. Chang'an housed Nestorian Christians, Zoroastrians, Manichaeans and Muslims; Japanese embassies studied Tang institutions wholesale. Wu Zetian (r. 690–705), China's only female emperor, ruled first through her sons and then in her own name.

Decline

The An Lushan Rebellion (755–763), launched by a frontier general, devastated the north and permanently weakened central control; the census population fell by tens of millions (partly through lost registration). Regional military governors (jiedushi) became hereditary powers, eunuchs dominated the court, and the Huang Chao rebellion (875–884) shattered what remained. The last Tang emperor was deposed in 907, opening the Five Dynasties period of division.
