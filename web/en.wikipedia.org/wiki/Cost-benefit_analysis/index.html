Cost–benefit analysis

Cost–benefit analysis (CBA), sometimes also called benefit–cost analysis, is a systematic approach to estimating the strengths and weaknesses of alternatives. It is used to determine options which provide the best approach to achieving benefits while preserving savings in, for example, transactions, activities, and functional business requirements. In government policy, CBA is widely used to evaluate proposed projects and regulations by expressing all their effects in monetary terms and discounting future costs and benefits to their present value.

Process

A typical CBA involves: defining the project's goals and the alternatives; identifying all impacts on affected parties; valuing each impact in money terms (using measures such as willingness to pay); discounting future values; and computing indicators such as net present value (NPV), the benefit–cost ratio and the internal rate of return. Sensitivity analysis tests how results change if key assumptions vary.

History and use

The concept was formalised by French engineer Jules Dupuit in the 1840s and developed in US federal water-resource practice under the Flood Control Act of 1936. In the United States, executive orders since 1981 have required CBA of major federal regulations (overseen by OIRA); in the United Kingdom, HM Treasury's Green Book governs appraisal of public spending.

Criticism

Critics argue that CBA can undervalue the environment, health and equity, depends heavily on the discount rate and on contested monetary valuations (such as the value of a statistical life), and may disguise political choices as technical calculations.
