Neutron radiation

Neutron radiation is a form of ionising radiation that consists of free neutrons. Typical phenomena are nuclear fission or nuclear fusion causing the release of free neutrons, which then react with nuclei of other atoms to form new nuclides — which, in turn, may trigger further neutron radiation. Free neutrons are unstable: a lone neutron decays to a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino with a mean lifetime of about 15 minutes.

Neutrons may be emitted from atoms only as a result of a nuclear reaction, such as fission, fusion, or neutron emission from excited nuclei, or from particle accelerator sources such as spallation. Because they carry no electric charge, neutrons interact only weakly with matter via the strong force and can travel long distances before interacting, making neutron radiation highly penetrating.

Interactions with matter

Neutrons interact with atomic nuclei through elastic and inelastic scattering, radiative capture, and (for some nuclei) fission. In hydrogen-rich material, elastic collisions with protons efficiently slow fast neutrons — the basis of neutron moderation and of neutron shielding design. Capture reactions can make materials radioactive (neutron activation), and capture on boron-10 or cadmium-113, which have huge cross-sections, is used in control rods and shielding.

Health effects

Neutron radiation is biologically damaging mainly through the recoil protons and capture gamma rays it produces in tissue. Its relative biological effectiveness is high and energy-dependent, so radiation weighting factors for neutrons range from about 2.5 to 20. Neutrons are a significant component of the radiation environment in nuclear workplaces, at high altitude, and in space, and were a major contributor to the prompt dose from nuclear weapons.

Shielding

Effective shielding first moderates fast neutrons with hydrogenous materials (water, polyethylene, concrete), then absorbs the slowed neutrons with boron or lithium compounds, while attenuating the secondary gamma rays with dense material. This layered approach contrasts with the simple high-density shielding used for gamma rays.

Sources

Sources include nuclear reactors, neutron generators, isotopic sources (californium-252, americium–beryllium), spallation facilities, cosmic-ray interactions in the atmosphere, and nuclear explosions.
