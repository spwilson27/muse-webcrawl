Heiau

A heiau is a Hawaiian temple or shrine. Made of stone or earth, heiau range from simple terraces for small family offerings to enormous platform complexes built by high chiefs. They were dedicated to a variety of purposes: worship of the gods, healing (heiau hōʻola), rain and fertility rites, fishing shrines, and the great war temples (luakini) at which major chiefs sought the favour of the war god Kū and where human sacrifice was performed. Heiau were sacred places governed by strict kapu (taboos), maintained by priests (kahuna), and central to the religious and political life of pre-contact Hawaiʻi.

Architecture

Typical elements include a rectangular stone enclosure or platform, an altar (lele), an oracle tower (ʻanuʻu) covered with bark cloth from which priests communicated with the gods, a pit for refuse of offerings, and carved wooden images (kiʻi) representing deities. The largest surviving example, Puʻukoholā Heiau at Kawaihae on Hawaiʻi Island, was built by Kamehameha I in 1790–1791 following a prophecy that its construction would enable him to unite the islands. Moʻokini Heiau on the Kohala coast is among the oldest, traditionally dated to around the 5th century AD in its first form.

After contact

Following the death of Kamehameha I in 1819, his son Liholiho abolished the kapu system (ʻAi Noa), and most heiau fell into disrepair or were destroyed. Many surviving sites are now protected as state or national historic sites, and some have been restored and reconsecrated for Native Hawaiian religious practice.
