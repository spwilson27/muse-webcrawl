History of the papacy

The history of the papacy is the history of the pope's role both spiritually and temporally, spanning nearly two thousand years from Saint Peter, traditionally regarded as the first bishop of Rome, to the present day. The papacy developed from the leadership of the early Roman church into the central institution of the medieval Latin West, survived schisms, the Reformation and the loss of its temporal states, and was redefined by the First and Second Vatican Councils.

Early Church (to 500)

According to Catholic tradition, the apostle Peter was martyred in Rome under Nero (c. 64–68). The bishops of Rome gradually claimed a primacy of honour among the churches, grounded in Rome's apostolic foundation and imperial status. After Constantine's conversion and the Edict of Milan (313), the church gained legal standing; Pope Leo I (440–461) articulated an influential theology of Petrine primacy and negotiated with Attila the Hun.

Middle Ages

The fall of the Western Empire left the papacy as a stabilising power in Italy; Gregory the Great (590–604) organised Rome's defence and sent missions to England. The alliance with the Franks led to the creation of the Papal States (756) and Charlemagne's coronation by Leo III in 800. The Gregorian Reform of the eleventh century asserted clerical independence and papal supremacy, producing conflict with emperors in the Investiture Controversy. The medieval papacy reached its height under Innocent III (1198–1216), but the Avignon Papacy (1309–1377) and the subsequent Western Schism (1378–1417), with rival claimants in Rome and Avignon, badly damaged its prestige.

Reformation and early modern period

The Renaissance popes patronised artists such as Michelangelo but were criticised for worldliness and corruption. The Protestant Reformation, begun by Martin Luther in 1517, permanently divided Western Christianity. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) launched the Catholic Counter-Reformation. In the following centuries the popes lost political influence in an increasingly secular Europe.

Modern era

Italian unification ended the Papal States in 1870; the same year the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility. The popes remained "prisoners in the Vatican" until the Lateran Treaty of 1929 created Vatican City. John XXIII convened the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), which modernised the Church's liturgy and its relations with other faiths. John Paul II (1978–2005), the first Polish pope, played a notable role in the fall of communism in Eastern Europe. His successors Benedict XVI — who resigned in 2013, the first pope to do so in nearly 600 years — and Francis continued to lead a church of over a billion members.
