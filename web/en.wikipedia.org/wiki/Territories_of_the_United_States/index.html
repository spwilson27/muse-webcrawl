Territories of the United States

The territories of the United States are sub-national administrative divisions overseen by the federal government of the United States. Unlike U.S. states and the District of Columbia, territories are not sovereign entities; Congress possesses "power to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations" for them under the Territory Clause of the Constitution (Article IV, Section 3). Territories are classified by whether they are incorporated (part of the United States proper, where the Constitution applies in full) and whether they are organized (having an organic act establishing a local government passed by Congress).

Inhabited territories

Five territories are permanently inhabited:

  • Puerto Rico — organized, unincorporated; a self-governing commonwealth since 1952; population about 3.2 million.
  • Guam — organized, unincorporated; Organic Act of 1950.
  • U.S. Virgin Islands — organized, unincorporated; purchased from Denmark in 1917.
  • Northern Mariana Islands — organized, unincorporated; a commonwealth in political union with the United States since 1978.
  • American Samoa — unorganized, unincorporated; its people are U.S. nationals rather than citizens at birth, the only inhabited territory with that status.

Residents of the territories elect non-voting delegates (Resident Commissioner in Puerto Rico's case) to the U.S. House of Representatives, may vote in presidential primaries, but cannot vote in presidential general elections and have no voting representation in the Senate.

Minor outlying islands

The United States Minor Outlying Islands comprise uninhabited or transiently staffed possessions including Midway Atoll, Wake Island, Johnston Atoll, Palmyra Atoll (the only incorporated territory, privately owned in part and administered as a National Wildlife Refuge), Kure Atoll, Baker Island, Howland Island, Jarvis Island, Kingman Reef and Navassa Island. Several are subjects of sovereignty disputes or are administered as wildlife refuges.

Legal status and the Insular Cases

The constitutional status of the unincorporated territories derives from the Insular Cases (1901 onward), in which the Supreme Court held that only "fundamental" constitutional rights apply of their own force in unincorporated territories. The doctrine remains controversial; critics, including sitting justices, have described it as resting on outdated and racially inflected premises. Historically, most continental territories (from the Northwest Territory onward) were organized, incorporated territories on a path to statehood; Alaska and Hawaii were the last incorporated territories admitted as states, in 1959. Status debates continue in Puerto Rico, where referendums have variously favoured statehood, and in Guam and the other territories.

Former territories and associated states

The Philippines and the Panama Canal Zone were formerly administered territories. The Federated States of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands and Palau are now sovereign states in free association with the United States under Compacts of Free Association — not territories, though the U.S. retains defence responsibilities and their citizens enjoy certain migration and work privileges.
