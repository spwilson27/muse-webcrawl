Scipio Africanus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Marble bust identified as Scipio Africanus, showing the shaven head of his portraits]

Publius Cornelius Scipio Africanus (236–183 BC) was a Roman general and statesman, one of the greatest commanders of antiquity and the man who defeated Hannibal. Surviving the catastrophe of Cannae (216 BC) as a young officer, he volunteered at twenty-five for the seemingly hopeless command in Spain, took New Carthage by storm (209 BC), destroyed the Carthaginian armies there at Baecula and Ilipa, then invaded Africa and, at Zama in 202 BC, beat Hannibal himself — ending the Second Punic War and earning the agnomen Africanus. Later embittered by political prosecutions of his family, he withdrew to his estate at Liternum, where he died in the same year as Hannibal, by tradition.

Spain and Zama

Scipio revolutionised Roman warfare: he retrained the legions in flexible tactics learned from Hannibal, used the manipular army's articulation at Ilipa (206 BC) in a double envelopment worthy of his master-enemy, and combined arms with diplomacy — winning over Numidian princes, above all Masinissa, whose cavalry proved decisive at Zama. At Zama (19 October 202 BC) he neutralised Hannibal's elephants with lanes in his formation and trumpet noise, survived the infantry struggle, and won when his and Masinissa's cavalry returned to strike Hannibal's rear — the pupil beating the master with his own methods.

Later life and reputation

Scipio served again against Antiochus III (victory at Magnesia, 190 BC, won in his presence though illness kept him from the field), but the 180s brought attacks on his brother Lucius' accounts and on Scipio himself; he retired in disgust. His tomb at Liternum bore, by tradition, the bitter epitaph 'Ungrateful fatherland, you shall not even have my bones.' Livy and Polybius (who knew his adopted grandson) made him Rome's model of the citizen-general; modern historians rank him with Alexander and Hannibal among antiquity's captains.
