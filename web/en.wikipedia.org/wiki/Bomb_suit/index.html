Bomb suit

A bomb suit, or explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) suit, is a heavy suit of body armour designed to protect a bomb disposal technician from the effects of an explosive device: principally fragmentation, blast overpressure, impact, and heat. Bomb suits are used by military EOD teams and civilian bomb squads during manual ("hands-on") render-safe procedures where remote methods cannot be used.

Design

A modern bomb suit consists of layered aramid fibre (such as Kevlar) padding covering the torso, groin, legs, and arms, supplemented by rigid ballistic plates over vital organs. The helmet incorporates a thick transparent polycarbonate visor and may include communications equipment, lighting, and a fan or air-cooling system to offset the suit's considerable heat burden. Many suits include a quick-release system and a protective spine board, because a wearer thrown by a blast may be unable to move in the heavy suit alone.

Protection and limitations

Bomb suits are tested against standardised fragment velocities and blast pressures. They significantly improve survivability against small devices and fragmentation, but they offer limited protection against the overpressure of large explosions at close range, and the arms and hands — which must remain dexterous — are comparatively exposed. For this reason, doctrine emphasises remote operations with robots wherever possible, reserving suited approaches for situations where a manual procedure is unavoidable.

In popular culture

The bomb suit was brought to wide public attention by the 2008 film The Hurt Locker, which depicted an American EOD team in Iraq and drew both praise for its portrayal of the profession and criticism from some technicians over its depiction of procedures.
