Latent image

A latent image is an invisible image produced in a photosensitive material, such as photographic film or paper, by exposure to light. The exposed material must be chemically developed to make the image visible, a process that amplifies the latent image many millions of times.

Mechanism

Photographic emulsions contain crystals of silver halide. When a photon is absorbed, it frees an electron that combines with a silver ion to form a tiny speck of metallic silver — only a few atoms — at a sensitivity site on the crystal. These specks form the latent image. During development, crystals bearing a latent-image speck are reduced entirely to metallic silver, while unexposed crystals remain largely unaffected and are later removed by the fixer.

Stability and fading

Latent images are not perfectly stable: over long periods, especially in heat and humidity, the silver specks can regress, causing latent image fading in film stored for a long time between exposure and development. Conversely, very low light levels can cause reciprocity failure because sub-latent specks decay before enough accumulate. The Gurney–Mott theory, proposed in 1938, remains the standard explanation of latent image formation.
