Protein domain

A protein domain is a conserved part of a given protein sequence and tertiary structure that can evolve, function, and exist independently of the rest of the protein chain. Domains vary in length from about 50 amino acids to 250 amino acids in length. Each domain forms a compact folded three-dimensional structure that is often independently stable and which usually carries out a specific molecular function, such as binding a ligand or catalysing a reaction.

Background

The concept of the domain was proposed in the 1970s as protein crystal structures revealed that many proteins consist of spatially distinct globular units connected by flexible linkers. Donald Wetlaufer defined domains as "stable units of protein structure that could fold autonomously". Because a domain can fold on its own, domains can be swapped between proteins by genetic engineering to make chimeric proteins, and in nature gene duplication, shuffling and recombination of domain-encoding segments have been a major engine of protein evolution.

Domains and protein architecture

Most eukaryotic proteins are multidomain proteins, assembled from combinations of a limited repertoire of domain families. Well-known examples of widespread domains include the SH2 and SH3 domains of signalling proteins, immunoglobulin domains of antibodies, zinc finger DNA-binding domains, and the kinase domain found in hundreds of protein kinases. A single domain family may appear in proteins of very different overall function — the same structural unit being reused in many contexts.

Identification and classification

Domains are identified by sequence similarity searches against databases such as Pfam, InterPro, SMART and PROSITE, and structurally classified in resources such as SCOP and CATH. Sequence motifs and hidden Markov models built from multiple alignments allow domains to be detected even in distantly related proteins. Understanding a protein's domain architecture is often the first step in predicting its function.

Domain boundaries

Deciding where one domain ends and another begins can be difficult: different methods — structural compactness, sequence conservation, or automated prediction algorithms — may assign slightly different boundaries. Linker regions between domains are often rich in charged or polar residues and are intrinsically flexible.
