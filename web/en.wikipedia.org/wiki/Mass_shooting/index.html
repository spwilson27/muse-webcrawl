Mass shooting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mass shooting is an incident in which multiple people are shot with a firearm. There is no single universally accepted definition: agencies and researchers differ on the number of victims required (three or four is common), whether the count includes the perpetrator, and whether the shooting must be indiscriminate or in a public place. The FBI defines a "mass killing" as an incident in which four or more people are killed; the Gun Violence Archive counts any incident in which four or more people are shot, not necessarily killed.

Definitions and counting

Counts vary dramatically depending on the definition used. Narrow definitions focused on public, indiscriminate rampages yield a few dozen incidents in the United States per decade, while broad counts that include gang-related and domestic shootings yield several hundred incidents per year. Related terms include "mass murder", "spree killing" and "active shooter", the last referring to an individual attempting to kill people in a confined, populated area.

Perpetrators and motives

Most perpetrators of public mass shootings are male and act alone. Motives documented in research include notoriety-seeking, grievance and revenge, extremist ideology, and severe personal crisis; many attackers show leakage of intent, plan extensively, and die at the scene by suicide or police fire. Criminologists debate the role of media contagion, in which heavy coverage of one attack appears to raise the probability of imitative attacks in the following days.

By country

The United States experiences the great majority of high-casualty public mass shootings among developed countries, an asymmetry usually attributed to the number and accessibility of firearms, though cultural and media factors are also debated. Mass shootings elsewhere — such as those in Norway (2011), New Zealand (2019, Christchurch) and various European countries — have often prompted legislative changes restricting firearms, as in Australia after the 1996 Port Arthur massacre.

Policy debate

Mass shootings drive recurring public debate about gun control, mental-health services, school security and media practices. In the United States the debate is sharply polarised and major federal legislation has rarely followed even very deadly attacks, while several states have enacted measures such as universal background checks, red flag laws and restrictions on high-capacity magazines or semi-automatic rifles.
