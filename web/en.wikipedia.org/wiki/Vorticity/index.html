Vorticity

In continuum mechanics, vorticity is a pseudovector field that describes the local spinning motion of a continuum near some point, as would be seen by an observer located at that point and travelling along with the flow. Mathematically, the vorticity vector ω is defined as the curl of the flow velocity field v: ω = ∇ × v.

Interpretation

Roughly speaking, vorticity is twice the local angular velocity of a small fluid parcel (solid-body-like rotation), but it also includes the contribution of shear: a flow can have nonzero vorticity even when the streamlines are straight, as in viscous flow past a wall. Regions where vorticity is concentrated are called vortices — examples range from tornadoes and hurricanes to wingtip vortices trailing aircraft and the vortex rings formed by dolphins and volcanoes.

Vorticity equation

The evolution of vorticity is governed by the vorticity equation, obtained by taking the curl of the Navier–Stokes equations. Its terms describe vortex stretching and tilting (which amplify vorticity in three dimensions), baroclinic generation at interfaces between fluids of different density, and diffusion of vorticity by viscosity. In an ideal, barotropic fluid, circulation and vorticity are conserved following the flow (Kelvin's theorem and Helmholtz's theorems), so vortex lines move with the fluid.

Applications

Vorticity is a central variable in meteorology (where relative and potential vorticity are used to analyse storms and large-scale circulation), aerodynamics, oceanography, and computational fluid dynamics — vorticity formulations solve for ω rather than velocity in some methods. In two-dimensional flow, vorticity is a scalar (the component perpendicular to the plane).
