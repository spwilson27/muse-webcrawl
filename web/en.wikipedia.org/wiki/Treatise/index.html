Treatise

A treatise is a formal and systematic written discourse on some subject, generally longer and treating it in greater depth than an essay, and more concerned with investigating or exposing the principles of the subject and its conclusions. A treatise is often book-length and addressed to specialists or advanced readers.

Characteristics

Treatises typically proceed by definition, division and demonstration, marshalling authorities and arguments in a sustained and orderly fashion. The form was a staple of medieval scholasticism — as in the theological treatises of Thomas Aquinas — and of early modern science and philosophy: Newton's Principia is cast as a mathematical treatise, and works such as Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689) used the form for political philosophy. In law, a treatise is an authoritative commentary synthesising a field of law, cited by practitioners and courts.

Related forms

The treatise differs from the monograph, which is a detailed study of a single specialised subject, and from the dissertation, which is written to satisfy the requirements of an academic degree. A short treatise may be called a tract — a usage preserved in religious "tracts" — while the German Abhandlung similarly denotes a scholarly treatment of a topic.
