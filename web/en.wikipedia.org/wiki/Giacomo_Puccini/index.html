Giacomo Puccini

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Giacomo Puccini in 1908, photographed in a fur-collared coat and soft hat, the world's most successful opera composer of his day]

Giacomo Antonio Domenico Michele Secondo Maria Puccini (22 December 1858 – 29 November 1924) was an Italian composer of opera, the last great master of the Italian tradition from Rossini to Verdi — and its richest: his operas are among the most performed in the world. A theatrical instinct of genius, an orchestra of Wagnerian sophistication, and melodies that pass immediately into the blood made La bohème, Tosca and Madama Butterfly the core of the modern repertoire.

His works are Le Villi (1884), Edgar (1889), Manon Lescaut (1893), La bohème (1896), Tosca (1900), Madama Butterfly (1904), La fanciulla del West (1910), La rondine (1917), Il trittico (1918, containing Gianni Schicchi) and the unfinished Turandot (premiered 1926).

Breakthrough and the big three

Born in Lucca into a dynasty of church musicians, Puccini walked to Pisa to hear Aida and resolved on opera. The publisher Ricordi backed him through failure to Manon Lescaut's triumph at Turin (1893). La bohème (Turin, 1896, under Toscanini) weathered cool reviews to conquer the world; Tosca's melodrama and Butterfly — booed at its La Scala premiere in 1904, triumphantly revised within months — completed the triptych of popular immortality. His private life (the long union with Elvira Bonturi, the scandal of his maid Doria Manfredi's suicide in 1909) was as operatic as his plots.

Turandot and legacy

Puccini died of throat cancer in Brussels in 1924 leaving Turandot's final duet in sketches; Franco Alfano completed it, and Toscanini, conducting the 1926 premiere, famously stopped where the master's notes ended (the story is opera legend, repeated as such). Puccini's mixture of verismo grit, exotic colour and unstoppable lyricism made him the last opera composer to command a truly global popular audience.
