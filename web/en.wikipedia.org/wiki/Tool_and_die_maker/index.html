Tool and die makers are highly skilled craftspeople of the machine shop trade who design, build, and repair the tooling used in mass production: stamping dies, moulds, jigs, fixtures, and cutting tools. They are considered among the most highly trained of machinists, because production tooling must be made to extremely tight tolerances — often within a few micrometres (ten-thousandths of an inch) — so that the parts it produces are consistent over production runs of thousands or millions of pieces.

Work

Working from engineering drawings and increasingly from CAD models, the tool and die maker selects stock, lays out the work, and machines components on lathes, milling machines, grinders, and electrical discharge machines (EDM). Dies for sheet-metal stamping must be fitted so that punch and die clearances shear the metal cleanly; moulds for plastics injection moulding must be polished, vented, and fitted with ejector mechanisms. The maker assembles the tool, tests it in a press or moulding machine, inspects sample parts, and corrects the tooling until the output meets specification.

Training

Training traditionally takes the form of a four- or five-year apprenticeship combining shop-floor experience with classroom study of mathematics, metallurgy, heat treatment, blueprint reading, and computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM). Modern toolrooms rely heavily on CNC machining centres and wire EDM, so contemporary tool and die makers combine traditional fitting skills with CNC programming. The trade supports industries including automotive, aerospace, appliance, and consumer-electronics manufacturing.
