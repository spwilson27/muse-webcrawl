Zenith

The zenith is the point in the sky directly above an observer, on the imaginary celestial sphere. It is the direction pointing directly "up" from the observer's location, opposite to the direction of gravity, and perpendicular to the local horizon plane. The point directly below the observer is the nadir.

Definition and use

The zenith is defined by the extension of a vertical line from the observer through the Earth's centre (approximately) to the celestial sphere. Astronomers use the zenith distance (or zenith angle) of a celestial object — the angular distance between the object and the zenith — as a fundamental coordinate in the horizontal coordinate system; it is the complement of the object's altitude above the horizon. The minimum possible zenith distance of the Sun determines whether an observer lies within the tropics: only between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn can the Sun pass exactly through the zenith.

Etymology

The word derives from medieval Latin cenit or senit, a scribal corruption of the Arabic samt ("path, direction"), from the phrase samt ar-ra's, "the direction of the head" (i.e., overhead). It entered English in the 14th century, and later acquired figurative meanings of a peak or highest point of achievement.

Related concepts

The zenith is one of the key reference points of observational astronomy, used in telescope pointing, geodesy, and satellite tracking; instruments such as zenith telescopes exploit stars passing near the zenith to measure the Earth's rotation and variations in latitude.
