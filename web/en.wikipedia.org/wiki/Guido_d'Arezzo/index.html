Guido d'Arezzo (Italian: [ˈɡwiːdo daˈrettso]; c. 991 – after 1033) was an Italian Benedictine monk and music theorist of the Medieval era. He is regarded as the inventor — or at least the great systematiser — of modern musical notation, whose staff notation replaced the unwieldy neumatic notation of his day and revolutionised the teaching and transmission of chant.

Life and work

Guido was educated at the abbey of Pomposa and later worked at the cathedral of Arezzo under Bishop Thebald. His treatise Micrologus (c. 1026) became the most widely read music treatise of the Middle Ages after Boethius. His Prologus in antiphonarium describes his improved notation: lines and spaces of a four-line staff (with coloured lines for F and C) allowing singers to read melodies at sight rather than learning them by rote over years.

Solmisation and the Guidonian hand

Guido is credited with the system of solmisation syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la, taken from the initial syllables of successive lines of the hymn Ut queant laxis to St John the Baptist, each line beginning a scale-step higher. (The syllable si/ti and the replacement of ut by do came later.) The Guidonian hand, a mnemonic diagram mapping notes onto the joints of the hand attributed to him or his followers, was for centuries the standard tool for teaching singers to locate pitches.

Legacy

Guido's notation underlies all subsequent Western staff notation, and his sight-singing method dramatically shortened the training of choir singers. The modern music file format GUIDO Music Notation is named in his honour, as is a crater on Mercury.
