Baltic languages

The Baltic languages are a branch of the Indo-European language family spoken natively by Baltic peoples, mainly in areas east of the Baltic Sea in Northern Europe. The only surviving languages of the branch are Lithuanian and Latvian, noted among Indo-European languages for retaining many archaic features believed to have been present in early Proto-Indo-European.

Classification

The branch divides into Western Baltic — including Old Prussian, extinct since the early 18th century, as well as Curonian, Semigallian and Selonian — and Eastern Baltic, comprising Lithuanian, Latvian and the extinct or moribund Latgalian and Samogitian varieties treated as dialects by some classifications. The relationship between Baltic and Slavic languages is debated; the two are often grouped as Balto-Slavic.

History

Baltic languages were once spoken over a much wider area extending toward Moscow and the upper Dnieper. Written records begin late: the first Lithuanian printed book dates to 1547, and Latvian texts to the 16th century, while Old Prussian is known mainly from 14th–16th century word lists and catechisms.

Features

Lithuanian in particular preserves a rich system of nominal declension with seven cases, mobile pitch accent, and conservative phonology, making the Baltic languages valuable for Indo-European reconstruction. Latvian has undergone greater phonological innovation, including fixed initial stress under Finnic influence.
