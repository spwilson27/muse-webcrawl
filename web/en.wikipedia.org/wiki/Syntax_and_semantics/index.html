Syntax and semantics

Syntax and semantics are two of the principal branches of linguistics. Syntax concerns the rules governing the structure of sentences — how words combine into phrases and clauses — while semantics concerns meaning: how words, phrases, and sentences convey information about the world or about possible states of affairs.

The distinction

A sentence can be syntactically well-formed yet semantically anomalous, as in Chomsky's "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously", and a meaningful message can be conveyed by syntactically deviant strings. Theories of grammar differ in how tightly they bind the two components. In generative grammar, syntactic structure is often taken to feed semantic interpretation; in formal semantics, model-theoretic methods assign truth conditions to expressions compositionally, following the principle that the meaning of a whole is a function of the meanings of its parts and their mode of combination.

Interfaces

Research on the syntax–semantics interface examines argument structure, scope, quantification, tense, and information structure. The division also appears in computer science, where a programming language has a syntax (its grammar) and semantics (the meaning of programs), studied through operational, denotational, and axiomatic semantics.
