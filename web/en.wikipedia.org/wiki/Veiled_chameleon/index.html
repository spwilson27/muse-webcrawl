The veiled chameleon (Chamaeleo calyptratus) is a species of chameleon native to the Arabian Peninsula in Yemen and Saudi Arabia. Other common names include the cone-head chameleon and the Yemen chameleon. It is one of the most commonly kept chameleon species in captivity. Introduced populations have become established in Hawaii and Florida, where it is considered an invasive species.

Description

The male veiled chameleon is predominantly green with yellow, blue and brown mottling, and grows to about 43–61 cm (17–24 in) in total length. Females are smaller, reaching about 35 cm (14 in), and are green with white, orange, yellow or tan mottling. Both sexes possess a prominent helmet-like casque on the head — taller in males — from which the names "veiled" and "cone-head" derive. Males also have tarsal spurs on the hind feet. Like other chameleons, it changes colour with mood, health, temperature and reproductive state; gravid females turn dark green with vivid yellow and blue spots.

Habitat and behaviour

In the wild the species lives in acacia plateaus, mountains and valleys, sheltering in trees and bushes. It feeds mainly on insects, caught with its long, sticky tongue, but also eats plant material, unusual among chameleons. Females lay large clutches of 20–70 eggs several times a year and can retain sperm between clutches.

In captivity

Because it is hardier and more prolific than most chameleons, the veiled chameleon is widely bred for the pet trade. It nevertheless requires tall, well-ventilated enclosures, high humidity with regular misting, UVB lighting and calcium supplementation; males are territorial and must be housed alone.
