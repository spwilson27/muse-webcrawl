The Akaike information criterion (AIC) is an estimator of prediction error and thereby a measure of the relative quality of statistical models for a given set of data. It was formulated by the Japanese statistician Hirotugu Akaike and first presented in 1973. Given a collection of candidate models fitted to the same data, AIC estimates the quality of each model relative to the others; the model with the smallest AIC is preferred. AIC thus provides a means of model selection that balances goodness of fit against model complexity.

Definition

Let k be the number of estimated parameters in the model and let L̂ be the maximum value of the likelihood function for the model. Then the AIC is

AIC = 2k − 2 ln(L̂)

The term 2k penalises models with more parameters, discouraging overfitting, while the likelihood term rewards fit. When the sample size n is small relative to k, the corrected criterion AICc adds a correction term and is recommended, for example when n/k is less than about 40.

Foundations

Akaike showed that AIC is, asymptotically, an approximately unbiased estimator of the expected relative Kullback–Leibler divergence between the fitted model and the unknown process that generated the data — in effect, an estimate of the information lost when the model is used to approximate reality. Differences in AIC between models (ΔAIC) can be converted into "Akaike weights", which may be interpreted as the probability that a given model is the best among the candidate set in the information-theoretic sense.

Comparison with other criteria

AIC differs conceptually from the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), which penalises parameters more strongly (by ln(n) per parameter) and assumes a "true" model is among the candidates. AIC aims at predictive accuracy rather than identification of a true model, and it does not require nested models or Bayesian priors. It is widely used in time-series analysis, ecology (through the Burnham and Anderson programme of information-theoretic inference), phylogenetics and machine learning.
