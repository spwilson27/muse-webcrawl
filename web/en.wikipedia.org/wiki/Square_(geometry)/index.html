In Euclidean geometry, a square is a regular quadrilateral, which means that it has four equal sides and four equal angles (90-degree angles, π/2 radian angles, or right angles). It can also be defined as a rectangle with two equal-length adjacent sides, or as a rhombus with one right angle. It is the two-dimensional analogue of the cube and more generally a special case of the hypercube.

Properties

A square with side length s has:

  • Perimeter: 4s
  • Area: s² — historically the source of the term "squaring" for raising a number to the second power
  • Diagonal length: s√2
  • Circumradius: s/√2
  • Inradius: s/2

The diagonals of a square are equal in length, bisect each other at right angles, and bisect the vertex angles. A square has four lines of reflectional symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 4 (through 90°, 180°, and 270°).

Characterisations

A convex quadrilateral is a square if and only if it is any one of the following: a rectangle with two adjacent equal sides; a rhombus with a right angle; a rhombus with all angles equal; a quadrilateral with four equal sides and four right angles; or a quadrilateral whose diagonals are equal, perpendicular bisectors of each other.

Other facts

The square is one of only three regular polygons that tile the plane by themselves (with the equilateral triangle and the regular hexagon). In non-Euclidean geometry, squares are more generally quadrilaterals with four equal sides and four equal angles; in spherical geometry a square's angles exceed 90°, and in hyperbolic geometry they are less than 90°.
