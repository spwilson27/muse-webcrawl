Parallax scrolling is a technique in computer graphics where background images move past the camera more slowly than foreground images, creating an illusion of depth in a two-dimensional scene and adding a sense of immersion. The technique grew out of the multiplane camera used in traditional animation since the 1930s, and became a hallmark of 2D video games in the 1980s and 1990s.

Principle

Parallax is the apparent shift of an object's position when viewed from different angles: nearby objects move faster across the visual field than distant ones. Parallax scrolling mimics this by dividing a scene into layers — distant sky and mountains, middle-distance hills and trees, near ground and obstacles — and scrolling each at a rate proportional to its supposed distance. The greater the differential, the stronger the depth effect.

In video games

The arcade game Moon Patrol (1982) is widely credited with popularising the technique, scrolling three layers of background as the player's moon buggy advanced. Home systems initially lacked hardware support, but consoles such as the Nintendo Entertainment System (with scrolling tricks like split-screen raster effects) and especially the Sega Genesis/Mega Drive and Super NES — whose graphics chips supported multiple independently scrolling playfields — made multilayer parallax a defining look of the 16-bit era, showcased in titles such as Sonic the Hedgehog and Shadow of the Beast. Modern 2D games still use layered scrolling for depth even when rendered with 3D hardware.

Methods

Implementations include the layer method (several full image layers composited in order), the sprite method (individual background elements moved as sprites), raster methods that change scroll offsets partway down the screen, and pattern/bitmap repetition of scrolling fields. Depth can also be faked by scaling layers as they move.

Web design

In the 2010s, "parallax" became a popular web-design trend in which background sections move at a different speed from foreground content as the user scrolls a page, used for product storytelling pages; the effect is now often reduced for accessibility and mobile performance reasons.
