The Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies began after the Netherlands East Indies campaign of early 1942 in the Pacific War and ended with Japan's surrender in August 1945. It shattered Dutch colonial authority and, despite its brutality, decisively accelerated the rise of Indonesian nationalism.

Invasion

Seeking the oil of Sumatra and Borneo, Japanese forces invaded in January 1942. The Allied ABDA command was destroyed in the Battle of the Java Sea (27 February 1942), and Dutch commander Hein ter Poorten surrendered unconditionally at Kalijati on 8 March 1942. Some 170,000 Dutch and other European civilians and soldiers were interned in camps where many perished.

Occupation rule

The archipelago was divided between the Imperial Army (Java and Sumatra) and Navy (the eastern islands), ruling for the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. The occupation exploited Indonesian resources and manpower: hundreds of thousands of romusha forced labourers were worked to death on projects such as the Burma and Pakanbaru railways, while rice requisitioning caused famine, particularly in Java. At the same time the Japanese banned Dutch, promoted Indonesian as a national language, and from 1943 increasingly cultivated nationalist leaders including Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta, organizing Indonesian military auxiliaries (PETA).

End and legacy

Two days after Japan's surrender announcement, Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed Indonesian independence on 17 August 1945, launching the Indonesian National Revolution against returning Dutch forces. The occupation thus marks the effective end of three centuries of Dutch rule.
