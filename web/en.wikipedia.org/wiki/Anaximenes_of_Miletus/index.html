Anaximenes of Miletus (c. 585 – c. 528 BC) was an Ancient Greek philosopher, the third and last of the Milesian school, and a younger associate of Anaximander. He held that the fundamental substance of the universe is air (aēr), and — crucially — proposed a definite mechanism of change: air becomes fire when rarefied, and wind, cloud, water, earth, and finally stone when progressively condensed. This made him the first thinker to explain qualitative change by a quantitative process of densification and thinning.

Philosophy

Just as breath (pneuma) holds the human being together, Anaximenes argued, air encompasses and governs the cosmos: "Just as our soul, being air, holds us together, so do breath and air encompass the whole world." He made the Sun, Moon, and stars fiery bodies carried on the air like leaves; the Earth itself is a flat disc riding on a cushion of air. He compared the sensation of breath through pursed lips (cold) versus an open mouth (warm) as everyday evidence for the effects of compression and rarefaction.

Astronomy and meteorology

Anaximenes held that the stars are fixed like nails in a crystalline vault, and explained rainbows, hail, and earthquakes naturalistically — earthquakes resulting from cracks forming as the earth dries. Though his astronomy was in some respects less bold than Anaximander's, his clear, simple theory, written in plain Ionic prose, was frequently cited by later Greek thinkers.

Legacy

Anaximenes influenced Diogenes of Apollonia, who revived air as the primary substance, and his rarefaction–condensation mechanism shaped later theories of matter. The lunar crater Anaximenes is named in his honour.
