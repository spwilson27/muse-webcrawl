Separation of church and state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The separation of church and state is a philosophical and juridical concept denoting the political distance between religious organisations and the state. In its strict forms (French laïcité, from the 1905 law), the state excludes religion from public institutions; in accommodationist forms, the state maintains neutrality among religions and between religion and non-religion while permitting public religious expression. The concept is distinct from, though related to, freedom of religion and secularism.

History and models

The phrase derives from Thomas Jefferson's 1802 letter describing the US First Amendment as building "a wall of separation between Church & State"; Roger Williams had argued the principle earlier, and Enlightenment thinkers — Locke's Letter Concerning Toleration above all — supplied theory. Models vary: France's assertive secularism (including restrictions on religious symbols in public schools), the US's non-establishment plus free-exercise pairing (permanently litigated over funding, prayer and exemptions), Germany's cooperative model (church taxes, religious education), England's established church with broad toleration, and India's "principled distance" among religions. Secularism's relationship to democracy and minorities' rights is debated in comparative politics, as are assertive secularist regimes (Kemalist Turkey) and religion-state fusions at the theocratic end of the spectrum.
