Modern monetary theory

MMT argues that currency-issuing governments are constrained by real resources, not by revenue

Modern monetary theory (MMT) is a heterodox macroeconomic framework that describes the operation of modern fiat-money systems. Its central claim is that a government that issues its own currency and borrows only in that currency cannot be forced to default and does not need tax revenue or bond sales in order to spend; the real limit on spending is the availability of real resources, whose excess demand shows up as inflation rather than insolvency.

Main propositions

MMT holds that government spending creates new money which taxation later removes from circulation; that taxes function to create demand for the currency and to control inflation rather than to "fund" spending; that unemployment represents evidence that government deficits are too small relative to private saving desires; and that a job guarantee — a public offer of work at a fixed wage to anyone who wants it — can serve as an automatic stabiliser and price anchor. Its leading academic proponents include Warren Mosler (who coined the term and the framework's popular form), L. Randall Wray, Stephanie Kelton, Bill Mitchell and Mathew Forstater, building on chartalism, Abba Lerner's "functional finance," and the work of Hyman Minsky and Wynne Godley.

Criticism

Mainstream economists criticise MMT for understating inflation risk, the exchange-rate consequences of deficits for open economies, and the political difficulty of raising taxes to cool demand. Supporters reply that the framework is primarily descriptive — an account of how monetary operations actually work — and that its policy claims follow from that description.
