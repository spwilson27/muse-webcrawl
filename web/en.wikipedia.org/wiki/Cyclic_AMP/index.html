Cyclic adenosine monophosphate

Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP, cyclic AMP, or 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate) is a second messenger important in many biological processes. It is a derivative of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and is used for intracellular signal transduction in many organisms, conveying the effects of hormones such as glucagon and adrenaline that cannot cross the cell membrane. cAMP was the first second messenger to be identified, through the work of Earl Sutherland, who received the Nobel Prize in 1971 for its discovery.

Synthesis and breakdown

cAMP is produced from ATP by the enzyme adenylyl cyclase, located at the cell membrane, in response to activation of G protein-coupled receptors: the stimulatory G protein (Gs) activates the cyclase, whereas the inhibitory G protein (Gi) suppresses it. Signal termination is achieved by phosphodiesterase enzymes, which hydrolyse cAMP to ordinary 5′-AMP. Compounds such as caffeine and theophylline raise cAMP levels weakly by inhibiting phosphodiesterases, and several drugs, including the asthma drug class of PDE inhibitors, exploit this pathway.

Functions

In eukaryotes, cAMP principally activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates target proteins and thereby regulates glycogen, sugar and lipid metabolism. In the classic example, adrenaline binding to liver cells raises cAMP, activates PKA and phosphorylase kinase, and triggers glycogen breakdown. cAMP also activates the transcription factor CREB, ion channels such as the HCN channels underlying pacemaker activity in the heart, and exchange proteins (Epac). In bacteria, cAMP binds the catabolite activator protein to regulate genes such as the lac operon; in the slime mould Dictyostelium it acts as an extracellular aggregation signal. Cholera toxin and pertussis toxin exert their effects by locking G proteins into states that keep adenylyl cyclase on or prevent its inhibition, respectively.
