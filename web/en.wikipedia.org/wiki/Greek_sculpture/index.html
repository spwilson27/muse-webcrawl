Greek sculpture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Greek sculpture is the sculpture of the ancient Greeks, produced from roughly 900 BC to the 1st century AD, with its classical development between about 650 and 27 BC. It is traditionally divided into the Archaic, Classical, and Hellenistic periods, and is regarded as foundational to the Western tradition of sculpture.

Archaic period

Early monumental stone sculpture, from about 650 BC, produced standing figures known as kouroi (male youths) and korai (maidens), frontal and stiffly posed in the Egyptian manner but with the characteristic "Archaic smile".

Classical period

In the 5th century BC sculptors such as Myron, Polykleitos, and Phidias perfected the free-standing bronze figure in naturalistic contrapposto, codified ideal proportions (Polykleitos's Canon), and executed the sculptural programme of the Parthenon. The 4th century brought the softer modelling of Praxiteles and the expressive work of Skopas and Lysippos.

Hellenistic period

After Alexander the Great, sculpture spread across the eastern Mediterranean, becoming more dynamic, emotional, and diverse in subject: the Laocoön Group, the Winged Victory of Samothrace, and the Venus de Milo date from this era. Many Greek bronzes survive only through Roman marble copies.

Materials and colour

Sculptures were carved in marble and cast in bronze, and were originally brightly painted (polychromy), a fact established by traces of pigment on surviving works.
