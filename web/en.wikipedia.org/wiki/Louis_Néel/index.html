Louis Eugène Félix Néel (22 November 1904 – 17 November 2000) was a French physicist who pioneered the study of the magnetic properties of solids. He shared the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics with Swedish astrophysicist Hannes Alfvén, receiving his half "for fundamental work and discoveries concerning antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism which have led to important applications in solid state physics."

Career

Néel studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and completed his doctorate at the University of Strasbourg under Pierre Weiss. In the 1930s he developed the theory of antiferromagnetism, in which neighbouring atomic magnetic moments align in opposite directions, cancelling the net magnetisation below a critical temperature — now called the Néel temperature. He later explained the behaviour of ferrites through the theory of ferrimagnetism, in which opposing sublattices have unequal moments.

During the Second World War he worked on naval defence, devising methods to protect French ships against magnetic mines. After the war he founded a major magnetism research centre at Grenoble, which became the CNRS Grenoble Magnetism Laboratory, and helped establish Grenoble as a centre for European physics, including the Institut Laue–Langevin.

Legacy

Néel's concepts underlie modern magnetic materials, computer memory and spintronics. The Néel wall (a type of domain wall), the Néel effect and other phenomena bear his name. He was also an early contributor to the theory of paleomagnetism and rock magnetism.
