The alpha process, also known as the alpha ladder, is one of the two classes of nuclear fusion reactions by which stars convert helium into heavier elements, the other being the triple-alpha process. It consists of a sequence of reactions in which a nucleus captures an alpha particle (a helium-4 nucleus), producing a new nucleus with four more nucleons and a charge two units higher. Purely alpha-particle nuclei are built up step by step.

The alpha ladder

The ladder begins with carbon-12, itself produced by the triple-alpha process. Successive captures give:

12C + α → 16O; 16O + α → 20Ne; 20Ne + α → 24Mg; 24Mg + α → 28Si; 28Si + α → 32S; 32S + α → 36Ar; 36Ar + α → 40Ca.

Each successive step requires higher temperatures, because the Coulomb barrier grows with the charge of the target nucleus. In practice the simple ladder is supplemented — and in massive stars largely superseded — by reactions on other isotopes, and the final stages of burning rearrange the products through photodisintegration and further captures in quasi-equilibrium.

Alpha elements

The elements produced predominantly by alpha captures — oxygen, neon, magnesium, silicon, sulfur, argon, calcium and titanium — are called the alpha elements. They are ejected mainly by core-collapse (Type II) supernovae, and their abundance relative to iron is a diagnostic of a star or galaxy's history: old, rapidly formed stellar populations are "alpha-enhanced", whereas iron-rich Type Ia supernovae, which explode later, contribute iron with little of the alpha elements.
