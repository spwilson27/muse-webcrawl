Brent–Salamin algorithm

The Brent–Salamin algorithm, also known as the Gauss–Legendre algorithm, is an iterative algorithm for computing π to very high precision. It was independently discovered by Richard Brent and Eugene Salamin in 1975–1976, building on much earlier work by Carl Friedrich Gauss and Adrien-Marie Legendre on the arithmetic–geometric mean.

Description

The algorithm initializes a₀ = 1, b₀ = 1/√2, t₀ = 1/4, p₀ = 1, then iterates: an+1 is the arithmetic mean of an and bn; bn+1 is their geometric mean; t is updated by subtracting p(an − an+1)²; and p doubles each step. The estimate (an+1 + bn+1)² / (4tn+1) converges to π extremely rapidly: the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each iteration, so only about 25 iterations are needed for tens of millions of digits (given arithmetic of sufficient precision).

History and use

Salamin's 1976 paper and Brent's independent work showed how the classical arithmetic–geometric mean iteration could yield π efficiently. Coupled with fast multiplication algorithms such as FFT-based methods, the Brent–Salamin algorithm underpinned record computations of π in the 1980s and 1990s, including those by Yasumasa Kanada. It has since been partly superseded for extreme records by the Chudnovsky algorithm, but remains a standard benchmark and pedagogical example of quadratic convergence.
