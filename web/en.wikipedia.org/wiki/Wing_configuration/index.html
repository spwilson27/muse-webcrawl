The wing configuration of a fixed-wing aircraft is its arrangement of lifting and related surfaces. Aircraft designs may be categorised by the number and position of wings: a monoplane has one pair of wings, a biplane two stacked pairs, and a triplane three. Since the 1930s most aircraft have been monoplanes, with the wing mounted low, mid, or high on the fuselage. Designers balance lift, drag, structural weight, stability, ground clearance, and field of view when choosing a configuration.

Number of wings

Early aircraft used multiplane wings because thin airfoils of the day needed external bracing to be strong enough; the stacked wings of a biplane form a rigid truss. As cantilever structures and thicker airfoils developed, the monoplane's lower drag made it dominant. A few biplanes remain in production for aerobatics and agricultural work, where compact span and agility matter.

Wing position and geometry

  • Low-wing: mounted at the bottom of the fuselage, as on most airliners; offers easy ground handling and landing-gear stowage.
  • Mid-wing: mounted at mid-height, common on fighters and aerobatic aircraft.
  • High-wing: mounted above the fuselage, as on the C-130 and many bush planes; gives good downward visibility and ground clearance.
  • Sweep and dihedral: wings may be swept back to delay shock waves at high speed, and angled upward (dihedral) or downward (anhedral) to adjust lateral stability.

Support

Wings may be cantilevered — supported entirely by internal structure, as on virtually all modern aircraft — or braced by external struts and wires, as on many light aircraft such as the Cessna 172. Strut bracing reduces structural weight but adds drag.
