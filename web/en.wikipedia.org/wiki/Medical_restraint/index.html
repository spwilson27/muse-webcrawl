Medical restraint

Medical restraints are physical devices or chemicals used to limit the movement of a patient in a medical setting, typically to prevent harm to the patient or to others, or to prevent interference with medical treatment. Physical restraints include limb holders, mitts, belts, vests, and bed rails; chemical restraints are medications — commonly antipsychotics or benzodiazepines — given primarily to control behavior rather than to treat an underlying condition.

Use and regulation

Restraints are used in emergency departments, intensive care units, psychiatry, and long-term care, for example with agitated or delirious patients or those at risk of removing life-sustaining tubes. Their use is regulated in many jurisdictions: in the United States, Medicare and Medicaid rules and Joint Commission standards require that restraint be a last resort, applied with the least restrictive means, ordered by a clinician, time-limited, and monitored. Many hospitals have adopted restraint-reduction programs emphasizing de-escalation, sitters, and early mobilization.

Risks and criticism

Physical restraints carry risks including pressure injuries, deconditioning, delirium, strangulation, and psychological distress, and are associated in some studies with increased agitation and injury. Patient advocates and professional bodies argue for minimizing restraint use as a matter of dignity and safety.
