Dauphin of France (French: Dauphin de France) was the title given to the heir apparent to the throne of the Kingdom of France from 1350 to 1791, and again during the Bourbon Restoration of 1814–1830. The title derived from the Dauphiné, a province in south-eastern France whose rulers bore the name "Dauphin" (from the dolphin on their coat of arms) and which the last independent Dauphin, Humbert II, sold to King Philip VI in 1349 on condition that the French heir bear the title and arms.

History

The first royal Dauphin was the future Charles V. The title was not merely honorific: the Dauphin traditionally held the Dauphiné as his appanage and was styled "Dauphin of Viennois". The wife of the Dauphin was known as the Dauphine. Perhaps the most famous Dauphin was Louis, the "Grand Dauphin" (1661–1711), son of Louis XIV, who predeceased his father; three of his descendants in turn bore the title. The young Louis XVII, imprisoned during the Revolution, is often called "the Lost Dauphin". The last Dauphin was Louis Antoine, Duke of Angoulême, son of Charles X, who abdicated in 1830.
