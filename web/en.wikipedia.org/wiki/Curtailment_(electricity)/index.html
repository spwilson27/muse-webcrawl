Curtailment (electricity)

Wind farms are the most commonly curtailed generators.

In electric power systems, curtailment is the deliberate reduction of output of a generator below what it could otherwise produce. It is most often applied to variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar power when the grid cannot absorb or transport all available generation.

Causes

Curtailment occurs when supply exceeds demand plus export capacity — for example on windy, low-demand nights or sunny spring afternoons (the "duck curve" in solar-heavy grids) — or when transmission congestion traps generation behind a bottleneck. System operators order curtailment to maintain the continuous balance between generation and load that keeps grid frequency stable.

Scale and economics

As wind and solar capacity has grown, curtailment volumes have risen sharply in grids such as those of California, Texas, Germany, the United Kingdom and China, sometimes reaching several percent of annual renewable output. Curtailed energy represents lost revenue for generators and wasted zero-carbon electricity, though compensation rules vary: some contracts pay for "deemed" generation, while in negative-price markets generators may curtail voluntarily to avoid paying to generate.

Mitigation

Strategies to reduce curtailment include building more transmission, energy storage, flexible demand and demand response, sector coupling (such as green hydrogen production and smart EV charging), and improved forecasting and market design.
