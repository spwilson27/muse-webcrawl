Pacinian corpuscle

Pacinian corpuscles, also called lamellar corpuscles, are mechanoreceptors found in the skin and various internal organs that detect high-frequency vibration and transient pressure changes. They are named after the Italian anatomist Filippo Pacini, who described them in 1831 (they had been noted earlier, and are also associated with Abraham Vater, hence "Vater–Pacini corpuscles").

Structure

A Pacinian corpuscle is an onion-like structure about 1 mm long, consisting of a nerve ending surrounded by many concentric layers (lamellae) of flattened Schwann-like cells separated by fluid. The lamellae act as a mechanical filter: steady pressure is dissipated through the layers, while rapid changes are transmitted to the nerve ending, making the receptor respond only to the onset and offset of pressure and to vibration, particularly around 200–300 Hz. Pacinian corpuscles are rapidly adapting receptors.

Location and function

They are located deep in the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, especially in the fingers, palms, and soles, as well as in the pancreas, periosteum, joint capsules, and mesentery. Their sensitivity to vibration transmitted through objects makes them important for tool use and texture perception of distant or subtle stimuli.
