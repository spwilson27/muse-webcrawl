Ignacio José de Allende y Unzaga (21 January 1769 – 26 June 1811) was a captain of the Spanish Army in New Spain who came to sympathise with the independence movement. Together with the priest Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla, he led the opening stage of the Mexican War of Independence.

Early life and conspiracy

Allende was born in San Miguel el Grande (now San Miguel de Allende, renamed in his honour) to a wealthy Creole family. He joined the provincial dragoon regiment and rose to captain. In 1809–1810 he took part in the conspiracies of Valladolid and Querétaro that plotted rebellion against Spanish rule; the professional military leadership of the planned uprising was to be his.

War of Independence

When the Querétaro conspiracy was discovered, Hidalgo issued the Cry of Dolores on 16 September 1810, and the revolt began prematurely. Allende became Hidalgo's principal lieutenant and the rebellion's most capable military commander. His disciplined troops won early victories, capturing Guanajuato after the storming of the Alhóndiga de Granaditas, and defeated royalist forces at Monte de las Cruces outside Mexico City in October 1810. Relations between the two leaders deteriorated — Allende deplored the looting and disorder of the insurgent masses and at one point considered removing or even poisoning Hidalgo. After the royalist victory at the Battle of Calderón Bridge in January 1811, Allende formally assumed command, but the retreating insurgents were betrayed and captured at the Wells of Baján in March.

Death and legacy

Allende was tried in Chihuahua and executed by firing squad on 26 June 1811. His head, like Hidalgo's, was displayed in a cage at the Alhóndiga in Guanajuato for a decade. He is honoured in Mexico as one of the founding fathers of the nation, and his remains rest in the Angel of Independence column in Mexico City.
