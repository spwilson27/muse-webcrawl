Community treatment order

CTOs allow compulsory treatment of psychiatric patients living in the community.

A community treatment order (CTO) is a legal order made by a court or mental health tribunal, or by a responsible clinician under mental health legislation, requiring a person with a mental disorder to comply with specified treatment while living in the community rather than in hospital. They are known by various names, including supervised discharge, involuntary outpatient commitment (in the United States), and assisted outpatient treatment (notably "Kendra's Law" in New York State).

Operation

CTOs typically apply to patients who have been detained in hospital and are being discharged, allowing conditions to be attached — for example, taking prescribed medication, attending appointments, and residing at a specified address. Non-compliance does not usually permit forcible treatment in the community, but can trigger recall to hospital for assessment and possible re-detention. CTOs exist in many jurisdictions, including most Australian states, Canada, New Zealand, Scotland, and (since the Mental Health Act 2007 amended the Mental Health Act 1983) England and Wales, where they replaced supervised discharge and are issued by the responsible clinician with the agreement of an approved mental health professional.

Evidence and debate

Advocates argue that CTOs provide a less restrictive alternative to hospital, break the "revolving door" pattern of repeated relapse and readmission, and protect patients and the public. Critics question their coercive character and civil-liberties implications. Research evidence is contested: some observational studies associate CTOs with reduced readmission and mortality, while the randomised OCTET trial in England (2013) found that CTOs did not reduce readmission rates compared with a control condition. Rates of use vary widely between jurisdictions and are disproportionately high for some ethnic minority groups, a disparity that has attracted sustained scrutiny.
