Putto

A putto (plural putti) is a figure in a work of art depicting a chubby male child, usually naked and sometimes winged. Putti are distinct from cherubim, who in theology rank among the angels: originally derived from classical antiquity's erotes — the infant attendants of Eros/Cupid — putti in Renaissance and later art are usually decorative, secular spirits of play, love and abundance rather than religious beings, though they can shade into the figures of infant angels. The word is Italian for "little boy" (from Latin putus, boy).

History

Infant cupids were common in ancient Greek and Roman art, appearing on sarcophagi, gems and wall paintings. The motif largely vanished in the Middle Ages but was revived in early Renaissance Italy — Donatello is credited with reintroducing the putto as an independent, exuberant figure in the 1420s–30s, notably on the singing gallery (cantoria) for Florence Cathedral and the exterior pulpit at Prato. From Italy the putto spread across Europe and became one of the most ubiquitous ornaments of Renaissance, Baroque and Rococo art, swarming over ceilings, fountains, tombs and altarpieces, carrying garlands, musical instruments, coats of arms or the instruments of Christ's Passion.

Iconography

Putti embody playful energies: they personify the seasons, the arts, love, wine and festivity, and in funerary art they may suggest the soul or mourning genii. Art historians distinguish the purely decorative putto from the amorino (little Cupid) and from the cherub proper; in practice the categories blur, and single winged child-angels on religious works are often simply called putti. The term also appears in heraldry and in the name of a family of medieval and Renaissance entertainments involving costumed child figures.
