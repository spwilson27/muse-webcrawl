Flashback (narrative)

TypeNarrative device
Greek termAnalepsis
OppositeFlashforward (prolepsis)

A flashback (sometimes called an analepsis) is an interjected scene or episode in a narrative that takes the reader or viewer back in time from the current point in the story. Flashbacks are used to provide backstory, reveal character motivation, supply exposition, or create suspense by withholding and then disclosing past events.

Types

An internal flashback returns to a point within the time frame already covered by the story, re-showing or re-examining earlier events — sometimes from a different character's perspective, which may revise the audience's understanding. An external flashback reaches back before the story's opening, as with the childhood scenes that explain an adult character's behaviour. Flashbacks may be triggered by a memory, a dream, an object, hypnosis, or narration by a character recounting the past.

History

The device is ancient: Homer's Odyssey is largely Odysseus's extended flashback narration of his wanderings. It became a staple of the nineteenth-century novel and, in cinema, of film noir, where voice-over confession structures whole plots — classics include Citizen Kane (1941), built as a mosaic of witnesses' flashbacks, and Rashomon (1950), whose contradictory flashbacks make the technique itself the subject. Television serials such as Lost made the character flashback a structural signature.

Criticism

Critics sometimes regard heavy reliance on flashbacks as a substitute for dramatising events in the present, and writing manuals advise that flashbacks stall forward momentum unless they change the meaning of the present-tense story.
