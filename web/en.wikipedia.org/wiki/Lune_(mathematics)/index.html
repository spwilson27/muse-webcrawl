Lune (mathematics)

In plane geometry, a lune is the concave-convex region bounded by two circular arcs. It is formed by taking the region inside one circle but outside another overlapping circle; the complementary convex-convex region bounded by two arcs is called a lens. The word comes from the Latin luna, "moon," since the figure resembles a crescent moon. On a sphere, the analogous figure bounded by two half great circles is a spherical lune (or digon).

Lune of Hippocrates

The lune of Hippocrates, studied by the Greek mathematician Hippocrates of Chios around 440 BC, was the first curvilinear figure whose area was calculated exactly. Hippocrates considered lunes erected on the sides of a right triangle inscribed in a semicircle and showed that the sum of the areas of the lunes on the legs equals the area of the triangle itself. The result raised hopes that the circle could be squared by similar means—an ambition shown impossible only in 1882, when Ferdinand von Lindemann proved π is transcendental.

Squarable lunes

Only five lunes constructible with compass and straightedge are "squarable"—that is, have an area equal to a constructible square. Three were known from antiquity (the cases associated with Hippocrates), and two more were found in the 18th and 19th centuries; a complete proof that no others exist was completed in the 20th century by Nikolai Chebotaryov and Anatoly Dorodnov, using Galois theory.
