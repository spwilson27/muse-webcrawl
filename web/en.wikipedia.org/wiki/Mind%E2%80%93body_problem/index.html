Mind–body problem

The mind–body problem is a central problem in the philosophy of mind concerning the relationship between the mind — thoughts, feelings, consciousness — and the physical body, in particular the brain. At its heart is the question of how mental phenomena can arise from, interact with, or be identical to physical processes.

Dualism

René Descartes argued for substance dualism: mind (res cogitans) and body (res extensa) are distinct substances that interact, in his account through the pineal gland. Dualism preserves the intuition that mental life is not merely physical, but faces the interaction problem — explaining how two fundamentally different substances can causally affect one another.

Physicalism and monism

Physicalist positions hold that mental states are physical states. Identity theory identifies mental states with brain states, while functionalism defines them by their causal roles and allows multiple realisability. Behaviourism, associated with Gilbert Ryle and B. F. Skinner, treated talk of minds as talk of behaviour and dispositions.

The hard problem

David Chalmers distinguished the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions from the "hard problem" of consciousness: why physical processing should be accompanied by subjective experience at all. Related puzzles include Thomas Nagel's question of what it is like to be a bat and Frank Jackson's knowledge argument featuring Mary the colour scientist.

Contemporary views

Current debates draw on neuroscience, cognitive science, and theories such as emergentism, panpsychism, and illusionism. The mind–body problem remains one of the most contested topics in philosophy, with no consensus solution.
