Two-photon excitation microscopy

Image placeholder: two-photon excitation microscopy

Two-photon excitation microscopy (TPEF or 2PEF) is a fluorescence microscopy technique that images living tissue up to about one millimetre in depth. It relies on two-photon absorption: a fluorophore is excited by the near-simultaneous absorption of two photons of roughly half the energy (twice the wavelength) that would be needed for single-photon excitation. The technique was first demonstrated in 1990 by Winfried Denk, James Strickler and Watt Webb at Cornell University.

Principle

Two-photon absorption is a nonlinear process whose probability depends on the square of the light intensity. Significant excitation therefore occurs only in the tiny focal volume of a tightly focused pulsed laser — typically a femtosecond titanium-sapphire laser — where photon density is highest. This provides inherent three-dimensional optical sectioning without a confocal pinhole, and confines photobleaching and photodamage to the focal plane rather than the whole illuminated cone.

Advantages

Because excitation uses near-infrared light (typically 700–1000 nm), scattering in tissue is reduced compared with visible light, so fluorescence can be generated and detected from hundreds of micrometres deep. The localised excitation also allows long-term imaging of living specimens, including the brains of anaesthetised and awake behaving animals through cranial windows.

Applications

Two-photon microscopy is a standard tool in neuroscience for imaging neuronal structure and calcium activity in vivo, and is also used in immunology, developmental biology, cancer research and kidney and skin physiology. Related multiphoton techniques using the same microscopes include second-harmonic generation and third-harmonic generation imaging, which produce contrast from non-fluorescent structures such as collagen.
