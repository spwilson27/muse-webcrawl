Hațeg Island was a large offshore island in the Tethys Sea during the Late Cretaceous Period, about 72 to 66 million years ago, in the Maastrichtian age. Its fossil-bearing strata are now exposed around the town of Hațeg in present-day Romania. The island is famous in palaeontology for its unusual fauna, in which dinosaurs were dramatically smaller than their mainland relatives — a classic example of island dwarfism, sometimes called the "island of the dwarf dinosaurs".

Fauna

The island's dinosaurs included the titanosaur Magyarosaurus, only about 6 metres long and roughly a tonne in weight — tiny beside mainland sauropods — and the hadrosauroid Telmatosaurus, as well as the small dromaeosaur Balaur bondoc, a stocky two-clawed predator. The ecosystem also included turtles, crocodyliforms, multituberculate mammals and many lizards and frogs. In place of large dinosaur predators, the apex animals were giant azhdarchid pterosaurs, above all Hatzegopteryx, named after Hațeg, whose wingspan reached 10–11 metres and which likely hunted on foot like a monstrous stork.

Island rule

Hațeg's fauna illustrates the "island rule": on islands, large animals tend to evolve smaller body sizes under resource limitation, while small animals may become giants. The dwarfism of the Hațeg dinosaurs was first recognised by the palaeontologist Franz Nopcsa in the early 20th century and has been confirmed by bone histology studies showing the dinosaurs were fully grown adults, not juveniles.
