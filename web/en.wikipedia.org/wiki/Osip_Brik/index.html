Osip Maksimovich Brik (16 January 1888 – 22 February 1945) was a Russian avant-garde writer and literary critic, one of the central figures of Russian Formalism and a close associate of the poet Vladimir Mayakovsky.

Life and work

Brik was born in Moscow to a wealthy Jewish family; his father was a jeweller. He studied law at Moscow University and graduated in 1911, but his interests soon turned to literature and linguistics. In 1915 he met Mayakovsky, and the poet moved into the Briks' Petrograd apartment — the beginning of one of the most famous ménages in Russian literary history, since Brik's wife Lilya Brik became Mayakovsky's lover and muse with her husband's acquiescence.

In 1916 Brik was a co-founder of OPOYAZ (the Society for the Study of Poetic Language) in Petrograd, together with Viktor Shklovsky and others, and he financed and edited the society's early collections on the theory of poetic language. His own contributions included the essay "Sound Repetitions" (1917), an analysis of sound patterning in the poetry of Pushkin and Lermontov, and "Rhythm and Syntax" (1927), which argued for a formal study of verse rhythm independent of meaning.

LEF and the literature of fact

In the 1920s Brik became, with Mayakovsky, co-editor of the journals LEF (1923–1925) and Novyi LEF (1927–1928), the organs of the Left Front of the Arts. There he championed the "literature of fact" (literatura fakta) — documentary prose, reportage, photography and film — against conventional fiction, and wrote programmatic essays such as "The Book That Is Demanded" and "Against 'Creative' Individuality." He also worked extensively in the early Soviet film industry, writing screenplays including Storm over Asia (1928) with director Vsevolod Pudovkin, and heading the scenario department at the Mezhrabpom-Rus studio.

Later years

As Socialist Realism hardened into orthodoxy in the 1930s, the formalists came under attack and Brik's publishing opportunities narrowed. He continued to write film scripts, historical essays and studies of Mayakovsky. He died of a heart attack in Moscow on 22 February 1945.
