Avalanche photodiode

TypePhotodetector
Working principleAvalanche multiplication (impact ionization)
Typical gain10 – 1,000 (linear mode); single-photon sensitivity in Geiger mode
MaterialsSilicon, germanium, InGaAs/InP

An avalanche photodiode (APD) is a highly sensitive semiconductor photodetector that exploits the photoelectric effect to convert light into electricity. Unlike an ordinary photodiode, an APD is operated at a high reverse bias voltage that accelerates photo-generated charge carriers to energies sufficient for impact ionization, producing an internal avalanche multiplication of the photocurrent. This internal gain allows APDs to detect much weaker optical signals than conventional p–n or p–i–n photodiodes.

Principle of operation

When a photon is absorbed in the depletion region of the diode, it creates an electron–hole pair. Under the strong electric field, the carriers gain kinetic energy and, upon colliding with the crystal lattice, generate additional electron–hole pairs through impact ionization. This cascade is the avalanche effect, characterized by a multiplication factor M that can range from a few to several hundred depending on bias voltage, temperature, and device structure.

The multiplication process introduces excess noise, described by the excess noise factor, which grows with M and depends on the ratio of ionization coefficients for electrons and holes. Materials in which one carrier type ionizes much more strongly than the other, such as silicon, give lower excess noise.

Operating modes

APDs are generally operated in linear mode, below the breakdown voltage, where the output current is proportional to the incident light intensity. Biased above breakdown in Geiger mode, a single photon can trigger a self-sustaining avalanche, enabling single-photon detection; such devices are called single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) and require quenching circuits to reset the avalanche.

Materials and applications

Silicon APDs cover the visible and near-infrared (roughly 400–1100 nm) and are used in laser rangefinders, lidar, and positron emission tomography. Germanium and indium gallium arsenide (InGaAs) devices on indium phosphide substrates extend sensitivity to the 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm telecommunications windows, making them standard detectors in long-haul optical fiber receivers. APDs are also used in particle physics detectors and quantum key distribution systems.
