The existence of God has been debated by philosophers since antiquity.

The existence of God is a subject of debate in theology, philosophy of religion, and popular culture. A wide variety of arguments for and against the existence of God can be found in metaphysics, epistemology, and value theory. In philosophical terms, the question involves the disciplines of epistemology (the nature and scope of knowledge) and ontology (the study of being), and positions range from theism (belief that God exists) through agnosticism (suspension of judgement) to atheism (absence of belief, or belief that God does not exist).

Arguments for the existence of God

  • Ontological argument — developed by Anselm of Canterbury (1078) and later Descartes and Leibniz: God, as the greatest conceivable being, must exist in reality, since existence is a perfection.
  • Cosmological arguments — from Aquinas's "Five Ways" to the kalām cosmological argument: whatever begins to exist has a cause, and the universe requires a first cause or necessary being.
  • Teleological (design) argument — the apparent order and fine-tuning of nature points to a designer; classically stated by William Paley's watchmaker analogy.
  • Moral arguments — objective moral values and duties are best explained by a divine lawgiver; also Kant's practical argument and arguments from religious experience and miracles.

Arguments against

  • Problem of evil — the existence of suffering, in its logical form (Mackie) and evidential form (Rowe), is held to count against an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God.
  • Argument from inconsistent revelations and the diversity of religions.
  • Parsimony (Occam's razor) — naturalistic explanations make God an unnecessary hypothesis.
  • Argument from nonbelief (the "hiddenness" of God) — a perfectly loving God would make his existence more evident.

Burden of proof and definitions

Much debate turns on definitions of "God" — from classical theism's omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent creator to narrower conceptions — and on where the burden of proof lies. Ignostics argue the question is meaningless until "God" is coherently defined. Surveys of professional philosophers (PhilPapers 2020) found a majority leaning toward atheism (about two-thirds of respondents), with roughly a fifth accepting or leaning toward theism.
