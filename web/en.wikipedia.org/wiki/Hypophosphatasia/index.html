Hypophosphatasia

Hypophosphatasia (also called deficiency of alkaline phosphatase or phosphoethanolaminuria) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder characterised by defective mineralisation of bone and teeth, caused by mutations in the ALPL gene encoding the tissue-nonspecific isoenzyme of alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP). It is estimated to affect roughly 1 in 100,000 live births in its severe forms, with milder forms more common.

Signs and symptoms

Severity varies widely and correlates with age of onset. The perinatal form, usually inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, presents with severely under-mineralised bones, a poorly formed rib cage, and respiratory failure, and was historically often lethal. Infantile disease brings failure to thrive, rickets-like skeletal changes, craniosynostosis, and seizures responsive to vitamin B6. Childhood and adult forms cause recurrent fractures, bone pain, muscle weakness, premature loss of deciduous teeth (with intact roots), and dental disease; odontohypophosphatasia affects only the teeth. Deficiency of TNSALP leads to accumulation of its substrates — inorganic pyrophosphate, phosphoethanolamine, and pyridoxal-5′-phosphate — which explains both the mineralisation defect and the B6-dependent seizures.

Diagnosis and treatment

A persistently low serum alkaline phosphatase activity with elevated substrate levels, together with genetic testing, establishes the diagnosis. In 2015 the enzyme replacement therapy asfotase alfa, a recombinant form of the enzyme, was approved for perinatal, infantile, and juvenile-onset disease, substantially improving survival and skeletal outcomes. Other care is supportive, and bisphosphonates — commonly used in other bone diseases — are generally avoided because they are pyrophosphate analogues that may worsen the mineralisation defect.
