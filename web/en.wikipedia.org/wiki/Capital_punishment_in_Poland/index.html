Capital punishment in Poland

The Polish penal code of 1997 abolished the death penalty for all crimes. (Image placeholder)

Capital punishment in Poland was abolished in 1997, and the last execution took place in 1988. The death penalty had been part of Polish law for centuries and was retained in the penal codes of the communist Polish People's Republic, where it was applied chiefly for murder and, earlier, for political and economic offences. Poland is a party to the European Convention on Human Rights, whose Protocols No. 6 and No. 13 prohibit the death penalty in peacetime and in all circumstances respectively.

History

In the interwar Second Polish Republic, executions were carried out by hanging. After the Second World War, the communist authorities used the death penalty extensively in the Stalinist period against members of the anti-communist underground; thousands were sentenced in political trials, and many were executed and buried in secret. The 1969 penal code retained capital punishment, most commonly imposed for aggravated murder. Executions were carried out by hanging, typically at Montelupich Prison in Kraków and other designated prisons.

The last execution in Poland was that of Stanisław Czabański, convicted of murder, who was hanged on 21 April 1988 in Kraków. A de facto moratorium followed during the political transition of 1989, and no further death sentences were carried out.

Abolition

The new penal code adopted in 1997, which entered into force on 1 September 1998, removed the death penalty entirely, replacing it with life imprisonment (with the possibility of restrictions on parole). The Constitution of 1997 protects human life and prohibits torture and cruel punishment, and Poland ratified the relevant Council of Europe protocols.

Public debate

Public opinion surveys have at times shown substantial support for restoring the death penalty, particularly after notorious crimes, and some political figures — including, in the mid-2000s, senior government officials — publicly advocated its reintroduction. Such proposals have consistently foundered on Poland's obligations under European Union and Council of Europe law, which make restoration effectively impossible for a member state.
