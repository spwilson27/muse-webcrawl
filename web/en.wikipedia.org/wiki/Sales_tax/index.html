Sales tax

A sales tax is a consumption tax imposed by a government on the sale of goods and services. A conventional or retail sales tax is levied at the point of sale on the final consumer, collected by the retailer and remitted to the taxing authority. Unlike a value-added tax (VAT), a conventional sales tax is charged only once, at the final purchase by the end user; businesses buying goods for resale are generally exempt and do not pay tax on intermediate transactions.

United States

The United States has no national sales tax; instead, 45 states and thousands of local jurisdictions levy their own. State rates range from about 2.9% (Colorado) to 7.25% (California), and combined state and local rates can exceed 10% in some cities. Five states — Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon — have no statewide sales tax. Many states exempt groceries, prescription medicines or clothing, or hold sales-tax holidays. The 2018 Supreme Court decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair allowed states to require remote and online sellers to collect tax based on economic nexus, greatly expanding collection on e-commerce.

Elsewhere

Most countries instead use a VAT or goods and services tax (GST), collected at each stage of production and distribution with credits for tax paid on inputs. Some jurisdictions, including parts of Canada, combine a federal GST with provincial sales taxes. India replaced a patchwork of state sales taxes with a unified GST in 2017.

Economics

Sales taxes are simple to administer at the retail level but can be regressive, taking a larger share of income from low-income households, which spend a greater proportion of their income on taxable consumption. Exemptions for necessities and refundable credits are commonly used to soften this effect.
