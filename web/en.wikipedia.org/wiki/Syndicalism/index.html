Syndicalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Syndicalism is a movement and theory within socialism that advances the radical syndicate (trade union) as both the weapon of working-class emancipation and the cell of the future society: production is to be owned and managed by workers organised in unions, the state abolished, and the general strike the means of transformation. Its French root (the CGT and the Charter of Amiens, 1906, which declared union independence from parties) joined revolutionary practice in Spain's CNT (whose collectivisations in 1936–37 were syndicalism's largest real-world experiment), Italy's USI and the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW, founded 1905) in North America.

Georges Sorel's Reflections on Violence (1908) gave the movement its mythic theory of the general strike. Syndicalism declined after the First World War — squeezed between communist parties (which subordinated unions to the party) and social-democratic unionism (which integrated them into bargaining states), while in Spain it was crushed by Franco. Its legacy persists in Anarcho-syndicalism, in union structures that prize direct action and rank-and-file control, and in recurring experiments with worker self-management.
