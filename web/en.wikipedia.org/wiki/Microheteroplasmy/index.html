Microheteroplasmy

Microheteroplasmy is the presence of low levels of mutated mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) within a cell, tissue or individual — typically mutations present in a small percentage, often given as roughly 1–2% or less, of the total mtDNA molecules. It is a low-frequency form of heteroplasmy, the coexistence of more than one type of mitochondrial genome in the same cell.

Background

Each human cell contains hundreds to thousands of copies of the mitochondrial genome. Conventional Sanger sequencing detects heteroplasmic variants only when they exceed roughly 10–20% of molecules, so microheteroplasmic variants long escaped notice. Deep sequencing revealed that low-level mtDNA mutations are in fact widespread: most individuals carry small proportions of many different variants, which arise from replication errors and accumulate with age as a result of the high mutation rate of mtDNA and the relaxed replication and bottleneck effects in mitochondrial inheritance.

Significance

Because mitochondrial function declines when the proportion of defective genomes passes a tissue-specific threshold, microheteroplasmic mutations are usually clinically silent, but they form a reservoir from which higher-level heteroplasmy can arise through genetic drift, clonal expansion (as seen in ageing muscle and in some tumours) or transmission through the germline bottleneck. Microheteroplasmy is studied in ageing research, mitochondrial disease and forensic science, where low-level variants can complicate or assist identification.
