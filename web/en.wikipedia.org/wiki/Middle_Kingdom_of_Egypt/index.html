Middle Kingdom of Egypt

The Middle Kingdom of Egypt (also known as the Period of Reunification) is the period in the history of ancient Egypt following a period of political division known as the First Intermediate Period. It lasted approximately from 2040 to 1782 BC, spanning the later part of the Eleventh Dynasty through the Twelfth Dynasty, and is followed by the Second Intermediate Period. During this period Egypt was reunified under a strong central government, and the era is noted for its literature, art, and expansion into Nubia.

Reunification

The Middle Kingdom began when Mentuhotep II of Thebes defeated the rival Herakleopolitan kings of Lower Egypt and reunited the Two Lands around 2040 BC. The Eleventh Dynasty rulers moved the capital to Thebes. Amenemhat I, founder of the Twelfth Dynasty, later established a new capital, Itjtawy, near the Faiyum, and his successors strengthened royal control over the provincial governors (nomarchs).

Height of the kingdom

Under Senusret III and Amenemhat III, Egypt reached a peak of prosperity and power. Senusret III reorganized the administration, campaigned deep into Nubia, and built a chain of massive fortresses along the Second Cataract, including Buhen and Semna. Large-scale irrigation and land reclamation projects were undertaken in the Faiyum Oasis, and trade reached Punt and the Levant. Middle Kingdom literature, composed in what became the classical stage of the Egyptian language, includes the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, the Story of Sinuhe, and wisdom texts such as the Instructions of Amenemhat.

Decline

During the Thirteenth Dynasty, royal authority weakened through a rapid succession of short-reigning kings, and control over the eastern Delta slipped to West Asian (Hyksos) rulers at Avaris, ushering in the Second Intermediate Period.
