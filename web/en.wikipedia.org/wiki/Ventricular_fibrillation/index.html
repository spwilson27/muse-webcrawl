Ventricular fibrillation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ECG strip showing the chaotic waveform of ventricular fibrillation
An ECG strip showing the chaotic waveform of ventricular fibrillation

Ventricular fibrillation (VF or V-fib) is a life-threatening heart rhythm disorder in which the ventricles quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing no effective cardiac output. It is the most common initial rhythm in sudden Cardiac arrest outside hospital and is uniformly fatal within minutes without CPR and Defibrillation — an electric shock to reset the heart's electrical system. Automated external defibrillators (AEDs) in public places exist largely to treat VF.

Mechanism and treatment

VF usually erupts from ischaemic or scarred myocardium (heart attack, cardiomyopathy) via re-entrant wavelets degenerating into electrical chaos; the ECG shows a shapeless baseline without QRS complexes. Treatment is the chain of survival: immediate CPR (which buys minutes), early defibrillation (survival falls ~10% per minute untreated), advanced life support, and post-arrest care (targeted temperature management, coronary intervention). Survivors often receive implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) against recurrence.

Epidemiology

Sudden cardiac death accounts for roughly 15–20% of mortality in industrialised countries; VF's share of presenting rhythms has declined as coronary disease patterns change (more arrests now show pulseless electrical activity or asystole), but it remains the shockable rhythm whose treatment defines emergency medicine's public mission.
