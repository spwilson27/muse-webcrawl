Totalitarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Totalitarianism is a political system and a form of government that prohibits all opposition parties, outlaws individual and group opposition to the state and its claims, and exercises extremely high — historically unprecedented — control and regulation over public and private life. The concept was coined in 1920s Italy (initially by fascism's critics, then adapted by Mussolini in a positive sense) and developed by theorists including Hannah Arendt (The Origins of Totalitarianism, 1951) and Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski.

Features

Friedrich and Brzezinski's classic syndrome includes: an official, all-embracing ideology; a single party, typically led by one man; a terroristic secret police; a monopoly of mass communications; a monopoly of weapons; and a centrally directed economy. Arendt emphasised ideology's claim to explain everything, terror as the regime's essence rather than a tool, and the production of "loneliness" and superfluousness in concentration and labour camps. The paradigmatic cases studied are Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union under Stalin, with scholars debating the concept's application to Maoist China, North Korea and others, and its Cold War polemical uses.

Legacy

While some historians question whether any state achieved truly total control, the concept shaped the self-understanding of post-war democracies and remains a reference point in discussions of surveillance, propaganda and mass mobilisation in the digital age.
