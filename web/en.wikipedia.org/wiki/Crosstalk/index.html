Crosstalk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In electronics, crosstalk is any phenomenon by which a signal transmitted on one circuit or channel of a transmission system creates an undesired effect in another circuit or channel. Crosstalk is usually caused by undesired capacitive, inductive, or conductive coupling from one circuit or channel to another.

Telecommunications

In telephony, crosstalk manifests as the leakage of speech from one connection into another, so that a caller can faintly hear an unrelated conversation. In analogue cable systems it was a familiar fault; modern digital systems are less susceptible but crosstalk remains an important limit in DSL and other services sharing multi-pair cables. Two measures are standard:

  • Near-end crosstalk (NEXT) — interference measured at the same end of the cable as the interfering transmitter.
  • Far-end crosstalk (FEXT) — interference measured at the opposite end of the cable from the transmitter.

Cable design

In structured cabling, crosstalk between twisted pairs is reduced by twisting each pair at a different rate, by maintaining pair geometry, and in higher categories of cable by internal separators and shielding. Crosstalk performance is a defining parameter of the Category 6 and higher cabling standards used for Ethernet.

Other uses

In integrated circuits, crosstalk arises from capacitive coupling between neighbouring interconnects and can cause glitches and timing errors in high-speed digital designs. In audio, it describes channel leakage, for example between the left and right channels of a stereo system. In cell biology, "crosstalk" is used metaphorically for interactions between signalling pathways.
