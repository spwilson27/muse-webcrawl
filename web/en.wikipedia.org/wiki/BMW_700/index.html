The 700's success is widely credited with saving BMW from bankruptcy and takeover.

The BMW 700 is a small rear-engined car produced by the German manufacturer BMW between 1959 and 1965. It was the first BMW automobile with a monocoque (unitary) structure, and its strong sales rescued the company from a severe financial crisis at the end of the 1950s.

Development

In the late 1950s BMW was in deep trouble: its large luxury cars sold poorly, the Isetta bubble car was fading, and a takeover by Daimler-Benz was on the table, narrowly rejected by shareholders at the dramatic annual meeting of December 1959. The 700, styled by Giovanni Michelotti and using an enlarged version of the air-cooled flat-twin motorcycle engine mounted at the rear, had been unveiled that June as a saloon and coupé. Early sales were strikingly strong — orders poured in, and the car brought in the cash and confidence that, together with investment by Herbert Quandt, allowed BMW to fund the New Class programme that redefined the company.

Design and variants

The 697 cc twin produced 30 hp (32 hp in the twin-carburettor Sport/Coupé Sport versions with up to 40 hp), giving modest but willing performance and a top speed around 125 km/h. Body styles comprised a two-door saloon, an elegant coupé, a rare convertible, and from 1963 the lengthened 700 LS. In competition, 700s won numerous touring-car class victories and hill-climb successes, driven by among others Hans Stuck. Production totalled 188,121 cars when the model was discontinued in 1965, by which time the New Class 1500 had secured BMW's future.
