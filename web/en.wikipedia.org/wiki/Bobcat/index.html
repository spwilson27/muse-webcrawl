Bobcat

The bobcat (Lynx rufus), also known as the red lynx, is a medium-sized cat native to North America. It ranges from southern Canada through most of the contiguous United States to Oaxaca in Mexico. It is listed as Least Concern on the IUCN Red List since 2002, because of its wide distribution and large population. Although it has been hunted extensively both for sport and fur, populations have proven stable, though declining in some areas.

The bobcat resembles other species of the midsize genus Lynx, but is on average the smallest of the four. Its coat is variable, though generally tan to grayish-brown, with black streaks on the body and dark bars on the forelegs and tail. Its spotted patterning acts as camouflage. The ears are black-tipped and pointed, with short, black tufts. Generally, an off-white color is seen on the lips, chin, and underparts. In the desert southwest, bobcats are lighter in color; those in forested northern areas are darker. Kittens are born well-furred and already spotted.

Characteristics

The bobcat is a medium-sized lynx, larger than a domestic cat but smaller than the Canada lynx. Adults measure 47.5 to 125 cm (18.7 to 49.2 in) in head-to-body length, with a stubby 9 to 20 cm (3.5 to 7.9 in) tail that gives the species its name. Adult males weigh 6.4 to 18.3 kg (14 to 40 lb), averaging around 9.6 kg (21 lb); females average about 6.8 kg (15 lb). The largest bobcat accurately measured on record weighed 22.2 kg (49 lb).

Its facial ruff and ear tufts are characteristic. The tail is short and bobbed, with black bars on top and a white underside, which is visible when the animal is walking away. The backs of the ears are black with a distinctive white spot, which may help kittens follow their mother in low light.

Distribution and habitat

The bobcat is an adaptable predator inhabiting wooded areas, as well as semi-desert, urban edge, forest edge, and swampland environments. It persists in much of its original range, and populations are healthy. In Mexico, bobcats are found in dry scrub, pine forest, and oak forest as far south as Oaxaca. Its range appears to be expanding northward into southern Canada as forests are cleared, and it may be hybridizing with the Canada lynx at the northern edge of its range.

Behavior and ecology

The bobcat is crepuscular, being most active at dawn and dusk. It keeps on the move from three hours before sunset until about midnight, and then again from before dawn until three hours after sunrise. Each night, it moves from 3 to 11 km (2 to 7 mi) along its habitual route. This behavior may vary seasonally, as bobcats become more diurnal during fall and winter in response to the activity of their prey, which are more active during the day in colder months.

Hunting and diet

The bobcat hunts animals of different sizes and adjusts its hunting techniques accordingly. With small prey, such as rodents, squirrels, birds, fish, and insects, it hunts in areas dense with that prey, lying, crouching, or standing still and waiting for victims to wander close. It then pounces, grabbing its prey with its sharp, retractable claws. For somewhat larger animals, such as rabbits and hares, it stalks from cover and waits until prey comes within 6 to 11 m (20 to 35 ft) before rushing in to chase. Less commonly, it feeds on larger animals, such as young ungulates, and other carnivores, such as fishers, and opportunistically on domestic animals including poultry and sheep.

Reproduction

Bobcats begin breeding by their second summer, though females may start as early as their first year. The dominant male travels with a female and mates several times, generally from winter until early spring. Litters of one to six kittens are born after a gestation of about 60 to 70 days, usually in a den in a hollow log, thicket, or rock crevice. The kittens open their eyes by their ninth or tenth day and are weaned at about two months. They stay with their mother through the winter and disperse before the following spring.

Conservation

The bobcat is listed in Appendix II of CITES, meaning it is not considered threatened with extinction, but hunting and trade must be closely monitored. Regulated hunting and trapping are permitted in many U.S. states, and the species' pelt is used in the international fur trade. Population estimates in the United States range from 2.35 million to 3.57 million individuals.
