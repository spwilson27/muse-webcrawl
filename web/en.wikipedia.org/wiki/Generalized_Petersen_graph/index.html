In graph theory, the generalized Petersen graphs are a family of cubic graphs formed by connecting the vertices of a regular polygon to the corresponding vertices of a star polygon. The family includes the Petersen graph itself and was described by H. S. M. Coxeter and later named in a 1950 paper by Coxeter, with the family studied systematically by Mark Watkins in 1969.

Definition

The generalized Petersen graph G(n, k), for integers n ≥ 3 and 1 ≤ k < n/2, has 2n vertices: an outer set u0, …, un−1 forming an n-cycle, and an inner set v0, …, vn−1 forming a star polygon in which vi is joined to vi+k (indices modulo n). Matching "spoke" edges join each ui to vi. The resulting graph is cubic (3-regular) with 3n edges.

Examples

G(5, 1) is the graph of the dodecahedron; G(5, 2) is the Petersen graph; G(6, 2) is the Dürer graph; G(8, 3) is the Möbius–Kantor graph; G(10, 2) is the dodecahedron-related Desargues graph (G(10, 3) by isomorphism). The triangular prism graph is G(3, 1) and the cube graph is G(4, 1).

Properties

Generalized Petersen graphs are always cubic and bridgeless. G(n, k) is vertex-transitive exactly when (n, k) = (5, 2) or k² ≡ ±1 (mod n). All are Hamiltonian except the Petersen graph and G(7, 2). Their chromatic properties vary: the Petersen graph is a well-known snark-like example requiring four colours for edge colouring, while others are class I. Isomorphisms between parameters occur: G(n, k) ≅ G(n, k′) when k′ ≡ ±k−1 or k (mod n).
