Spinal disc herniation

Spinal disc herniation
SynonymsSlipped disc, ruptured disc, prolapsed disc
SpecialtyOrthopaedics, neurosurgery
SymptomsBack or neck pain, radiating limb pain (sciatica), numbness, weakness
CausesAge-related disc degeneration, trauma, heavy lifting, genetics
DiagnosisPhysical examination, MRI
TreatmentActivity modification, analgesics, physiotherapy, epidural injections, discectomy

Spinal disc herniation is a condition in which a tear in the outer fibrous ring (annulus fibrosus) of an intervertebral disc allows the soft inner material (nucleus pulposus) to bulge outwards, potentially compressing nearby spinal nerves or the spinal cord. It is commonly called a "slipped disc", although the disc does not literally slip out of place.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on the level affected. Lumbar herniations typically cause lower back pain radiating down the leg in the distribution of the sciatic nerve (sciatica), accompanied by numbness, tingling, or weakness. Cervical herniations cause neck pain radiating into the arm. A large central herniation in the lumbar spine can compress the cauda equina, producing cauda equina syndrome — saddle anaesthesia, bladder or bowel dysfunction — which is a surgical emergency.

Causes and pathophysiology

With age, discs lose water content and the annulus develops fissures, so less force is required to produce herniation. Heavy lifting with poor technique, twisting injuries, obesity, smoking, and a genetic predisposition all increase risk. Herniated material provokes an inflammatory response in addition to mechanical compression, which contributes to nerve-root pain.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with history and neurological examination, including the straight-leg raise test for lumbar radiculopathy. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred imaging study and shows the herniation and any nerve compression. Imaging findings of disc bulges are common in asymptomatic people and must be correlated with symptoms.

Treatment

Most herniations improve within weeks with conservative care: staying active, analgesics and anti-inflammatory drugs, and physiotherapy. Epidural steroid injections may give short-term relief of leg pain. Surgery — most commonly microdiscectomy — is considered for persistent disabling symptoms, progressive weakness, or cauda equina syndrome, and generally relieves leg pain faster than conservative care, though long-term outcomes often converge.
