Joseph Ettor

Joseph James Ettor (October 6, 1885 - February 19, 1948) was an American labor organizer and a leading figure in the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW). He is best known for his role in the 1912 Lawrence Textile Strike, the "Bread and Roses" strike.

Early life

Ettor was born in New York City to an Italian immigrant father who worked in steel mills. He left school young, worked in a variety of manual jobs on the West Coast, and joined the labor movement, first in the Socialist Party and then in the IWW, founded in 1905. A gifted speaker fluent in several languages, he became one of the union's most effective organizers among immigrant workers.

Lawrence Textile Strike

When textile workers in Lawrence, Massachusetts, walked out in January 1912 after a pay cut, Ettor was sent by the IWW to lead the strike. He organized the strikers - some 25,000 workers of dozens of nationalities - into a disciplined relief and picketing system. After striker Anna LoPizzo was shot dead during a clash, Ettor and fellow organizer Arturo Giovannitti were arrested and charged as accessories to murder, though neither was present at the shooting. Their imprisonment became a national cause celebre; both were acquitted in November 1912 after a celebrated trial in Salem.

Later years

Ettor remained active in the IWW through the 1910s, organizing among miners, lumber workers and farm laborers, but he was expelled from the union in 1916 amid internal disputes over tactics and centralization. He later settled in California, worked in farming and real estate, and gradually withdrew from radical politics. He died in 1948.
