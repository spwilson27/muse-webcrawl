Cultural lag


Material culture changes faster than the norms that regulate it

The term cultural lag refers to the notion that culture takes time to catch up with technological innovations, and the resulting social problems that are caused by this lag. In other words, cultural lag occurs whenever there is an unequal rate of change between different parts of a culture, causing a gap between material culture and non-material culture. The concept was introduced by the sociologist William Fielding Ogburn in his 1922 work Social Change with Respect to Culture and Original Nature.

Ogburn's theory

Ogburn distinguished material culture (technology, inventions, tools) from adaptive culture (laws, beliefs, customs, institutions). Because inventions accumulate rapidly, material culture changes quickly, while the norms and institutions that govern its use adjust slowly, producing periods of social maladjustment. His classic example was the lag between the widespread adoption of the automobile and the development of traffic laws, road systems, and safety norms.

Examples and criticism

Common modern examples include reproductive technologies advancing faster than ethical and legal frameworks, digital communication outrunning privacy law, and artificial intelligence raising regulatory questions before norms settle. Critics have argued that the theory underestimates how values themselves drive technological development, that "lag" presumes norms ought to adjust to technology, and that the distinction between material and non-material culture is not always clear-cut.
