English orthography

English orthography is the system of spelling conventions and rules used in writing the English language. It is widely noted for its complexity and apparent irregularity: the 26 letters of the Latin alphabet must represent more than 40 phonemes, and correspondences between letters and sounds are heavily influenced by the word's history and morphology. Estimates of the number of distinct grapheme–phoneme patterns run into the hundreds, although the system is more regular than it first appears when word structure and etymology are taken into account.

History

Old English was written with an adapted Latin alphabet (including the runic-derived letters þ, ð and æ) whose spelling was comparatively phonemic. After the Norman Conquest, French-trained scribes respelled English using French conventions — introducing qu for Old English cw, ch for /tʃ/, and ou for /uː/. The introduction of printing in the 1470s began to fix spellings just as the Great Vowel Shift was transforming pronunciation, freezing many spellings in their pre-shift values. Compositors and editors also lengthened or shortened words to justify lines, and sixteenth-century scholars inserted silent letters to display (sometimes mistaken) Latin origins, as in debt (Latin debitum) and island (falsely linked to insula).

Characteristics

English spelling preserves morphological relationships at the expense of phonetic transparency: sign/signal, electric/electricity and the regular written plural -s (though pronounced /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/) reflect meaning rather than sound. Loanwords retain traces of their source languages, from French (machine) to Italian (piano) and Greek (psychology).

Variation and reform

British and American spellings diverge chiefly where Noah Webster and other reformers regularised American usage (color, center, traveled) against Samuel Johnson's dictionary conventions. Numerous broader reform proposals — from the nineteenth-century phonetic movements to the Simplified Spelling Board and the Shaw alphabet — have failed to win adoption, leaving English with one of the deepest (least phonemic) alphabetic orthographies in common use.
