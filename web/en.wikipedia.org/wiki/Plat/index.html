In the United States, a plat (rhyming with "flat") or plat map is a cadastral map, drawn to scale, showing the divisions of a piece of land. United States General Land Office surveyors first drew township plats under the Public Land Survey System, recording the divisions of public lands; in modern usage a plat most often shows how a tract has been divided into lots and blocks for a subdivision, together with streets, alleys, easements, and the bearings and dimensions of boundaries.

Platting

"Platting" is the legal process by which a developer subdivides land and creates new legal lots. Typically a preliminary plat is submitted for municipal review — checking lot sizes, road layouts, drainage, and utilities against the subdivision ordinance — followed by a final plat prepared and sealed by a licensed surveyor. Once approved and recorded with the county recorder or register of deeds, the plat becomes the official reference for legal descriptions of the lots ("Lot 7, Block 3, of the recorded plat of…"), and dedication of streets and public areas shown on it takes effect.

Contents

A plat typically includes the subdivision name, a north arrow and scale, lot and block numbers, boundary bearings and distances, monument descriptions (such as iron pins set at lot corners), certificates of ownership, surveyor's certification, and approvals by the planning commission and governing body. Amendments are made by a replat, and lots may be combined or boundaries adjusted by related recorded instruments.

Other uses of the term

County assessors maintain plat books (now usually digital) showing ownership parcels for taxation. The word "plat" is also an old term for a small plot of land, and appears in surveying and gardening contexts; it is distinct from a "plot" mainly by tradition.
