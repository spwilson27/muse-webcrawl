Structural variation

The main classes of structural variation

Structural variation (SV) is the variation in the structure of an organism's chromosome. It consists of many kinds of variation in the genome of one species, and usually includes microscopic and submicroscopic types, broadly defined as variants affecting DNA segments of roughly 1 kilobase (kb) or larger—although older definitions began at 50 base pairs, and smaller variants shade into insertions, deletions and indels.

Types

The main classes are: deletions and insertions of sequence; tandem duplications and other copy-number variations (CNVs), in which a segment is present in a different number of copies; inversions, in which a segment is reversed in orientation; and translocations, in which segments move between positions or chromosomes. Mobile element insertions (retrotransposons such as Alu and L1 elements) are a major source of structural variation in primate genomes.

Detection and effects

Structural variants were first studied cytogenetically—banding patterns revealed large rearrangements long before sequencing. Array comparative genomic hybridisation, paired-end and split-read analysis of short-read sequencing, and now long-read sequencing and optical mapping have progressively improved detection. Thousands of structural variants are present in a typical human genome, affecting more base pairs overall than single-nucleotide variants. Although most are benign, structural variants can disrupt genes, alter dosage of dosage-sensitive genes, or rewire regulatory landscapes, causing disorders such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (a duplication), Hunter syndrome (inversions), and many cancers, in which rearrangements can create fusion genes such as BCR–ABL.
