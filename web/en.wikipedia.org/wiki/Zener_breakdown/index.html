Zener breakdown is a type of electrical breakdown in a reverse-biased p–n junction diode, occurring when a sufficiently strong electric field causes electrons to tunnel quantum-mechanically from the valence band of the p-type material directly into the conduction band of the n-type material. It is named after the American physicist Clarence Melvin Zener, who described the effect in 1934, and it is the operating principle of the Zener diode used for voltage regulation.

Mechanism

Zener breakdown requires a thin depletion region, obtained by heavily doping both sides of the junction. Under reverse bias, the electric field across this narrow region becomes extremely intense (of the order of millions of volts per metre). The field distorts the energy bands until filled valence-band states on the p-side align with empty conduction-band states on the n-side, separated by only a few nanometres, and electrons tunnel through the forbidden gap in large numbers. Because tunnelling depends on the field rather than on carrier multiplication, the breakdown is sharp and stable.

Zener versus avalanche breakdown

In avalanche breakdown, by contrast, carriers accelerated by the field collide with lattice atoms and knock out further carriers in a chain reaction; it occurs in lightly doped junctions with wide depletion regions and at higher voltages (generally above about 7 V). The two mechanisms also differ in temperature behaviour: the Zener breakdown voltage decreases as temperature rises (negative temperature coefficient), because the band gap narrows, while the avalanche voltage increases. Around 5–6 V both effects coexist and their coefficients can approximately cancel — a fact exploited in precision voltage references. In practice, commercial "Zener diodes" rated above roughly 5.6 V actually operate mainly by avalanche breakdown, though the name has stuck for the whole family.

Applications

Diodes designed to operate in controlled breakdown provide inexpensive voltage references and regulators, overvoltage and electrostatic-discharge protection, and waveform clipping. Because the breakdown is non-destructive when current is limited by a series resistor, the device can maintain an almost constant voltage over a wide range of currents.
