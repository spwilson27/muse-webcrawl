Reg Sprigg

Image placeholder: the Ediacara Hills, where Sprigg found his fossils.

Reginald Claude Sprigg AO (1 March 1919 – 2 December 1994) was an Australian geologist and conservationist who, in 1946, discovered the Ediacara biota, the oldest known complex multicellular life forms, in the Ediacara Hills of the Flinders Ranges in South Australia. The discovery, initially dismissed by much of the scientific establishment, was later recognised as one of the most important palaeontological finds of the twentieth century, extending the fossil record of large organisms back before the Cambrian explosion.

Career

Sprigg studied geology at the University of Adelaide, where he was a protégé of Sir Douglas Mawson. While working as a government geologist surveying old mine sites in 1946, he found impressions of soft-bodied organisms in quartzite rocks that had previously been assumed to be early Cambrian or barren. He named genera including Dickinsonia and Spriggia; the genus Spriggina was later named in his honour.

Later work

Sprigg went on to found the petroleum exploration company Santos and the geological consultancy Geosurveys of Australia, and established the Arkaroola wilderness sanctuary in the northern Flinders Ranges, becoming a pioneering figure in Australian conservation and outback tourism. The Ediacaran Period, formally ratified in 2004, took its name from the locality of his discovery.
