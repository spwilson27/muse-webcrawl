Action research

Action research is a philosophy and methodology of research in the social sciences that seeks to bring about social change while simultaneously studying it. Rather than separating the researcher from the subject, action research involves practitioners and community members as co-researchers who investigate their own practice or situation and act on the findings. It is characterised by a repeating cycle of planning, acting, observing and reflecting.

The action research spiral: each cycle of action and reflection feeds the next.

History

The term was coined in the 1940s by the social psychologist Kurt Lewin, who argued that "research that produces nothing but books will not suffice" and developed action research in studies of intergroup relations and workplace democracy, including the Harwood factory studies. In Britain, the Tavistock Institute applied related ideas to organisational change (sociotechnical systems). From the 1970s, Lawrence Stenhouse and John Elliott developed action research in education — the teacher-as-researcher movement — while in Latin America, Orlando Fals Borda and others developed participatory action research (PAR), in which marginalised communities direct the inquiry into their own conditions, an approach influenced by Paulo Freire's pedagogy of the oppressed.

The cycle

The canonical process, often drawn as a spiral following Lewin's model and the later work of Kemmis and McTaggart, consists of:

  1. Plan — identify a problem and design an intervention, collaboratively.
  2. Act — carry out the intervention in the real setting.
  3. Observe — gather evidence about its effects.
  4. Reflect — evaluate the results together and plan the next, revised cycle.

Applications

Action research is used in education (teachers studying their own classrooms), healthcare (clinical and nursing improvement), community development, social work, business and organisational development. Its validity criteria are debated: because the researcher is also a participant seeking change, critics question its objectivity and generalisability, while defenders argue for alternative standards such as "outcome validity" and democratic participation, and counter that conventional detached research often fails to produce usable knowledge.
