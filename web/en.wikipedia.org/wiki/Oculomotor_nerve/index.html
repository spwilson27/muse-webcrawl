The oculomotor nerve, also known as the third cranial nerve (CN III), is a cranial nerve that supplies most of the muscles that move the eye and the eyelid, together with parasympathetic fibres that constrict the pupil and accommodate the lens. It arises from the midbrain and reaches the orbit through the superior orbital fissure. Damage to it produces a characteristic "down and out" eye, a drooping eyelid (ptosis) and, when parasympathetic fibres are involved, a dilated pupil.

Structure

The nerve has two components. The somatic motor fibres arise from the oculomotor nucleus in the midbrain at the level of the superior colliculus; the parasympathetic fibres arise from the adjacent Edinger–Westphal nucleus. The nerve emerges from the front of the midbrain between the cerebral peduncles, passes between the posterior cerebral and superior cerebellar arteries, pierces the dura, runs in the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and divides into superior and inferior divisions before entering the orbit.

Function

The superior division supplies the superior rectus and levator palpebrae superioris (which raises the eyelid). The inferior division supplies the medial rectus, inferior rectus and inferior oblique, and carries the parasympathetic fibres, which relay in the ciliary ganglion and continue to the sphincter pupillae (pupil constriction) and ciliary muscle (accommodation). Altogether CN III controls most eye movements except lateral movement (CN VI) and depression in adduction (CN IV).

Course

Its long intracranial course makes the nerve vulnerable at several points: beside the posterior communicating artery, at the tentorial edge where it may be compressed by the herniating uncus, and within the cavernous sinus alongside the trochlear and abducens nerves and the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve.

Clinical significance

Complete oculomotor palsy presents with ptosis, a dilated non-reactive pupil, and the eye resting "down and out" because of the unopposed lateral rectus and superior oblique. A pupil-involving palsy is a classic warning of a posterior communicating artery aneurysm compressing the nerve, since parasympathetic fibres run superficially; by contrast, microvascular (diabetic) palsies often spare the pupil. Causes include aneurysm, raised intracranial pressure with uncal herniation, diabetes, and tumours or inflammation in the cavernous sinus.
