Memory erasure

Memory erasure is the selective forgetting or removal of memories — whether deliberate, therapeutic, or fictional. In neuroscience, research into memory reconsolidation has shown that when a memory is retrieved it becomes temporarily labile, and that pharmacological or behavioural interventions during this window can weaken or alter it. In fiction, memory erasure is a recurring science-fiction trope, depicted in stories from Men in Black to Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind.

Scientific background

Memories are not erased in the brain in the manner of deleting a computer file; forgetting normally involves decay, interference, or retrieval failure. However, experiments — notably studies in the early 2000s showing that the beta-blocker propranolol, administered at memory reactivation, could dampen the emotional response associated with traumatic memories — suggested that the emotional component of memories in conditions such as post-traumatic stress disorder might be reduced without removing the factual memory itself. Anisomycin and other protein-synthesis inhibitors produced similar reconsolidation-blocking effects in animal studies.

Ethical issues

The prospect of targeted memory modification raises ethical questions about personal identity, authenticity, criminal responsibility, and consent. Bioethics bodies, including the U.S. President's Council on Bioethics in its 2003 report Beyond Therapy, have debated whether dampening painful memories could undermine the lessons and moral weight of experience.

In popular culture

Memory erasure features prominently in fiction: the "Neuralyzer" of the Men in Black films, the targeted erasure procedure in Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind (2004), the memory charms of the Harry Potter series, and the mind-wiping technologies of many dystopian narratives. It is also invoked in political rhetoric, for example in comparisons to the "memory hole" of George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four.
