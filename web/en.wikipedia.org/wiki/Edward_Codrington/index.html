Admiral Sir Edward Codrington, victor of Navarino

Admiral Sir Edward Codrington, GCB (27 April 1770 – 28 April 1851) was a British naval officer of the early nineteenth century, remembered chiefly as commander of the allied fleet that destroyed the Turkish-Egyptian fleet at the Battle of Navarino in 1827 — the decisive event securing Greek independence, and the last great battle fought entirely under sail. He had earlier served at the Glorious First of June and commanded HMS Orion at Trafalgar.

Early career

Born at Dodington, Gloucestershire, the third son of Edward Codrington (1732–1775), Codrington entered the Royal Navy at 13 as a midshipman. He served aboard HMS Queen, flagship of Lord Howe, at the Glorious First of June (1794), afterwards carrying Howe's despatches home. Promoted captain, he commanded the frigate HMS Druid and other ships on convoy and cruising service in the Channel and on the North American station. In 1805 he took command of the 74-gun HMS Orion, which joined the fleet off Cádiz; at the Battle of Trafalgar (21 October 1805) Orion, stationed towards the rear of Collingwood's column, engaged the French Swiftsure and Argonaute and the Spanish Bahama, and took possession of the great Spanish flagship Santísima Trinidad after the action.

Later service and Navarino

After service in the Walcheren expedition (1809) and on the North American station during the War of 1812, Codrington was promoted rear-admiral and in December 1826 given command of the Mediterranean Station. Britain, France and Russia had signed the Treaty of London (1827) to enforce an armistice in the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire. At Navarino Bay on 20 October 1827, Codrington's allied fleet — 11 ships of the line and frigates of Britain, France and Russia, with Codrington flying his flag in HMS Asia — sailed into the bay to confront the anchored Turkish-Egyptian fleet of Ibrahim Pasha. A shot fired at a British boat sparked a general engagement in which some 60 Ottoman-Egyptian vessels were destroyed with heavy loss of life, at a cost of no allied ships lost.

The victory effectively ended Ottoman sea power in Greece, but the Wellington government, fearing it had blundered into war, called it an "untoward event" and Codrington was recalled in 1828, though formally vindicated and showered with foreign honours, including Russia's Order of St George and France's Legion of Honour; Greece later awarded him the Order of the Redeemer and named a square in Pylos for him.

Later life

Codrington served as MP for Devonport (1832–39), promoted admiral, and held the Portsmouth command (1839–42), where he took an interest in the welfare of ordinary seamen. He died in London on 28 April 1851 and was buried at St Peter's Church, Drayton Beauchamp; a memorial stands in All Saints, Dodington. His son Henry John Codrington also became an admiral.
