The Sopwith Triplane was a British single-seat fighter aircraft designed and manufactured by the Sopwith Aviation Company during the First World War. It was the first military triplane to see operational service, and its outstanding manoeuvrability and climb shocked German opponents when it appeared over the Western Front in early 1917.

Design

Designed by Herbert Smith, the Triplane used the fuselage of the earlier Sopwith Pup fitted with three narrow-chord wings of equal span. The arrangement gave a large wing area in a compact span, yielding an excellent rate of climb and superb handling. Most aircraft were powered by a 130 hp Clerget 9B rotary engine and armed with a single synchronised Vickers gun.

Operational history

The Triplane entered service with the Royal Naval Air Service in late 1916. The all-Canadian "Black Flight" of No. 10 (Naval) Squadron, led by Raymond Collishaw, claimed 87 German aircraft between May and July 1917 flying Triplanes. During the Battle of Arras in "Bloody April" 1917 the type was one of the few Allied fighters able to out-manoeuvre German Albatros scouts.

Only 147 were built, and production problems and the arrival of the superior Sopwith Camel led to the Triplane's withdrawal by the end of 1917. Its impact was nonetheless considerable: a captured Triplane inspired the German Fokker Dr.I triplane made famous by the Red Baron.
