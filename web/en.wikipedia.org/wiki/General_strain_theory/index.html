General strain theory (GST) is a theory of criminology developed by Robert Agnew. First presented in 1992, it extends the classic strain theory of Robert K. Merton by broadening the sources of strain beyond the failure to achieve monetary success, and by emphasising the role of negative emotions — particularly anger — in motivating criminal coping.

Types of strain

Agnew identified three major categories of strain: (1) the failure to achieve positively valued goals, which now includes goals such as autonomy, status and respect, not merely money; (2) the removal (or threatened removal) of positively valued stimuli, such as the death of a loved one, divorce, or moving schools; and (3) the presentation of negatively valued stimuli, including abuse, victimization, discrimination, and adverse school or work conditions. Later revisions distinguished objective strain (events most people dislike) from subjective strain (events a particular individual dislikes), and vicarious and anticipated strain.

Mechanism

Strains generate negative emotions such as anger, frustration, depression and anxiety. Anger is especially criminogenic because it lowers inhibitions, creates a desire for revenge, and energises action. Whether an individual responds with crime depends on coping resources, social support, social control, beliefs, and associations with criminal peers. Agnew identified certain strains as most likely to lead to crime: those seen as high in magnitude, unjust, associated with low social control, and creating pressure or incentive for criminal coping — such as parental rejection, harsh discipline, homelessness, and criminal victimization.

Applications and reception

GST has been applied to explain delinquency, bullying victimization effects, terrorism, white-collar offending, and disparities in crime across gender and community contexts. Empirical tests generally find support for the links between major strains, anger, and offending, though effect sizes vary by strain type; the theory is regarded as one of the leading micro-level theories in contemporary criminology.
