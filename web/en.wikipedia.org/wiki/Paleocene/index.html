The Paleocene, or Palaeocene, is the first geological epoch of the Paleogene Period in the modern Cenozoic Era. It spans the time from 66 to 56 million years ago, beginning immediately after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event that ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs. The name, coined by Wilhelm Philipp Schimper in 1874, combines Greek palaios (old) and Eocene — the "old part of the Eocene".

Geology and climate

The Paleocene begins at the K–Pg boundary, marked worldwide by a thin clay layer enriched in iridium, evidence of the Chicxulub asteroid impact in what is now Mexico. The continents were still largely in late-Mesozoic positions: South America, Antarctica, and Australia had not fully separated; the Tethys Sea was closing as India moved north toward Asia; and extensive epicontinental seas covered parts of North America and Europe. Climate was generally warm, with a cooling tendency in the early Paleocene followed by warming; frost-free forests extended to high latitudes, and there were no permanent polar ice caps. The epoch ends with the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, an abrupt global warming event about 56 million years ago associated with a massive release of carbon.

Life

With the non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, and marine reptiles gone, ecosystems were rebuilt by the survivors. Mammals, previously mostly small, diversified explosively into emptied niches, though most Paleocene forms were archaic groups — multituberculates, condylarths, pantodonts, taeniodonts, and early primate-like plesiadapiforms — rather than members of modern orders, most of which appear later. Some terrestrial vertebrates reached notable size, including the giant flightless bird Gastornis in Europe and North America and the early snake Titanoboa, a 13-metre constrictor from the coal mines of Cerrejón, Colombia, a little after the epoch's start. Birds diversified rapidly, marine life recovered more slowly, and lush coal-forming forests spread across northern continents; the famous fossil deposits of the epoch include the Fort Union Formation of Montana and the Messel-like early recovery floras studied worldwide.

Subdivisions

The Paleocene is divided into three ages (stages): the Danian (66–61.6 Ma), the Selandian (61.6–59.2 Ma), and the Thanetian (59.2–56 Ma). In North American land-mammal terms it corresponds to the Puercan through Clarkforkian ages.
