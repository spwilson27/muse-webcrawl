Algebraic expression

In mathematics, an algebraic expression is an expression built up from integer constants, variables, and the algebraic operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and exponentiation by a rational number. For example, 3x² − 2xy + c is an algebraic expression.

Terminology

Algebra has its own vocabulary for describing parts of an expression. In the expression 3x² − 2xy + c, the numbers 3 and −2 are coefficients, x and y are variables, c is a constant, and the individual parts separated by + and − signs are called terms. The small raised 2 in x² is an exponent.

Conventions

By convention, letters at the beginning of the alphabet (a, b, c) usually denote constants, while letters toward the end (x, y, z) denote variables. Multiplication is often implied rather than written: 3x means 3 × x. Terms are ordered from highest to lowest power in a polynomial, and expressions can be manipulated using the commutative, associative and distributive laws.

Rational and irrational expressions

An algebraic expression that is the quotient of two polynomials is called a rational expression; one involving roots of variables, such as √(x + 3), is an irrational expression. Not every mathematical expression is algebraic: those involving transcendental functions such as sin x or 2ˣ are called transcendental expressions.

Expressions and equations

An expression alone does not state a truth: it represents a value that depends on the values assigned to its variables. Setting two expressions equal gives an equation; requiring one to be greater than another gives an inequality. Evaluating an expression means substituting numbers for the variables and computing the result.

History

The systematic manipulation of expressions developed from the rhetorical algebra of al-Khwarizmi through the syncopated notation of Diophantus to the fully symbolic algebra introduced by François Viète and René Descartes in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.
