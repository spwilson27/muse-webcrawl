Theodor W. Adorno (born Theodor Ludwig Wiesengrund; September 11, 1903 – August 6, 1969) was a German philosopher, sociologist, psychologist, musicologist, and composer known for his critical theory of society. He was a leading member of the Frankfurt School of critical theory, whose work came to influence the New Left and the intellectual climate of postwar Europe.

Life

Born in Frankfurt to a wine merchant father and a musically gifted mother, Adorno studied philosophy, sociology, psychology, and music, and composed under Alban Berg in Vienna. He joined the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, but the Nazi rise to power forced him into exile—first to Oxford, then to the United States, where he worked with Max Horkheimer in Los Angeles and on the Princeton Radio Research Project. He returned to Frankfurt in 1949, rebuilt the Institute, and became one of West Germany's most prominent—and controversial—public intellectuals.

Work

With Horkheimer he wrote Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944/1947), which argued that Enlightenment rationality had turned into a new mythology and coined the concept of the "culture industry"—the mass production of standardized cultural goods that reconciles people to domination. He was a principal author of The Authoritarian Personality (1950), a landmark study of prejudice. His later major works include Minima Moralia (1951), a book of aphorisms written in exile; Negative Dialectics (1966), his philosophical magnum opus, which insists on the primacy of the non-identical over conceptual systems; and the posthumous Aesthetic Theory (1970). A trained musician, he championed Schoenberg's twelve-tone music in Philosophy of New Music (1949) and wrote extensively on Mahler, Beethoven, and Wagner.

Legacy

Adorno's thought shaped critical theory, aesthetics, and the sociology of music. His dictum that to write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric remains one of the most debated statements in postwar culture.
