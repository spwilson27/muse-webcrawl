Charioteer of Delphi

The Charioteer of Delphi, also known as the Heniokhos ("reins-holder"), is one of the best-preserved examples of ancient Greek bronze sculpture. Cast about 478–474 BC, it formed part of a life-size victory monument at the Sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi, dedicated by Polyzalos, tyrant of Gela in Sicily, to commemorate a chariot victory in the Pythian Games.

Discovery

The statue was unearthed in 1896 by French excavators beneath the Sacred Way at Delphi, where it had been buried — probably by a rockfall or an earthquake — together with fragments of its chariot, four horses and a stable boy. Burial protected the bronze from the recycling that destroyed nearly all ancient bronze statuary.

Description

The charioteer stands upright in the long chiton of a racing driver, a belt high at the waist and straps across the shoulders to keep the garment from billowing. He holds the reins in his right hand; the left arm is lost. His head is turned slightly, with inlaid onyx eyes, silver eyelashes and a silver fillet inlaid with meanders around his hair. The figure belongs to the Severe style, transitional between Archaic stiffness and Classical naturalism, and is remarkable for the combination of serene restraint and finely observed detail in the feet, hands and drapery.

Interpretation

The monument celebrated Polyzalos's victory in the chariot race of 478 or 474 BC. The driver depicted is not the tyrant himself but a professional charioteer, presented in the moment after victory, calm and controlled at the height of triumph.
