Nous

Greekνοῦς (noûs)
TranslationsMind, intellect, intelligence, intuition
Key thinkersAnaxagoras, Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus
Related termsLogos, psyche, noesis, intellectus

Nous (Greek νοῦς, noûs), sometimes equated with intellect or intelligence, is a key concept in classical Greek philosophy referring to the mind's capacity for direct understanding — the faculty by which first principles and truths are grasped, as distinct from discursive reasoning (dianoia) or sense perception. In cosmology it also denotes a divine, ordering Mind.

Anaxagoras

The pre-Socratic philosopher Anaxagoras made Nous the ordering principle of the universe: an infinite, self-ruled, unmixed Mind that set the original mixture of all things rotating and thereby separated them into the ordered cosmos. Socrates, in Plato's Phaedo, recounts his excitement at the idea — and his disappointment that Anaxagoras used Mind only to start the motion and explained everything else mechanically.

Plato and Aristotle

For Plato, noesis is the highest form of cognition, the direct apprehension of the Forms. Aristotle gave nous its most influential treatment: in the De Anima he distinguishes a receptive (passive) intellect from an active intellect (nous poietikos) that "makes all things"; in the Nicomachean Ethics nous apprehends the indemonstrable first principles from which demonstration proceeds; and in the Metaphysics the prime mover is pure nous, "thought thinking itself". The identity of Aristotle's active intellect — divine, individual, or both — was debated for centuries by Alexander of Aphrodisias, Averroes, Aquinas and others.

Later developments

In Neoplatonism, Nous is the second hypostasis, emanating from the One and containing the intelligible world of Forms; through Plotinus it influenced Christian, Islamic and Jewish thought, where cosmic intellects mediated between God and the world. In modern British slang, unrelated scholarly usage, "nous" simply means common sense or practical intelligence.
