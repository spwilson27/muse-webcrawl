Seven heavenly virtues

The seven heavenly virtues are a traditional list in Christian moral teaching formed by combining the four cardinal virtues of classical antiquity — prudence, justice, temperance and fortitude — with the three theological virtues of faith, hope and charity named by St Paul in 1 Corinthians 13. Together they were held to describe the character of the Christian life, and in medieval catechesis they were commonly set against the seven deadly sins.

The list

  • Prudence — the ability to judge between actions with regard to appropriate conduct at a given time.
  • Justice — giving each person what is due to them.
  • Temperance — restraint and moderation in the exercise of one's desires.
  • Fortitude (courage) — endurance and the ability to confront fear and uncertainty.
  • Faith — belief in God and in the doctrines of the Church.
  • Hope — expectation of eternal life and trust in God's promises.
  • Charity (love) — selfless love of God and neighbour, regarded by Paul as the greatest of the virtues.

Origins

The cardinal virtues descend from Plato's Republic and were developed by Aristotle, the Stoics and later Ambrose, Augustine and Thomas Aquinas. The pairing of seven virtues against seven vices was popularised in works such as Prudentius's Psychomachia (c. 410), an allegory of the battle between virtues and vices for the human soul.
