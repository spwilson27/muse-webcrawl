Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is a type of breast cancer that begins in the milk-producing glands (lobules) of the breast and invades surrounding tissue. It is the second most common type of invasive breast cancer after invasive ductal carcinoma, accounting for roughly 10 to 15 percent of all invasive breast cancers. ILC most often affects women in their late 50s and early 60s.

Signs and symptoms

Unlike more common breast cancers, ILC frequently does not form a discrete lump. Instead, the cancer cells invade the breast tissue in single-file strands, producing subtle thickening, firmness or swelling, changes in skin texture, or a newly inverted nipple. Because of this growth pattern, ILC can be difficult to detect on physical examination and on mammography, and tumours are sometimes larger or present in multiple sites — and in both breasts — at the time of diagnosis.

Pathology

ILC is characterised by the loss of the cell-adhesion protein E-cadherin, usually through mutation of the CDH1 gene, which causes tumour cells to become discohesive and infiltrate stroma in single files ("Indian file" pattern) and concentric rings around ducts. The classic type A and B forms, and variants such as pleomorphic lobular carcinoma, are recognised. Most ILCs express oestrogen and progesterone receptors and lack HER2 amplification. Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) is a related non-invasive lesion often found adjacent to ILC.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by core-needle or surgical biopsy. Imaging with mammography has reduced sensitivity for ILC; ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging are often used to define the extent of disease and to check the opposite breast. ILC has a distinctive pattern of spread, with relatively higher rates of metastasis to the gastrointestinal tract, ovaries, peritoneum and leptomeninges compared with ductal cancers.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment follows the same principles as for other breast cancers: surgery (lumpectomy or mastectomy), radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and endocrine (hormone) therapy, the latter being particularly important given the high rate of hormone-receptor positivity. Long-term outcomes at five years are comparable to or slightly better than invasive ductal carcinoma, although ILC has a tendency toward late recurrence, so differences may narrow or reverse over longer follow-up.
