Portuguese India Armadas

The Portuguese India Armadas (Armadas da Índia) were the annual fleets dispatched by the Portuguese Crown from Lisbon to India, and back, from 1498 until the monopoly of the Crown's India trade was effectively dismantled in the 18th century. The fleets followed the Carreira da Índia ("India Run"), the sea route around the Cape of Good Hope opened by Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1497–1499.

Organisation

The India trade was a royal monopoly, organised in Lisbon by the Casa da Índia. An armada — typically between three and a dozen large ships, though early fleets could be larger — sailed from Lisbon around March to catch the monsoon winds of the Indian Ocean, aiming to reach India by September. The homeward fleet left India around January, riding the winter monsoon, and reached Lisbon in the summer. The timing was unforgiving: missing the monsoon meant months of delay, called invernada (wintering), usually at Mozambique Island.

Early fleets

The first armadas were world-changing events: Pedro Álvares Cabral's fleet of 1500, which reached Brazil on the outward swing before continuing to India; Vasco da Gama's great fourth armada of 1502; and the fleets of Francisco de Almeida and Afonso de Albuquerque, which built Portugal's maritime empire in the East — the Estado da Índia — with strongholds at Goa, Malacca, Hormuz and Mozambique.

Ships and dangers

The workhorse of the run was the nau, a large carrack of 400–2,000 tons, among the biggest ships in the world at the time. The voyage was perilous: shipwreck on the outward and homeward legs, scurvy, storms in the Cape of Good Hope region, and attacks by French, English and Dutch rivals later in the run's history. Famous losses such as the São João (1552) and São Bento (1554) on the South African coast produced a literature of shipwreck narratives, the História Trágico-Marítima.

Decline

From the late 16th century, the burden of defending the empire, corruption, and Dutch and English competition eroded the armadas' profitability. The system of annual Crown fleets gradually gave way to licensed private trade and chartered companies, but a form of the Carreira da Índia continued into the 18th century.
