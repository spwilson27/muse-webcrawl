De Haas–van Alphen effect

The de Haas–van Alphen effect, often abbreviated dHvA, is a quantum mechanical effect in which the magnetic susceptibility of a pure metal crystal oscillates as the intensity of the applied magnetic field is increased. It was discovered in 1930 by Wander Johannes de Haas and his student Pieter Martinus van Alphen at Leiden, in measurements on bismuth.

Explanation

The oscillations arise from the quantization of electron orbits in a magnetic field into Landau levels. As the field varies, the levels pass through the Fermi energy periodically in the inverse field, producing periodic changes in the free energy and hence in magnetization. The period of oscillation is directly related to the extremal cross-sectional areas of the material's Fermi surface.

Applications

The effect is a principal experimental tool for mapping Fermi surfaces of metals and intermetallic compounds, and it is closely related to the Shubnikov–de Haas effect in electrical resistance.
