Austria-Hungary, often referred to as the Austro-Hungarian Empire or the Dual Monarchy, was a multinational constitutional monarchy in Central Europe between 1867 and 1918. It was created by the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, which transformed the Austrian Empire into a real union of two states — the Austrian lands (Cisleithania) and the Kingdom of Hungary (Transleithania) — sharing a monarch, a common army, and joint foreign and finance ministries, while retaining separate parliaments and governments.

At its height Austria-Hungary was the second-largest country in Europe by area and the third most populous. Its more than 50 million inhabitants comprised over a dozen nationalities, and managing their competing aspirations shaped, and ultimately undermined, the state's politics.

History

The Compromise followed Austria's defeats by Prussia (1866) and was championed by Emperor Franz Joseph I and Hungarian statesman Ferenc Deák. The Dual Monarchy industrialised rapidly in its western half, built extensive railways, and saw Vienna flourish as a centre of modernism in art, music, and thought. In 1878 it occupied Bosnia and Herzegovina, annexing them in 1908 — a move that poisoned relations with Serbia and Russia.

The assassination of the heir, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, by Gavrilo Princip in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered the July Crisis; Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia and declaration of war on 28 July 1914 set off the First World War. Defeat in 1918 brought collapse: national councils proclaimed independent states, Charles I renounced participation in government in November 1918, and the treaties of Saint-Germain (1919) and Trianon (1920) ratified the empire's dissolution into Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and territories ceded to Poland, Romania, Italy, and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes.
