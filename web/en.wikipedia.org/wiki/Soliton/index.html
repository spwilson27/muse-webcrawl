Soliton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics and physics, a soliton is a self-reinforcing solitary wave packet that maintains its shape while it propagates at a constant velocity. Solitons arise in nonlinear dispersive media, where the tendency of a wave packet to spread (dispersion) is exactly balanced by nonlinear effects that steepen it. After colliding with other solitons, a soliton emerges largely unchanged except for a phase shift — a particle-like property that inspired the name.

History

The phenomenon was first described by the Scottish engineer John Scott Russell, who in 1834 observed a single smooth hump of water travelling for miles along the Union Canal near Edinburgh without changing form; he called it the "wave of translation". The behaviour was explained in 1895 by Diederik Korteweg and Gustav de Vries, whose Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation admits such solutions. Interest revived in 1965 when Norman Zabusky and Martin Kruskal, studying the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam problem numerically, found that KdV solitons survive collisions intact; they coined the term soliton by analogy with particles such as the proton. The inverse scattering transform, developed in 1967 by Gardner, Greene, Kruskal, and Miura, provided a general method for solving such equations.

Applications

Solitons appear in many physical settings. In optical fibres, pulses governed by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation can propagate over transoceanic distances with minimal distortion, and soliton concepts shaped long-haul fibre-optic communications. Related structures occur in shallow-water waves, plasmas, Josephson junctions, Bose–Einstein condensates, and models of elementary particles, proteins, and DNA. Topological solitons, such as kinks, skyrmions, and magnetic vortices, owe their stability to topology rather than to a balance of dispersion and nonlinearity.
