Ulster Scots dialect

Ulster Scots is a group of Scots dialects spoken in parts of Ulster in Ireland. It is generally considered a dialect of Scots, although some promote it as a distinct language. Its native speakers are typically called the Ulster Scots people, descendants of the Lowland Scots settlers who arrived during the Plantation of Ulster in the seventeenth century.

History

Scots speakers began arriving in Ulster in large numbers during the Plantation of Ulster in the early 1600s, settling mainly in counties Antrim, Down and northern Londonderry, and later in the Laggan district of east Donegal. Their speech developed in relative isolation, absorbing some vocabulary from Irish and from Hiberno-English while retaining archaic Scots forms that later disappeared in Scotland itself.

Status and recognition

The status of Ulster Scots has long been contested: linguists generally regard it as a dialect (or group of dialects) of Scots, whereas some cultural activists have argued for recognition as a separate language. Under the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, Ulster Scots was recognised as part of the cultural wealth of Northern Ireland, and the Ulster-Scots Agency (Tha Boord o Ulstèr-Scotch) was established to promote its study and use. It is recognised under Part II of the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages.

Features

Ulster Scots shares the characteristic features of Scots: the preservation of the velar fricative in words such as nicht (night) and loch; negative forms such as dinnae and cannae; and vocabulary including wee (small), bairn (child), aye (yes) and oxter (armpit). Donegal varieties show stronger Irish influence, while the speech of the Glens of Antrim differs from the more Scots-like varieties of the east coast.

Literature

The dialect has a literary tradition going back to the "weaver poets" of counties Antrim and Down, such as Samuel Thomson and James Orr, who wrote in Scots in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. A revival of interest in the late twentieth century produced new poetry, prose and translation work, including renderings of parts of the Bible.
