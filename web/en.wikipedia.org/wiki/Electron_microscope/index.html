Electron microscope
Invented1931, Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska
TypesTEM, SEM, STEM
ResolutionDown to ~0.05 nm (aberration-corrected)

An electron microscope is a microscope that uses a beam of electrons instead of light to image specimens. Because electrons have far shorter wavelengths than visible-light photons (de Broglie wavelength), electron microscopes achieve magnifications and resolutions orders of magnitude beyond optical microscopes, revealing ultrastructure at nanometre and even atomic scale.

Types

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) passes electrons through an ultrathin specimen. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) scans a focused beam across a surface and detects secondary/backscattered electrons for 3-D-like images. Scanning transmission (STEM) combines approaches. Cryo-EM of vitrified biomolecules revolutionised structural biology, winning the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

History and use

Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska built the first TEM in 1931; Ruska received the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics. Electron microscopes are essential in materials science, semiconductor inspection, virology, cell biology and forensics. Specimens usually require vacuum-compatible preparation, staining with heavy metals, or cryogenic freezing.
