A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corporation v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935) — popularly known as the "sick chicken case" — was a unanimous decision of the United States Supreme Court that invalidated central provisions of the National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA), a cornerstone of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal.

Background

The Schechter brothers operated a wholesale poultry business in Brooklyn, New York, buying chickens from out of state and selling them to local retailers. They were prosecuted for violating the Live Poultry Code promulgated under the NIRA, including rules on maximum hours, minimum wages, and the sale of diseased chickens — one unfit bird giving the case its nickname.

Holding

Writing for a unanimous Court, Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes held on two grounds: first, that Congress had unconstitutionally delegated legislative power to the President and to industry code authorities without adequate standards (the nondelegation doctrine); and second, that the commerce Schechter conducted was local rather than interstate, its effects on interstate commerce too indirect for federal regulation once the chickens had "come to rest" in New York.

Significance

Decided on "Black Monday" (27 May 1935) alongside two other anti-New Deal rulings, the decision effectively ended the industrial codes programme. It remains one of the only cases in which the Supreme Court has struck down a federal statute on nondelegation grounds, and is frequently cited in modern debates over the administrative state.
