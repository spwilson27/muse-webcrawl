Fog of war
The uncertainty in situational awareness experienced by participants in military operations.

The fog of war (German: Nebel des Krieges) is the uncertainty in situational awareness experienced by participants in military operations. The term seeks to capture the uncertainty regarding one's own capability, adversary capability, and adversary intent during an engagement, operation, or campaign. Military forces try to reduce the fog of war through military intelligence and friendly force tracking systems.

Origin

The concept derives from the Prussian military analyst Carl von Clausewitz, who wrote in his book Vom Kriege (1832, published in English as On War):

"War is the realm of uncertainty; three quarters of the factors on which action in war is based are wrapped in a fog of greater or lesser uncertainty. A sensitive and discriminating judgment is called for; a skilled intelligence to scent out the truth."

Clausewitz paired fog with "friction" — the accumulation of chance, error, physical exertion, and unforeseen difficulties that makes even simple operations hard to execute. Later German doctrine also invoked the term Friktion. The phrase "fog of war" itself does not appear verbatim in Clausewitz but became the standard shorthand for his argument in English-language military writing.

Levels and modern usage

Analysts distinguish fog at the tactical level (not knowing enemy positions in an engagement), the operational level (the disposition of forces across a theatre), and the strategic and political level (misjudging intentions, alliances, and the consequences of action). Friendly fire incidents, such as the 1994 shootdown of two U.S. Black Hawk helicopters over Iraq, are commonly attributed in part to the fog of war.

Modern technologies — satellites, reconnaissance aircraft and drones, radar, signals intelligence, GPS-based blue force tracking, and networked command systems — are often presented as means to "lift" the fog, an ambition associated with the 1990s concept of a revolution in military affairs. Critics argue that information overload, deception, electronic warfare, and the inherent ambiguity of data mean the fog can thicken as well as thin; the related notion of "friction" remains in doctrine. The 2003 documentary The Fog of War, featuring former U.S. Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara, carried the term to a wide public audience.

In simulation and gaming

"Fog of war" is also the name of a game mechanic in strategy video games and tabletop wargames, in which parts of the map are hidden from the player until explored by their units, with explored-but-unobserved areas shown in a dimmed state. Popularized by titles such as Warcraft and StarCraft, the mechanic simulates reconnaissance and uncertainty. Relatedly, chess variants such as Kriegspiel hide the opponent's pieces entirely.
