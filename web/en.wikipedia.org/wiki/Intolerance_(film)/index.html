Intolerance (film)

Directed by: D. W. Griffith
Released: 1916
Running time: ~197 minutes (original cut longer)
Country: United States

Intolerance is a 1916 American anthology silent film directed by D. W. Griffith. Regarded as one of the most influential films of the silent era, it interweaves four parallel storylines, each separated by centuries, illustrating humanity's recurring intolerance: the fall of ancient Babylon, the Passion of Jesus, the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre in 16th-century France, and a modern American melodrama of strikers and moral reformers.

Production

Griffith conceived the film partly in response to criticism of his earlier The Birth of a Nation (1915). Intolerance was among the most expensive films of its time, with enormous sets — notably the Babylonian court, with walls reportedly 300 feet high — and thousands of extras. Its complex cross-cutting between eras was unprecedented and initially confused audiences.

Reception and legacy

The film was a commercial disappointment on release but came to be celebrated by critics and filmmakers; Soviet directors such as Sergei Eisenstein studied its montage. Griffith later re-edited two of the stories into separate features, The Fall of Babylon and The Mother and the Law. In 1989, Intolerance was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry.
