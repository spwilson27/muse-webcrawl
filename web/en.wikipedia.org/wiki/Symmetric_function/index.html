Symmetric function

In mathematics, a symmetric function of n variables is one whose value is unchanged by any permutation of its variables: it is invariant under the action of the symmetric group. Symmetric polynomials — such as x + y and xy for two variables — form an algebra, and their study is fundamental in algebra, algebraic combinatorics, and representation theory. The notion extends to the ring of symmetric functions in infinitely many variables, which is central to the theory of Schur functions.

Symmetric polynomials

The elementary symmetric polynomials e1, …, en in n variables are the sums of products of distinct variables taken k at a time; by Vieta's formulas they are (up to sign) the coefficients of a polynomial expressed through its roots. The fundamental theorem states that every symmetric polynomial can be written uniquely as a polynomial in the elementary symmetric polynomials. Other important bases include the power sum symmetric polynomials pk = x1k + … + xnk and the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials hk; Newton's identities relate the three families.

Ring of symmetric functions

Passing to infinitely many variables and bounding the degree yields the ring Λ of symmetric functions, in which the Schur functions sλ, indexed by partitions λ, form a distinguished basis. Schur functions are the characters of irreducible polynomial representations of the general linear group and of the symmetric group, and satisfy the Littlewood–Richardson rule for their products. The Hall inner product makes Λ a self-dual graded Hopf algebra, linking combinatorics, geometry of Grassmannians, and representation theory.

Applications

Symmetric functions appear in the theory of equations (discriminants, Galois theory), enumerative combinatorics (plane partitions, Young tableaux), and mathematical physics. Generalizations include quasisymmetric functions, Macdonald polynomials, and Jack polynomials.
