Roadkill

Roadkill refers to animals that are killed on roads by vehicles. Wildlife-vehicle collisions are a significant cause of mortality for many species, a threat to biodiversity, a source of damage and injury to motorists, and a subject of study in road ecology.

Scale of the problem

Estimates of roadkill vary widely by country and method. In the United States, hundreds of millions of birds and tens of millions of mammals are estimated to be killed by vehicles each year, in addition to enormous numbers of reptiles, amphibians, and insects. Collisions with large animals such as deer cause substantial human injury and death: deer-vehicle collisions in the United States number over a million annually and result in hundreds of human fatalities and billions of dollars in damage.

Certain species are especially vulnerable. Slow-moving animals such as turtles and hedgehogs, animals that freeze in headlights, migrating amphibians crossing roads to reach breeding ponds, and scavengers feeding on carrion already on the road are all at high risk. For some rare species — including certain populations of tortoises, big cats, and otters — road mortality can threaten population viability.

Causes

Roads fragment habitats, so animals must cross them to reach food, water, mates, and seasonal ranges. Factors increasing risk include high traffic volume and speed, roads running along habitat edges or migration routes, roadside vegetation that attracts animals, and salt or grain spills that draw wildlife to the road surface. Night-time collisions are common, particularly in autumn during deer mating season.

Mitigation

Measures to reduce roadkill include wildlife crossings — overpasses and underpasses designed for animals, often combined with fencing that funnels animals toward them. Famous examples include the Banff National Park crossings in Canada, where dozens of structures help maintain connectivity for bears, wolves, and ungulates. Other measures include warning signs, reduced speed limits in hotspots, animal detection systems that alert drivers, roadside reflectors, amphibian tunnels, and culverts adapted for fish and small fauna. Long-term monitoring with citizen-science reporting apps helps identify collision hotspots where mitigation will be most effective.

Ecological effects

Beyond direct mortality, roads create barrier effects, isolating populations and reducing gene flow. Roadkill carcasses subsidise scavenger populations such as crows, raccoons, and vultures, which in turn may increase predation pressure on other species. Studies of roadkill also serve as valuable data sources on species distribution, disease, and population trends.

In some jurisdictions, road-killed game animals may legally be salvaged for food, and roadkill cuisine has a fringe cultural presence, but consumption carries health risks from contamination and spoilage.
