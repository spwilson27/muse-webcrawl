Sacred Scripture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sacred Scripture, in Catholic theology, is the Bible: the written Word of God, comprising the Old and New Testaments, believed to be inspired by the Holy Spirit and to teach without error the truths necessary for salvation. Together with Sacred Tradition, it forms the single sacred deposit of the Word of God entrusted to the Church.

Canon

The Catholic canon of Scripture contains 73 books: 46 in the Old Testament — including the deuterocanonical books such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom and the Maccabees — and 27 in the New Testament. The canon was affirmed by the Council of Trent in 1546 in response to the Protestant Reformers, who recognised a shorter Old Testament canon. The Latin Vulgate of Jerome was long the Church's official translation; modern Catholic Bibles are translated from the original Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek.

Interpretation

The Second Vatican Council's constitution Dei Verbum (1965) teaches that Scripture must be read in light of the tradition of the Church, the analogy of faith, and attention to literary forms and historical context, and that the Magisterium serves as its authoritative interpreter. The Pontifical Biblical Commission and modern Catholic scholarship apply historical-critical methods alongside spiritual senses of interpretation — literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical.
