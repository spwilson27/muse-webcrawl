Neuropsychopharmacology

Neuropsychopharmacology is an interdisciplinary science related to psychopharmacology (the study of how drugs affect the mind) and fundamental neuroscience. It involves the study of the mechanisms by which drugs act on the nervous system and behaviour, and the use of that understanding to develop treatments for psychiatric and neurological disorders such as schizophrenia, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder and addiction.

History

The field emerged in the 1950s with a series of largely serendipitous discoveries: the antipsychotic effect of chlorpromazine (1952), the antidepressant effects of the monoamine oxidase inhibitor iproniazid and the tricyclic imipramine, and the use of lithium for mania. These drugs gave rise to the first biological hypotheses of mental illness — most famously the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia and the monoamine hypothesis of depression — and prompted systematic laboratory study of neurotransmitter systems. The founding of the American College of Neuropsychopharmacology in 1961 is often taken as a marker of the field's institutional beginning. Later decades added selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, atypical antipsychotics, and a shift from simple neurotransmitter-deficit models toward circuit-level and neuroplasticity-based accounts.

Research methods

Neuropsychopharmacologists study drug action at levels from molecular pharmacology (receptor binding, second messengers, gene expression) to systems neuroscience and behaviour. Techniques include animal models of psychiatric conditions, neuroimaging in humans (PET occupancy studies, pharmacological fMRI), genetics and, increasingly, computational modelling. A central theme is how psychoactive compounds — whether therapeutic drugs, anaesthetics or drugs of abuse — alter synaptic transmission in dopamine, serotonin, glutamate, GABA and other neurotransmitter systems.

Major drug classes studied

  • Antipsychotics — dopamine D2 receptor antagonists and partial agonists.
  • Antidepressants — MAO inhibitors, tricyclics, SSRIs, SNRIs and newer agents such as ketamine/esketamine.
  • Anxiolytics and hypnotics — benzodiazepines and related GABAergic drugs.
  • Mood stabilisers — lithium and anticonvulsants.
  • Psychostimulants — used in ADHD and narcolepsy, and studied as drugs of abuse.
  • Psychedelics — a renewed research area for depression and PTSD.
