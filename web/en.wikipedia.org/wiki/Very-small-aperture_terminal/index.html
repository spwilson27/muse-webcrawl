Very-small-aperture terminal

A very-small-aperture terminal (VSAT) is a two-way satellite ground station with a dish antenna that is smaller than 3.8 metres. VSATs access satellites in geosynchronous orbit or geostationary orbit to relay data from small remote earth stations (terminals) to other terminals (in mesh topology) or master earth station "hubs" (in star topology).

History

VSAT networks grew rapidly in the 1980s as Ku-band satellites allowed small dishes to receive sufficient signal. Early large networks connected retail chains, petrol stations and bank branches for transaction and inventory data; the technology later spread to rural telephony, distance learning, maritime and aeronautical communications, and consumer satellite internet.

Operation

A typical VSAT terminal consists of an outdoor unit (dish, feed, and block upconverter and low-noise block downconverter) and an indoor modem. In a star network, remote terminals communicate only through a central hub, requiring two satellite hops for terminal-to-terminal traffic; mesh networks allow single-hop connections. Data rates range from a few kbit/s for narrowband applications to tens of Mbit/s for broadband services. The high propagation delay of geostationary links (about 250 ms one way) is a characteristic limitation.
