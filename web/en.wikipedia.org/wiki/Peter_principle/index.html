Peter principle

Formulated by: Laurence J. Peter

Source: The Peter Principle (1969), with Raymond Hull

Statement: "Employees rise to the level of their incompetence."

The Peter principle is a management concept developed by Laurence J. Peter and published in his 1969 book The Peter Principle: Why Things Always Go Wrong, written with Raymond Hull. It states that people in a hierarchy tend to rise to "a level of respective incompetence": employees are promoted based on their success in previous jobs until they reach a level at which they are no longer competent, because skills in one job do not necessarily translate to success in the next.

Reasoning

Peter argued that in a hierarchy, competent employees are rewarded with promotion; promotion continues until the employee reaches a position whose demands exceed their abilities. Since such an employee is no longer promoted — but rarely demoted — the hierarchy gradually fills with people working at their level of incompetence, and the actual work is done by those not yet promoted beyond their competence. A corollary holds that "in time, every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties."

Research and influence

Though written partly as satire, the principle anticipated serious research on promotion practices. A 2018 study of sales workers by Benson, Li and Shue found that firms consistently promoted the best salespeople into management even though sales performance negatively predicted managerial value added — empirical support for the Peter principle. Related ideas include the Dilbert principle, in which incompetent workers are deliberately promoted to management to limit the damage they can do.
