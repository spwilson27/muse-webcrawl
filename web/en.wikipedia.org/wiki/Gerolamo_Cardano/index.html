Gerolamo Cardano

Born: 24 September 1501, Pavia, Duchy of Milan
Died: 21 September 1576, Rome
Fields: Mathematics, medicine, philosophy, astrology
Known for: Ars Magna; solution of cubic and quartic equations; Liber de Ludo Aleae

Gerolamo Cardano (also Girolamo or Geronimo; French: Jérôme Cardan; Latin: Hieronymus Cardanus; 24 September 1501 – 21 September 1576) was an Italian polymath of the Renaissance: physician, mathematician, philosopher, astrologer, inventor, and gambler. He wrote more than 200 works and is regarded as one of the most influential mathematicians of his era.

Ars Magna and algebra

His book Ars Magna (1545) was the first Latin treatise devoted to algebra and gave the first published solutions of the cubic and quartic equations — the cubic obtained from Tartaglia under an oath of secrecy, and the quartic discovered by Cardano's student Lodovico Ferrari. Publishing them sparked a bitter priority dispute. In solving cubics Cardano wrestled with square roots of negative numbers, an early encounter with what became complex numbers.

Probability and other work

Cardano's Liber de Ludo Aleae ("Book on Games of Chance," written c. 1564, published posthumously), drawn from his lifelong gambling, contains the first systematic treatment of probability, including the concepts of equally likely outcomes and the power of chance. As a physician he was among the most celebrated in Europe, treating cardinals and princes; he described typhus fever clinically and wrote on medicine, mechanics (the Cardan joint and Cardan grille bear his name), and natural philosophy.

Life

The illegitimate son of the Milanese jurist Fazio Cardano, he studied at Pavia and Padua, endured poverty and scandal, lost one son to execution and another to dissipation, and in 1570 was arrested by the Inquisition — he had cast a horoscope of Jesus — spending months in prison. He spent his final years in Rome on a papal pension, and his autobiography De propria vita remains a classic of the genre.
