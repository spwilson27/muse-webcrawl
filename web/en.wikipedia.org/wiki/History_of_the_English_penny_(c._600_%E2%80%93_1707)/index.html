History of the English penny (c. 600 – 1707)

The history of the English penny from about 600 to 1707 covers the coin that formed the backbone of English currency for over a millennium. Introduced in Anglo-Saxon England, inspired by the Frankish denier, the silver penny (denarius, abbreviated "d.") was for centuries virtually the only coin struck.

Anglo-Saxon and Norman coinage

Early small thick coins known as sceattas gave way around 760 to the broad flan penny of Offa of Mercia. After the Norman Conquest, William I continued the penny, and periodic recoinages maintained its weight and fineness; 240 pennies made one pound sterling.

Late medieval developments

Edward I's recoinage of 1279 introduced the long-cross penny to deter clipping. Larger denominations such as the groat (4d) joined the penny from the 14th century, but the penny remained fundamental; Henry VII issued the first testoon (shilling).

Tudor debasement to 1707

Henry VIII's Great Debasement reduced the silver content drastically; Elizabeth I restored the coinage in 1560–61. Milled coinage replaced hammered coins after 1662. The story ends with the Acts of Union 1707, which created the British penny. See also Penny (English coin).
