Clovis points are the characteristically shaped projectile points associated with the Clovis culture of Paleo-Americans in North America. They are named after the city of Clovis, New Mexico, near where the first examples were found in 1929 in association with the bones of extinct Pleistocene animals such as mammoths and bison.

Description

Clovis points are lanceolate (leaf-shaped) bifaces, typically 4 to 20 centimetres long, made by pressure flaking and percussion flaking. Their most diagnostic feature is the flute: a shallow channel flake removed from the base up each face, usually extending one-quarter to one-half the length of the point. The flute is thought to have aided hafting onto a wooden spear shaft or foreshaft. The basal edges are often ground smooth, presumably to prevent the binding from being cut.

Distribution and dating

Points of the Clovis style have been found across nearly all of North America, from Alaska to Mexico, and are generally dated to roughly 13,050 to 12,750 calibrated years before present, a span of only a few centuries. Their wide and rapid distribution has been taken as evidence of a fast-moving colonizing population, though later research emphasizes regional variation and the possibility of earlier human presence on the continent.

Function

Clovis points were primarily spear points, used with thrusting spears or possibly the spear-thrower. At kill sites they are found with the remains of mammoths, mastodons, giant bison, and other now-extinct megafauna, indicating that Clovis hunters hunted big game, though they also exploited smaller animals and plant foods.
