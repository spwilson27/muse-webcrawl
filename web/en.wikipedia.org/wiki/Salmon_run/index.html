Salmon run

The salmon run is the time when salmon, which have migrated from the ocean, swim to the upper reaches of rivers where they spawn on gravel beds. After spawning, most Pacific salmon die, and the salmon life cycle starts over again with the new generation of juvenile salmon.

Life cycle

Salmon eggs are laid in freshwater streams, typically at high latitudes or in cool headwaters. The eggs hatch into alevins, develop into fry and then smolts, and migrate downstream to the ocean, where they spend one to several years feeding and growing. Adults then return to their natal streams to spawn—a feat of navigation thought to rely on the Earth's magnetic field and on the salmon's acute sense of smell, which homes in on the unique chemistry of the home stream.

The run

During the run, salmon stop feeding and their bodies change: males of several species develop hooked jaws (kypes) and humped backs, and their silver ocean colouration gives way to red, green, or dark spawning colours. They leap waterfalls and rapids and are preyed upon by bears, eagles, and other animals. The journey may cover hundreds or even thousands of kilometres; Yukon River Chinook salmon travel over 3,000 km.

Ecological importance

The carcasses of spawned salmon are a major seasonal food source for wildlife and transfer marine-derived nutrients—nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon—into freshwater and riparian ecosystems, fertilizing forests far from the sea. The runs also support commercial, recreational, and Indigenous fisheries, and are the basis of major fishing economies in Alaska, British Columbia, and the Russian Far East. See salmon as food.

Threats

Many runs have declined because of dams blocking migration, habitat loss, overfishing, hatchery effects, and warming waters. Conservation measures include dam removal, fish ladders, and fishing quotas.
