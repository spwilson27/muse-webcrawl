Television comedy

Television comedy is a category of broadcasting that comprises television programmes produced to amuse their audiences, principally through humour. Television comedy developed from radio comedy and vaudeville traditions and became one of the most popular forms of programming in the medium's history.

Formats

Situation comedy

The situation comedy or sitcom is the most durable television comedy format: a fixed cast of characters in a recurring setting — a family home, a workplace — whose lives generate self-contained comic episodes. Early examples such as I Love Lucy (1951–57) established the multi-camera studio format with a live audience; later landmarks include Cheers, Seinfeld and Friends. Single-camera sitcoms shot on location, without laugh tracks, became prominent in the 2000s with shows like The Office and 30 Rock, the former also popularizing the mockumentary style.

Sketch comedy

Sketch comedy consists of short comic scenes performed by an ensemble. Landmarks include Monty Python's Flying Circus in Britain and Saturday Night Live in the United States, on air since 1975.

Other formats

Other comedy forms include stand-up comedy specials, panel shows and game shows, improvisational comedy, comedy news programmes such as The Daily Show, dramedy, and animated comedy, from The Simpsons — the longest-running American scripted primetime series — onward.

History

Many early television comedies were adaptations of radio successes. The genre's conventions — the half-hour slot, the episodic reset, the syndication-friendly episode order — were shaped largely by American network television, while British comedy developed shorter series runs with greater creative control by writer-performers.
