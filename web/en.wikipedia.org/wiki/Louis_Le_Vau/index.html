Louis Le Vau

Louis Le Vau (1612 – 11 October 1670) was a French Baroque architect, who worked for King Louis XIV of France. One of the founding figures of French classical architecture, he is best known for his work at Vaux-le-Vicomte and for designing the expansion of the Palace of Versailles that set the model for the great royal palace.

Early career

Le Vau was born in Paris into a family of stonemasons and builders. He rose quickly, becoming a successful designer of aristocratic town houses (hôtels particuliers) on the Île Saint-Louis, including the Hôtel Lambert, built for the financier Jean-Baptiste Lambert in the 1640s. His Parisian hôtels made his reputation as the most fashionable architect of the day.

Vaux-le-Vicomte

For the finance minister Nicolas Fouquet, Le Vau designed the château of Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661), working with the painter Charles Le Brun and the landscape gardener André Le Nôtre. The ensemble — château, interiors and formal gardens conceived as a single design — was a landmark in French architecture. Its splendour provoked the jealousy of the young Louis XIV, who arrested Fouquet and took the whole design team into royal service.

Royal works

In royal service Le Vau rebuilt the Château de Vincennes, completed the Louvre's south wing and the Tuileries, and designed the Collège des Quatre-Nations in Paris, now the seat of the Institut de France. At Versailles he wrapped Louis XIII's hunting lodge in a new stone "envelope" (1668–1670) with terraces facing the gardens, creating the basic disposition of the palace that Jules Hardouin-Mansart later expanded. He also designed the Versailles menagerie and orangery. Le Vau died in Paris in 1670, at the height of his career.

Style and legacy

Le Vau's style combined Italian Baroque fluency with French restraint, favouring clear massing, domed central pavilions and oval salons. Together with François Mansart he defined the first phase of French Classicism, and his trio of collaborations with Le Brun and Le Nôtre established the model of the integrated palace-and-garden composition emulated across Europe.
