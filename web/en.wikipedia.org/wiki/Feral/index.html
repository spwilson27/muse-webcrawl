Feral

A feral animal is one that lives in a wild state but is descended from domesticated individuals—having escaped from captivity or domestication, or been abandoned, and established a self-sustaining wild population. The term is most often applied to animals such as feral cats, dogs, pigs, horses, and goats. It is distinct from a truly wild animal, whose ancestors were never domesticated, and from a stray, which lives wild individually but may still be socialized or dependent on humans.

Examples

Famous feral populations include the mustangs of North America (descended from horses brought by Spanish colonists), Australia's feral camels, donkeys, water buffalo, and the dingo (an ancient feral dog lineage), feral pigs (including razorbacks) in the Americas and Australasia, and the feral cats found worldwide. Feral pigeons in cities descend from domestic rock doves.

Effects

Feral animals can have major ecological impacts, particularly on islands and in Australia, where native species evolved without mammalian predators or competitors. Feral cats are implicated in declines and extinctions of birds, mammals, and reptiles; feral pigs damage soils, crops, and water catchments, and spread disease. Conversely, some feral populations are valued—mustangs are protected under U.S. law, and feral livestock breeds such as the Soay sheep are conserved as genetic resources. On uninhabited islands, eradication programs have removed feral goats, cats, and rats to restore ecosystems (for example on the Galápagos Islands).

Feral children

The term is also applied, controversially, to alleged "feral children"—children said to have lived in isolation from human contact—but documented cases are rare and many historical accounts are considered unreliable or involve children with prior abuse or developmental disorders.
