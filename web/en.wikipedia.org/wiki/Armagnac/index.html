Armagnac

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Armagnac is a distinctive type of brandy produced in the Armagnac region of Gascony, southwestern France. It is distilled from wine, usually made from a blend of grapes including Ugni blanc, Baco blanc, Folle blanche, and Colombard, and matured in oak barrels. Armagnac was one of the first areas in France to begin distilling spirits — local records date the practice to 1411 — making it older than Cognac, though it remains far less widely known commercially.

Production

Unlike Cognac's double pot distillation, Armagnac is typically distilled once in a continuous column still, the alambic armagnacais, producing a spirit of about 52–60% alcohol that retains pronounced flavour compounds. It is then aged in barrels of local Monlezun black oak (and Limousin oak), softening over years or decades. Producers traditionally sell vintage-dated Armagnacs from a single harvest year, unusual among brandies.

Appellation

The AOC Armagnac area, formalised in 1936, comprises three districts in the Gers, Landes, and Lot-et-Garonne departments: Bas-Armagnac (reputed finest, sandy soils), Armagnac-Ténarèze, and Haut-Armagnac. Age statements follow rules similar to Cognac's (VS, VSOP, XO, hors d'âge), with minimum ages rising by category. The name derives from the historic county of Armagnac, whose counts were major figures of medieval Gascon politics — unrelated to the Irish city of Armagh despite the similar spelling.
