CTLA-4, or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4, also known as CD152, is a protein receptor that functions as an immune checkpoint and downregulates immune responses. It is found on the surface of T cells and acts as an "off switch" when bound to CD80 or CD86 on antigen-presenting cells.

Function

CTLA-4 is homologous to the T-cell costimulatory protein CD28, and both molecules bind to CD80 and CD86. CTLA-4 binds CD80 and CD86 with greater affinity and avidity than CD28, outcompeting CD28 for its ligands and transmitting an inhibitory signal to the T cell. It is expressed by activated T cells and is constitutively expressed by regulatory T cells, where it is essential for their suppressive function. Much of the surface-expressed CTLA-4 is rapidly internalised, so the protein is largely intracellular at any moment; a key mechanism of action is trans-endocytosis, in which CTLA-4 captures and removes CD80/CD86 from antigen-presenting cells.

History and clinical significance

CTLA-4 was identified in 1987 by Pierre Golstein and colleagues. James Allison's laboratory showed in the mid-1990s that blocking CTLA-4 could unleash anti-tumour immunity in mice, a discovery that led to the first checkpoint inhibitor drug, the monoclonal antibody ipilimumab, approved for melanoma in 2011. Allison shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

Conversely, CTLA-4–Ig fusion proteins such as abatacept mimic CTLA-4's inhibition and are used to suppress immunity in rheumatoid arthritis and transplant rejection. Genetic variation in CTLA4 is associated with autoimmune diseases including type 1 diabetes, Graves' disease and Hashimoto's thyroiditis; inherited CTLA-4 haploinsufficiency causes a severe immune dysregulation syndrome.
