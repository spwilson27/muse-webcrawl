Doktor Faustus. The Life of the German Composer Adrian Leverkühn, Told by a Friend (German: Doktor Faustus. Das Leben des deutschen Tonsetzers Adrian Leverkühn, erzählt von einem Freunde) is a novel by the German author Thomas Mann, published in 1947. The novel is a reworking of the Faust legend set in Germany in the first half of the 20th century, paralleling the career of its fictional protagonist — the composer Adrian Leverkühn — with the catastrophic course of German history under Nazism.

Plot

The story is narrated by Serenus Zeitblom, a humanist scholar and childhood friend of Leverkühn, who writes the biography between 1943 and 1945 as Germany collapses in war. Leverkühn, a brilliant but cold and isolated composer, deliberately contracts syphilis in the hope that the disease will heighten his creative powers, and in a fevered dialogue with the Devil (or a hallucination of him) he seals a pact: twenty-four years of genius in exchange for renouncing love. His music grows ever more radical — Mann drew on the twelve-tone technique of Arnold Schoenberg, working from musical advice supplied by Theodor W. Adorno — until Leverkühn breaks down in 1940, confessing his pact before a gathering of friends, and lapses into mental illness until his death ten years later.

Themes

The novel is widely read as an allegory of Germany's "pact with the devil" under National Socialism: the bargain of a cultured, inward, musical nation that exchanges its soul for power, and of the fateful alliance between aesthetic radicalism and political catastrophe. Mann later documented the novel's genesis in The Story of a Novel (1949). Schoenberg publicly objected to his compositional method being attributed to the fictional, syphilitic Leverkühn, prompting Mann to add an acknowledgement of Schoenberg's intellectual property in later editions.

Reception

Doktor Faustus is generally regarded as one of Mann's greatest works and one of the major German novels of the 20th century. It was adapted into a 1982 film directed by Franz Seitz.
