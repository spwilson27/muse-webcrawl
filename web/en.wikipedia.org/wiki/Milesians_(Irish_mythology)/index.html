Milesians (Irish mythology)

The Milesians or sons of Míl Espáine are a legendary people in Irish mythology. In the Lebor Gabála Érenn ("The Book of the Taking of Ireland"), a medieval pseudohistory, they are the final wave of settlers to invade Ireland, representing the ancestors of the modern Irish (the Gaels). They sail from Iberia and defeat the Tuatha Dé Danann, the supernatural race then ruling Ireland.

The legend

According to the Lebor Gabála, the Milesians descend from Míl Espáine (Míl of Spain), whose ancestor Gael Glas is linked through a genealogical line to Noah and to the Tower of Babel. Míl's uncle Íth, having sighted Ireland from a tower in Brigantia (in Galicia), sailed there and was killed by the Tuatha Dé Danann kings. His kin then launched a full invasion led by Míl's sons, among them Éremón, Éber Finn, Ír, and the poet and judge Amergin Glúingel.

On landing, the Milesians met the three goddesses of the land — Banba, Fódla, and Ériu — each of whom asked that the island bear her name; Ériu's name became Ireland's. At Tara, Amergin negotiated with the Tuatha Dé Danann, who asked for a three-day truce during which the Milesians must withdraw beyond the "ninth wave" from shore. When the Milesians returned, the Tuatha Dé raised a magical storm, but Amergin's poetic invocation calmed the sea, and the Milesians landed and defeated them at the battles of Sliabh Mis and Tailtiu.

Afterward, Éremón and Éber divided Ireland between them — north and south — and became its first Milesian kings. The Tuatha Dé Danann retreated into the sídhe mounds and became the fairy folk of later folklore. The agreement between the peoples is sometimes said to have been mediated by Amergin, who awarded the surface of Ireland to the Milesians and the underground to the Tuatha Dé.

Historicity

Modern scholars regard the Milesian invasion as a literary invention of medieval Irish monks, designed to give the Irish a biblical-style origin history connecting them to classical and scriptural genealogies. The scheme nonetheless influenced Irish identity and historiography for centuries, and the "sons of Míl" remain a byword for the Gaelic Irish in poetry and tradition.
