Cot–caught merger

Distribution of the cot–caught merger across North American English dialects.

The cot–caught merger, also known as the LOT–THOUGHT merger, is a phonemic merger in some varieties of English in which the vowels of words like cot and caught — historically /ɒ/ and /ɔː/ — are pronounced the same, so that pairs such as cot/caught, Don/dawn, stock/stalk and knotty/naughty become homophones.

Distribution

The merger is complete or well advanced in Canada (where it is a defining feature of Canadian English), in the Western United States, in parts of New England, in Pittsburgh and much of the Inland North's fringe, and in Scotland. It is absent from most of the Southern and Eastern United States, where the two vowels remain distinct; in the New York City area the THOUGHT vowel is characteristically raised. The merger has been spreading through the United States during the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, especially among younger speakers.

Form of the merged vowel

The quality of the merged vowel varies by region: it may be an unrounded [ɑ] (as in much of the West and Canada), a rounded [ɒ], or something between [ɔ] and [ɑ]. Where the merged vowel is unrounded, the merger is typically accompanied by the father–bother merger, producing a single low back vowel phoneme.

Effects

Because the merger neutralises a contrast, speakers of merged dialects often cannot hear the distinction made by unmerged speakers, and spelling pronunciations or hypercorrections may occur. The merger is a classic case study in sociolinguistics and dialectology, prominently documented by William Labov and colleagues in the Atlas of North American English.
