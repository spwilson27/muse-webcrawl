The beta sheet (β-sheet, also beta pleated sheet) is a common motif of the regular protein secondary structure. Beta sheets consist of beta strands (β-strands) connected laterally by at least two or three backbone hydrogen bonds, forming a generally twisted, pleated sheet. A beta strand is a stretch of polypeptide chain typically 3 to 10 amino acids long with backbone in an extended conformation.

History

The first beta-sheet structure was proposed by William Astbury in the 1930s, based on X-ray fibre diffraction of keratin. The modern definition of the beta sheet, with its characteristic pleating and hydrogen-bonding pattern, was developed by Linus Pauling and Robert Corey in 1951, the same work that introduced the alpha helix.

Structure

In a beta sheet, the polypeptide backbone is extended rather than coiled, and adjacent strands are held together by hydrogen bonds between backbone amide and carbonyl groups. The pleating arises from the tetrahedral geometry of the alpha carbon atoms, which causes successive side chains to point alternately above and below the plane of the sheet.

Parallel and antiparallel sheets

Beta strands can be arranged in two ways. In an antiparallel sheet, consecutive strands run in opposite directions, giving straight, regularly spaced hydrogen bonds. In a parallel sheet, all strands run in the same direction and the hydrogen bonds are slanted. Mixed sheets combine both arrangements. Antiparallel sheets are generally somewhat more stable because of their more linear hydrogen bonds.

Motifs

Common supersecondary structures built from beta strands include the beta hairpin, the Greek key motif, the beta-alpha-beta motif and the beta-meander. Beta sheets are also the core of many larger folds such as beta barrels and beta propellers.

Role in disease

Abnormal aggregation of proteins into stacked beta sheets, called amyloid, is associated with diseases including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and prion diseases. The cross-beta structure of amyloid fibrils, in which beta strands run perpendicular to the fibril axis, is extremely stable and resists degradation.
