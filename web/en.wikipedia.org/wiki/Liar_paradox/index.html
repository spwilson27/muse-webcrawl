Liar paradox

Liar paradox
FieldLogic, philosophy of language
Classic form"This statement is false."
Ancient sourceEubulides of Miletus (4th c. BCE); Epimenides
TypeParadox of self-reference

In philosophy and logic, the classical liar paradox or liar's paradox is the statement of a liar that they are lying: for instance, declaring that "I am lying". If the liar is indeed lying, then the statement is true; but if the statement is true, then the liar is lying, and so the statement is false. The paradox is that the statement cannot consistently be assigned a truth value.

History

The paradox is traditionally attributed to the Megarian philosopher Eubulides of Miletus (4th century BCE). An older, weaker version is associated with the Cretan prophet Epimenides, who is quoted as saying "All Cretans are liars" — a statement discussed by the apostle Paul in the Epistle to Titus. Medieval logicians (the insolubilia literature) and Islamic and Indian philosophers devoted extensive analysis to the problem.

Formulations and related paradoxes

The strengthened liar — "This sentence is not true" — resists solutions that merely declare the original neither true nor false, since it then appears to be not true after all. Related constructions include Curry's paradox, Yablo's paradox (an infinite sequence of sentences with no direct self-reference), the barber paradox, and Russell's paradox in set theory. Self-reference of this kind also underlies Gödel's incompleteness theorems, in which arithmetic sentences can, in effect, state their own unprovability.

Proposed solutions

Major families of solutions include: Alfred Tarski's hierarchy of languages, in which truth for a language can only be defined in a metalanguage, so no language can contain its own truth predicate; Arthur Prior's view that every statement implicitly asserts its own truth; paracomplete approaches (notably Saul Kripke's theory of truth with truth-value gaps, built on fixed-point semantics); paraconsistent and dialetheist approaches (Graham Priest), which accept that the liar is both true and false; and contextualist and revision theories (Anil Gupta, Hans Herzberger) that treat truth as unstable under semantic evaluation. No solution commands universal assent, and the liar remains a central test case for theories of truth.
