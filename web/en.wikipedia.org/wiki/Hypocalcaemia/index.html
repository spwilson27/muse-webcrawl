Hypocalcaemia
SpecialtyEndocrinology, internal medicine
DefinitionLow serum calcium, typically total calcium below about 2.1 mmol/L (8.5 mg/dL), adjusted for albumin, or low ionized calcium
SymptomsParaesthesia, muscle cramps, tetany, seizures, prolonged QT interval
CausesHypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypomagnesaemia, acute pancreatitis

Hypocalcaemia (also spelled hypocalcemia) is a low level of calcium in the blood serum. The normal range of total serum calcium is roughly 2.1–2.6 mmol/L (8.5–10.5 mg/dL), and less than about 2.1 mmol/L is generally considered hypocalcaemia, though because roughly half of blood calcium is bound to albumin, measured values are corrected for albumin concentration; the physiologically active fraction is ionized calcium. Because calcium is essential for nerve conduction, muscle contraction, and blood clotting, significant deficiency increases neuromuscular excitability.

Signs and symptoms

Mild or chronic hypocalcaemia may be asymptomatic. Acute or severe hypocalcaemia causes perioral and fingertip paraesthesia (tingling), muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm, and tetany; latent tetany can be elicited by Chvostek's sign (facial twitching on tapping the facial nerve) and Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm after inflating a blood-pressure cuff). Severe cases may progress to laryngospasm, seizures, and cardiac effects including a prolonged QT interval and arrhythmia. Long-standing hypocalcaemia can cause dry skin, brittle nails, cataracts, dental abnormalities, and basal ganglia calcification.

Causes

Calcium levels are regulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH), vitamin D, and calcitonin acting on bone, kidney, and intestine. Common causes of hypocalcaemia include:

Hypoparathyroidism — most often following thyroid or parathyroid surgery, but also autoimmune or genetic (as in DiGeorge syndrome); vitamin D deficiency or resistance, which reduces intestinal calcium absorption; chronic kidney disease, which impairs vitamin D activation and phosphate excretion; hypomagnesaemia, which suppresses PTH secretion and action (a frequent cause in alcohol use disorder and with certain drugs); and acute pancreatitis, in which calcium is deposited in areas of fat necrosis. Other causes include massive transfusion (citrate binding), sepsis, certain medications (bisphosphonates, cinacalcet, some anticonvulsants), hungry bone syndrome after parathyroidectomy, and pseudohypoparathyroidism, in which tissues resist PTH.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed by measuring total calcium with albumin (or ionized calcium directly), followed by tests to determine the cause: PTH, phosphate, magnesium, creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D. An ECG may show QT prolongation. Low PTH points to hypoparathyroidism; appropriately high PTH suggests vitamin D deficiency, kidney disease, or PTH resistance, and interpretation of phosphate levels helps distinguish among these.

Treatment

Symptomatic or severe hypocalcaemia is treated with intravenous calcium gluconate (preferred over calcium chloride peripherally because it is less irritating to veins), with cardiac monitoring; magnesium deficiency must be corrected concurrently or calcium will not normalize. Chronic management uses oral calcium supplements and vitamin D preparations — calcitriol (active vitamin D) in hypoparathyroidism and kidney disease — while the underlying cause is addressed. In chronic hypoparathyroidism, the goal is a low-normal calcium level to relieve symptoms while avoiding hypercalciuria and kidney stones.
