Garifuna


Garifuna musicians performing in Central America.

The Garifuna (also spelled Garinagu, singular Garifuna) are a people of mixed West African and Amerindian ancestry who live along the Caribbean coasts of Honduras, Guatemala, Nicaragua and Belize, with a large diaspora in the United States. They descend from African captives who were shipwrecked or escaped to the island of Saint Vincent in the 17th century and intermarried with the indigenous Island Carib (Kalinago) and Arawak peoples.

History

Known to the British as "Black Caribs", the Garifuna of Saint Vincent resisted colonial rule under leaders such as Joseph Chatoyer. After their defeat in the Second Carib War, the British deported some 5,000 Garifuna in 1797 to the island of Roatán off Honduras; from there they spread along the Central American coast, founding communities that remain today. Honduras marks Garifuna Settlement Day on 12 April, and Belize celebrates Garifuna Settlement Day on 19 November.

Language and culture

The Garifuna language belongs to the Arawakan family and incorporates vocabulary from Carib, French, English and Spanish. UNESCO proclaimed the language, dance and music of the Garifuna a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001. Garifuna culture is noted for its music — especially punta and paranda, driven by drums and turtle-shell percussion — its cassava-based cuisine (such as ereba bread and hudut), and ancestor rituals such as the dügü.
