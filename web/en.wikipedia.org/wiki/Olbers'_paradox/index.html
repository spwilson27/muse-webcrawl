Olbers' paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of concentric shells of stars around an observer, each shell contributing equal light]
In an infinite static universe, every line of sight ends on a star.

Olbers' paradox (or the dark night sky paradox) is the argument that the darkness of the night sky conflicts with the assumption of an infinite and eternal static universe: in such a universe, every line of sight from Earth should eventually reach a star, and the whole sky should be as bright as a star's surface. Named after Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers, who discussed it in 1823 (it had been raised by Kepler, Halley and Chéseaux earlier), the paradox dissolves in the modern cosmology: the universe has a finite age, so light from most stars has not reached us; and expansion further dims distant light by Redshift.

The resolution's first clear statement is often credited to Edgar Allan Poe's prose poem Eureka (1848) — 'the only way we can account for the voids... is to suppose the distance of the invisible background so immense that no ray from it has yet been able to reach us at all' — and Kelvin gave a quantitative version in 1901. The night sky's darkness is thus direct, naked-eye evidence that the universe had a beginning: the paradox's endurance in textbooks reflects its power as an argument that cosmology can be done from an armchair.

Why dust doesn't save the static universe

Intervening dust absorbing starlight was the nineteenth century's attempted rescue — but dust in thermal equilibrium would heat until it re-radiated as much light as it absorbed, restoring the bright sky. Only finite age and expansion (both draining energy from arriving light) truly darken the night.
