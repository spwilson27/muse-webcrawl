The United Farm Workers of America, or more commonly just United Farm Workers (UFW), is a labor union for farmworkers in the United States. It originated from the merger of two organizations, the Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee (AWOC) led largely by Larry Itliong, and the National Farm Workers Association (NFWA) led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta. Their alliance grew out of the Delano grape strike, which began in September 1965 against grape growers in Delano, California, and the union was formally established on 22 August 1966.

Delano grape strike and boycott

Filipino workers of the AWOC struck for higher wages; Chávez's NFWA voted to join them. The five-year strike combined marches — including a 1966 pilgrimage from Delano to Sacramento — nonviolent protest, and a nationwide consumer boycott of table grapes that won broad public support. In 1970 the growers signed contracts recognizing the union, a landmark victory for farm labor. The UFW's symbol, a black Aztec eagle on a red flag, and its motto ¡Sí, se puede! ("Yes, we can!") became icons of the Chicano movement.

Later history

The union helped secure the California Agricultural Labor Relations Act of 1975, the first law in the continental United States granting farmworkers collective bargaining rights. UFW membership declined in later decades amid internal conflicts and competition from the Teamsters, and after Chávez's death in 1993 the union remained active in advocacy on pesticides, heat illness protection, immigration reform, and farmworker wages.
