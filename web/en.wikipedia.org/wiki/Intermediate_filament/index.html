Intermediate filament

Intermediate filaments (IFs) are protein filaments of the cytoskeleton, with an average diameter of 10 nanometres — between that of the thinner microfilaments (actin filaments) and the thicker microtubules. They are found in most animal cells and provide mechanical strength and structural support.

Types

There are about 70 different genes coding for various intermediate filament proteins, grouped into six types: keratins (types I and II), vimentin and related proteins (type III), neurofilament proteins (type IV), lamins (type V), and nestin and others (type VI). Examples include keratins in epithelial cells, vimentin in mesenchymal cells, desmin in muscle, and neurofilaments in neurons.

Properties

Unlike actin filaments and microtubules, intermediate filaments have no inherent polarity and do not serve as tracks for motor proteins. They are more stable and less soluble, forming rope-like structures that resist tensile forces and help cells withstand mechanical stress. Mutations in keratin genes cause skin-blistering diseases such as epidermolysis bullosa simplex.
