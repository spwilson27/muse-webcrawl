United States debt ceiling

The United States debt ceiling is a statutory limit on the total amount of national debt the Treasury may issue. Established in its modern form by the Second Liberty Bond Act of 1917 and consolidated in 1939, it requires Congress to authorize borrowing beyond a set dollar amount.

History

Before 1917 Congress authorized each debt issue separately. The ceiling simplified wartime finance. It has been raised, extended or suspended many dozens of times under presidents of both parties.

Standoffs

Debates in 2011, 2013 and 2023 brought the government close to the so-called X-date, when extraordinary measures would be exhausted. The 2011 standoff led to a downgrade of US credit by Standard & Poor's. The Fiscal Responsibility Act of 2023 suspended the ceiling through January 1, 2025.

Debate

Supporters say the limit forces discussion of fiscal policy. Critics argue it creates a risk of default over spending already authorized, and note few other countries use a similar mechanism; Denmark is a rare example with a ceiling set far above actual debt.
