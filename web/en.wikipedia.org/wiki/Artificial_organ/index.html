Artificial organ

Artificial organ
DefinitionHuman-made device or tissue implanted or integrated into a person to replace or support a natural organ
ExamplesArtificial heart; dialysis (artificial kidney); cochlear implant; artificial pancreas; ventricular assist devices
Related fieldBiomedical engineering; tissue engineering; regenerative medicine

An artificial organ is a human-made device, or engineered tissue, that is implanted into or integrated with a living person to replace, duplicate, or augment the function of a natural organ. Artificial organs may be wholly mechanical or electromechanical, may combine synthetic materials with living cells (bioartificial organs), or may be grown from the patient's own cells through tissue engineering. They are used when an organ has failed or is failing and a donor organ is unavailable, unsuitable, or needed only temporarily.

Examples

Heart

Devices range from ventricular assist devices, which support one side of the heart, to total artificial hearts that replace it entirely. The Jarvik-7, implanted in Barney Clark in 1982, was the first permanent total artificial heart; modern devices such as the SynCardia temporary total artificial heart are used mainly as a bridge to transplantation.

Kidney

Hemodialysis functions as an external artificial kidney, filtering blood through a dialyser; Willem Kolff built the first practical dialysis machine in the 1940s. Implantable and wearable artificial kidneys remain an active research goal.

Other organs and systems

The cochlear implant restores a sense of hearing by electrically stimulating the auditory nerve; artificial pancreases couple continuous glucose monitors with insulin pumps in closed-loop systems; extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) temporarily performs the work of heart and lungs; and prosthetic limbs, artificial skin, retinal implants, and bioartificial liver devices extend the concept across many body systems.

Bioartificial organs and tissue engineering

Bioartificial organs seed living cells onto scaffolds—sometimes a donor organ stripped of its own cells ("decellularised")—to reproduce biological functions that machines cannot, such as the liver's metabolic roles. Laboratory-grown bladders constructed from patients' own cells were among the first engineered tissues implanted in humans, and 3D bioprinting is being explored for fabricating tissues and, eventually, organs.

Challenges

Persistent obstacles include blood clotting on synthetic surfaces, infection at device interfaces, power supply for implants, immune rejection of biological components, and the sheer complexity of organs like the liver and kidney. Cost and the ethics of access also shape the field; artificial organs are frequently used as a "bridge" that keeps a patient alive until transplantation, rather than as permanent replacements.
