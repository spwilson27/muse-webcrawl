Anglo-Irish Treaty

Anglo-Irish Treaty
Signed6 December 1921, London
PartiesUnited Kingdom; Irish Republic (representatives)
EffectCreation of the Irish Free State as a dominion; end of the Irish War of Independence

The Anglo-Irish Treaty, formally the Articles of Agreement for a Treaty Between Great Britain and Ireland, was signed in London on 6 December 1921 between representatives of the British government and envoys of the unilaterally declared Irish Republic. It ended the Irish War of Independence and established the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion of the British Empire, while allowing Northern Ireland to opt out — confirming the partition of Ireland.

Terms

The Irish Free State would have dominion status comparable to Canada, with its own parliament and government, but the King remained head of state, members of the Irish parliament would swear an oath of allegiance to the Crown, and Britain retained three deep-water "Treaty Ports" (Berehaven, Queenstown/Cobh and Lough Swilly) for naval defence. A Boundary Commission was to determine the final border with Northern Ireland.

Negotiations and split

The Irish delegation, led by Arthur Griffith and Michael Collins, negotiated under a threat from Lloyd George of "immediate and terrible war" if the treaty were rejected. Éamon de Valera, who had stayed in Dublin, repudiated the settlement's oath and dominion status. The Dáil approved the treaty narrowly, by 64 votes to 57, on 7 January 1922, after bitter debates. De Valera resigned, and the split within Sinn Féin and the IRA led directly to the Irish Civil War (1922–23), in which Collins was killed.

Aftermath

The Free State came into being on 6 December 1922. The Boundary Commission's failure in 1925 left the border unchanged. Successive Irish governments dismantled the treaty's remaining constraints: the oath was abolished in 1933, the ports returned in 1938, and a republic was declared in 1949.
