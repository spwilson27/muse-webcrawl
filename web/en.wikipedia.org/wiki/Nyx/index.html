Nyx (Ancient Greek: Νύξ, "Night") is the Greek goddess and personification of the night. A primordial deity born of Chaos, she is one of the oldest figures in Greek mythology — so powerful, according to the Iliad, that even Zeus feared to anger her.

Mythology

In Hesiod's Theogony, Nyx emerges from Chaos and, with her brother Erebus (Darkness), bears Aether (Brightness) and Hemera (Day). Alone, she produces a host of personified powers: Moros (Doom), Thanatos (Death), Hypnos (Sleep), the Oneiroi (Dreams), Momus (Blame), Oizys (Misery), the Hesperides, the Keres and Fates, Nemesis (Retribution), Apate (Deceit), Philotes (Love), Geras (Old Age) and Eris (Strife). Hesiod describes her house at the ends of the earth, where Night and Day cross paths at a bronze threshold, never home at the same time.

In Homer's Iliad, Hypnos recalls that he once fled Zeus's anger by taking refuge with Nyx, and Zeus abandoned the pursuit "in fear of doing anything that might displease swift Night" — the clearest indication of her stature among the gods. Orphic tradition elevated her further: in some Orphic cosmogonies Nyx, rather than Chaos, is the first principle, and Zeus seeks her counsel at her oracle-cave.

Cult and legacy

Nyx received little cult worship, though she was honoured at Megara and appeared in art — most famously on the Pergamon Altar — usually as a winged figure in a star-spangled veil, sometimes driving a chariot or holding her children Hypnos and Thanatos. Her Roman name, Nox, lives on in words such as "equinox", and modern astronomy has attached her name to a moon of Pluto, Nix (spelled with an i to avoid confusion with an asteroid).
