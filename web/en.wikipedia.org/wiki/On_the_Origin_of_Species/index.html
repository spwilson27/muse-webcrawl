On the Origin of Species (or, more completely, On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, or the Preservation of Favoured Races in the Struggle for Life), published on 24 November 1859, is a work of scientific literature by Charles Darwin that is considered to be the foundation of evolutionary biology. It presented a body of evidence that the diversity of life arose by common descent through a branching pattern of evolution, and proposed natural selection as the mechanism.

Background

Darwin developed his theory after his voyage on HMS Beagle (1831–36), formulating natural selection by 1838, but delayed publication for two decades while amassing evidence. In 1858 Alfred Russel Wallace sent Darwin a paper outlining the same idea, prompting a joint presentation to the Linnean Society and Darwin's rush to publish the "abstract" that became the Origin.

Contents

The book opens with variation under domestication and in nature, proceeds to the struggle for existence and natural selection, then addresses difficulties (the fossil record, hybridism, instinct), the geological succession of organisms, geographical distribution, and classification and morphology, concluding that species descend with modification from common ancestors. The single illustration is a branching diagram of divergence — the origin of the "tree of life" image.

Publication and reception

The first edition of 1,250 copies sold out immediately. Darwin revised the work through six editions (the word "evolution" first appeared in the sixth, 1872). The book convinced much of the scientific community of evolution within a generation, though natural selection as the mechanism gained full acceptance only with the modern synthesis of the 1930s–40s.

Influence

The Origin transformed biology, geology, philosophy and popular culture, establishing evolution as the unifying theory of the life sciences.
