Hyperfocal distance

In optics and photography, the hyperfocal distance is the distance beyond which all objects can be brought into acceptable focus when the lens is focused at a particular point. One common definition is the focusing distance which gives the maximum depth of field: when a lens is focused at the hyperfocal distance, everything from half that distance to infinity will be acceptably sharp.

Formula

The hyperfocal distance H is approximated by

H ≈ f² / (N · c)

where f is the focal length, N is the f-number and c is the circle of confusion limit for the format. (Some formulations add one focal length, H = f + f²/(Nc), a difference negligible at ordinary distances.) Stopping down (larger N) or using a shorter focal length decreases the hyperfocal distance and increases depth of field.

Use in practice

Landscape photographers use hyperfocal focusing to maximise sharpness from the near foreground to the horizon, often with the aid of depth-of-field scales engraved on older lenses or hyperfocal tables and apps. In street and documentary photography, zone focusing at the hyperfocal distance with a stopped-down wide-angle lens permits rapid shooting without refocusing. Because "acceptable sharpness" depends on the chosen circle of confusion — and on viewing conditions, sensor resolution and diffraction at very small apertures — hyperfocal calculations are best treated as a guide rather than an absolute.
