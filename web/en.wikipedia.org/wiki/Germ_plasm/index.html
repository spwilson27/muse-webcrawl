Germ plasm

Germ plasm is a biological concept developed in the 19th century by the German biologist August Weismann. In Weismann's germ plasm theory (German: Keimplasma), heredity takes place by means of the germ cells (gametes such as egg and sperm cells), which contain the germ plasm — the hereditary material — while the other cells of the body, the somatoplasm, neither produce nor are produced from germ plasm in a way that transmits acquired information.

Weismann's theory

Weismann set out the theory in essays from 1883 onward and in his book Das Keimplasma: eine Theorie der Vererbung (1892; English: The Germ-Plasm: A Theory of Heredity, 1893). Its key claims were the continuity of the germ plasm from generation to generation — the germ line is effectively immortal, passing from parent to offspring while the body (soma) that carries it is built anew and dies in each generation — and the "Weismann barrier": information cannot flow from somatic cells back into the germ cells, so characteristics acquired by the body during life (such as injuries, training, or mutilations) cannot be inherited. Weismann famously tested this by cutting off the tails of mice for many generations and showing that their offspring were still born with full-length tails.

Significance

The germ plasm theory was a decisive blow against the inheritance of acquired characteristics (Lamarckism) and provided a conceptual basis for Mendelian genetics and, later, the modern synthesis and the distinction between genotype and phenotype. The molecular nature of the "germ plasm" was later identified with DNA in the chromosomes of the gametes. In modern developmental biology, the term "germ plasm" is used more specifically for the special cytoplasm in the eggs of many animals (germ plasm sensu stricto) that determines which cells become the germ cells of the embryo — a material-based mechanism contrasting with inductive specification of germ cells in other species, including mammals.

Other uses

In agriculture and conservation, "germplasm" denotes the living genetic resources — seeds, tissues, or breeding animals — maintained for plant and animal breeding and biodiversity conservation, as in seed banks and germplasm collections.
