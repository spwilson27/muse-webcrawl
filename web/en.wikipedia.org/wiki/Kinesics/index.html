Kinesics

Kinesics is the interpretation of body motion communication, including facial expressions, gestures, posture, gait and the visible movements of the arms and legs. It is one of the principal branches of nonverbal communication, alongside proxemics (the use of space) and chronemics (the use of time).

History

The term was coined in 1952 by the American anthropologist Ray Birdwhistell, who argued that bodily movement is a learned, structured form of communication comparable to language, and that no gesture carries meaning in isolation from its cultural context. In his book Introduction to Kinesics (1952) and later work, Birdwhistell estimated that words carry only a minority of the social meaning exchanged in face-to-face interaction.

Categories of movement

Researchers commonly distinguish several kinds of kinesic behaviour:

  • Emblems — gestures with a direct verbal equivalent, such as a thumbs-up for approval.
  • Illustrators — movements that accompany and emphasise speech.
  • Affect displays — facial expressions and postures conveying emotion.
  • Regulators — movements that manage turn-taking in conversation, such as nodding.
  • Adaptors — self-directed movements, such as fidgeting, often linked to anxiety or habit.

Cultural variation

Kinesic signals vary substantially between cultures: the same gesture may be friendly in one society and obscene in another. Birdwhistell insisted that kinesics therefore had to be studied within its cultural setting, and later scholars such as Paul Ekman investigated which facial expressions, if any, are universal.
