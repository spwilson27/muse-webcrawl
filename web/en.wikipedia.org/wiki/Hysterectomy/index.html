Hysterectomy

Hysterectomy is the surgical removal of the uterus and sometimes the cervix. When the ovaries and fallopian tubes are also removed, the procedure is called a hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. It is the most commonly performed major gynaecological surgical procedure, and after hysterectomy a person can no longer become pregnant or menstruate.

Types and approaches

A total hysterectomy removes the uterus and cervix; a subtotal (supracervical) hysterectomy leaves the cervix in place; a radical hysterectomy, performed for certain cancers, also removes surrounding tissue and the upper vagina. The operation may be performed through an abdominal incision, vaginally, or by laparoscopic or robot-assisted techniques; minimally invasive routes generally involve shorter hospital stay and faster recovery, and vaginal hysterectomy is preferred where feasible.

Indications

Common indications include uterine fibroids causing symptoms, endometriosis and adenomyosis, pelvic organ prolapse, abnormal uterine bleeding unresponsive to other treatment, and gynaecological cancers of the uterus, cervix, or ovaries, as well as precancerous conditions. Emergency hysterectomy may be required for uncontrollable postpartum haemorrhage. Because the procedure ends fertility and carries surgical risks — bleeding, infection, injury to bladder, ureter or bowel, and thromboembolism — guidelines recommend considering less invasive alternatives, such as hormonal therapy, uterine artery embolisation, or myomectomy, for benign disease when appropriate.

Aftermath

If the ovaries are conserved, menopause occurs at roughly the usual age, though possibly slightly earlier; if they are removed before natural menopause, surgical menopause follows immediately and hormone replacement is usually considered. Long-term associations under study include pelvic floor disorders and cardiovascular effects when oophorectomy is performed at a young age.
