Treaty of Edinburgh

The Treaty of Edinburgh was a treaty drawn up on 6 July 1560 between the Commissioners of Queen Elizabeth I of England, representatives of the French monarchy, and the Scottish Lords of the Congregation. It ended the Siege of Leith and the Auld Alliance's military presence in Scotland.

Terms

France agreed to withdraw its troops from Scotland; French fortifications at Leith and elsewhere were to be demolished. Mary, Queen of Scots, and her husband Francis II of France were to cease using the arms and style of England and Ireland, implicitly acknowledging Elizabeth's title. A council of twelve, chosen jointly by Mary and the Scottish nobility, would assist in governing during the queen's absence, and an amnesty was granted to the Lords of the Congregation who had rebelled against the regent, Mary of Guise (who had died during the siege).

Significance

The treaty marked a decisive Protestant and pro-English turn in Scottish politics, consolidating the Scottish Reformation that Parliament enacted weeks later. Mary, however, never ratified the treaty, and her refusal to renounce her claim to the English throne remained a source of Anglo-Scottish tension for decades.
