Lao (ລາວ, phasa Lao), sometimes referred to as Laotian, is a Kra–Dai language of the Lao people. It is spoken in Laos, where it is the official language, as well as in northeast Thailand, where it is usually referred to as Isan. Lao serves as a lingua franca among the citizens of Laos, who also speak approximately 90 other languages, many of which are unrelated to Lao.

As with most languages in the region, the Lao alphabet is an abugida. While there is no official romanization system for Lao, France, which ruled Laos as a protectorate for part of the 19th and most of the 20th centuries, developed a system that is still used in Laos, while Thailand uses a different system based on the Thai alphabet.

History

The Lao language is descended from the Tai languages spoken in what is now southern China and northern Vietnam. The Tai peoples migrated southward into Southeast Asia over the course of the first millennium AD. The Lao script was developed from the Khmer script, itself adapted from the Pallava script of southern India, in the period following the establishment of the kingdom of Lan Xang in the 14th century.

During French colonial rule (1893–1954), Lao was written with the Lao alphabet derived from the older system. In 1975, after the communist takeover, the Pathet Lao government simplified the spelling, eliminating several letters and silent letters and standardizing the language on the dialect of the capital, Vientiane.

Dialects

Lao is divided into a number of regional dialects, including those of Vientiane (the standard), northern Lao (Luang Prabang), northeastern, central, and southern Lao. The Isan language of northeastern Thailand is closely related and largely mutually intelligible with Lao, though over time Thai influence and the use of the Thai script have caused some divergence.

Phonology

Modern standard Lao has six tones in non-checked syllables: mid, low, high, rising, high falling, and low falling. The consonant inventory includes unaspirated and aspirated stops, and the vowel system distinguishes short and long vowels. Syllable structure is generally (C)V(C), with a restricted set of final consonants.

Grammar

Lao is an analytic language with subject–verb–object word order. It lacks inflection; grammatical relations are expressed through word order and particles. Classifiers are used with numerals and demonstratives. As in other Tai languages, pronouns vary according to the relative social status of speaker and listener.

Writing system

The Lao script is an abugida written from left to right. It consists of 27 consonants, vowel signs written around the consonant, and four tone marks. The modern script descends from the Sukhothai-era developments of the Khmer script via the Tai Tham tradition.
