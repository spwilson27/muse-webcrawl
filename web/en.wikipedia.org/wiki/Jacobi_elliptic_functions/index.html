In mathematics, the Jacobi elliptic functions are a set of basic elliptic functions. They are found in the description of the motion of a pendulum (see also pendulum (mathematics)), as well as in the design of electronic elliptic filters. While trigonometric functions are defined with reference to a circle, the Jacobi elliptic functions are a generalization which refer to other conic sections, the ellipse in particular. The relation to trigonometric functions is contained in the notation, for example, by the matching notation sn for sin.

The functions were introduced by Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi in his 1829 treatise Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum, building on the elliptic integrals studied by Legendre and the inversion idea first pursued by Abel and Gauss.

Definition

Let the elliptic integral of the first kind be

u = ∫0φ dθ / √(1 − m sin²θ)

Then the amplitude is φ = am(u), and the three principal functions are defined by

sn u = sin φ, cn u = cos φ, dn u = √(1 − m sin²φ)

where m = k² is the parameter and k the modulus. They satisfy sn²u + cn²u = 1 and dn²u + m·sn²u = 1. In the limiting cases the functions degenerate familiarly: for m = 0, sn u = sin u, cn u = cos u, dn u = 1 (trigonometric); for m = 1, sn u = tanh u, cn u = dn u = sech u (hyperbolic).

The twelve functions

Taking quotients and reciprocals of the three principal functions gives nine further functions — ns, nc, nd, sc, sd, cd, cs, ds, dc — conventionally labelled by Glaisher's notation, in which the letters name which of the functions appear in the numerator and denominator relative to the poles of the fundamental rectangle.

Periodicity and properties

Each Jacobi function is doubly periodic and meromorphic in the complex plane, with periods involving the complete elliptic integrals K(k) and iK(k′), where k′ = √(1−k²) is the complementary modulus: sn has periods 4K and 2iK′, cn has 4K and 2K + 2iK′, and dn has 2K and 4iK′. Derivatives are simple: d/du sn u = cn u · dn u; d/du cn u = −sn u · dn u; d/du dn u = −m · sn u · cn u. Addition formulas and Landen and Gauss transformations underpin fast numerical evaluation by the arithmetic-geometric mean.

Applications

The exact solution for a simple pendulum's angle uses the Jacobi amplitude and sn function; Jacobi functions likewise solve the Euler equations for a free rigid body, describe nonlinear waves (cnoidal waves in shallow water), appear in elliptic (Cauer) filter design in electronics, and in conformal mapping of rectangles.
