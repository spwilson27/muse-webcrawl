Mass incarceration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mass incarceration is a term used by historians and sociologists to describe the substantial increase in the number of incarcerated people in the United States' prisons over the past forty years, and more generally the country's extensive system of imprisonment and criminal justice supervision. The United States has the largest prison population in the world: at its peak in 2008, roughly 2.3 million people were held in federal and state prisons and local jails, an incarceration rate several times the historical norm and far higher than that of comparable democracies.

Causes

Scholars attribute the growth, which began in the early 1970s after decades of stable rates, to changes in policy rather than to crime rates alone. Contributing factors include the War on Drugs, mandatory minimum sentencing laws, truth-in-sentencing statutes, "three strikes" laws, longer sentences, reduced use of parole, and the increased prosecution of offences that previously drew probation. Michelle Alexander's influential book The New Jim Crow (2010) argued that mass incarceration functions as a system of racial control, pointing to stark racial disparities: Black Americans are imprisoned at several times the rate of white Americans.

Effects

Beyond the prison population itself, the system carries wide social consequences: disenfranchisement of people with felony convictions, barriers to employment and housing for those with criminal records, fiscal costs exceeding tens of billions of dollars a year, and effects on families and communities with high rates of imprisonment. The broader "carceral population" under correctional supervision, including probation and parole, numbered in the millions.

Reform and decline

Since around 2009 the incarcerated population has declined modestly, driven by sentencing reforms, drug-policy changes, state-level initiatives and bipartisan criticism of the system's costs and racial effects. The First Step Act of 2018 modestly reduced federal sentences and expanded early-release provisions. Debate continues over whether these changes represent the end of the mass incarceration era or only a limited adjustment.
