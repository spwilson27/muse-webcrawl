Shift dress

A shift dress is a short, loose-fitting dress that hangs straight down from the shoulders with no defined waist. Characterized by a simple, unfitted silhouette, clean lines, and usually a high neckline and knee-length hem, the shift became one of the defining garments of 1960s fashion.

History

The shift grew out of the sack dress introduced in the late 1950s by designers such as Cristóbal Balenciaga and Hubert de Givenchy, which loosened the fitted hourglass silhouette of the post-war era. In the 1960s the style was shortened and simplified, becoming associated with youthful mod fashion and designers such as Mary Quant and André Courrèges.

One of the most famous versions was created by Lilly Pulitzer, whose brightly printed shifts—originally designed to hide juice stains from her Florida citrus stand—became a staple of American resort wear. Jacqueline Kennedy's preference for simple shift dresses helped popularize the style in the United States.

Design

A shift dress is typically sleeveless or short-sleeved, with darts at the bust to give slight shaping while keeping the overall line straight. Because it has no waist seam, it is comfortable and easy to wear, and the style has returned to fashion repeatedly in later decades.
