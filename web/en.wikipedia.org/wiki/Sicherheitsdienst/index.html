Sicherheitsdienst

The Sicherheitsdienst des Reichsführers-SS ("Security Service of the Reichsführer-SS"), or SD, was the intelligence agency of the SS and the Nazi Party in Nazi Germany. Established in 1931, the SD was the first Nazi intelligence organisation and was considered a sister organisation of the Gestapo, with which it was integrated into the Reich Main Security Office (RSHA) in 1939. Its director, Reinhard Heydrich, intended for the SD to bring every aspect of German life under continual supervised control and observation.

History

Heinrich Himmler established the SD in 1931 as the Ic-Dienst, a small party intelligence office, and appointed the former naval officer Reinhard Heydrich to run it. Renamed the Sicherheitsdienst in 1932, it expanded rapidly after the Nazi seizure of power, competing with other intelligence services such as the military Abwehr. In 1934 it was declared the sole political intelligence service of the Nazi Party, and in 1936 Heydrich gained control of the political police (Gestapo) throughout the Reich.

Functions

The SD's domestic branch (SD-Inland) monitored German public opinion and produced the secret "Reports from the Reich" on morale and dissent. Its foreign branch (SD-Ausland) conducted espionage abroad and ran operations such as the Venlo incident of 1939, in which British intelligence officers were kidnapped on the Dutch border. SD personnel staffed many of the Einsatzgruppen killing units in Eastern Europe and provided much of the leadership of the RSHA.

Aftermath

The SD was declared a criminal organisation at the Nuremberg trials. Its surviving members were subject to denazification proceedings, though many foreign-intelligence personnel of Amt VI later found employment with Western intelligence services during the early Cold War.
