Vèvè

A vèvè (also spelled vévé or veve) is a religious symbol commonly used in different branches of Vodou throughout the African diaspora, such as Haitian Vodou and Louisiana Voodoo. The vèvè acts as a "beacon" for the lwa (spirits) and will serve as a lwa's representation during rituals.

History

The vèvè is believed to have originated in the Amerindian cultures native to Haiti, combined with West and Central African influences. Historically, the Arawak and Taíno peoples, who were native to the island of Hispaniola, used similar ground drawings in their religious practices. These were combined with symbols brought by enslaved Africans, particularly from the Kongo and Fon peoples, whose ritual ground drawings and cosmograms (such as the Kongo cosmogram, or dikenga) bear strong resemblances to the vèvè.

Function

Functionally, the vèvè serves as a focal point for the invocation of a particular lwa during a ceremony. Each lwa has one or more vèvè associated with them, which incorporates their distinctive characteristics and attributes. Offerings of food, drink, and other items specific to the lwa are placed upon or around the vèvè.

In Haitian Vodou, the vèvè is typically drawn on the floor of the peristyle (temple) by the houngan (priest) or manbo (priestess) using cornmeal, flour, ashes, or other powdered substances. The drawing of the vèvè is accompanied by songs and prayers, and the act of drawing itself is a form of offering and a ritual in its own right.

Examples

Well-known vèvè include those of Papa Legba, the guardian of the crossroads, whose design features a cross-like pattern; Erzulie Freda, the lwa of love, whose vèvè centers on a heart; Baron Samedi, lord of the dead, whose vèvè prominently features a cross atop a tomb; and Damballa, the serpent lwa, whose vèvè depicts undulating serpent forms.
