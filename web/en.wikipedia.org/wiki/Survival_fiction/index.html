Survival fiction

The castaway story is the archetypal survival narrative

Survival fiction is a genre of literature, film, and games in which one or more characters must physically survive in hostile circumstances, often in wilderness, after a disaster, or in a post-apocalyptic world. The focus is on practical problem-solving — finding food, water, shelter, and rescue — and on the psychological endurance of the protagonists. It is closely related to, but distinct from, practical survival skills.

History

Daniel Defoe's Robinson Crusoe (1719), inspired by the real castaway Alexander Selkirk, founded the "Robinsonade" tradition of island-survival stories, continued in Johann Wyss's The Swiss Family Robinson (1812) and Jules Verne's The Mysterious Island (1874). Jack London's short story "To Build a Fire" (1908) brought the struggle against cold to a bleak extreme. In children's literature, Scott O'Dell's Island of the Blue Dolphins (1960) and Gary Paulsen's Hatchet (1986) made the lone young survivor a staple.

Post-apocalyptic and modern forms

Twentieth-century survival fiction broadened into post-apocalyptic works such as A Canticle for Leibowitz (1960) and Cormac McCarthy's The Road (2006), and dystopian survival contests such as The Hunger Games (2008). Cinema added castaway dramas like Cast Away (2000) and survival thrillers such as 127 Hours (2010) and The Revenant (2015). Video games created the survival game genre, including Minecraft, Rust, and The Long Dark, in which hunger, temperature, and resource management are simulated mechanics.

Themes

Common themes include self-reliance, the thin line between civilisation and savagery (as in William Golding's Lord of the Flies, 1954), ingenuity with limited materials, and the moral choices forced by scarcity.
