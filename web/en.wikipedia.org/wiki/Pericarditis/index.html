Pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium, the fibrous sac surrounding the heart. It most characteristically causes sharp central chest pain that is worse when lying flat or breathing in and relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. Pain may radiate to the shoulder or trapezius ridge. Other symptoms include fever, shortness of breath and palpitations.

Signs and diagnosis

On auscultation, a pericardial friction rub — a scratchy, grating sound — may be heard. The ECG classically shows widespread concave ("saddle-shaped") ST-segment elevation and PR-segment depression. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by ECG changes, elevated inflammatory markers, and echocardiography to detect any associated pericardial effusion. Acute pericarditis is diagnosed when at least two of four criteria are present: typical chest pain, friction rub, characteristic ECG changes, and new or worsening effusion.

Causes

In developed countries, most cases are idiopathic and presumed viral (e.g. coxsackievirus, echovirus, adenovirus). Other causes include bacterial infection (notably tuberculosis, a leading cause in the developing world), autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis, myocardial infarction (early, and late as Dressler syndrome), uraemia, malignancy, chest trauma and certain drugs.

Treatment and prognosis

First-line treatment is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (aspirin or ibuprofen) combined with colchicine, which reduces recurrence. Most cases settle within weeks. Complications include recurrent pericarditis (in up to about 30% without colchicine), pericardial effusion with cardiac tamponade — an emergency treated by pericardiocentesis — and, rarely, chronic constrictive pericarditis requiring surgical pericardiectomy.
