Pentateuch

LanguageBiblical Hebrew

The Pentateuch (from Greek Πεντάτευχος, pentáteukhos, "five scrolls") is the first division of the Hebrew Bible, comprising the five books of Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. In Judaism it is called the Torah ("teaching" or "law") and is the most sacred part of the Tanakh; in Christianity it forms the first five books of the Old Testament and is traditionally attributed to Moses, and so is also called the Five Books of Moses.

Contents

The Pentateuch traces a continuous narrative from the creation of the world and the primeval history (Genesis 1–11), through the stories of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph, to the Exodus of the Israelites from Egypt, the revelation at Mount Sinai and the giving of the Law, the wilderness wanderings, and Moses' final speeches and death in Deuteronomy, on the threshold of the promised land of Canaan. Interwoven with the narrative are extensive legal collections, including the Ten Commandments, the Covenant Code, the Holiness Code in Leviticus, and the Deuteronomic Code.

Composition

Traditional Jewish and Christian belief holds that Moses wrote the Pentateuch (excepting the account of his death). Since the 19th century, critical scholarship has understood the text as a composite work assembled over centuries. The classic Documentary Hypothesis associated with Julius Wellhausen distinguished four sources — Yahwist (J), Elohist (E), Deuteronomist (D), and Priestly (P) — combined by editors, with a final form reached by around the 5th–4th centuries BC, in the Persian period. Although scholars today dispute the details — many favour supplementary or fragmentary models, and some date the Priestly material earlier — there is broad consensus that the Pentateuch reached substantially its present form by the early Second Temple period.

Significance

The Pentateuch is foundational to Judaism, where the Torah scroll is the centrepiece of synagogue worship and its weekly portions are read through annually. It is equally foundational to Christianity and is regarded with reverence in Islam, where the Tawrat is considered a revealed book given to Moses. Its narratives and laws have profoundly shaped law, ethics, and literature in the Western and Islamic worlds.
