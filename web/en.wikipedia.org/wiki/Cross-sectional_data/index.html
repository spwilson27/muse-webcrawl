Cross-sectional data

In statistics and econometrics, cross-sectional data is a type of data collected by observing many subjects (such as individuals, firms, countries, or regions) at the same point in time, or at approximately the same point in time, ignoring differences in time. Analysis of cross-sectional data usually consists of comparing the differences among selected subjects.

Comparison with other data types

Cross-sectional data differ from time series data, in which a single subject is observed at many points in time, and from panel (longitudinal) data, in which many subjects are observed repeatedly over time. A classic example of cross-sectional data is the individual wages of many workers measured in a single year, used to estimate the returns to education. Repeated cross-sections — such as annual national surveys of different samples — are also common.

Analysis

Cross-sectional regression is a standard tool for estimating relationships across units, for example the effect of firm size on profitability or of class size on test scores. Because units are observed only once, cross-sectional studies cannot directly track change over time, and they are vulnerable to unobserved heterogeneity between subjects; economists therefore pay attention to omitted-variable bias and often combine cross-sectional evidence with time-series or panel designs. In epidemiology, cross-sectional studies measure the prevalence of a condition in a population at one point in time.
