DNA replication

DNA replication is the biological process of producing two identical replicas of DNA from one original DNA molecule. It is the basis for biological inheritance and occurs in all living organisms. DNA replication is semiconservative: each strand of the original double helix serves as a template for the production of a new, complementary strand, as demonstrated by the Meselson–Stahl experiment in 1958.

Initiation and the replication fork

Replication begins at specific sites called origins of replication, where initiator proteins recruit helicase enzymes that unwind the double helix, forming a replication fork. Single-strand binding proteins stabilise the separated strands, and topoisomerases relieve the torsional strain ahead of the fork. Because DNA polymerase can only synthesise DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction and requires a primer, the enzyme primase lays down short RNA primers from which synthesis begins.

Elongation

The strand synthesised continuously toward the fork is the leading strand; the other, synthesised in short discontinuous pieces called Okazaki fragments, is the lagging strand. In eukaryotes the main replicative polymerases are DNA polymerase ε (leading strand) and DNA polymerase δ (lagging strand); in E. coli the principal enzyme is DNA polymerase III. RNA primers are removed and the gaps filled, and DNA ligase seals the nicks between fragments. A proofreading exonuclease activity keeps the error rate extremely low, roughly one mistake per 109 nucleotides after mismatch repair.

Termination and telomeres

Replication forks meeting from adjacent origins complete the duplication of linear eukaryotic chromosomes, but the "end replication problem" means chromosome ends (telomeres) shorten with each division in most somatic cells. The enzyme telomerase extends telomeric repeats in germline and stem cells, using an internal RNA template. Errors or failures in replication can cause mutations, genome instability and cancer.
