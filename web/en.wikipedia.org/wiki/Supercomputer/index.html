Supercomputer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A supercomputer is a computer with a high level of performance compared to a general-purpose computer. The performance of a supercomputer is commonly measured in floating-point operations per second (FLOPS) instead of million instructions per second (MIPS). Since 2017, some supercomputers have performed over 1017 FLOPS (a hundred quadrillion FLOPS); the first machines to exceed one exaFLOPS (1018) arrived in the early 2020s.

History

The term emerged in the 1960s with machines such as the CDC 6600 (1964), designed by Seymour Cray and regarded as the first commercially successful supercomputer. Cray's subsequent machines (Cray-1, 1976) popularised vector processing. Later architectures moved to massively parallel designs with thousands of processors, and modern systems are clusters of many nodes combining CPUs with GPU accelerators. China, the United States, Japan, and Europe have competed for the top positions on the TOP500 list, which ranks systems by the LINPACK benchmark; notable machines include Fugaku (Japan), and the US exascale systems Frontier and El Capitan.

Architecture

Supercomputers emphasise extremely fast interconnects, high memory bandwidth, and parallel processing across tens of thousands to millions of cores. Power consumption and cooling are major design constraints: large systems draw tens of megawatts and are commonly liquid-cooled.

Applications

  • Weather forecasting and climate modelling
  • Nuclear weapons simulation (see stockpile stewardship)
  • Computational fluid dynamics and engineering simulation
  • Molecular modelling, protein folding, and drug discovery
  • Astrophysics and fusion energy research
  • Large-scale data analysis and artificial intelligence training
