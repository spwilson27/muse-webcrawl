Gonorrhea

CauseNeisseria gonorrhoeae
TransmissionSexual contact; mother to child at birth
DiagnosisNucleic acid amplification testing
TreatmentCeftriaxone (antibiotic)

Gonorrhea (or gonorrhoea), colloquially known as the clap, is a sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by the bacterium Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It commonly infects the mucous membranes of the urethra, cervix, rectum, throat, and eyes. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 80 million new infections occur each year worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

Many infections, especially in women and at pharyngeal and rectal sites, cause no symptoms. When symptoms occur, men typically experience a burning sensation when urinating and a white, yellow, or green discharge from the penis; women may notice increased vaginal discharge, painful urination, or bleeding between periods. Untreated infection can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and epididymitis, and can spread to the blood or joints (disseminated gonococcal infection). Newborns exposed during birth can develop a severe eye infection (ophthalmia neonatorum) that may cause blindness.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by nucleic acid amplification testing of urine or swabs. Gonorrhea was once readily treated with penicillin, but the bacterium has progressively developed resistance to sulfonamides, penicillin, tetracycline, fluoroquinolones, and increasingly to macrolides. Current guidelines recommend ceftriaxone, a third-generation cephalosporin, as first-line therapy, and the emergence of extensively drug-resistant strains is a major public health concern. Prevention relies on condom use and screening of at-risk populations; no vaccine is available.
