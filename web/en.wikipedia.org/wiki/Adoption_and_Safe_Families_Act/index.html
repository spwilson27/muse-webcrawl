Adoption and Safe Families Act

EnactedNovember 19, 1997
Signed byPresident Bill Clinton
Public Law105-89
SubjectChild welfare, foster care, adoption

The Adoption and Safe Families Act (ASFA, Public Law 105-89) is a United States federal law enacted in 1997 that amended the Social Security Act's child welfare provisions. It shifted the emphasis of federal child welfare policy toward children's health and safety and toward moving children in foster care more quickly into permanent homes, particularly through adoption.

Provisions

ASFA made the child's safety the paramount concern in placement decisions, clarified that "reasonable efforts" to reunify families need not be made in cases of aggravated circumstances such as a parent's murder of another child or felony assault of the child. It required states to initiate termination of parental rights when a child had been in foster care for 15 of the preceding 22 months (subject to exceptions), shortened timelines for permanency hearings, and created adoption incentive payments to reward states for increasing the number of adoptions from foster care.

Effects and debate

Adoptions from foster care rose substantially in the years after ASFA's passage. Critics have argued that the timelines can sever family ties before parents — often those struggling with addiction or poverty — can complete reunification services, while supporters credit the law with reducing the time children spend drifting in foster care. ASFA remains the principal framework of US federal child welfare law, later supplemented by legislation such as the Fostering Connections Act (2008) and the Family First Prevention Services Act (2018).
