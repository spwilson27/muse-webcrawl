Section 28

Section 28
LegislationLocal Government Act 1988, Section 28 (Section 2A in Scotland)
Enacted24 May 1988
RepealedScotland 2000; England and Wales 2003
EffectProhibited local authorities from "promoting homosexuality" or teaching "the acceptability of homosexuality as a pretended family relationship"

Section 28, known in Scotland as Section 2A, was a legislative clause of the British Local Government Act 1988 that prohibited local authorities in England, Scotland, and Wales from "promoting homosexuality" and from promoting "teaching in any maintained school of the acceptability of homosexuality as a pretended family relationship." Enacted under Margaret Thatcher's Conservative government, it was the first new anti-homosexuality law in the United Kingdom in nearly a century.

Origins and passage

The clause arose amid controversy over materials in schools and local-authority funding of gay organizations, and amid the AIDS crisis, which had intensified public debate and prejudice. Despite large protests — including demonstrators abseiling into the House of Lords and invading a BBC news broadcast — the clause passed and came into force on 24 May 1988. No prosecution was ever brought under it, but its practical effect was a powerful chilling one: schools and councils censored themselves, teachers avoided discussing homosexuality or intervening in homophobic bullying, and library and arts funding for LGBT material was withdrawn.

Opposition and repeal

Section 28 galvanised the modern British LGBT rights movement. The campaign against it led to the founding of Stonewall in 1989 and OutRage! in 1990. The Scottish Parliament repealed the clause in 2000 after a contentious public campaign; England and Wales followed in 2003 under Tony Blair's Labour government. In 2009, Conservative leader David Cameron apologised for his party's support of Section 28, calling it a mistake.

Legacy

Section 28 remains a defining reference point in British LGBT history, frequently cited in debates over education and equality, and is credited with uniting and professionalising British gay activism. Its long shadow — teachers uncertain what they were permitted to say — persisted in schools for years after repeal.
