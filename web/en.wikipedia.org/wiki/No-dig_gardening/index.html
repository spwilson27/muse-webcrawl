No-dig gardening

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


No-dig beds topped with compost mulch

No-dig gardening is a cultivation method in which the soil is not turned over by digging or tilling. Instead, organic matter — typically compost, manure, or mulch — is spread on the surface and left for soil organisms, especially earthworms, to incorporate. Proponents argue that avoiding tillage preserves soil structure, fungal networks, and soil carbon, while suppressing weeds and reducing labour.

Principles

  • Disturb the soil as little as possible; never invert it.
  • Keep the surface covered with organic mulch or living plants.
  • Feed the soil life with regular additions of compost rather than synthetic fertiliser.
  • Remove perennial weeds initially by smothering with cardboard or sheet mulch, then plant through the mulch.

History

The approach draws on the work of Japanese farmer-philosopher Masanobu Fukuoka ("do-nothing farming"), the no-till work of Edward Faulkner (Plowman's Folly, 1943), and Ruth Stout's deep-mulch method in the United States. In Britain it was popularised from the late 1970s by market gardener Charles Dowding, whose trials comparing dug and undug beds reported equal or better yields with far less weeding on the no-dig plots.

Advantages and criticisms

Advocates cite reduced weed germination (weed seeds are not brought to the surface), improved water retention, protection of mycorrhizal fungi, and lower physical effort. Critics note the need for large quantities of compost, which may have to be imported, potential problems on very compacted or waterlogged soils, and slug pressure under mulch. No-dig is a core practice of regenerative agriculture and permaculture design.
