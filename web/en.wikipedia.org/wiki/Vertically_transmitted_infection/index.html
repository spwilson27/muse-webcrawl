Vertically transmitted infection

Routes by which infections pass from mother to child.

A vertically transmitted infection is an infection caused by pathogens (such as bacteria and viruses) that use mother-to-child transmission — that is, transmission directly from the mother to an embryo, fetus, or baby during pregnancy, childbirth, or the postnatal period (including through breastfeeding). Such transmission can occur when the mother has a pre-existing disease or becomes infected during pregnancy. Nutritional deficiencies may exacerbate the risks of perinatal infections.

Types of infection

Bacteria, viruses, and other organisms are able to be passed from mother to child. The classic teaching mnemonic TORCH groups together Toxoplasmosis, Other agents (including syphilis, varicella-zoster, and parvovirus B19), Rubella, Cytomegalovirus, and Herpes simplex virus. Other important vertically transmitted infections include HIV, hepatitis B, group B streptococcus, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Listeria.

Routes of transmission

Transmission may occur across the placenta during pregnancy (in utero), through contact with maternal secretions and blood during labour and delivery, or after birth through breast milk. The timing of infection strongly influences outcomes: infections in the first trimester may cause miscarriage or congenital malformations, while later infections more often cause growth restriction or neonatal illness.

Prevention and treatment

Routine antenatal screening for HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B, rubella vaccination before pregnancy, intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis for group B streptococcus, and antiretroviral therapy for mothers living with HIV have greatly reduced mother-to-child transmission. In the case of HIV, elective caesarean delivery and antiretroviral prophylaxis for the newborn further reduce risk.
