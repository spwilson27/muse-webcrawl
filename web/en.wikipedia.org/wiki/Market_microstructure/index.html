Market microstructure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Market microstructure is a branch of financial economics that studies how the detailed institutional rules and processes of exchange — the "microstructure" of markets — determine prices, quotes, trades and trading costs. Whereas traditional finance often assumes frictionless markets, microstructure research explains how trading actually occurs: who trades, when, under what rules, and with what effect on price formation.

Core concepts

  • Bid–ask spread: the difference between the best quoted buying and selling prices. Microstructure theory decomposes the spread into order-processing costs, inventory costs borne by dealers, and adverse-selection costs arising from trading against better-informed participants.
  • Price discovery: the process by which new information is incorporated into transaction prices through the flow of orders and quotes.
  • Liquidity: the ability to trade quickly, in size, and with little price impact; commonly characterised by tightness (spread), depth (volume available) and resiliency (speed of recovery after trades).
  • Information asymmetry: models in which some traders possess private information, influencing quotes and trading strategies; canonical models include those of Kyle (1985) and Glosten and Milgrom (1985).
  • Market design: the comparison of trading mechanisms — continuous limit order books, periodic call auctions, dealer (quote-driven) markets and hybrid structures — and of rules on tick sizes, transparency and order types.

History

The field is often traced to Harold Demsetz's 1968 paper "The Cost of Transacting," which analysed the bid–ask spread as the price of immediacy. The availability of high-frequency intraday data from the 1980s onward allowed empirical testing of microstructure models and spurred research on intraday patterns of volatility, volume and spreads. Modern topics include high-frequency trading, dark pools, fragmentation of trading across venues, and the market effects of algorithmic execution.

Applications

Microstructure research informs exchange design, securities regulation, transaction-cost analysis for institutional investors, and the measurement of market quality and efficiency.
