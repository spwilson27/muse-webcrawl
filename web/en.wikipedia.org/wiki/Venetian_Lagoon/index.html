The Venetian Lagoon (Italian: Laguna di Venezia) is an enclosed bay of the Adriatic Sea in northeastern Italy, in which the city of Venice is located. The lagoon covers about 550 square kilometres (210 sq mi) and is the largest wetland in the Mediterranean basin. Only about 8 percent of its area is dry land — the city of Venice and numerous smaller islands — while roughly 11 percent is permanently covered by open water or dredged channels and the remaining 80 percent is tidal mudflat and salt marsh.

Formation and geography

The lagoon formed after the last Ice Age as rivers including the Po, Adige, Brenta and Piave deposited sediment along the coast and longshore currents built up barrier islands (lidi). Three inlets — Lido, Malamocco and Chioggia — connect the lagoon to the Adriatic and allow the tides that flush it twice daily. Major islands include Murano, famous for glassmaking, Burano for lace, Torcello, and the barrier islands of Lido and Pellestrina, which protect the lagoon from the open sea.

Human management

For centuries the Republic of Venice actively managed the lagoon, diverting rivers such as the Brenta and Piave away from it to prevent silting, maintaining channels and sea defences such as the murazzi. The lagoon provided Venice with its defence: the shallow, shifting channels were navigable only to local pilots, and no enemy fleet ever took the city from the sea.

Environmental issues

In the 20th century, groundwater extraction at the industrial port of Marghera caused subsidence, and dredging of deep shipping channels increased erosion and the severity of flooding (acqua alta) in Venice. The MOSE system of mobile flood barriers at the three inlets, first operated in 2020, is designed to protect the city from exceptional tides. The lagoon and Venice were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
