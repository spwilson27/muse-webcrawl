Child Ballads

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Child Ballads are 305 traditional ballads from England and Scotland, and their American variants, anthologised by Francis James Child during the second half of the 19th century. Their lyrics and Child's studies of them were published as The English and Scottish Popular Ballads in ten volumes (1882–1898). Child Ballads are conventionally cited by their number in the collection, much as scholars cite the Roud Folk Song Index for other traditional songs.

Origins and themes

The ballads range in age from medieval romances to broadsides of the 17th and 18th centuries. Recurrent themes include Robin Hood (a cycle occupying numbers 117–154), King Arthur, border reivers, tragic love, supernatural encounters, historical events such as the battles of Otterburn and Flodden, and religious legends. Famous examples include "Tam Lin" (No. 39), "Lord Randall" (No. 12), "Barbara Allen" (No. 84), "The Daemon Lover" (No. 243), "Thomas Rymer" (No. 37) and "Sir Patrick Spens" (No. 58).

The collection

Child, a professor at Harvard, built on earlier collections such as Thomas Percy's Reliques (1765) and the Danish Danmarks gamle Folkeviser, gathering variants from manuscripts, broadsides and oral tradition and printing extensive headnotes comparing each ballad with analogues across Europe. Though he died before completing the planned introduction, the collection established the canon of English-language traditional balladry.

Influence

Child Ballads formed the bedrock of the British and American folk revivals. Songs from the collection were recorded by artists including Joan Baez, Steeleye Span, Fairport Convention and Martin Carthy, and many passed into the Appalachian tradition collected by Cecil Sharp in the early 20th century.
