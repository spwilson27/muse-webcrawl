Preacher

A preacher is a person who delivers sermons or homilies on religious topics to an assembly of people. Preachers are most often associated with Christianity, where preaching — the proclamation and exposition of the gospel — is a central function of ordained ministers and, in many traditions, of lay speakers as well. The term is applied more loosely to religious exhorters in other faiths, and figuratively to anyone who lectures others on moral conduct ("preachy" being the pejorative adjective).

History

Preaching in the Christian tradition traces to Jesus and the apostles, and was developed by figures such as John Chrysostom, famed for his eloquence. Medieval mendicant orders, notably the Franciscans and the Dominicans — the "Order of Preachers" — made itinerant preaching central to their mission. The Protestant Reformation dramatically elevated the sermon, making it the centrepiece of worship in Reformed and evangelical churches; Puritan preaching manuals systematised sermon structure. Revival movements produced celebrated open-air and pulpit preachers such as George Whitefield, John Wesley, Charles Spurgeon, and in the twentieth century Billy Graham.

Roles and styles

Preachers may be parish ministers preaching weekly to a congregation, itinerant evangelists, street preachers, or broadcasters (televangelists). Styles range from the expository — verse-by-verse exposition of a text — to the topical and the revivalist appeal. In traditions such as African American churches, preaching is often a call-and-response performance with strong rhetorical and musical elements. Analogous offices exist in other religions, including the Islamic khatib, who delivers the Friday khutbah. See also sermon and homiletics.
