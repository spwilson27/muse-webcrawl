Ptah is an ancient Egyptian deity, a creator god and patron of craftsmen and architects. In the triad of Memphis, he is the husband of Sekhmet and the father of Nefertem. He was also regarded as the father of the sage Imhotep. Ptah was worshipped from at least the Early Dynastic Period, and his cult centre at Memphis was one of the most important in Egypt for over three thousand years.

Ptah is generally represented in the guise of a mummiform man with a closely fitting skullcap, holding a sceptre that combines the was, djed, and ankh symbols. His shrouded form is pierced only by his hands, and he wears a straight false beard of the kind worn by gods rather than the curved beard of pharaohs.

Memphite theology

According to the Memphite theology recorded on the Shabaka Stone, Ptah created the world not by physical labour but by the thought of his heart and the speech of his tongue: he conceived the world in his heart and brought it into being by speaking its name. This doctrine, in which creation occurs through intellect and word, has often been compared with later philosophical ideas of the logos.

Cult and influence

The high priests of Ptah at Memphis bore the title "Greatest of the Directors of Craftsmanship", reflecting Ptah's role as divine craftsman. His great temple at Memphis, whose enclosure wall gave rise to the name Hikuptah ("House of the Soul of Ptah"), is thought to be the origin, via Greek Aigyptos, of the name "Egypt". The Greeks identified Ptah with their smith-god Hephaestus. In later periods Ptah was frequently combined with the funerary gods Sokar and Osiris as Ptah-Sokar-Osiris, and the sacred Apis bull was regarded as a living manifestation of Ptah.
