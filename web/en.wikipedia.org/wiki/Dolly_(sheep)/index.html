Dolly (sheep)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dolly (5 July 1996 – 14 February 2003) was a female domestic sheep, and the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell. She was created at the Roslin Institute in Scotland by a team led by Ian Wilmut and Keith Campbell, using somatic cell nuclear transfer from a mammary gland cell of a six-year-old Finn Dorset ewe; her birth, announced in February 1997, proved that a cell from an adult body could be reprogrammed to create a whole new animal.

Creation and life

Of 277 reconstructed eggs, Dolly was the only lamb born. She was named after singer Dolly Parton (because the donor cell came from a mammary gland). Dolly lived at Roslin, bred normally and produced six lambs. She developed arthritis unusually young and a progressive lung disease caused by a virus common in housed sheep, and was euthanised at six and a half years old — about half a sheep's typical lifespan.

Significance

Early concern that clones might age prematurely (based on Dolly's shortened telomeres) was eased by later studies of other cloned sheep, including Dolly's genomic 'sisters', which aged normally. Dolly's preserved body is displayed at the National Museum of Scotland. Her creation ignited worldwide debate on cloning ethics, especially regarding humans, and accelerated stem-cell research — including the later development of induced pluripotent stem cells.
