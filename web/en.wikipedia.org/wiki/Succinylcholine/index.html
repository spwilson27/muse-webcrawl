Succinylcholine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trade names: Anectine, Quelicin, others
Class: Depolarising neuromuscular blocker
Onset: 30–60 seconds (IV)
Duration: 5–10 minutes

Succinylcholine, also known as suxamethonium, is a medication used to cause short-term paralysis as part of general anaesthesia. It is given to help with endotracheal intubation and to provide skeletal muscle relaxation during surgery or mechanical ventilation. It is administered by intravenous or intramuscular injection.

Medical uses

Succinylcholine has an exceptionally rapid onset and short duration of action, which makes it the traditional drug of choice for rapid sequence induction of anaesthesia, when a patient's airway must be secured quickly, for example in emergency surgery or in patients at risk of aspiration. It is also used to facilitate intubation in the emergency department and intensive care, and to prevent muscular contractions during electroconvulsive therapy.

Mechanism of action

Succinylcholine is a depolarising neuromuscular blocking agent. Structurally, it resembles two molecules of acetylcholine joined together. It binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the motor end plate, initially causing muscle fasciculations, then persistent depolarisation that renders the muscle unable to respond to further stimulation, producing flaccid paralysis. It is rapidly hydrolysed in the plasma by the enzyme butyrylcholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase), accounting for its brief effect.

Adverse effects

Side effects include muscle pain after surgery, increased intraocular and intragastric pressure, rises in serum potassium (dangerous in burns, crush injuries, and some neuromuscular diseases), bradycardia, and prolonged paralysis in patients with atypical or deficient pseudocholinesterase. Succinylcholine is a triggering agent for malignant hyperthermia in susceptible individuals, a potentially fatal reaction requiring treatment with dantrolene.

History

The compound was first described in the early twentieth century and introduced into clinical practice around 1951. Despite its side-effect profile, it remains on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, although the non-depolarising agent rocuronium (with its reversal agent sugammadex) is increasingly used as an alternative.
