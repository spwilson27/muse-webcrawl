Oomoto

Oomoto (大本, "great origin" or "great foundation"), also known as Oomoto-kyo, is a Japanese new religious movement that arose within the Shinto tradition in the late nineteenth century. It is one of the best-known of the Shinto-derived new religions and has influenced several other movements, including the background from which aikido emerged.

History

The movement traces its origin to 1892, when Deguchi Nao (1837–1918), an illiterate peasant woman from Ayabe, received what she understood as revelations from a deity and began producing automatic writings known as the O-fudesaki. Her son-in-law Deguchi Onisaburo (1871–1948) became the movement's organizer and co-founder, systematizing its teachings in works such as the Reikai Monogatari and promoting an energetic program of missionary activity, interfaith cooperation, and the international auxiliary language Esperanto.

Oomoto was suppressed by the Japanese government in incidents in 1921 and 1935, during which its headquarters were destroyed and leaders imprisoned, largely because its teachings about a coming world renewal were seen as subversive. After the Second World War it was re-established and resumed activity.

Beliefs and practices

Oomoto teaches the worship of one supreme God under many manifestations, the sanctity of agriculture and the arts, and the coming spiritual renewal of the world. It maintains headquarters at Ayabe and Kameoka in Kyoto Prefecture, promotes traditional Japanese arts such as the tea ceremony and Noh, and has long supported Esperanto as a tool for world peace.
