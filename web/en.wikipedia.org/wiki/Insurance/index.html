Insurance

Insurance is a means of protection from financial loss in which, in exchange for a fee (a premium), one party (the insurer) agrees to compensate another party (the policyholder) upon the occurrence of a specified loss, damage, illness or death. It is a form of risk management used to hedge against the risk of a contingent or uncertain loss: the losses of the few are spread across the premiums of the many. The insurer pools risks from many policyholders, using actuarial science to estimate expected claims and set premiums; insurers often transfer part of their own risk to reinsurers.

Principles

Insurance rests on several doctrines. Insurable interest: the policyholder must stand to lose from the event insured. Utmost good faith (uberrima fides): both parties must disclose material facts. Indemnity: most non-life policies restore the insured to their prior position, no more. Subrogation: after paying a claim, the insurer may pursue third parties responsible for the loss. Contribution and proximate cause govern multiple policies and causation. Because the insured is protected, insurance creates moral hazard and adverse selection, which insurers counter with deductibles (excesses), exclusions, underwriting and claims investigation.

History

Risk-spreading arrangements are ancient: Babylonian merchants used bottomry contracts recorded in the Code of Hammurabi, and Chinese and Indian traders spread cargoes across many vessels. Marine insurance developed in medieval Italian ports and in London's coffee houses — Lloyd's grew from Edward Lloyd's coffee house (c. 1688). Fire insurance followed the Great Fire of London (1666); life insurance was placed on an actuarial footing in the 18th century (the Equitable Life, 1762). Modern insurance expanded with industrialisation, compulsory motor and employers' liability cover, and 20th-century welfare states' social insurance for health, unemployment and pensions.

Types of insurance

  • Life insurance — pays on death or survival to a term; includes term, whole-life and annuities.
  • Health insurance — covers medical costs, privately or through social schemes.
  • Property insurance — fire, theft, flood and other damage to buildings and contents.
  • Liability (casualty) insurance — legal liability to third parties, including motor third-party and professional indemnity.
  • Motor, marine, aviation and transport — vehicles, ships, aircraft and cargo.
  • Specialty lines — travel, pet, crop, credit, cyber, directors' and officers' cover, and more.

The insurance industry

Insurers earn money from underwriting and from investing premium income (the "float") before claims are paid. The industry is heavily regulated for solvency — through regimes such as the EU's Solvency II and US state-based regulation — because insurers must remain able to pay claims decades into the future. The largest markets are the United States, China, Japan and the United Kingdom. Distribution runs through agents, brokers and, increasingly, direct online and comparison channels; insurtech firms apply data analytics and automation to underwriting and claims.
