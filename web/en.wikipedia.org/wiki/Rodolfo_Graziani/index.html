Rodolfo Graziani, 1st Marquis of Neghelli (11 August 1882 – 11 January 1955) was an Italian military officer in the Kingdom of Italy's Royal Army, primarily remembered for his campaigns in Africa during the Fascist era. As governor of Cyrenaica and commander in Libya, and later as Viceroy of Italian Ethiopia, he employed brutal methods — concentration camps, mass reprisals and poison gas — that earned him the names "Butcher of Fezzan" and "Butcher of Ethiopia". He commanded the Italian Tenth Army in the disastrous invasion of Egypt in 1940 and ended the war as defence minister of Mussolini's Italian Social Republic.

Early career and Libya

Born at Filettino, Graziani served in Eritrea before the First World War, became Italy's youngest colonel during that war, and in the 1920s directed the "reconquest" of Libya. As vice-governor and then governor of Cyrenaica (1930–1934), he waged a counter-insurgency against the Senussi resistance of Omar Mukhtar: the Bedouin population was deported to concentration camps in the desert, where tens of thousands died, livestock was slaughtered, and a barbed-wire barrier was built along the Egyptian frontier. Mukhtar was captured and hanged in 1931. Graziani's methods subdued Cyrenaica at an enormous human cost documented by historians such as Angelo Del Boca.

Ethiopia

In the Second Italo-Ethiopian War (1935–1936) Graziani commanded the southern front from Italian Somaliland, advancing to take Neghelli and Harar; he succeeded Pietro Badoglio as Viceroy of Ethiopia in 1936. After an assassination attempt against him in Addis Ababa on 19 February 1937, his troops carried out the Yekatit 12 massacre, killing thousands of Ethiopians over three days; the monastery of Debre Libanos was also destroyed and its monks executed. He was recalled in late 1937.

Second World War and after

Made Chief of Staff of the Army in 1939, Graziani took command in Libya in 1940 and, under pressure from Mussolini, invaded Egypt in September 1940, halting at Sidi Barrani. The British Operation Compass destroyed his Tenth Army and took over 100,000 prisoners; he resigned in March 1941. After the 1943 armistice he joined the German-backed Italian Social Republic as minister of defence, commanding the Ligurian Army. In 1950 an Italian military court sentenced him to 19 years for collaboration, but he was released after a few months; he became honorary president of the neo-fascist Italian Social Movement and died in Rome in 1955. Ethiopia's requests that he be tried for war crimes were never granted, and controversy over his commemoration in Italy — including a publicly funded mausoleum at Affile in 2012 — continues.
