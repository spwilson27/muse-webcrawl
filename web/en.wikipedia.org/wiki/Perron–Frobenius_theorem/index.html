Perron–Frobenius theorem

Perron–Frobenius theorem
FieldLinear algebra
Proved byOskar Perron (1907); Ferdinand Georg Frobenius (1912)
ConcernsEigenvalues and eigenvectors of positive and nonnegative matrices

In matrix theory, the Perron–Frobenius theorem, proved by Oskar Perron (1907) and Ferdinand Georg Frobenius (1912), asserts that a real square matrix with positive entries has a unique eigenvalue of largest magnitude, with a corresponding eigenvector whose components are all positive.

Statement

Let A be an n × n matrix with all entries strictly positive. Then:

  • There is a positive real number λP, called the Perron root or Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue, which is an eigenvalue of A and is strictly larger in absolute value than any other eigenvalue.
  • λP is a simple eigenvalue (algebraic multiplicity one).
  • There exists an eigenvector v for λP whose entries are all strictly positive (the Perron vector), and it is the only (up to scaling) eigenvector with nonnegative entries.

Frobenius extended the result to irreducible nonnegative matrices, where the spectral radius is still a simple eigenvalue with a positive eigenvector, though other eigenvalues may share its magnitude.

Applications

The theorem underpins the analysis of Markov chains: a stochastic matrix has spectral radius 1, and the Perron vector gives the stationary distribution. It is also used in population dynamics (Leslie matrices), economics (Leontief input–output models), demography, and the ranking of web pages — Google's PageRank algorithm is essentially a Perron vector computation for a modified link matrix.

History

Perron published the positive-matrix result in 1907. Frobenius generalised it in 1912, developing the theory of irreducible nonnegative matrices and introducing concepts such as the index of imprimitivity.
