Abraham (Arabic: إِبْرَاهِيم, romanized: Ibrāhīm) is a prophet and messenger of God in Islam, appearing in 35 chapters of the Quran, more than any other biblical character apart from Moses. He is held to have been a hanif — a primordial monotheist — and the archetype of submission to God.

Quranic narrative

The Quran depicts Abraham rejecting the idolatry of his people, debating with a king (often identified as Nimrod), and being thrown into a fire from which God saves him. He is commanded to sacrifice his son — Islamic tradition generally identifies the son as Ishmael (Ismail) — and is stopped by God, an event commemorated at Eid al-Adha. Together with Ishmael, he is said to have raised the foundations of the Kaaba in Mecca.

Legacy

Abraham is called Khalilullah, the "Friend of God", and the Quran presents the "religion of Abraham" (millat Ibrahim) as the original monotheistic faith that Islam restores. Muslims invoke blessings on Abraham in daily prayers, and the stations associated with him, including the Maqam Ibrahim near the Kaaba, feature in the Hajj pilgrimage.
