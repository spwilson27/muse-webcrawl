Forest law

Forest law in medieval England was a body of law, separate from the common law, that governed the royal forests — large tracts of land (not necessarily wooded) reserved by the Crown for hunting. Introduced by the Norman kings, especially William the Conqueror, forest law was notoriously harsh and a persistent source of baronial grievance.

Scope and offences

Within a royal forest, the law protected the "vert" (the vegetation providing cover) and the "venison" (the game, chiefly red deer, fallow deer, roe deer, and wild boar). Offences included hunting the king's deer, cutting timber, clearing land (assarting), and encroaching on the forest (purpresture). Early penalties included blinding, mutilation, and death; these were progressively replaced by fines and imprisonment.

Administration

Forests were administered by foresters, verderers, and regarders, with special courts — the court of attachment, the swainmote, and the itinerant forest eyre presided over by justices in eyre. The New Forest in Hampshire is the most famous surviving example.

Decline

The Charter of the Forest (1217), issued alongside the reissue of Magna Carta, moderated the law, disafforested lands seized by earlier kings, and abolished death and mutilation for taking venison. Forest law decayed over the following centuries as forests were sold or disafforested, though fragments of the system survive, notably in the New Forest and the Forest of Dean.
