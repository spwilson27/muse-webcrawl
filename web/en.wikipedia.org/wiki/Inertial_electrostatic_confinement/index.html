Inertial electrostatic confinement

Inertial electrostatic confinement (IEC) is a class of nuclear fusion devices that use electric fields to heat and confine ions to fusion conditions. The best-known IEC device is the fusor (Farnsworth–Hirsch fusor), in which ions are accelerated by a high voltage toward a central region where they may collide and fuse.

Principle

In a fusor, a spherical wire cage (the inner grid) is held at a large negative potential inside a vacuum chamber. Positively charged fuel ions introduced into the chamber accelerate toward the centre, pass through the grid, and oscillate back and forth; collisions between ions and between ions and background gas produce fusion reactions — typically deuterium–deuterium or deuterium–tritium. A visible "star mode" of glowing plasma beams forms through the grid apertures.

History and uses

The concept was developed in the 1950s–60s by Philo Farnsworth and Robert Hirsch. IEC devices easily produce neutrons and are used commercially as neutron generators and by many amateur experimenters ("fusioneers"), but fundamental losses — especially collisions with the grid and non-Maxwellian energy distributions — make them unsuitable, in their simple form, for net power generation.
