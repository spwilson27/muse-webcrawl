War Measures Act

The War Measures Act (French: Loi sur les mesures de guerre) was a Canadian statute that gave the federal government sweeping emergency powers in time of war, invasion, or insurrection. Enacted in 1914, it was invoked three times — during both World Wars and during the October Crisis of 1970 — before being repealed in 1988 and replaced by the Emergencies Act.

Invocations

First World War: The Act was invoked in August 1914; under it, more than 8,500 "enemy aliens", mostly Ukrainians and other Europeans, were interned in camps across Canada, and civil liberties were widely curtailed.

Second World War: Invoked in 1939, the Act underpinned conscription measures, censorship, and — most controversially — the internment and forced relocation of about 22,000 Japanese Canadians from the Pacific coast from 1942, along with the seizure of their property.

October Crisis: In October 1970, after the Front de libération du Québec (FLQ) kidnapped British diplomat James Cross and Quebec minister Pierre Laporte, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau's government invoked the Act — its only use in peacetime. Nearly 500 people were arrested and detained, most without charge, and troops patrolled Montreal. Laporte was murdered by his captors; Cross was released. Quebec's legislature later passed its own special legislation, and the detentions remained controversial.

Legacy

The Act was criticised for granting extraordinary power with few safeguards: a proclamation could not be challenged in the courts, and its use against Japanese Canadians and in 1970 made it a symbol of emergency overreach. The Emergencies Act of 1988, which replaced it, added parliamentary oversight, time limits, and explicit protection of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
