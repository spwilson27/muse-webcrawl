Western alienation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Western alienation, in Canadian politics, refers to the perception among residents of the western provinces — British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba — that the federal government in Ottawa neglects the region's interests in favour of central Canada, particularly Ontario and Quebec. The concept has shaped party politics and periodic separatist movements in the West for more than a century.

Causes and grievances

Recurring grievances include federal control of natural resources, freight rates, tariffs that historically favoured central Canadian manufacturing, the equalization payment system, and Ottawa's energy policies. A defining episode was the National Energy Program of 1980, introduced by the government of Pierre Trudeau, which Alberta's oil industry perceived as an appropriation of provincial resource revenues. The phrase "The West wants in" became the slogan of the Reform Party of Canada, founded in 1987, which channelled western frustration into demands for Senate reform and fiscal decentralization.

Contemporary expression

Western alienation surged again in the late 2010s amid pipeline disputes and carbon pricing, spawning the informal "Wexit" movement advocating separation of western provinces. Surveys show that feelings of alienation fluctuate with economic conditions and the governing party in Ottawa.
