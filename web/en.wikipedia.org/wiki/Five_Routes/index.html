Five Routes

In American football, the term five routes most commonly refers to the basic group of pass routes on the standard route tree run by a receiver: the flat, the slant, the out, the in (dig) family of intermediate routes, and the deep go (fly) route — though the label is also used informally for the first five branches of the nine-route tree taught to receivers at most levels of the game.

The route tree

The conventional route tree numbers routes so that even numbers break toward the middle of the field and odd numbers break toward the sideline, in increasing depth:

  • 1 – Flat: an immediate break to the sideline at or behind the line of scrimmage.
  • 2 – Slant: a quick three-step diagonal break toward the middle.
  • 3 – Comeback: a deep stem downfield followed by a break back toward the sideline and the quarterback.
  • 4 – Curl: a 10–12 yard stem, turning back to face the quarterback.
  • 5 – Out: a break to the sideline at about 10–12 yards.
  • 6 – Dig (in): a break across the middle at 12–15 yards.
  • 7 – Corner: a deep break toward the corner of the end zone.
  • 8 – Post: a deep break toward the goal post.
  • 9 – Go (fly/fade): a straight sprint downfield.

Usage in play design

Coaches combine the five fundamental routes — short (flat, slant), intermediate (out, dig/curl) and deep (go) — into route concepts that stretch a defence horizontally and vertically at the same time, such as the flood, smash and levels concepts. Receivers adjust their routes against man or zone coverage ("option" or "choice" routes), converting, for example, a go route into a comeback against soft coverage.

Other uses

"Five routes" is also the English name sometimes given to transport groupings, most notably the five sacred routes (Gokaidō) of Edo-period Japan — the Tōkaidō, Nakasendō, Kōshū Kaidō, Ōshū Kaidō and Nikkō Kaidō that radiated from Nihonbashi in Edo — and to various sets of five trade or pilgrimage roads in historical geography.
