Kerr effect

Placeholder image: a Kerr cell in an optical setup.

The Kerr effect, also called the quadratic electro-optic effect, is a change in the refractive index of an isotropic material in response to an applied electric field. The induced birefringence is proportional to the square of the field strength, distinguishing it from the linear Pockels effect. It was discovered in 1875 by the Scottish physicist John Kerr.

Physical description

In a Kerr medium, an electric field aligns polar molecules or distorts electron clouds so that the material behaves like a uniaxial crystal whose optic axis lies along the field. The refractive-index difference between polarisations parallel and perpendicular to the field is Δn = λ K E², where K is the Kerr constant of the material and λ the wavelength of light.

Applications

The Kerr cell, a glass cell filled with a high-K liquid such as nitrobenzene between electrodes, works as an extremely fast optical shutter, with response times in the nanosecond range; Kerr cells were historically used in high-speed photography, laser Q-switching, and optical communications. The related optical Kerr effect, in which the field is that of a light beam itself, drives phenomena in nonlinear optics such as self-focusing and Kerr-lens mode-locking of lasers.

Magneto-optic Kerr effect

A distinct phenomenon sharing Kerr's name is the magneto-optic Kerr effect, the rotation of the polarisation of light reflected from a magnetised surface, widely used to study magnetic materials and in magneto-optical data storage.
