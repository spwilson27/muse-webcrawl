Ezekiel (Hebrew: יְחֶזְקֵאל, Yəḥezqēʾl, "God strengthens") is the central protagonist of the Book of Ezekiel in the Hebrew Bible. A priest of the Jerusalem Temple who was deported to Babylonia in the exile of 597 BCE, he is counted among the major prophets and is renowned for some of the most vivid and strange visions in biblical literature.

Life

According to the book that bears his name, Ezekiel was the son of Buzi, of a priestly family, and was among the Judean captives taken to Babylon by Nebuchadnezzar II after the first siege of Jerusalem. He settled at Tel Abib on the Chebar canal (usually identified with the Kebar river in Mesopotamia) and received his prophetic call in the fifth year of the exile, around 593 BCE. His ministry, dated internally across more than two decades, spans the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BCE and the early years of hope for restoration.

The Book of Ezekiel

The book's 48 chapters fall broadly into three parts: oracles of judgement against Judah and Jerusalem (chapters 1–24), oracles against the foreign nations (25–32), and oracles of consolation and restoration (33–48). Ezekiel is famous for his inaugural vision of the divine chariot — the merkabah — borne by four living creatures, each with four faces, later identified with the cherubim and the four evangelists in Christian tradition. Other celebrated passages include the allegory of the foundling girl (chapter 16), the parable of the valley of dry bones (chapter 37), in which scattered bones come together and live, and the concluding vision of a restored temple, city and land (chapters 40–48).

Themes

Ezekiel emphasises individual responsibility — "the soul that sins shall die" — against the proverb that children suffer for their parents' sins, alongside the holiness of God, the glory of the Lord departing from and returning to the temple, and the promise of a new heart and a new spirit. His symbolic actions, such as lying bound on one side for months, lying siege to a model of Jerusalem, and shaving his head, are among the most extreme in prophetic literature.

Veneration

Ezekiel is recognised as a prophet in Judaism, Christianity and Islam (where he is associated by many commentators with the Qur'anic figure Dhul-Kifl). A tomb in Al Kifl, Iraq, has been venerated for centuries as the burial place of the prophet by Jews, Christians and Muslims alike.
