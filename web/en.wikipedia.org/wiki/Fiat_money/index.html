Fiat money

Fiat money is a type of currency that is not backed by a precious metal, such as gold or silver, or by any other tangible asset or commodity. Fiat currency is typically designated by a government to be legal tender, and it is often authorized and regulated by a central authority, such as a central bank. In a fiat system, the currency is usually made of paper or metal and is not directly convertible into a fixed amount of a commodity. The word fiat is Latin for "let it be done" or "it shall be", reflecting that the money's value is established by decree.

History

China was the first country to use paper money, during the Tang and especially the Song dynasty; the jiaozi notes of the 11th century and the paper currency of the Yuan dynasty under Kublai Khan circulated as state-issued money. Chinese fiat experiments repeatedly ended in over-issue and inflation, and the Ming dynasty abandoned paper money in the 15th century. In the West, early examples include the colonial American bills of credit and the French assignats of the 1790s, which likewise depreciated through over-issue. Through the 19th and early 20th centuries most major currencies were instead convertible into gold under the gold standard or, after 1944, into US dollars themselves convertible into gold under the Bretton Woods system. When President Richard Nixon suspended dollar convertibility into gold in 1971 (the "Nixon shock"), the world moved to a system of pure fiat currencies with floating exchange rates, which remains in place today.

Characteristics

Fiat money derives its value from the trust and confidence of the people who use it, from the requirement that taxes be paid in it, and from its status as legal tender for settling debts. Because it is not redeemable for a commodity, the supply of fiat money is controlled by monetary policy: central banks manage it through interest rates, open market operations, and reserve requirements, typically aiming for price stability. Its intrinsic value is close to zero — a banknote is worth far more as money than as paper — so its purchasing power depends entirely on the credibility of the issuer and the balance of supply and demand.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue that fiat money gives governments flexibility to respond to economic crises, since the money supply is not constrained by a fixed stock of metal, and that seigniorage (the profit from issuing money) accrues to the state. Critics point to the risk of inflation and hyperinflation when governments finance deficits by printing money, citing episodes such as Weimar Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, and Zimbabwe in the 2000s. Unlike commodity money, fiat currency can also lose value through loss of confidence, although well-managed fiat systems have proved durable.

Alternatives

Alternatives to fiat money include commodity money (gold and silver coinage), representative money (notes redeemable for a commodity), and, since 2009, decentralized cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, which are not issued by any government. Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are a digital form of fiat money issued directly by central banks.
