Nuclear chain reaction

A nuclear chain reaction occurs when one single nuclear reaction causes an average of one or more subsequent nuclear reactions, leading to the possibility of a self-propagating series of these reactions. In a fission chain reaction, a heavy nucleus such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239 absorbs a neutron and splits, releasing energy and two or three additional neutrons, which can in turn induce further fissions.

History

The concept was conceived by Hungarian physicist Leó Szilárd in 1933, shortly after the neutron was discovered. Szilárd patented the idea and later, with Enrico Fermi, developed the first artificial self-sustaining chain reaction in the Chicago Pile-1 reactor on 2 December 1942, as part of the Manhattan Project.

Control

In nuclear reactors, chain reactions are kept at a steady critical state using control rods of neutron-absorbing material such as boron or cadmium, together with a moderator that slows neutrons to thermal energies. The presence of delayed neutrons, emitted seconds after fission by some fission products, makes mechanical control of the reaction practical. In a nuclear weapon, by contrast, a supercritical mass is assembled so rapidly that the reaction grows explosively before the core blows itself apart.

Natural chain reactions

About 1.7 billion years ago, natural fission reactors operated at Oklo in present-day Gabon, where groundwater moderated uranium deposits rich enough in uranium-235 to sustain intermittent chain reactions.
