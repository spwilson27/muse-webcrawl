Ecological niche

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hutchinson's niche as an n-dimensional hypervolume

In ecology, a niche is the match of a species to a specific environmental condition — how it responds to the distribution of resources and competitors and how it in turn alters those same factors. The concept has been formulated in three classic ways: Joseph Grinnell defined the niche largely by habitat requirements; Charles Sutherland Elton emphasised the species' functional role in the community (what it eats and what eats it); and G. Evelyn Hutchinson formalised it as an n-dimensional hypervolume of environmental conditions within which the species can persist indefinitely.

Fundamental and realised niches

Hutchinson distinguished the fundamental niche — the full set of conditions a species could occupy in the absence of interactions — from the realised niche, the smaller portion actually occupied because of competition, predation and other biotic pressures. The competitive exclusion principle holds that two species cannot long occupy identical niches; where they overlap, character displacement and niche partitioning (by diet, time, or microhabitat, as in Robert MacArthur's warblers) reduce direct competition.

Applications

Niche theory underlies species-distribution modelling used to predict range shifts under climate change, assess invasion risk, and plan conservation. Modern debates consider whether community assembly is governed mainly by niche differences or by near-neutral drift, as proposed by Stephen Hubbell's unified neutral theory.
