Time-lapse photography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Time-lapse photography is a technique in which the frequency at which film frames or photographs are captured (the frame rate) is much lower than the frequency used to view the sequence. When played at normal speed, time appears to be moving faster and thus lapsing. Processes that would normally appear subtle and slow to the human eye — the motion of the Sun and stars across the sky, cloud movement, plant growth, or the progress of a construction project — become clearly visible over seconds or minutes.

Technique

A time-lapse sequence can be made by filming at a low frame rate or, more commonly today, by taking still photographs at intervals with an intervalometer and assembling them into video. Choosing the interval is a balance: too short, and the file is unwieldy and motion appears jerky; too long, and events are missed. Exposure smoothing ("the holy grail" of day-to-night transitions) and small camera movements on motorized sliders or pan heads add polish. A moving variant, in which the camera travels through space during a long sequence, is called hyperlapse.

History and uses

The technique dates to the earliest decades of cinema: French pioneer Georges Méliès used related tricks, and F. Percy Smith's 1910 film The Birth of a Flower showed plants growing with time-lapse. Walt Disney's nature documentaries and later films such as Koyaanisqatsi (1982) brought time-lapse to wide audiences. Today built-in time-lapse modes on smartphones and action cameras have made it an everyday technique for documenting weather, astronomy, biology and urban life.
