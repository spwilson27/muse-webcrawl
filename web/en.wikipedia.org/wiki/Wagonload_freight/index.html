Wagonload freight

Wagonload freight is a form of rail freight transport in which individual freight wagons from different customers are collected, sorted in marshalling yards, and combined into trains for the main haul, then distributed again to their destinations. It contrasts with block trains (unit trains), in which a whole train carries a single customer's load from one origin to one destination, and with less-than-wagonload services for smaller consignments.

Operations

In a typical wagonload network, local trip workings collect wagons from industrial sidings and bring them to a marshalling yard, where they are sorted over a hump into classification tracks for different destinations. Trunk trains move the wagons between yards, and the reverse process delivers them to the receiving customer. This hub-and-spoke system allows railways to serve many small and medium-volume shippers but incurs time and handling costs at each yard.

Decline and revival

Wagonload traffic declined steeply in the later 20th century, especially in Britain and North America, as road haulage captured smaller consignments and marshalling yards closed. It remains significant in much of continental Europe, where operators such as DB Cargo and the Xrail alliance have sought to improve the reliability and competitiveness of single-wagonload services; environmental policy favouring modal shift from road to rail has renewed interest in the segment.
