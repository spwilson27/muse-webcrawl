Confirmation theory

Confirmation theory is the branch of philosophy of science and epistemology concerned with the logic by which evidence supports, confirms, or disconfirms scientific hypotheses and theories. Its central question is: under what conditions, and to what degree, does a body of evidence E confirm a hypothesis H?

Hypothetico-deductivism

On the classical hypothetico-deductive account, a hypothesis is confirmed by evidence when the evidence is a true observational consequence deduced from the hypothesis, and disconfirmed when a deduced consequence turns out false. This simple picture faces difficulties: irrelevant conjunctions (if E confirms H, it appears also to confirm H conjoined with any arbitrary claim), and the problem of deciding which part of a theoretical network is at fault when a prediction fails (the Duhem–Quine problem).

Nicod's criterion and the ravens paradox

Jean Nicod proposed that a universal generalization "All Fs are Gs" is confirmed by its positive instances (an F that is G) and falsified by an F that is not G. Combined with the equivalence condition — whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms any logically equivalent hypothesis — this yields Carl Hempel's famous ravens paradox: since "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens," observing a white shoe (a non-black non-raven) paradoxically seems to confirm that all ravens are black.

Bayesian confirmation theory

The dominant contemporary framework is Bayesian confirmation theory, in which evidence E confirms hypothesis H (relative to background knowledge) if and only if learning E raises the probability of H: P(H|E) > P(H). Degrees of confirmation can then be measured by quantities such as the probability difference, the ratio P(H|E)/P(H), or the likelihood ratio P(E|H)/P(E|¬H). Bayesianism gives natural treatments of the ravens paradox (a white shoe confirms the raven hypothesis only minutely, because it is one observation among an enormous reference class), of the evidential weight of novel predictions, and of the "problem of old evidence." Related approaches include error statistics, likelihoodism, and inference to the best explanation.
