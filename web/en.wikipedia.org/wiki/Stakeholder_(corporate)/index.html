Stakeholder (corporate)

In a corporation, a stakeholder is a member of a group without whose support the organisation would cease to exist, in the formulation of the Stanford Research Institute, where the term was first used in its modern sense in 1963. The concept was developed into stakeholder theory by R. Edward Freeman in his 1984 book Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach: stakeholders are any groups or individuals who can affect, or are affected by, the achievement of the organisation's objectives.

Types of stakeholder

Stakeholders are commonly divided into:

  • Internal stakeholders — shareholders (owners), employees and managers;
  • External stakeholders — customers, suppliers, creditors, competitors, government and regulators, trade unions, local communities and society at large, including, in some accounts, the natural environment and future generations.

Primary stakeholders have a direct, usually contractual, relationship with the firm; secondary stakeholders, such as the media and activist groups, can influence it indirectly. Scholars such as Mitchell, Agle and Wood proposed classifying stakeholders by their possession of power, legitimacy and urgency, identifying the "salient" stakeholders to whom managers actually pay attention.

Stakeholder theory

Stakeholder theory holds that managers should take account of the interests of all stakeholders, not only maximise value for shareholders. It stands in contrast to shareholder value doctrine, popularised by Milton Friedman's 1970 essay arguing that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. The debate shapes modern discussions of corporate social responsibility, ESG (environmental, social and governance) criteria, and benefit corporations. In August 2019 the US Business Roundtable issued a statement redefining the purpose of a corporation as a commitment to all stakeholders — customers, employees, suppliers, communities and shareholders — a symbolic milestone widely reported as a shift away from shareholder primacy.

Critics argue that serving many masters dilutes accountability and managerial focus; defenders answer that long-term shareholder value itself depends on maintaining the support of employees, customers and communities.
