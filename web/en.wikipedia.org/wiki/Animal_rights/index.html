Animal rights

Animal rights is the philosophy according to which many or all sentient animals have moral worth independent of their utility to humans, and that their most basic interests — such as avoiding suffering — should be afforded the same consideration as similar interests of human beings. Advocates oppose the assignment of fundamental rights to nonhuman animals being denied on grounds of species membership alone, a position they call speciesism.

Key thinkersPeter Singer, Tom Regan, Gary Francione
Key textsAnimal Liberation (1975), The Case for Animal Rights (1983)

History

Ideas trace to ancient critiques of animal cruelty, 19th-century anti-vivisection movements and the founding of the RSPCA (1824). The modern movement took off in the 1970s with Richard Ryder coining speciesism and Peter Singer's Animal Liberation (1975); Tom Regan argued for rights on deontological grounds.

Positions and practice

The movement campaigns against factory farming, animal testing, fur and animals in entertainment; many supporters adopt vegetarianism or veganism and practise direct action. It is distinguished from animal welfare, which seeks humane treatment without necessarily challenging animal use. Philosophical debates concern sentience, personhood and legal standing for great apes and cetaceans.
