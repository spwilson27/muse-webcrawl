Acid

An acid is a molecule or ion capable of either donating a proton (a hydrogen cation, H+), known as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, or forming a covalent bond with an electron pair, known as a Lewis acid. The first category of acids are the proton donors, or Brønsted–Lowry acids. In the special case of aqueous solutions, proton donors form the hydronium ion H3O+ and are known as Arrhenius acids. Brønsted and Lowry generalized the Arrhenius theory to include non-aqueous solvents. A Brønsted–Lowry acid is usually a chemical compound; meanwhile, a Lewis acid is typically an atom or ion.

Definitions and concepts

Arrhenius definition

In 1884, Svante Arrhenius attributed the properties of acidity to hydrogen ions, H+, present in aqueous solutions. An Arrhenius acid is a substance that, when added to water, increases the concentration of H+ ions in the water. Chemists often write H+(aq) to refer to the hydrogen ion, but a bare hydrogen nucleus (a proton) does not exist alone in aqueous solution; it bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion, H3O+.

Brønsted–Lowry definition

The Brønsted–Lowry definition, formulated in 1923 by Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted and Thomas Martin Lowry, is the most widely used today: an acid is a proton donor, and a base is a proton acceptor. Unlike the Arrhenius definition, it does not require the acid to be in aqueous solution. When an acid donates a proton, it forms its conjugate base; conversely, a base that accepts a proton forms its conjugate acid.

Lewis definition

In the same year, Gilbert N. Lewis proposed a more general definition based on electron pairs: a Lewis acid is a substance that can accept a pair of electrons to form a covalent bond, while a Lewis base donates the electron pair. This definition includes species such as boron trifluoride (BF3), which act as acids despite containing no hydrogen.

Properties

Acids are commonly sour in taste (the word "acid" derives from the Latin acidus, meaning sour), turn blue litmus paper red, react with metals to produce hydrogen gas, and react with bases in neutralization reactions to form salts and water. Aqueous acids with a pH lower than 7 are more acidic the lower the pH; the term "acid" is also used loosely for any chemical that can react with a base, even in the Lewis sense.

Strength

Acid strength is measured by the acid dissociation constant, Ka, or equivalently its negative logarithm, pKa. Strong acids, such as hydrochloric acid, dissociate almost completely in water, whereas weak acids, such as acetic acid, dissociate only partially. Common strong acids include hydrochloric, sulfuric, nitric, and perchloric acids; common weak acids include acetic, citric, and hydrofluoric acids.

Applications

Acids have many industrial and biological uses. Sulfuric acid is one of the most produced industrial chemicals, used in fertilizer manufacture and mineral processing. Hydrochloric acid is secreted by the stomach to aid digestion. Vinegar, a dilute solution of acetic acid, is used in food preservation and cooking. Citric acid occurs naturally in citrus fruits, and ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is an essential nutrient.
