Habsburg Law

The Habsburg Law (German: Habsburgergesetz) was a law passed by the Constituent National Assembly of Austria on 3 April 1919. It dethroned the House of Habsburg-Lorraine as rulers of Austria, exiled those members who would not renounce dynastic claims and declare loyalty to the republic, and confiscated the dynasty's property in Austria. The law became part of the Austrian constitutional order.

Background

After the collapse of the Austro-Hungarian Empire in November 1918, Emperor Charles I "renounced participation" in state affairs but did not formally abdicate. The republican assembly, fearing restoration attempts, responded with the Habsburg Law in April 1919, days after Charles left for exile in Switzerland.

Provisions

The law expelled members of the House of Habsburg-Lorraine from Austria unless they renounced all claims to the former ruling house and declared themselves loyal citizens of the republic. It also transferred the dynasty's Austrian assets — including palaces, estates, and the Habsburg family fund — to the republic, in part to benefit war victims and invalids.

Later developments

The law was a factor in the failure of Charles's two restoration attempts in Hungary (1921). Otto von Habsburg, the last crown prince, renounced his claims in 1961 and was permitted to return to Austria in 1966 after a constitutional dispute. The exile aspects of the law were progressively softened, and in 2011 the remaining electoral disqualifications for dynasty members were removed, allowing Habsburgs to stand for the federal presidency.
