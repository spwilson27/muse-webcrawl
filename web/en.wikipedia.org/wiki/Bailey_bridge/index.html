Bailey bridge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Bailey bridge spanning a river, its prefabricated steel panels clearly visible]

A Bailey bridge is a type of portable, pre-fabricated truss bridge, developed in 1940–41 by the British for military use during the Second World War. It was designed by civil servant Sir Donald Bailey and was widely praised — Field Marshal Montgomery credited it as a major contribution to Allied victory.

Design

The bridge is assembled from standard steel panels (3.05 m long) pinned together, with transoms, stringers and timber or steel decking. Panels can be stacked in single, double or triple configurations to increase load capacity. All parts are light enough to be carried and launched by soldiers without cranes — the bridge is pushed out across the gap on rollers from one bank — yet strong enough for tanks.

History and use

Bailey bridges were first used in North Africa in 1942 and proved vital in Italy and northwest Europe, including the Rhine crossings of 1945. The system remains in military inventories and is frequently used for civilian disaster relief, with many wartime examples still serving as permanent rural bridges.
