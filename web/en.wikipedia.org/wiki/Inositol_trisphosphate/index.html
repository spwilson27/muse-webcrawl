Inositol trisphosphate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate
AbbreviationsIP3; InsP3
FormulaC6H15O15P3
RoleSecond messenger

Inositol trisphosphate, more precisely inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (abbreviated IP3), is a soluble inositol phosphate signaling molecule that acts as a second messenger in cells. It is produced by the hydrolysis of the membrane phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) by the enzyme phospholipase C, a reaction that also generates diacylglycerol (DAG), a second messenger that remains in the membrane.

Mechanism of action

After production, IP3 diffuses through the cytosol and binds to IP3 receptors, ligand-gated calcium channels located principally on the endoplasmic reticulum. Binding opens the channels and releases stored Ca2+ into the cytosol, raising intracellular free calcium concentration. The resulting calcium signals regulate a wide range of processes, including muscle contraction, secretion, fertilization, cell growth, and apoptosis. Together with DAG's activation of protein kinase C, the IP3/DAG pathway is one of the principal routes by which G protein-coupled receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases transduce signals.

Synthesis and degradation

Phospholipase C isoforms (PLC-β activated by Gq-family G proteins, PLC-γ activated by receptor tyrosine kinases, and others) cleave PIP2 to yield IP3 and DAG. IP3 signaling is terminated by its stepwise dephosphorylation to inositol, or by phosphorylation via IP3 3-kinase to inositol 1,3,4,5-tetrakisphosphate. Lithium, used in the treatment of bipolar disorder, inhibits inositol monophosphatase and thereby slows recycling of inositol, a basis of the inositol-depletion hypothesis of lithium's action.

History

The importance of inositol phosphates in signaling emerged from work by Lowell and Mabel Hokin in the 1950s on stimulated phospholipid turnover, and was established decisively in 1983 when Michael Berridge and colleagues demonstrated that IP3 releases calcium from intracellular stores.
