Ibuprofen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ibuprofen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used worldwide for pain, fever and Inflammation — in headaches, dental pain, period pain, muscle injuries and arthritis. It was developed in the 1960s by a Boots team led by Stewart Adams, seeking a safer alternative to Aspirin, and launched in the UK in 1969 before becoming a common over-the-counter medicine.

Action and use

Like other NSAIDs, ibuprofen blocks cyclo-oxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin production — reversibly, unlike aspirin. It is taken by mouth as tablets, liquids (common for children) or applied as gels. Typical over-the-counter adult dosing is 200–400 mg every 4–6 hours.

Risks

NSAIDs can cause stomach irritation and ulcer bleeding (reduced by taking with food), kidney problems, raised blood pressure and, with regular high-dose use, increased cardiovascular risk. They can worsen asthma in sensitive people and interact with blood-pressure medicines and anticoagulants. They are usually avoided in late pregnancy.
