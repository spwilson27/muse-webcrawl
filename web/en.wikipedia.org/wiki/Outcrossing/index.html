Outcrossing

Outcrossing (or outbreeding) is the practice or natural process of mating an individual with an unrelated or genetically distinct partner, in contrast to inbreeding. In plants it is called cross-pollination between different individuals (xenogamy). Outcrossing increases genetic diversity and heterozygosity in the offspring, generally masking deleterious recessive alleles and producing "hybrid vigour" (heterosis).

Mechanisms in nature

Many organisms have evolved mechanisms that enforce outcrossing: dioecy (separate male and female individuals) in animals and some plants; self-incompatibility systems and separation of male and female flowers or flowering times in plants; and behavioural avoidance of kin in many animals. Conversely, species that routinely self-fertilise or inbreed may suffer inbreeding depression when genetic diversity falls too low.

In breeding practice

Plant and animal breeders deliberately outcross to introduce new traits — such as disease resistance — into a line or breed, or to restore vigour to an inbred population. In dog breeding, an outcross mates dogs of different lines or even different breeds; in conservation biology, introducing unrelated individuals ("genetic rescue") has been used to save small populations, as with the Florida panther. Excessively distant crosses, however, can produce outbreeding depression, breaking up locally adapted gene combinations.
