Wave soldering

Wave soldering is a bulk soldering process used in the manufacture of printed circuit boards. A printed circuit board is passed over a pan of molten solder, in which a pump creates a standing wave; as the board contacts the wave, solder adheres to the exposed metal areas and forms joints with the component leads.

Process

The process has four stages: flux application, preheating, wave soldering, and cooling. Flux removes oxides and promotes wetting; preheating activates the flux and reduces thermal shock; the wave itself forms the joints; and controlled cooling solidifies the solder without cracking.

Advantages and limits

Wave soldering is fast and economical for through-hole assemblies and for mixed-technology boards. It is less suited to fine-pitch surface-mount components, which are generally soldered by reflow instead. Defects such as bridging, insufficient fill, and solder balls are controlled by adjusting wave height, conveyor speed, and solder temperature.
