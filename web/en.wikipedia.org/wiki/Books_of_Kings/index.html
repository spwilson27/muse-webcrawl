The Books of Kings (Hebrew: סֵפֶר מְלָכִים, Sefer Melakhim) are the ninth and tenth books of the Hebrew Bible, found as two books (1 Kings and 2 Kings) in most modern editions. Together they conclude the Deuteronomistic history, a narrative running from the death of David to the release of the exiled king Jehoiachin in Babylon.

Contents

1 Kings opens with the succession of Solomon, the building of the Temple in Jerusalem, and Solomon's apostasy, which leads to the division of the kingdom after his death into the northern Kingdom of Israel and the southern Kingdom of Judah. The narrative then alternates between the two kingdoms, evaluating each king by his fidelity to Yahweh.

The prophets Elijah and Elisha dominate the middle chapters. 2 Kings recounts the fall of Israel to Assyria in 722 BCE, the reforms of Hezekiah and Josiah, and finally the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple by Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon in 586 BCE, followed by the Babylonian exile. The book ends on a muted note of hope with the elevation of Jehoiachin at the Babylonian court.

Composition

Scholars generally regard Kings as part of the Deuteronomistic history, composed and edited in stages between the late 7th and mid-6th centuries BCE from earlier sources, including royal annals ("the Book of the Acts of Solomon", "the Book of the Chronicles of the Kings of Israel/Judah") and prophetic legends. Its theological framework judges history by obedience to the covenant and centralisation of worship in Jerusalem.

Themes

Recurring themes include the conditional promise to the Davidic dynasty, the condemnation of "high places" and foreign cults, the word of Yahweh fulfilled through prophecy, and repentance as a means of averting judgement.
