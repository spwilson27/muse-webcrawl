Fordism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fordism is the system of mass production and mass consumption that characterised 20th-century industrial capitalism, named after Henry Ford. As developed at Ford's Highland Park and River Rouge plants, it combined the moving assembly line, standardised parts and products, scientific management of labour, and — crucially — wages high enough for workers to buy the goods they made.

Origins

Ford's adoption of the moving assembly line in 1913 cut the time to build a Model T chassis from over 12 hours to about 93 minutes, and the car's price fell steadily. In 1914 Ford introduced the five-dollar day, roughly doubling typical pay, partly to reduce staff turnover caused by the monotony of line work and partly in recognition that mass production required mass consumers. The Italian theorist Antonio Gramsci analysed "Americanism and Fordism" in his Prison Notebooks, noting its ambition to create a new type of worker and society.

As an economic regime

In regulation theory and economic history, Fordism denotes the post-1945 growth model of the advanced economies: unionised mass production, rising real wages tied to productivity, welfare states, and stable consumer demand, which together powered the postwar boom. Its crisis from the 1970s — slower productivity, saturated markets, oil shocks — led theorists to speak of post-Fordism: flexible specialisation, just-in-time production, global supply chains and service employment.

Criticism

Critics emphasise Fordism's deskilling of craft labour, monotonous work, environmental costs, paternalistic social control (Ford's Sociological Department policed workers' private lives) and Henry Ford's own anti-union violence. The term is nevertheless retained as shorthand for an entire industrial age.
