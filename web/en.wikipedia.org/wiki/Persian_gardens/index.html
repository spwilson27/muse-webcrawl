Persian gardens

The tradition of the Persian garden dates back over four thousand years and is one of the most influential garden traditions in the world. The Persian word pairidaeza ("walled enclosure") passed into other languages as "paradise", reflecting the garden's role as an earthly image of heaven. Nine Persian gardens in Iran were inscribed collectively on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2011.

Design

The classic form is the chahar bagh ("four gardens"), a quadrilateral plot divided by intersecting water channels into four parts, symbolising the four elements, the four rivers of paradise, or the Zoroastrian sacred elements. Key features include an enclosing wall, running water supplied by qanats and channelled into pools and fountains, shade trees and fruit trees planted in sunken beds, and a pavilion positioned for views along the main axis. Irrigation engineering — capturing scarce water and distributing it by gravity — is integral to the design.

History and influence

Cyrus the Great is credited with a geometric garden at Pasargadae in the sixth century BC, the earliest known chahar bagh. The tradition flourished under successive Iranian dynasties and spread east and west: it shaped Islamic gardens from al-Andalus to Mughal India, where it appears in the Taj Mahal's garden and Humayun's Tomb, and influenced Renaissance and later European garden ideas through Islamic intermediaries. Examples include Fin Garden in Kashan, Eram Garden in Shiraz and Shazdeh Garden near Mahan.

Symbolism

Beyond aesthetics, the Persian garden is a microcosm: enclosed order set against the desert, sacred geometry, and a setting for poetry, philosophy and court life, celebrated throughout Persian literature from Saadi's Gulistan ("Rose Garden") onwards.
