History of economic thought

The history of economic thought is the study of the different thinkers and theories in the subjects of political economy and economics from the ancient world to the present day. Economic thought developed unevenly across civilisations, with contributions from Greek philosophers, medieval scholastics, and Islamic scholars long before economics emerged as a distinct discipline in the eighteenth century.

Ancient and medieval thought

Aristotle distinguished between household management (oikonomia) and wealth acquisition, and medieval thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas analysed the "just price" and condemned usury. Islamic scholars including Ibn Khaldun anticipated ideas about taxation, division of labour and the rise and fall of dynasties. In India, the Arthashastra attributed to Kautilya offered detailed advice on state finance and administration.

Mercantilism and physiocracy

From the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, mercantilist writers argued that national wealth depended on accumulating precious metals through a favourable balance of trade, justifying state intervention and colonial expansion. The French physiocrats, led by François Quesnay, countered that land was the sole source of wealth and advocated laissez-faire and a single tax on land.

Classical economics

Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) founded modern economics, arguing that markets coordinated by an "invisible hand" could generate prosperity, and championing the division of labour and free trade. David Ricardo developed theories of comparative advantage, rent and distribution; Thomas Malthus warned of population pressure on subsistence; and John Stuart Mill synthesised classical doctrine in the mid-nineteenth century. Karl Marx subjected capitalism to a sweeping critique, developing a labour theory of value and a theory of historical materialism.

The marginal revolution and neoclassical economics

In the 1870s William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras independently founded marginal analysis, shifting attention from classes to individual choice and utility. Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) integrated supply and demand analysis and dominated textbooks for decades. The Austrian school, welfare economics and early econometrics all grew from this neoclassical turn.

Keynesianism and after

The Great Depression prompted John Maynard Keynes's The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), which justified government demand management. Post-war economics split between Keynesian macroeconomics, monetarism led by Milton Friedman, and later new classical and new Keynesian schools. Meanwhile game theory, behavioural economics, development economics and institutional approaches broadened the discipline's scope.
