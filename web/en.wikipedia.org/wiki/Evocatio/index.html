Evocatio

Evocatio (Latin: "calling out" or "calling forth", from evoco, evocare) was a ritual in ancient Roman religion by which a besieging Roman general formally invited the tutelary deity of an enemy city to abandon it and transfer their allegiance to Rome, in return for promises of a temple and cult in Rome. The rite reflected the Roman belief that a city could not be taken while its divine protector remained loyal, and it was one of several Roman practices for incorporating foreign gods into the Roman pantheon.

The ritual

Before an assault on a fortified city, the Roman commander would address the city's god — whose true name was often a closely guarded secret, since knowing a deity's name gave power over them — and vow to provide equal or greater honours in Rome: a temple, games and sacrifices. Once the deity was understood to have consented and departed, the city could be stormed without impiety, and its cult statue would often be carried to Rome and installed in the promised temple.

Historical instances

The most famous recorded evocatio is that of Juno Regina from the Etruscan city of Veii in 396 BC. According to Livy, after the Roman victory, soldiers asked the goddess's statue whether she wished to go to Rome; the statue was said to have nodded assent, and a temple to Juno Regina was dedicated on the Aventine Hill in 392 BC. Another prominent instance occurred at the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC, when Scipio Aemilianus was said to have performed an evocatio — the actual formula is preserved by Macrobius — calling the gods of Carthage to leave the city. The rite is also associated with the siege of Isaura Vetus and other campaigns.

The preserved formula

Macrobius, in the Saturnalia, preserves a formal prayer said to have been used at Carthage: "Whether god or goddess, under whose protection are the people and city of Carthage, and you especially who have undertaken to be the patron of this city and people, I pray and worship and crave grace of you, that you abandon the people and city of Carthage..." The prayer offered the gods a temple and games in Rome in exchange for their favour.

Related practices

Evocatio is related to the Roman practice of lectisternium and to the introduction of foreign cults such as that of Cybele (Magna Mater) in 204 BC. As a security measure, Rome kept its own tutelary deity's true name secret, fearing an enemy might perform an evocatio against the city itself; the concealment of the name is mentioned by Pliny the Elder and Plutarch.
