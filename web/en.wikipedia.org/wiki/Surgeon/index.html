Surgeon

A surgeon is a medical specialist who performs surgery — operative procedures that physically change body tissues, typically to treat injuries, diseases or deformities. In modern medicine, surgeons are physicians (or, in some countries, dentists, podiatrists or veterinarians within their fields) who have completed specialised postgraduate training in a surgical discipline.

Specialties

Surgical specialties include general surgery, orthopaedic surgery, neurosurgery, cardiothoracic surgery, plastic surgery, vascular surgery, colorectal surgery, paediatric surgery, trauma surgery, transplant surgery, urology and ophthalmology, among others. Many surgeons subspecialise further, for example in hand surgery or surgical oncology.

Training

Training is long: after medical school, a residency in surgery typically lasts five to seven years, often followed by a one- or two-year fellowship. In the United Kingdom and some Commonwealth countries, fully trained surgeons traditionally use the courtesy title "Mr", "Miss", "Mrs" or "Ms" rather than "Dr" — a survival from the era when surgeons were trained by apprenticeship rather than at university, and distinct from physicians.

History

Surgery was historically practised by barber-surgeons and battlefield practitioners, with limited success before the nineteenth-century introduction of anaesthesia and antisepsis transformed it into a major branch of medicine. Figureheads include Ambroise Paré, Joseph Lister and William Stewart Halsted, who founded the first surgical residency programme.
