Tumor suppressor gene

A tumor suppressor gene (TSG), or anti-oncogene, is a gene that regulates a cell during cell division and replication. If a cell grows uncontrollably, it will result in cancer. When a tumor suppressor gene is mutated, it results in a loss of function or reduction in its expression. Combined with other genetic changes, this could allow the cell to grow abnormally. The loss of function for these genes may be even more significant in the development of human cancers than the activation of oncogenes.

Two-hit hypothesis

In 1971 Alfred Knudson proposed the "two-hit hypothesis" to explain hereditary retinoblastoma: because tumor suppressor genes are typically recessive, both copies of the gene in a cell must be inactivated for the protective effect to be lost. People who inherit one defective copy need only one further somatic mutation in the remaining copy to develop tumors, explaining the earlier onset of hereditary cancers.

Examples and functions

Classic tumor suppressors include RB1, the first to be identified, whose protein (pRb) restrains entry into S phase; TP53, encoding the p53 "guardian of the genome", which is mutated in more than half of human cancers and triggers cell-cycle arrest, senescence, or apoptosis in response to DNA damage; and APC, mutated in familial adenomatous polyposis and most colorectal cancers. Others, such as BRCA1 and BRCA2, act as "caretakers" involved in DNA repair, while "gatekeeper" genes like RB1 and APC directly control cell proliferation. Loss of tumor suppressor function can also occur through epigenetic silencing, such as promoter methylation, without any change to the DNA sequence.
