Moral realism

Moral realism is the position in meta-ethics that moral sentences express propositions that are true or false in virtue of objective features of the world — that there are moral facts, independent of anyone's beliefs or attitudes about them, much as there are physical facts. It is a form of cognitivism combined with a success theory: our moral claims are truth-apt and (at least some of them) true.

Variants

Realists divide over whether moral properties are natural or non-natural. Naturalists such as the Cornell realists (Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon, David Brink) identify moral properties with natural properties investigated by the sciences. Non-naturalists, following G. E. Moore's critique of the naturalistic fallacy and recently defended by Derek Parfit, hold that moral truths are sui generis. "Robust" realists maintain that moral facts are stance-independent and irreducible.

Objections and replies

Classical challenges include J. L. Mackie's "argument from queerness" and his argument from relativity, which led Mackie to defend an error theory instead; the is–ought problem associated with Hume; and evolutionary debunking arguments (Sharon Street, Richard Joyce) claiming that natural selection would have shaped our moral beliefs regardless of any moral truth. Realists respond with companions-in-guilt arguments, noting that similar debunking logic would threaten epistemic and mathematical norms, and with third-factor explanations correlating moral truths with survival-relevant facts.
