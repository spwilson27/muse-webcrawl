Analogous structures

Analogous structures are features of different species that perform a similar function but evolved independently, rather than being inherited from a common ancestor. Their existence is a hallmark of convergent evolution, in which unrelated lineages adapt in similar ways to similar environmental pressures. Analogous structures are contrasted with homologous structures, which share a common evolutionary origin even when their functions differ.

The wings of insects, birds, and bats are analogous: all serve flight but evolved independently

Examples

The classic example is the wing. Insect wings are outgrowths of the exoskeleton, bird wings are modified forelimbs supported by feathers, and bat wings are membranes stretched between elongated fingers. All enable flight, yet they arose separately in each lineage. Similarly, the streamlined bodies of sharks (fish), ichthyosaurs (extinct reptiles), and dolphins (mammals) reflect convergent adaptation to fast swimming.

Other examples include the camera-like eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods such as the octopus, the succulent water-storing stems of American cacti and African euphorbias, and the burrowing forms of moles and mole crickets.

Analogy versus homology

Distinguishing analogy from homology is central to reconstructing evolutionary relationships. The anatomist Richard Owen coined both terms in the nineteenth century, and Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection recast them: homologies testify to descent with modification from a common ancestor, while analogies testify to similar selection pressures. Modern phylogenetic methods, especially DNA sequence comparison and cladistics, help separate superficial similarity (homoplasy) from similarity due to shared ancestry.
