Hoi An

Hoi An (Vietnamese: Hội An) is a city in Quảng Nam Province on the central coast of Vietnam, situated on the northern bank of the Thu Bồn River near its mouth on the South China Sea. Its exceptionally well-preserved old town, a major Southeast Asian trading port from the fifteenth to the nineteenth century, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1999.

History

The area was part of the Champa kingdom before coming under Vietnamese control. From the late sixteenth century, under the Nguyễn lords, Hoi An — known to Europeans as Faifo — became one of the busiest ports in Southeast Asia, attracting Chinese, Japanese, Dutch, Portuguese and other merchants trading in silk, ceramics, sugar and spices. Japanese and Chinese merchant communities built assembly halls, temples and covered bridges; the famous Japanese Covered Bridge dates to the 1590s. The port declined in the nineteenth century as the Thu Bồn River silted up and trade shifted to Da Nang, which preserved the old town largely intact.

Old town

The old town contains over a thousand timber-frame buildings, including Chinese assembly halls (such as the Fujian and Cantonese halls), family chapels, merchant houses combining Vietnamese, Chinese and Japanese features, and French colonial buildings. The streets are closed to most motor traffic at set times, and the town is famous for its colourful silk lanterns and monthly lantern festivals. Hoi An is also noted for tailoring, Cao lầu noodles, and nearby An Bàng and Cửa Đại beaches; seasonal flooding of the old town occurs most years.
