Great Mosque of Córdoba

The Great Mosque of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita-Catedral de Córdoba) is a historic mosque-turned-cathedral in Córdoba, Andalusia, Spain, and one of the greatest monuments of Islamic architecture. Construction began in 784–786 under Abd al-Rahman I, the Umayyad emir of Córdoba, and the mosque was enlarged repeatedly by his successors over two centuries, reaching its final form of some 23,000 m² under al-Mansur in 987.

Architecture and history

The prayer hall is famed for its forest of columns — around 856 survive of an original 1,293 — supporting tiers of red-and-white double arches (horseshoe arches below, semicircular above), and for the exquisite mihrab and maqsurah added under al-Hakam II with Byzantine-influenced gold mosaics. After Ferdinand III captured Córdoba in 1236 the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral, and in the 16th century a Renaissance nave was inserted into its center — a change Emperor Charles V is said to have regretted, remarking that the builders had destroyed something unique to build something commonplace. The monument is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Spain's most visited landmarks.
