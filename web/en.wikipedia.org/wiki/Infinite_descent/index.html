Infinite descent

Infinite descent is a method of mathematical proof, closely related to mathematical induction and proof by contradiction. It proceeds by showing that if a statement were true for some positive integer (or some well-ordered quantity), it would also be true for a strictly smaller one, and so on indefinitely — an impossibility, since the positive integers cannot descend forever. Hence the statement is false for all cases.

History

The method was used in ancient Greek mathematics, for instance in proofs of the irrationality of √2, and was developed into a systematic technique by Pierre de Fermat in the 17th century. Fermat used infinite descent to prove, among other results, that no right triangle with integer sides has a square area, a special case of what later became known as Fermat's Last Theorem for the exponent 4.

Method

To prove that no object with a given property exists, one assumes a counterexample exists and chooses one that is minimal with respect to some measure (such as size, height, or a positive integer parameter). One then constructs from it a strictly smaller counterexample. Iterating produces an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, contradicting the well-ordering principle. Therefore no counterexample exists.

Applications

Classical applications include proving the irrationality of √2 (from a putative fraction p/q in lowest terms one derives a smaller fraction), the non-existence of integer solutions to x⁴ + y⁴ = z², and various results in the theory of Diophantine equations and algebraic number theory.
