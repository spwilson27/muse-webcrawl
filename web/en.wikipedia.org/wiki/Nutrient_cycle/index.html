Nutrient cycle

A nutrient cycle (or ecological recycling) is the movement and exchange of inorganic and organic matter back into the production of living matter. Energy flows directionally through an ecosystem — entering as sunlight and leaving as heat — but nutrients are finite and must be recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, potassium and other elements circulate between organisms and the physical environment in biogeochemical cycles.

How cycling works

Producers (plants and photosynthetic microbes) take up inorganic nutrients from soil and water; consumers obtain them by eating; and when organisms die or excrete waste, decomposers — bacteria and fungi — break down the organic matter and release the nutrients in mineral form, in a process called mineralisation. Earthworms and other detritivores fragment litter and aerate soil, speeding the process. Nutrients also enter ecosystems from rock weathering, atmospheric deposition and, for nitrogen, biological fixation, and can leave through leaching, erosion, harvesting and gaseous losses.

Major cycles

  • Carbon cycle — photosynthesis and respiration exchange carbon between the atmosphere, biosphere, oceans and sediments.
  • Nitrogen cycle — fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification convert nitrogen between N2, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and organic forms, largely through microbial action.
  • Phosphorus cycle — a sedimentary cycle with no significant atmospheric phase; phosphorus weathers from rocks and accumulates in ocean sediments, often limiting growth in freshwater ecosystems.
  • Sulfur and water cycles — link atmospheric, terrestrial and aquatic reservoirs.

Human impacts

Humans have profoundly altered nutrient cycles: the Haber–Bosch process roughly doubled the flow of reactive nitrogen into the biosphere; mined phosphate and synthetic fertilisers wash into waterways, causing eutrophication and coastal "dead zones"; fossil-fuel burning adds carbon and nitrogen oxides; and deforestation reduces nutrient retention in soils. Ecologists quantify cycling with budgets of inputs, outputs and internal fluxes, a framework formalised by classics such as the Hubbard Brook ecosystem studies.
