Absolute pitch

Absolute pitch (AP), often called perfect pitch, is the ability to identify or produce a musical note of a given pitch without the benefit of a reference tone. A person with absolute pitch can hear a note — for example, F♯ — and name it immediately, or sing a specified note on demand. It is distinct from relative pitch, the trainable ability to identify intervals between notes.

Development

Absolute pitch is strongly associated with musical training begun in early childhood, typically before the age of six, suggesting a critical-period effect similar to language acquisition. It is significantly more common among speakers of tonal languages such as Mandarin and Vietnamese, whose speakers appear to recruit pitch-naming machinery used for word tones. Genetic studies indicate a heritable component, with linkage reported to several chromosomal regions.

Characteristics

Possessors vary in accuracy by timbre, register and note: most are faster and more accurate for white-key notes and for the timbre of their own instrument. Absolute pitch can be a mixed blessing — some musicians are distressed by slightly out-of-tune performances or by historical pitch standards (e.g., Baroque A = 415 Hz), and the ability may drift with age, commonly shifting sharp by up to a semitone in later life.

Research and controversy

Whether adults can acquire genuine absolute pitch is debated. Most training studies produce only partial, timbre-specific gains ("pseudo-absolute pitch"), though a small number of studies report that intensive training, and in one well-known experiment the drug valproate combined with training, can reopen a period of AP learning in adults. AP is studied as a model trait at the intersection of genetics, language and early experience.
