The Technische Universität Dresden (TU Dresden or TUD) is a public research university in Dresden, the capital of the German state of Saxony. Founded in 1828 as a technical school, it is one of the oldest technical universities in Germany and, with around 29,000 students, one of the largest. It is a member of the TU9 alliance and has held the title of "University of Excellence" under the German Universities Excellence Initiative since 2012.

History

The institution began in 1828 as the Technical School (Technische Bildungsanstalt) of Saxony, intended to train engineers for the kingdom's growing industries. It became a Royal Saxon Polytechnic in 1871 and a Technische Hochschule in 1890. After the Second World War — during which Dresden and the campus were heavily damaged by bombing — it was rebuilt in the German Democratic Republic and renamed Technische Universität Dresden in 1961, absorbing institutions for transport, forestry and medicine. Following German reunification in 1990 it expanded from a purely technical profile into a comprehensive university with the humanities, social sciences, economics and a medical faculty.

Organisation and research

The university is organised into five schools encompassing 17 faculties, covering engineering, natural sciences, mathematics, humanities, social sciences, business and medicine. Its medical faculty and university hospital Carl Gustav Carus form one of Germany's major academic medical centres. Research strengths include microelectronics and nanoelectronics — Dresden brands itself "Silicon Saxony" — biomedicine and bioengineering, materials science, energy, and mobility. The university coordinates the DRESDEN-concept alliance with the city's many non-university research institutes (Max Planck, Fraunhofer, Helmholtz and Leibniz), making Dresden one of Germany's densest research locations.

Campus and student life

The main campus lies south of the city centre in the Südvorstadt district, around the Saxon State and University Library (SLUB). The university is noted for its rich student culture, including more than 30 student-run clubs in the city — among the highest densities in Germany — and historic lecture buildings alongside modern research centres such as the Center for Regenerative Therapies.
