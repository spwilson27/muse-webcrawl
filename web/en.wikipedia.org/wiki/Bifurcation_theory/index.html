Bifurcation theory

Bifurcation theory is the mathematical study of changes in the qualitative or topological structure of a family of solutions to a system of equations, usually differential equations. A bifurcation occurs when a small smooth change made to a parameter value causes a sudden qualitative change in the system's behaviour—for example, when a stable equilibrium loses stability and gives rise to oscillations.

Types of bifurcations

Local bifurcations in continuous systems are classified by how the eigenvalues of the linearized system cross the imaginary axis. They include the saddle-node (fold) bifurcation, in which two equilibria collide and annihilate; the transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations, in which equilibria exchange stability; and the Hopf bifurcation, in which a limit cycle is born from an equilibrium. In discrete-time systems (maps), the analogous period-doubling (flip) bifurcation leads, along the Feigenbaum route, to chaos.

Normal forms and codimension

Bifurcations are studied through normal forms—simple polynomial models that capture the local behaviour—and are classified by codimension, the number of parameters that must be varied to encounter them generically. Global bifurcations, such as homoclinic and heteroclinic bifurcations, involve global rearrangements of invariant manifolds rather than purely local eigenvalue conditions.

Applications

Bifurcation theory is used in physics (buckling, lasers, phase transitions), biology (population dynamics, neural excitability, morphogenesis), engineering (vibration and control), and climate science (tipping points).
