Interlingua is an international auxiliary language developed between 1937 and 1951 by the American International Auxiliary Language Association (IALA). It is a naturalistic constructed language: its vocabulary, grammar, and other features are drawn from the common elements of several widely spoken European languages — chiefly Latin and the Romance languages — rather than invented schematically. As a result, speakers of Romance languages can often understand written Interlingua at first sight, and speakers of English can recognise much of its vocabulary through Latin-derived words.

The 1951 Interlingua–English Dictionary launched the language publicly.

Development

The IALA, founded in 1924 with support from Alice Vanderbilt Morris and others, researched existing auxiliary languages before deciding to construct its own under linguists including Alexander Gode, who authored the language's grammar and its 27,000-word dictionary in 1951. After the IALA closed in 1954, promotion passed to the Union Mundial pro Interlingua (UMI), founded in 1955.

Features

Interlingua grammar is deliberately simple: nouns do not have grammatical gender, adjectives are invariable, and verbs conjugate with few forms. Words are admitted if they occur in at least three of the source languages (Spanish/Portuguese counted jointly, with English, French, Italian, and German and Russian as controls). The language is not to be confused with Giuseppe Peano's earlier Latino sine flexione, which was also at one time called Interlingua.

Use

Interlingua has a small but persistent international community, with conferences, periodicals, literature, and an online presence. It found a niche in scientific communication in the 1950s–1970s, when journals published summaries in Interlingua. Supporters present it as a neutral, easily learned bridge language rooted in Europe's shared linguistic heritage.
