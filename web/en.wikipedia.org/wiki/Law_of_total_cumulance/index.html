Law of total cumulance

Conditioning a random variable on another decomposes its cumulants

In probability theory, the law of total cumulance is a generalisation of the law of total expectation, the law of total variance, and the law of total covariance, expressed in terms of cumulants. It was introduced by David Brillinger in 1969. The law relates the cumulants of a random variable X to the cumulants of its conditional distribution given another random variable Y.

Statement

For the first cumulant (the mean), the law reduces to the law of total expectation:

κ1(X) = E[κ1(X | Y)]

For the second cumulant (the variance), it reduces to the law of total variance:

κ2(X) = E[κ2(X | Y)] + κ2(E[X | Y])

For higher orders, the law involves a sum over partitions of the set of indices: the cumulant κn(X) equals the sum, over all partitions π of {1, …, n}, of the cumulant of the conditional cumulants indexed by the blocks of the partition. In the case of more than one random variable, the same partition structure gives joint cumulants, generalising the law of total covariance.

Applications

The law of total cumulance is used in statistics and stochastic modelling wherever conditional moment structure matters, including the analysis of mixture distributions, random sums and branching processes, time-series cumulant spectra, and the study of point processes, where Brillinger's work on cumulant measures found wide application.
