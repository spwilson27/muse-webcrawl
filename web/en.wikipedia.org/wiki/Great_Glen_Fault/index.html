Great Glen Fault

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The long straight valley of the Great Glen cutting across the Scottish Highlands, Loch Ness in the foreground]
The long straight valley of the Great Glen cutting across the Scottish Highlands, Loch Ness in the foreground

The Great Glen Fault is a major strike-slip fault zone that runs the length of Scotland's Great Glen — the straight valley containing Loch Ness, Loch Lochy and Loch Linnhe — from Inverness to Fort William and beyond. Formed during the Caledonian orogeny some 430–390 million years ago, it accommodated hundreds of kilometres of sideways movement between crustal blocks, and its line still guides the valley, the Caledonian Canal and modest modern seismicity.

2. Today

Geology

The fault belongs to the system of great fractures created as the ancient continents Laurentia, Baltica and Avalonia collided to raise the Caledonian mountains. Estimates of its total displacement range from about 100 km to several hundred kilometres, mainly sinistral (left-lateral) in its main phase; correlating rock units across it — the Moine and Dalradian successions — remains a classic problem of Scottish geology.

Today

The fault is not dead: small earthquakes occur along the Glen (an 1816 Inverness shock damaged buildings), and the straight glen — filled by glaciers and now by lochs and Thomas Telford's canal (1822) — makes the fracture visible from orbit, one of Britain's most legible geological features.
