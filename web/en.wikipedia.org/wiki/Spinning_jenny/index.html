Spinning jenny

The spinning jenny is a multi-spindle spinning frame, and was one of the key developments in the industrialisation of textile manufacturing during the early Industrial Revolution. It was invented in 1764, or possibly 1765, by James Hargreaves in Stanhill, Lancashire, England. The device reduced the amount of work needed to produce cloth, with a worker able to work 8 or more spools at once; later versions operated 80 or more spindles.

Invention

According to tradition, Hargreaves conceived the machine after his daughter Jenny knocked over a spinning wheel; observing the spindle continue to revolve upright, he realised several spindles could be mounted vertically and turned together—though the story may be embellished, and the machine's name may honour his wife or daughter. Hargreaves built the first jenny with 8 spindles, later increasing the count to 16, 24, and eventually 80 or more. He patented the machine in 1770.

Operation

The jenny held a frame of multiple rovings feeding vertical spindles. A moving carriage drew the rovings out while the spindles turned, twisting and winding the yarn in a sequence of motions imitating the hand spinner's actions, coordinated by the operator turning a wheel and moving the carriage back and forth. Because the yarn was drawn out without rollers, jenny-spun yarn was relatively soft and fine, suitable mainly for weft (the threads woven across the loom); stronger warp yarn came later from Arkwright's water frame.

Impact

The spinning jenny spread rapidly, at first in cottages and small workshops, helping shift textile production toward factories. Its introduction provoked anxiety among hand spinners; in 1768 a mob broke into Hargreaves' house and destroyed his machines. Together with the flying shuttle (which had created a yarn shortage) and later the spinning mule, the jenny transformed cotton manufacturing and helped launch the Industrial Revolution in Lancashire.
