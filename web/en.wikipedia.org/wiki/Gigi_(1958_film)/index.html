Gigi is a 1958 American musical film from Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, directed by Vincente Minnelli and based on Colette's 1944 novella of the same name. With a score by Frederick Loewe and Alan Jay Lerner — the team behind My Fair Lady — the film swept the Academy Awards, winning all nine Oscars for which it was nominated, including Best Picture.

Plot

In Belle Époque Paris, young Gilberte "Gigi" is being raised by her grandmother Madame Alvarez and given finishing lessons by her great-aunt Alicia in how to become a fashionable courtesan, as the women of her family have been. Family friend Gaston Lachaille, a wealthy and bored sugar heir, visits the Alvarez apartment as a refuge from his glittering social round and treats Gigi as a little sister. When Gaston, humiliated by a mistress's infidelity, takes Gigi to the seaside at Trouville and then proposes that she become his mistress in the family tradition, both are forced to recognise that their affection has become love; Gaston defies convention and asks for her hand in marriage.

Cast and songs

Leslie Caron plays Gigi, Louis Jourdan is Gaston, and Maurice Chevalier steals the film as Gaston's roguish uncle Honoré, who opens and closes the story with "Thank Heaven for Little Girls". Chevalier and Hermione Gingold (Madame Alvarez) share the nostalgic duet "I Remember It Well"; other numbers include "It's a Bore", "The Night They Invented Champagne", "I'm Glad I'm Not Young Anymore" and the title song "Gigi", which won the Academy Award for Best Original Song. The film was shot on location in Paris, with Cecil Beaton's costumes and designs drenched in period detail.

Reception and legacy

Produced by Arthur Freed, Gigi was MGM's last great musical and, briefly, the most Oscar-honoured film ever made, surpassing the eight awards of Gone with the Wind and From Here to Eternity before Ben-Hur took eleven the following year. Audrey Hepburn had played Gigi on Broadway in 1951 in a stage adaptation. The film was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry in 1991. Lerner later adapted the film for the Broadway stage (1973), and a revised version returned to Broadway in 2015.
