Isotope geochemistry

Isotope geochemistry is the study of the concentrations and ratios of isotopes of chemical elements in Earth materials — rocks, minerals, water, ice, sediments and fossil remains — to investigate geological processes, the age of materials, and past environmental conditions. Measurements are made to very high precision, usually with mass spectrometers, and results for stable isotopes are conventionally reported as delta (δ) values in parts per thousand (per mil, ‰) relative to an international standard.

Radiogenic isotopes and dating

Radioactive parent isotopes decay to radiogenic daughter isotopes at known rates, providing clocks for dating rocks and meteorites: uranium–lead (U–Pb), potassium–argon and argon–argon (K–Ar/Ar–Ar), rubidium–strontium (Rb–Sr), samarium–neodymium (Sm–Nd) and rhenium–osmium (Re–Os) are the workhorse systems. Isochron methods, which use several samples cogenetic with the rock, eliminate the need to know the initial daughter concentration. Radiogenic isotope ratios also act as tracers: because reservoirs such as the mantle, crust and seawater have distinct Sr, Nd, Pb and Hf isotope signatures, they can fingerprint the sources of magmas and sediments.

Stable isotopes

Stable isotope systems — principally hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and sulfur — are fractionated by physical, chemical and biological processes: lighter isotopes react slightly faster and evaporate more readily. Oxygen isotopes in ice cores and marine microfossils record past temperatures and ice volumes, underpinning much of palaeoclimate science; Harold Urey's pioneering work in the 1940s founded the field. Carbon isotopes reveal photosynthesis pathways and carbon-cycle changes, sulfur isotopes track microbial sulfate reduction and ancient ocean chemistry, and hydrogen isotopes trace the hydrological cycle. Newer "non-traditional" stable isotope systems (iron, molybdenum, calcium, silicon and others) are used to probe redox conditions, weathering and biological cycling.

Applications

Applications extend from dating the Earth and meteorites and modelling mantle evolution, through reconstructing climate from ice cores, corals and lake sediments, to archaeology (provenancing artefacts and tracing ancient diets and migration using strontium and oxygen isotopes in teeth), hydrology, forensics and environmental pollution tracing.
