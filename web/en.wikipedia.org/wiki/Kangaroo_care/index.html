Kangaroo care

Kangaroo care, also called kangaroo mother care (KMC) or skin-to-skin care, is a technique of newborn care in which infants, especially preterm and low-birth-weight babies, are held in prolonged skin-to-skin contact against a parent's chest. The name refers to the way marsupials such as kangaroos carry their young in a pouch.

History

The method was developed in 1978–79 by paediatricians Edgar Rey Sanabria and Héctor Martínez at the Instituto Materno Infantil in Bogotá, Colombia, in response to shortages of incubators and high infection and abandonment rates. Kangaroo care was promoted internationally by Nathalie Charpak and the Kangaroo Foundation, and the World Health Organization issued a practical guide in 2003. In 2022 the WHO recommended KMC as routine care for all preterm or low-birth-weight infants, to begin immediately after birth.

Components and effects

The original model combines continuous skin-to-skin positioning, exclusive or near-exclusive breastfeeding, and early discharge with close follow-up. Trials and meta-analyses show that kangaroo care reduces mortality in low-birth-weight infants, lowers risks of severe infection, sepsis and hypothermia, stabilises heart rate, breathing and temperature, and improves breastfeeding rates and mother–infant bonding. The parent's chest acts as a thermal regulator, warming or cooling in response to the baby's temperature.

Use

Kangaroo care is used both as an incubator substitute in low-resource settings and as a complement to intensive care in high-income neonatal units, where shorter intermittent sessions are common. Fathers and other caregivers can also provide skin-to-skin contact.
