Fish locomotion

Fish locomotion is the variety of types of animal locomotion used by fish, principally swimming. Most fish swim by contracting muscles on either side of the body in waves that travel from head to tail, pushing water backwards and the fish forwards. Different groups emphasise different parts of the body or different fins, producing a spectrum of gaits from the whole-body undulation of eels to the stiff-bodied, tail-driven sprinting of tunas. Some fish also "walk" on the sea floor, glide above the surface, or move on land.

Body-caudal fin locomotion

In body and/or caudal fin (BCF) swimming, thrust is generated by the trunk and tail fin. The modes are arranged by how much of the body undulates:

  • Anguilliform — the whole body passes through large-amplitude waves, as in eels; efficient at low speed and in confined spaces but relatively slow.
  • Subcarangiform — undulation confined mainly to the rear half of the body, as in trout.
  • Carangiform — movement concentrated in the last third of the body and the tail, as in jacks and herrings.
  • Thunniform — the body is stiff and nearly all thrust comes from a high-aspect-ratio crescent tail oscillating side to side, as in tunas and some sharks; the fastest and most efficient cruising gait.
  • Ostraciiform — the body is rigid and only the tail fin sculls back and forth, as in boxfishes.

Median and paired fin locomotion

Other fish swim mainly with their fins (MPF locomotion), favouring manoeuvrability over speed. Seahorses flutter their dorsal fin; triggerfishes undulate dorsal and anal fins together (balistiform); wrasses row with their pectoral fins (labriform); rays flap or undulate broad pectoral discs (rajiform); and knifefishes ripple a long anal fin (gymnotiform), which allows them to swim backwards as readily as forwards. Many species combine gaits, using fins for slow precision and the tail for bursts of speed.

Hydrodynamics and energetics

Fish swimming is described by the Strouhal number and by the pattern of vortices shed in the wake, classically a reverse von Kármán vortex street that indicates thrust. The mucus-covered skin, the shape of the body and the stiffness of the red (aerobic, cruising) and white (anaerobic, burst) muscle systems all affect efficiency. Most fish are neutrally buoyant thanks to the swim bladder, freeing them from spending energy on lift; sharks instead generate lift with their pectoral fins and heterocercal tail.

Other forms of movement

Beyond swimming, mudskippers and walking catfish travel on land using their pectoral fins; flying fish glide for tens of metres above the surface after launching with a specialised tail; remoras hitchhike on larger animals; and many benthic species punt or walk along the bottom with modified fins — a behaviour that has made fish such as the frogfish and the "walking" handfish models for the evolution of terrestrial locomotion.
