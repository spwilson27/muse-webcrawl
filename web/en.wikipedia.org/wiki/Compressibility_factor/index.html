Compressibility factor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In thermodynamics, the compressibility factor (Z), also known as the compression factor or the gas deviation factor, describes the deviation of a real gas from ideal gas behaviour. It is defined as the ratio of the molar volume of a gas to the molar volume of an ideal gas at the same temperature and pressure:

Z = pVm / (RT) = p / (ρRT / M)

where p is pressure, Vm molar volume, T temperature, and R the gas constant. For an ideal gas, Z = 1 at all conditions.

Interpretation

When Z < 1, attractive intermolecular forces dominate and the gas is more compressible than an ideal gas — it occupies less volume than predicted. When Z > 1, repulsive forces (the finite size of molecules) dominate and the gas occupies more volume than an ideal gas would. At low pressures Z approaches 1 for all gases; large deviations occur at high pressures and near the critical point.

Generalised compressibility chart

By the principle of corresponding states, Z for many gases is approximately a universal function of reduced temperature Tr = T/Tc and reduced pressure pr = p/pc, measured relative to the critical point. Engineers use generalised compressibility charts (Nelson–Obert charts) based on this correlation to estimate gas properties without a full equation of state.

Relation to equations of state

Real-gas equations of state, such as the van der Waals, Redlich–Kwong, and Peng–Robinson equations, each imply a specific Z as a function of state. In statistical mechanics, Z can also be expanded in the virial series in powers of density, whose coefficients encode two-body, three-body, and higher intermolecular interactions.

Air

For air at moderate conditions Z is close to 1 (about 0.99–1.00 near ambient pressure), but at pressures of hundreds of bar and low temperatures it deviates by several percent, which matters in compressed-air storage, diving gas calculations, and custody transfer of natural gas.
