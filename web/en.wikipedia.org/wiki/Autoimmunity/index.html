Autoimmunity is the system of immune responses of an organism against its own healthy cells, tissues and other normal constituents. Any disease resulting from such an aberrant immune response is termed an autoimmune disease. Prominent examples include coeliac disease, diabetes mellitus type 1, Graves' disease, inflammatory bowel disease, multiple sclerosis, alopecia areata, Addison's disease, pernicious anemia, psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus.

Tolerance and its failure

The immune system normally avoids attacking the self through central tolerance — deletion of strongly self-reactive lymphocytes during development in the thymus and bone marrow — and peripheral tolerance mechanisms including regulatory T cells, anergy and activation-induced cell death. Autoimmunity arises when these controls fail, through combinations of genetic susceptibility (notably HLA associations), environmental triggers such as infections (molecular mimicry), hormonal influences, and defects in genes such as AIRE or FOXP3.

Classification

Autoimmune diseases are often divided into organ-specific disorders, in which a single tissue is targeted (e.g. the pancreatic beta cells in type 1 diabetes or the thyroid in Hashimoto's thyroiditis), and systemic diseases, in which widespread antigens are attacked (e.g. lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's syndrome). Autoantibodies are key biomarkers, though their presence alone does not prove disease, since low-level autoimmunity is common and can be physiological.

Treatment

Treatment generally aims to suppress or modulate the immune response with glucocorticoids, conventional immunosuppressants and targeted biologic agents such as TNF inhibitors or B-cell-depleting antibodies, alongside hormone or nutrient replacement for destroyed glands. Research seeks antigen-specific therapies that restore tolerance without broad immunosuppression.
