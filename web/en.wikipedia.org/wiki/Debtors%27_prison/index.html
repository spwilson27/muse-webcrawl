Debtors' prison

PurposeDetention of people unable to pay debts
Famous examplesFleet Prison, Marshalsea (London)
Abolition (England)Debtors Act 1869 (with exceptions)

A debtors' prison was a prison for people unable to pay their debts. Until the mid-nineteenth century, debtors' prisons were a common way to deal with unpaid debt in Western countries: debtors could typically be held indefinitely, often in harsh conditions, until they paid through work or family support — a system critics noted made repayment harder.

In England

London's Fleet Prison and Marshalsea prison housed thousands of debtors; inmates who could pay fees obtained better rooms and even permission to live nearby, while the poorest lived in squalid communal wards. Charles Dickens's father was imprisoned in the Marshalsea in 1824, an experience reflected in Little Dorrit. Imprisonment for debt was largely abolished in England by the Debtors Act 1869.

In the United States

Debtors' prisons existed in colonial and early America but were abolished by federal law in 1833 and by most states around the same period; the Supreme Court later held that imprisoning people solely for inability to pay fines violates the Fourteenth Amendment. Modern critics apply the term to incarceration arising from unpaid court fines and fees.
