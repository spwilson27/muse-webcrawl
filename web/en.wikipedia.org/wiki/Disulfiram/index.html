Disulfiram, sold under the trade name Antabuse among others, is a medication used to support the treatment of chronic alcoholism by producing an acute sensitivity to ethanol. Drinking alcohol while taking disulfiram causes an unpleasant — and potentially dangerous — reaction including flushing, pounding headache, nausea, vomiting, palpitations and low blood pressure, deterring relapse. It was the first medication approved for alcohol dependence, in 1951 in the United States.

Mechanism

Disulfiram irreversibly inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, the enzyme that converts acetaldehyde to acetate. If alcohol is consumed, acetaldehyde accumulates to five to ten times normal levels, producing the disulfiram–ethanol reaction within 15–30 minutes and lasting for hours; enzyme recovery takes up to two weeks, so reactions can occur well after the last dose. Patients must avoid hidden alcohol sources such as sauces, mouthwash and some medicines.

History and use

The compound was first synthesised in 1881 and used in the rubber industry, where workers who drank developed the characteristic reaction — disulfiram is chemically related to the vulcanisation accelerator thiuram. Danish researchers Erik Jacobsen and Jens Hald investigated it in the 1940s (originally seeking a treatment for intestinal parasites) and confirmed its alcohol-sensitising effect. Modern evidence suggests disulfiram works best with supervised administration and in motivated, abstinence-committed patients; it is contraindicated in severe heart disease, psychosis and severe liver impairment. Because it also inhibits dopamine β-hydroxylase, disulfiram has been studied for cocaine dependence, and its metabolite diethyldithiocarbamate is investigated in oncology and infectious disease.
