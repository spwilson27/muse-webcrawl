Île de la Cité

The Île de la Cité is a natural island in the Seine River in the centre of Paris, France. It is the historic heart of the city: the site of the Parisii settlement of Lutetia, of the medieval royal palace, and of Notre-Dame Cathedral. Together with the neighbouring Île Saint-Louis, it forms the city's oldest districts, spanning parts of the 1st and 4th arrondissements.

History

The island was the stronghold of the Celtic Parisii tribe before the Roman conquest, after which Lutetia grew around it. Merovingian and Capetian kings built their palace at its western end; the Sainte-Chapelle (1248) and the cathedral of Notre-Dame (begun 1163) made it the religious and judicial centre of the kingdom. Baron Haussmann's 19th-century rebuilding cleared much of its dense medieval housing for administrative buildings, greatly reducing its resident population.

Landmarks

  • Notre-Dame Cathedral — the Gothic cathedral at the island's eastern end, site of the square marking "kilometre zero" of French roads.
  • Sainte-Chapelle — Louis IX's palatine chapel, famed for its 13th-century stained glass.
  • Conciergerie — former palace and prison where Marie Antoinette was held during the Revolution.
  • Pont Neuf — despite its name ("New Bridge"), the oldest surviving bridge in Paris, opened in 1607.
  • Place Dauphine — a quiet triangular square near the western point.
