ſ

The long s, ⟨ſ⟩, is an archaic form of the lowercase letter s in the Latin script. It was used in most European languages written with the Latin alphabet from the early Middle Ages until the early 19th century, in both handwriting and print, in combination with the "short" or "round" s (the modern form) which was generally reserved for word-final position.

Usage

Typical rules in English printing were: long s at the beginning and in the middle of words, round s at the end of words and after a long s (so "ſucceſs" for "success", where the final letter is round). The long s is easily confused with the letter f, from which it differs in having no full crossbar — only a small nub at the left in most typefaces. Gothic (Fraktur) scripts used the long s with related rules, giving rise to modern German conventions for s.

Decline and legacy

Printers abandoned the long s around 1800 as typefaces were simplified; John Bell and the Baskerville-influenced modern types dropped it, and by the 1820s it had largely vanished from English printing, somewhat later in German Fraktur. The character survives in the integral sign ∫, introduced by Leibniz from the long s of Latin summa, in the "ſ" sometimes seen in historical reproductions and mock-antique signage, and encoded in Unicode at U+017F. It should not be confused with long shot — an unrelated term.
