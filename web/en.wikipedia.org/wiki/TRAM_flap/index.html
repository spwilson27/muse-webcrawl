TRAM flap

The TRAM flap (transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap) is a type of reconstructive surgery used most commonly for breast reconstruction after mastectomy. The procedure, first described by Hartrampf in 1982, transfers skin, fat and part of the rectus abdominis muscle from the lower abdomen to the chest to form a new breast mound, using the patient's own tissue rather than an implant.

Technique

In a pedicled TRAM flap, the tissue remains attached to its original blood supply in the rectus muscle and is tunnelled under the skin to the chest. In a free TRAM flap, the tissue is completely detached along with its blood vessels and reconnected microsurgically at the chest. Related perforator techniques, such as the DIEP flap, spare the muscle by dissecting out only the perforating vessels.

Risks and considerations

Because the flap uses abdominal tissue, the operation resembles a tummy tuck at the donor site, but it weakens the abdominal wall and carries risks including flap necrosis from insufficient blood supply, fat necrosis, and abdominal hernia or bulge. The TRAM flap was for many years the most common autologous breast reconstruction technique, though muscle-sparing perforator flaps have become increasingly preferred.
