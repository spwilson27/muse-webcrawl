The First Epistle of John is the first of the Johannine epistles of the New Testament, and the twenty-third book of the Christian Bible in most orderings. Written in Koine Greek, probably near the end of the first century AD, it is traditionally attributed to John the Evangelist, though many modern scholars regard it as the work of the Johannine community associated with the Gospel of John.

Content

Unlike most New Testament letters, 1 John has no greeting or named recipients and reads more like a sermon or tract. Its themes include the confession that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (against opponents often identified as early Gnostics or docetists), the contrast between light and darkness, righteousness and sin, and above all love: "God is love" (4:8, 4:16). The letter sets out "tests" of genuine fellowship with God — right belief, obedience, and love of fellow believers.

Comma Johanneum

The letter is famous for the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7–8), a Trinitarian gloss absent from early Greek manuscripts that entered later Latin-influenced printings, including the King James Version.
