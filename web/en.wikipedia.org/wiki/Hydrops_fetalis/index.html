Hydrops fetalis or fetal hydrops is a serious fetal condition defined as abnormal fluid accumulation in two or more fetal compartments — including ascites, pleural effusions, pericardial effusion and generalised skin oedema — often accompanied by placental thickening and excess amniotic fluid. It is the end point of many fetal disorders and historically carried a very high mortality.

Types and causes

Immune hydrops

Immune hydrops results from maternal alloimmunisation against fetal red-cell antigens, classically Rh(D) incompatibility: maternal antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal red blood cells, causing severe anaemia and high-output cardiac failure. Widespread use of anti-D immunoglobulin prophylaxis has made this form uncommon in developed countries.

Non-immune hydrops

Non-immune hydrops fetalis (NIHF) now accounts for roughly 85–90 percent of cases. Causes include fetal anaemia from other causes (e.g. parvovirus B19 infection, alpha-thalassaemia major), cardiovascular malformations and arrhythmias, chromosomal abnormalities such as Turner syndrome and Down syndrome, structural anomalies, congenital infections, and metabolic storage diseases.

Diagnosis and management

Hydrops is usually detected on obstetric ultrasound; middle cerebral artery Doppler can estimate fetal anaemia non-invasively. Management depends on cause and gestation and may include intrauterine fetal transfusion for anaemia, treatment of arrhythmias, thoraco-amniotic shunting for large pleural effusions, and planned delivery at a tertiary centre. Maternal "mirror syndrome" (Ballantyne syndrome), in which the mother's condition mirrors the hydropic fetus, is a recognised complication.

Prognosis

Prognosis varies widely with the underlying cause and gestational age at presentation; overall perinatal mortality remains substantial, particularly for cases diagnosed early in gestation or associated with structural or chromosomal anomalies.
