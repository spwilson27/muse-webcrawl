Elliptic function

In complex analysis, an elliptic function is a meromorphic function on the complex plane that is periodic in two independent directions. Just as a doubly periodic function repeats over a lattice of parallelograms, an elliptic function is determined by its values on a single period parallelogram. Elliptic functions arose historically from the study of elliptic integrals — inverting them led Niels Henrik Abel and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi in the 1820s to define functions doubly periodic in the complex plane.

Properties

By a theorem of Liouville, a holomorphic doubly periodic function must be constant, so non-constant elliptic functions necessarily have poles. Within one period parallelogram an elliptic function has equally many poles and zeros (counted with multiplicity), and the sum of its residues is zero; a function with a single simple pole therefore cannot exist. The smallest examples have either one double pole — the Weierstrass ℘-function — or two simple poles, such as Jacobi's elliptic functions.

Weierstrass and Jacobi functions

The Weierstrass function ℘(z) satisfies the differential equation (℘′)² = 4℘³ − g₂℘ − g₃ and gives a parametrisation of an elliptic curve; every elliptic function can be expressed rationally in terms of ℘ and its derivative. Jacobi's functions sn, cn and dn instead arise as inverses of elliptic integrals and resemble trigonometric functions, to which they reduce in limiting cases.

Significance

Elliptic functions connect complex analysis, number theory and algebraic geometry. The values of the j-invariant organise elliptic curves; modular forms generalise the transformation theory of elliptic functions; and the theory underlies applications from the exact solution for a simple pendulum's period to elliptic-curve cryptography.
