The Ge'ez script (ግዕዝ, Gəʽəz), also called the Ethiopic script and, in Ethiopian tradition, fidäl ("alphabet"), is an abugida used to write several languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea. It originated as an abjad (consonant-only alphabet) to write the Ge'ez language and developed its characteristic vowel modifications around the 4th century CE, making it one of the oldest alphabets still in use in the world.

Structure

In an abugida, each consonant has an inherent vowel, and other vowels are indicated by systematic modifications of the consonant's shape. The Ge'ez script has 26 basic consonantal characters, each appearing in seven forms or "orders" corresponding to the vowels ä, u, i, a, e, ə, and o, giving a core syllabary of 182 signs. Additional characters exist for labialised consonants and for sounds needed by languages such as Amharic and Tigrinya, which use modified sets of extra characters.

History

The script descends from the Ancient South Arabian script, brought across the Red Sea in the first millennium BCE. The earliest inscriptions, from the kingdom of Aksum, were written without vowel marks; the fully vocalised script appears by the reign of Ezana in the 4th century CE, when Aksum adopted Christianity and the Bible was translated into Ge'ez. Words were originally separated by a vertical bar-like mark (፡), and sentences ended with ።; spaces replaced the word divider in the modern era under European influence.

Modern use

Ge'ez itself is now mainly a liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches, but the script remains in daily use for Amharic (the working language of Ethiopia's federal government), Tigrinya (of Eritrea and the Tigray region), Tigre, and several other languages. The script is encoded in the Unicode blocks "Ethiopic" and its supplements.
