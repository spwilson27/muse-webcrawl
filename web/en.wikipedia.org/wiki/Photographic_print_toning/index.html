Photographic print toning


Toning is a photographic process that changes the colour of black-and-white prints or increases their archival stability by converting the image silver to another compound.

In photography, toning is a method of changing the colour of black-and-white photographs. In analog photography, it is a chemical treatment carried out on silver-based photographic prints. Toning changes the hue of the print, changes its contrast and tonal range, and — importantly for preservation — can replace the metallic silver image with a more chemically stable compound.

Types of toner

Sepia toning converts silver to silver sulfide, giving warm brown tones; sulfide toning (including selenium and sulfur toners used archivally) provides the greatest image permanence. Selenium toning produces subtle shifts from neutral to purplish-brown and is the standard for archival fine-art prints. Gold toning yields blue-black tones and was prized for portraiture; iron (blue) toning gives Prussian-blue images; copper toning gives red-brown hues; and carbon or platinum effects can be imitated with direct toners.

Techniques

Toners may act directly on the image silver or work through a bleach-and-redevelop process (indirect toners), which allows split toning: the shadows and highlights take different colours when partial toning is stopped early. In the digital era, toning effects are commonly imitated in software, while duotone and tritone printing apply related ideas in reproduction.
