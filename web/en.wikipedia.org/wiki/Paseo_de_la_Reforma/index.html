Paseo de la Reforma

Paseo de la Reforma
LocationMexico City, Mexico
LengthAbout 14.7 km (present-day extent)
Commissioned1860s, under Maximilian I
Known landmarksAngel of Independence, Diana the Huntress Fountain, Zona Rosa, Chapultepec

Paseo de la Reforma is a wide avenue that runs diagonally across the heart of Mexico City. Modelled on the great boulevards of European capitals such as Vienna's Ringstraße and the Champs-Élysées in Paris, it is lined with monuments, office towers, hotels, and embassies, and serves as the city's principal stage for celebrations, parades, and public protests.

History

The avenue was laid out in the 1860s during the Second Mexican Empire. Emperor Maximilian I commissioned it — initially called the Paseo de la Emperatriz (Promenade of the Empress) — to connect the National Palace area with his residence at Chapultepec Castle, shortening his consort Carlota's route across the city. After the restoration of the Republic under Benito Juárez it was renamed for the Reform (La Reforma) era. Under Porfirio Díaz, the boulevard was embellished with monuments to national heroes, and it was progressively extended through the twentieth century toward the north of the city.

Landmarks

The most famous monument on the avenue is the Angel of Independence (El Ángel), a victory column inaugurated in 1910 for the centenary of independence, whose base houses the remains of insurgent heroes. Other landmarks include the Diana the Huntress Fountain, the Fountain of Petróleos, the Estela de Luz tower, and the skyscrapers of the financial district, among them Torre Mayor and the BBVA México tower. The avenue passes the Zona Rosa nightlife district and ends by the gates of Chapultepec Park.

Public life

On Sundays the avenue is closed to motor traffic for the Muévete en Bici programme, filling it with cyclists and pedestrians. It is the customary route for military parades, championship celebrations by sports fans, and mass demonstrations, making it one of Mexico's most symbolically charged public spaces.
