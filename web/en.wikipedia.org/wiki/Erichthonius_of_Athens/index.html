Erichthonius of Athens

The birth of Erichthonius (image placeholder)

Erichthonius (Ancient Greek: Ἐριχθόνιος) was a legendary early ruler of ancient Athens in Greek mythology. According to one tradition, he was born of the soil (autochthonous) after the god Hephaestus attempted to embrace the goddess Athena: Hephaestus's semen fell upon the earth (or upon Athena's leg and was wiped away with wool thrown to the ground), and from the earth sprang Erichthonius. Athena placed the infant in a small chest and entrusted it to the daughters of Cecrops, warning them not to open it; overcome by curiosity, they did so, and were driven mad by the sight of the child — depicted as a serpent, or a child entwined by serpents — and threw themselves from the Acropolis.

Reign

As king of Athens, Erichthonius was credited with founding the Panathenaic festival in honour of Athena and with setting up the wooden cult image of the goddess on the Acropolis. Some accounts credit him with the invention of the four-horse chariot, modelled on the chariot of the sun, and with introducing the use of silver money. He was said to have been the son or successor of Amphictyon or of Dardanus traditions in some variant lists; in the standard Athenian king list he succeeded Amphictyon and was in turn succeeded by his son Pandion I.

Interpretation

Erichthonius embodied the Athenian claim to autochthony — that the Athenians had sprung from the very soil of Attica. The Erechtheion on the Acropolis, named after the related figure Erechtheus (with whom Erichthonius was sometimes conflated or treated as grandfather), preserved traces of his cult and of the sacred serpent of Athena.
