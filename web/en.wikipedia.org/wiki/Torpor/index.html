Torpor


Bats commonly use brief daily torpor bouts to conserve energy.

Torpor is a state of decreased physiological activity in an animal, usually marked by a reduced body temperature and metabolic rate. Torpor enables animals to survive periods of reduced food availability. The term "torpor" can refer to the time a hibernator spends at low body temperature, lasting days to weeks, or it can refer to a period of low body temperature and metabolism lasting less than 24 hours, as in "daily torpor".

Animals that undergo daily torpor include birds (even tiny hummingbirds, notably Cypselomorphae) and some mammals, including many marsupial species, rodent species (such as mice), and bats. During the active part of the day, such animals maintain normal body temperature and activity levels, but their metabolic rate and body temperature drop during a portion of the day (often night) to conserve energy.

Physiology

During torpor, metabolic rate may fall to as little as 1–5% of the resting rate in some small mammals, and body temperature drops correspondingly toward ambient temperature, though it remains regulated at a lower set point. Heart rate, breathing rate, and oxygen consumption all decline sharply. Entry into and arousal from torpor are controlled processes: arousal in particular is energetically expensive, powered largely by brown adipose tissue thermogenesis in mammals. The depth and duration of torpor bouts are influenced by ambient temperature, food availability, body condition, and photoperiod.

Torpor, hibernation, and aestivation

Hibernation is an extended form of torpor used to survive winter conditions: hibernators such as ground squirrels, marmots, and some bears spend weeks to months in a season of repeated torpor bouts interrupted by brief periodic arousals. Aestivation is the analogous state used to survive hot, dry periods, employed by animals such as lungfish and some snails and rodents. Some animals, such as the fat-tailed dwarf lemur of Madagascar, hibernate in a tropical climate, showing that torpor responds to resource scarcity rather than cold alone.

Adaptive value

Torpor saves energy when food is scarce or when foraging gains are low relative to thermoregulatory costs. It can reduce water loss, allow animals to exploit unpredictable environments, and may reduce predation risk during inactivity. However, prolonged torpor has costs: suppressed immune function, accumulated sleep debt, oxidative stress on rewarming, and lost opportunities for foraging and reproduction. Many species therefore balance torpor depth against these costs, arousing periodically.

Evolution and distribution

Torpor occurs across the endothermic lineages — in monotremes, marsupials, and placental mammals, and in many birds — and in some fishes and reptiles as a general depression of activity. Its wide and scattered distribution suggests it is an ancient and flexible physiological strategy. Studies of torpor have attracted interest for possible biomedical applications, including organ preservation and long-duration human spaceflight.
