A psalm is a sacred song or hymn, in particular any of the 150 songs and poems constituting the Book of Psalms in the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. The English word derives, through Latin psalmus, from the Greek psalmos, "song sung to the harp", itself a translation of the Hebrew mizmor, a song accompanied by a stringed instrument. Individual psalms function as self-contained liturgical poems: they may be sung, chanted or read, and are classified by form critics into genres such as hymns of praise, laments, thanksgiving songs, royal psalms and wisdom psalms.

Characteristics of an individual psalm

Most psalms are written in Hebrew verse, whose defining device is parallelism: paired lines in which the second echoes, intensifies or contrasts the first. Many carry superscriptions of uncertain antiquity attributing authorship — ledawid, "of/for David", being the most common — or giving performance directions ("To the choirmaster", "A song of ascents"). Psalm 23, "The LORD is my shepherd", and Psalm 137, "By the rivers of Babylon", are among the most widely known single psalms in world literature.

Use

Individually, psalms are used in Jewish prayer (where selected psalms constitute Tehillim recitation), in Christian liturgy and the Liturgy of the Hours, in funerals and memorials, and in private devotion. Metrical settings of single psalms — paraphrases fitted to hymn tunes — formed the core of Reformed worship, and composers from Monteverdi to Bernstein have set individual psalms as concert works.
