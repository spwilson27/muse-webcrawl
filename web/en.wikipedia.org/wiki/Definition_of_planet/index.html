Definition of planet

The definition of the term planet has changed several times since the word was coined by the ancient Greeks. For most of history there was no formal definition, but the discovery of ever more planet-like bodies in the outer Solar System — culminating in the discovery of Eris in 2005 — forced the International Astronomical Union (IAU) to adopt a formal definition in 2006. Under that definition, Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet, a decision that remains controversial.

The IAU definition

Resolution 5A, adopted at the IAU General Assembly in Prague on 24 August 2006, defines a planet as a celestial body that: (a) is in orbit around the Sun; (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape; and (c) has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. A body satisfying (a) and (b) but not (c), and which is not a satellite, is classified as a dwarf planet. The definition applies by its terms only to the Solar System; exoplanets are addressed by a separate 2003 working statement based on the deuterium-burning mass limit.

History

The Greek word planetes ("wanderers") referred to the Sun, Moon and the five visible planets. The Copernican revolution reclassified Earth as a planet and demoted the Sun and Moon. Uranus (1781) and Neptune (1846) were added without controversy, as briefly were Ceres and the other early asteroids, which were later demoted once their population was understood — a precedent often cited in the Pluto debate. Pluto, discovered in 1930, was initially believed to be Earth-sized; as its true size shrank and the Kuiper belt was discovered from 1992, its status weakened.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics, notably Alan Stern, object to the "clearing the neighbourhood" criterion as vague and as making planethood depend on location rather than intrinsic properties. A rival geophysical definition, proposed in 2017, defines a planet as a sub-stellar mass body that has never undergone nuclear fusion and has enough self-gravity to be spheroidal — a definition that would restore Pluto and include large moons such as Europa and Earth's Moon. The debate continues in planetary science and in public culture.
