Ancient music
PeriodPrehistory – c. AD 500 (varies by culture)
Key survivalsHurrian songs, Seikilos epitaph, Delphic hymns, bone flutes

Ancient music refers to the musical systems and practices of prehistoric and ancient cultures — among them Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome, China, India, and the pre-Columbian Americas — before the development of written musical traditions of the medieval period. Evidence comes from archaeological instruments, iconography, literary descriptions, music theory treatises, and a small number of surviving notated pieces.

Prehistory and the earliest instruments

The oldest widely accepted musical instruments are bone and ivory flutes from caves in the Swabian Jura of Germany, dated to about 40,000 years ago; the claimed Neanderthal "Divje Babe flute" remains controversial. Lyres and harps appear in Mesopotamian and Egyptian art of the third millennium BC, including the elaborate lyres buried in the Royal Tombs of Ur (c. 2600 BC).

Mesopotamia and the earliest notation

Cuneiform tablets from Ugarit (c. 1400 BC) preserve the Hurrian songs, including the Hymn to Nikkal, the oldest substantially complete notated song known. Babylonian texts describe tuning systems and intervals, suggesting a heptatonic scale theory. Music was central to temple ritual, royal ceremony, and epic performance.

Greece and Rome

The Greeks developed an extensive theory of harmonics and modes (harmoniai) associated with Pythagoras and later writers such as Aristoxenus, and music held a central place in education, drama, and religious festivals. Instruments included the lyre, the kithara, and the double-reed aulos. Some sixty fragments of Greek notation survive, among them the Delphic hymns (2nd century BC) and the Seikilos epitaph (1st–2nd century AD), the oldest complete surviving composition. Roman music inherited Greek instruments and practice, adding large brass instruments such as the cornu and hydraulis (water organ).

Other traditions

China developed bronze bell sets (bianzhong) tuned to precise pitches by the Zhou dynasty, and Indian tradition produced the chant of the Sāmaveda and the theoretical treatise Nāṭya Śāstra. Reconstruction of ancient music — through replicas of instruments and deciphered notations — is an active field, and modern performers have recorded many recovered pieces.
