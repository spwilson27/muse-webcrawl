Self-Reliance

"Self-Reliance" is an 1841 essay written by American philosopher and essayist Ralph Waldo Emerson. It contains the most thorough statement of one of Emerson's recurrent themes: the need for each individual to avoid conformity and false consistency, and to follow his own instincts and ideas. It is the source of famous aphorisms such as "Trust thyself", "Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist", and "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds".

History

Emerson developed the essay's ideas over many years in his journals and lectures. The first full draft grew out of sermons and lectures given in the 1830s, including "Trust Yourself". It was published in Essays: First Series in 1841.

Themes

The essay urges readers to trust their own intuition — the "aboriginal" self that Emerson held to be a spark of the divine, universal mind. Society, he argues, conspires against individuality: "Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members." Conformity and reliance on precedent blunt original thought, while genuine greatness comes from speaking one's latent conviction. Emerson also addresses prayer, travel, property and imitation of others, treating each as a temptation to surrender one's own genius.

Influence

"Self-Reliance" became a foundational text of American individualism and transcendentalism, influencing figures from Henry David Thoreau to later writers on civil disobedience and personal authenticity. Its counsel to trust oneself has been both celebrated as liberation and criticised as licence for egotism.
