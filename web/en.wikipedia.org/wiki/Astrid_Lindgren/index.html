Astrid Lindgren (14 November 1907 – 28 January 2002) was a Swedish writer of fiction and screenplays, best known for her children's books featuring Pippi Longstocking, Emil of Lönneberga, Karlsson-on-the-Roof and the Children of Noisy Village. Her works have been translated into around 100 languages and have sold roughly 200 million copies worldwide, making her among the most translated authors of all time.

Life and career

Lindgren grew up on a farm near Vimmerby in Småland, whose landscape and characters suffuse her fiction. She invented Pippi — the strongest girl in the world, who lives alone at Villa Villekulla with a horse and a monkey — as stories for her daughter Karin, and the book was published in 1945 after winning a competition at Rabén & Sjögren, where Lindgren then worked as a children's book editor for over 25 years. Her later books take on graver themes: The Brothers Lionheart (1973) addresses death, and Ronia the Robber's Daughter (1981) became a classic of children's fantasy.

Public influence

Lindgren was a formidable public figure in Sweden. Her 1976 satirical fairy tale "Pomperipossa in Monismania," mocking a marginal tax rate of 102% on her income, contributed to the fall of the long-governing Social Democratic government. Her advocacy also led to strengthened Swedish animal-protection law (the "Lex Lindgren," 1988). The Astrid Lindgren Memorial Award, founded by the Swedish government in 2002, is the world's largest children's literature prize.
