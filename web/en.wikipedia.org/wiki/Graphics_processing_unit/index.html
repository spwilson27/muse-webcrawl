Graphics processing unit

A graphics processing unit (GPU) is a specialised electronic circuit designed to accelerate the creation of images and video. Its massively parallel architecture — thousands of smaller processing cores — makes it far more efficient than a general-purpose CPU at the repetitive calculations involved in rendering and, increasingly, in scientific computing and machine learning.

History

Graphics accelerators developed through the 1980s and 1990s from fixed-function display chips. NVIDIA's GeForce 256 (1999), marketed as the first "GPU", integrated transform and lighting on-chip. Programmable shaders followed, and NVIDIA's CUDA platform (2007) opened GPUs to general-purpose computing (GPGPU).

Architecture and uses

GPUs execute the stages of the graphics pipeline in hardware, but their throughput on dense linear algebra has made them the dominant hardware for training deep neural networks; data-centre GPUs now underpin large language models. GPUs are supplied as discrete graphics cards, integrated into CPUs and SoCs, and in clusters for high-performance computing.
