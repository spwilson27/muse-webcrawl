Assemblage (art)

Assemblage
An assemblage of found objects
MediumThree-dimensional composition of found objects and materials
Term coinedJean Dubuffet, early 1950s
Key artistsMarcel Duchamp, Kurt Schwitters, Joseph Cornell, Robert Rauschenberg, Louise Nevelson
RelatedCollage, readymade, found object, bricolage

Assemblage is an artistic form or medium in which three-dimensional compositions are made from found objects and materials not originally intended as art supplies. As the three-dimensional counterpart of collage, assemblage retains the identities of its components while combining them into a new whole.

History

The term was coined by Jean Dubuffet in the early 1950s for his own works incorporating butterfly wings, stones, and other materials. Its antecedents include Marcel Duchamp's readymades (from 1913), Kurt Schwitters's Merz constructions of street debris, and Pablo Picasso's constructions of wood and sheet metal. In the United States, Joseph Cornell assembled dreamlike boxed worlds from the 1930s, and in the 1950s Louise Nevelson's black wooden box walls and Robert Rauschenberg's "combines" — hybrids of painting and sculpture incorporating stuffed animals, tyres, and bedding — brought assemblage to prominence. The Museum of Modern Art's 1961 exhibition The Art of Assemblage, curated by William C. Seitz, surveyed the movement internationally.

Techniques and legacy

Assemblage artists collect, sort, and join materials by gluing, nailing, welding, or boxing them; the worn surfaces and prior uses of found objects bring associations of memory, consumer culture, and decay into the work. The practice fed into pop art, junk art, arte povera, installation art, and much contemporary sculpture.
