Trade unions in Australia

Trade unions in Australia have played a central role in the country's industrial and political history. Australia was one of the first countries where organised labour achieved major reforms, including the eight-hour day (first won by Melbourne stonemasons in 1856), and the Australian labour movement helped found one of the world's first labour parties to form government. The peak body is the Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU), founded in 1927.

An Eight Hours Day procession in Sydney.

History

Unions of skilled tradesmen formed from the 1850s, and by the 1880s powerful unions existed among shearers, miners, seamen, and wharf labourers. The defeat of the great strikes of 1890–94 — the maritime strike, and the shearers' strikes that gave rise to the song "Waltzing Matilda" — convinced unionists to pursue political action, leading to the formation of the Australian Labor Party. The Harvester Judgement of 1907 established the principle of a basic wage sufficient for a family, and for much of the 20th century Australian wages were set through a distinctive system of compulsory conciliation and arbitration.

Union density peaked at over 60% of the workforce in the early 1950s. Since the 1980s, membership has declined sharply — to around 12–14% of employees by the 2020s — reflecting structural change, enterprise bargaining reforms, and legislative changes such as the WorkChoices legislation of 2005, whose unpopularity contributed to the Howard government's defeat in 2007 and the "Your Rights at Work" union campaign.

Structure

Australian unions are mostly organised by industry or occupation, and many amalgamated into "super unions" during the 1990s. Major unions include the Australian Workers' Union, the Australian Manufacturing Workers' Union, the Construction, Forestry and Maritime Employees Union, the Australian Education Union, the Australian Nurses and Midwifery Federation, and the Shop, Distributive and Allied Employees Association. Unions are registered under federal and state industrial law and are affiliated to the ACTU and state labour councils.

Political role

Most unions are affiliated with the Australian Labor Party, funding it and supplying delegates to its conferences, though the relationship has varied over time. The movement has also been shaped by historic splits, notably the Labor split of 1955 and the role of Catholic anti-communist "Groupers".
