Correlation does not imply causation

The phrase “correlation does not imply causation” refers to the inability to legitimately deduce a cause-and-effect relationship between two events or variables solely from an observed association or correlation between them. In Latin, the fallacy is sometimes expressed as cum hoc ergo propter hoc (“with this, therefore because of this”).

Explanation

When two variables A and B are correlated, several possibilities exist: A may cause B; B may cause A (reverse causation); a third variable C may cause both (a confounding factor); the relationship may involve feedback; or the correlation may be coincidence, especially with many comparisons or small samples. Humorous compilations of spurious correlations — for example, the near-perfect association between the divorce rate in Maine and per-capita margarine consumption — illustrate the point.

Establishing causation

Although correlation alone is insufficient, it can be evidence consistent with causation, and scientists use various methods to strengthen causal inference: randomized controlled experiments, temporal precedence, dose-response relationships, natural experiments, and criteria such as those proposed by Austin Bradford Hill in 1965 for epidemiology (strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, and analogy). Modern causal inference frameworks include Judea Pearl's do-calculus and the potential outcomes model.

Uses and counterpoints

The slogan is often invoked to rebut weak statistical arguments in journalism and debate. Philosophers and statisticians caution that the phrase is sometimes misused to dismiss correlations that genuinely form part of a larger causal case — for decades, tobacco companies argued that the correlation between smoking and lung cancer did not prove causation while experimental, mechanistic, and epidemiological evidence accumulated.
