New Deal art

New Deal art refers to the artwork and public art programmes created in the United States during the Great Depression under President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Between 1933 and 1943, several federal programmes employed artists and commissioned murals, sculptures, posters, photographs and easel paintings for public buildings.

Programmes

The principal programmes were the Public Works of Art Project (PWAP, 1933–34), the Treasury Department's Section of Painting and Sculpture (1934–43), which commissioned murals and sculpture for post offices and courthouses through anonymous competitions, the Works Progress Administration's Federal Art Project (1935–43), the largest of the programmes, and the Treasury Relief Art Project. The Farm Security Administration's photography unit, employing photographers such as Dorothea Lange and Walker Evans, is often grouped with them.

Style and subject matter

New Deal art favoured accessible realism and themes of American life: labour, agriculture, industry, regional history and democratic ideals. Influenced by Mexican muralism and Regionalism, post office murals in particular depicted local history, landscapes and work. Thousands of murals and sculptures survive in public buildings across the country.

Legacy

The programmes provided relief employment to thousands of artists — at its peak the Federal Art Project employed over 5,000 — and shaped American public art, though they also drew criticism over government patronage, censorship and political content.
