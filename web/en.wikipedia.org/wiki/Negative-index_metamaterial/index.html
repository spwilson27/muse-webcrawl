Negative-index metamaterial

A negative-index metamaterial or negative refractive index metamaterial (NIM) is a metamaterial engineered so that its refractive index takes a negative value over some range of frequencies. Such materials exhibit electromagnetic properties unattainable in naturally occurring substances, because both the electric permittivity ε and the magnetic permeability μ are simultaneously negative.

History

The theoretical possibility was first described by the Russian physicist Victor Veselago in 1968, who showed that a medium with simultaneously negative ε and μ would reverse Snell's law, the Doppler effect, and Cherenkov radiation. The idea remained largely a curiosity until 2000, when John Pendry proposed a perfect lens made of such a material, and David R. Smith and colleagues experimentally demonstrated a composite structure with negative refractive index at microwave frequencies.

Properties

In a negative-index material, the Poynting vector and the wave vector point in opposite directions, so phase velocity and group velocity are antiparallel. Consequences include reversed refraction at an interface with an ordinary positive-index medium, flat-lens focusing without curvature, and the possibility of super-resolution imaging beyond the diffraction limit.

Applications

Proposed applications include superlenses for lithography and microscopy, compact antennas, beam steering, and electromagnetic cloaking devices. Losses in the resonant metallic structures used to build NIMs remain a major obstacle, particularly at optical frequencies.
