Art gallery problem

For a polygon with n vertices, ⌊n/3⌋ guards are always sufficient and sometimes necessary.

The art gallery problem is a classical problem in computational geometry: given a polygon representing the floor plan of an art gallery, determine the minimum number of guards (or cameras) needed so that every point inside the polygon is visible to at least one guard. A guard is assumed to see along straight line segments in all directions (360° field of view), with vision blocked only by the polygon's walls.

The art gallery theorem

The problem was posed by Victor Klee in 1973. Václav Chvátal proved in 1975 that for a simple polygon with n vertices, ⌊n/3⌋ guards are always sufficient and sometimes necessary. Steve Fisk gave a famously short proof: triangulate the polygon, 3-colour the vertices of the triangulation so each triangle gets one vertex of each colour, and place guards at the least-used colour class — every triangle, and hence the whole polygon, is then watched.

Variants and complexity

Extensions place guards on edges or in the interior, allow polygons with holes, consider orthogonal polygons (for which ⌊n/4⌋ guards suffice), and model outdoor terrain guarding. The problem of computing the true minimum is computationally hard — it is NP-hard and indeed complete for the existential theory of the reals (∃ℝ), as shown in 2017 — so research concentrates on approximation algorithms and special cases. Related visibility problems concern illuminating polygons, watchman routes, and sensor placement in robotics.
