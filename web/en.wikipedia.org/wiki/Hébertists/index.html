Hébertists

Hébertists
Leader: Jacques René Hébert
Active: 1791–1794
Ideology: Radical republicanism, dechristianization, direct democracy
Base: Cordeliers Club, Paris Commune

The Hébertists (French: Hébertistes), or Exaggerators (Exagérés), were a radical political faction during the French Revolution associated with Jacques René Hébert, editor of the popular newspaper Le Père Duchesne. Drawn largely from the Cordeliers Club and the Paris Commune, they championed the interests of the sans-culottes, demanded price controls and harsh measures against hoarders and counter-revolutionaries, and pushed the Revolution in an increasingly radical direction.

Program and activities

Hébertists pressed for the maximum on grain prices, requisitioning, the levée en masse, and terror against speculators and aristocrats. They were leading advocates of dechristianization: replacing the Catholic calendar with the Revolutionary calendar, closing churches, staging the Festival of Reason at Notre-Dame in November 1793, and pressuring priests to abdicate and marry. Prominent figures included Hébert, Pierre Gaspard Chaumette (procureur of the Commune), Anacharsis Cloots, François Chabot, and Jean-Baptiste Gobel, the constitutional bishop of Paris who famously renounced his office.

Fall

In early 1794 the Hébertists' calls for renewed popular insurrection and their agitation over food shortages alarmed the Committee of Public Safety. Robespierre and Saint-Just, who opposed dechristianization excesses and defended the Cult of the Supreme Being, struck first. Hébert and eighteen associates were arrested in March 1794, convicted by the Revolutionary Tribunal on largely fabricated conspiracy charges, and guillotined on 24 March 1794. Their fall, followed weeks later by the execution of Danton's more moderate Indulgents, left the Committee unchallenged but deprived the regime of its popular base — a step toward the Thermidorian Reaction of July 1794.

Legacy

Historians such as Albert Soboul portrayed the Hébertists as spokesmen of the sans-culotte movement; others have seen them as demagogues whose atheist campaign alienated provincial France. The term "Hébertist" later became a byword for ultra-revolutionary extremism.
