Wahhabism (Arabic: الوهابية, al-Wahhābiyyah) is a reformist religious movement within Sunni Islam, based on the teachings of the 18th-century Arab scholar Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792). It is the official Islamic tradition of Saudi Arabia, alongside the Hanbali school of jurisprudence. Followers generally prefer to call themselves Salafis or Muwahhidun ("those who assert the unity of God"), regarding "Wahhabi" as a derogatory label coined by opponents.

Doctrine

The movement centres on tawhid — the absolute oneness and uniqueness of God. Ibn Abd al-Wahhab taught that many contemporary practices — supplication at saints' tombs, seeking intercession of the dead, building over graves, and celebrating the Prophet's birthday — constituted shirk (idolatry) that excluded their practitioners from Islam. Adherence was defined by al-wala' wa-l-bara' (loyalty to believers and disavowal of unbelievers). Scripture was to be interpreted literally and directly, in the tradition of Ahmad ibn Hanbal and Ibn Taymiyya, rejecting theological speculation (kalam) and blind adherence (taqlid) to the medieval schools.

History

In 1744 Ibn Abd al-Wahhab formed a pact with Muhammad ibn Saud, ruler of Diriyah, uniting his religious reform with Saudi political expansion. The resulting First Saudi State conquered most of Arabia, sacked the Shia city of Karbala (1802), and briefly occupied Mecca and Medina before being destroyed by Ottoman-Egyptian forces in 1818. A second Saudi state and, after 1932, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia restored the alliance between the Al Saud and the religious establishment descended from the founder (the Al al-Shaykh).

From the 1970s, Saudi oil wealth funded a worldwide propagation of Wahhabi/Salafi doctrine through mosques, schools, publishing, and charities, giving the movement influence far beyond Arabia. Critics within and outside Islam have associated Wahhabism with religious intolerance and have debated its influence on militant movements; Saudi authorities and scholars reject these links. Since 2017, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has curtailed the religious establishment's social powers in Saudi Arabia, promising a return to "moderate Islam" and restricting the religious police.
