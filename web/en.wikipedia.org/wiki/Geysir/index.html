Geysir (Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈceiːsɪr̥]), sometimes known as The Great Geysir, is a geyser in the Haukadalur geothermal area of southwestern Iceland, on the slopes of Laugarfjall hill. It is the first geyser known to modern Europeans, and the word geyser in English derives from its name — from the Icelandic verb geysa, "to gush".

History of activity

Research indicates that Geysir has been active for about 10,000 years. The oldest written accounts of the Haukadalur hot springs date to 1294, when earthquakes activated or reactivated springs in the valley. In its most active phases, Geysir could hurl boiling water up to 60 metres into the air; a measurement in 1845 claimed a height of 170 metres.

Geysir's activity has always been strongly affected by earthquakes, which open or close its conduit, and by human intervention. In the 1930s a channel was cut through the silica rim of its basin to lower the water level and stimulate eruptions, and for decades eruptions were sometimes induced with soap for special occasions such as Iceland's national day. Following earthquakes in 2000, Geysir erupted again to heights of up to 122 metres over two days, but it has since returned to long periods of dormancy with only occasional eruptions.

The Haukadalur area

Geysir lies on the Golden Circle tourist route, near the waterfall Gullfoss and Þingvellir National Park. Its neighbour, the geyser Strokkur, erupts reliably every 5–10 minutes to heights of 15–30 metres and is now the main attraction of the site, which also includes boiling mud pots, fumaroles, and other hot springs such as Litli Geysir.
