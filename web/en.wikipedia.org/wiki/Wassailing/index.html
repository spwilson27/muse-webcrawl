Wassailing

Wassailing is an English folk tradition associated with Twelfth Night and the Christmas season, involving singing, drinking, and blessing. The word derives from the Old Norse toast ves heil and the Old English wæs hæl — "be in good health" — answered by drinc hæl, "drink and be healthy".

Forms of the custom

In the house-visiting wassail, singers went from door to door carrying a wassail bowl of spiced ale or cider, offering songs and good wishes in exchange for food, drink, or money — a custom closely related to carolling. In the orchard wassail, practised especially in the cider counties of the West Country such as Somerset and Devon, villagers gathered in apple orchards, typically on Twelfth Night or Old Twelfth Night (17 January), to sing to the trees, pour cider over their roots, place cider-soaked toast in the branches for the birds, and make noise to drive away evil spirits and encourage a good harvest.

Modern revivals

Many orchard wassails were revived in the late 20th and early 21st centuries by cider makers, morris sides and community orchards, and the custom is now celebrated at farms and gardens across southern England, often involving a Wassail King or Queen and the firing of shotguns through the branches.
