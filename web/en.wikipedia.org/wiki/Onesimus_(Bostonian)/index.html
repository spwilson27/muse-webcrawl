Onesimus (Bostonian)

Onesimus (c. 1670s–1700s) was an enslaved African man in Boston, Massachusetts, whose knowledge of inoculation was central to the introduction of smallpox inoculation in colonial America during the epidemic of 1721.

Inoculation in Boston

Onesimus was enslaved by the Puritan minister Cotton Mather, who had been given him in 1706. When Mather asked Onesimus whether he had had smallpox, Onesimus described a practice used in his homeland in West Africa: material from the pustule of an infected person was placed into a cut in the skin of a healthy person, producing a mild case of the disease and immunity thereafter — the procedure known as inoculation or variolation. Mather also found corroborating accounts from other enslaved Africans and from reports of the practice in the Ottoman Empire.

The 1721 epidemic

When smallpox struck Boston in 1721, Mather persuaded physician Zabdiel Boylston to perform inoculations despite fierce public opposition, including a bomb thrown through Mather's window. Of roughly 250 people inoculated, only about 2 percent died, compared with about 14–15 percent of those who caught smallpox naturally. The episode was an early milestone in the history of immunization, though Onesimus's own role was long overlooked.

Later life

Onesimus partially purchased his freedom in 1716, after which Mather required him to continue household work; little is recorded of his later life. In 2016, Boston magazine readers named him among the best Bostonians of all time, and his contribution is now widely commemorated.
