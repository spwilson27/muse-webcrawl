Triticale

Triticale (× Triticosecale) is a hybrid of wheat (Triticum) and rye (Secale) first bred in laboratories during the late 19th century in Scotland and Germany. The name combines the Latin names of its parent genera. Commercially available triticale is almost always a second-generation hybrid, produced by crossing two kinds of primary triticales.

History

The first "true" triticale was produced in 1888 by the German botanist Wilhelm Rimpau, who managed to obtain a partially fertile hybrid. Earlier, in 1875, the Scottish botanist Stephen Wilson reported an infertile wheat–rye cross. The development of the alkaloid colchicine to double chromosome numbers in the 1930s made fertile amphidiploid triticales practical, and systematic breeding programmes expanded in the mid-20th century, notably in Canada, Mexico (at CIMMYT) and Poland.

Characteristics

Triticale was bred to combine the grain quality and yield potential of wheat with the disease resistance, winter hardiness and tolerance of poor soils of rye. It generally requires less fertilizer and fewer fungicide applications than wheat and performs well on marginal land. Modern varieties have largely overcome early problems such as shrivelled grain and high levels of ergot susceptibility.

Cultivation and uses

Poland, Germany, Belarus, France and Russia are among the largest producers; world production has been on the order of 15–17 million tonnes in recent years. The grain is used mainly as animal feed, but also for milling and baking (usually blended with wheat flour), breakfast cereals, and as a forage and cover crop. Triticale is also of interest as a bioenergy feedstock because of its high biomass yield.
