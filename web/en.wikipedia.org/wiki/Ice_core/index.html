Ice core

Ice core
Oldest continuous record: ~800,000 years (EPICA Dome C)
Key sites: Greenland, Antarctica
Measures: temperature proxies, gases, ash, dust

An ice core is a core sample removed from an ice sheet or high mountain glacier. Because ice forms from the incremental build-up of annual snow layers, lower layers are older than upper ones, so an ice core contains ice formed over tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years — a frozen archive of past climate.

What cores reveal

Trapped air bubbles preserve samples of ancient atmosphere, directly showing past carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide concentrations. Oxygen and hydrogen isotope ratios in the water act as a thermometer. Dust, volcanic ash (tephra), sea salt, pollen and cosmogenic isotopes such as beryllium-10 record eruptions, aridity and solar activity.

Major projects

Greenland projects GRIP, GISP2 and NGRIP recovered records spanning the last glacial cycle, revealing abrupt Dansgaard–Oeschger warmings. In Antarctica, Vostok cores reached about 420,000 years; the European EPICA Dome C core extended the continuous record to roughly 800,000 years, and the international Beyond EPICA project aims for 1.5 million years. Mountain cores from the Andes and Himalayas record tropical climate. Dating combines layer counting, flow modelling and volcanic tie points. See Paleoclimatology and Greenland ice sheet.
