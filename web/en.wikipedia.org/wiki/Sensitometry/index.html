Sensitometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sensitometry is the scientific study of light-sensitive materials, especially photographic film. It was pioneered in the 1890s by Ferdinand Hurter and Vero Charles Driffield, whose work established the methods still used to describe the response of an emulsion to light.

The characteristic curve

The central tool of sensitometry is the characteristic curve, also called the Hurter–Driffield curve or H&D curve. It plots the optical density of the developed emulsion (vertical axis) against the logarithm of the exposure (horizontal axis). The curve has three main regions: the toe, where density rises slowly and shadow detail is recorded; the straight-line portion, whose slope (called gamma) measures contrast; and the shoulder, where the emulsion approaches its maximum density Dmax. The minimum density of the film base plus fog is called Dmin.

Quantities measured

From the characteristic curve, sensitometrists derive the film speed (and hence standards such as ISO speed), the exposure latitude, the average gradient, and the contrast index. Testing is done with a sensitometer, which exposes the film through a calibrated step wedge, after which the densities are read with a densitometer.

Applications

Beyond photography, sensitometric methods are used in radiography, motion-picture laboratory control, printing, and the calibration of other detector systems, including modern digital sensors, whose response curves are analysed in an analogous way.
