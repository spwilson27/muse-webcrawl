PL/I on IBM System/360

The PL/I programming language was designed for, and first implemented on, the IBM System/360 family of mainframe computers, announced in 1964. Under the nested path PL/IBM System/360 this article covers that original implementation: the language–machine pairing that defined PL/I in practice for most of its users.

Development

PL/I grew out of a joint IBM and SHARE user-group effort, originally under the name NPL ("New Programming Language"), intended to replace both Fortran and COBOL with a single language for the new System/360 line. The name was changed to PL/I ("Programming Language One") before its release. IBM's Hursley laboratory in England was responsible for much of the language design and the first compilers.

Compilers

IBM delivered two principal System/360 compilers. The PL/I (F) compiler, released with OS/360, was a large program that required substantial memory by the standards of the day; the "F" referred to the memory class of System/360 models it targeted. For the smaller DOS/360 operating system, IBM supplied a subset compiler, PL/I (D). The later Optimizing and Checkout compilers of the 1970s improved code quality and interactive debugging.

Characteristics

The System/360 implementation established the language's defining features: default attributes for variables, extensive built-in functions, stream and record I/O, multitasking support in OS/360 installations, preprocessor facilities, and ON-condition exception handling. The machine's byte-addressable architecture and support for both binary and packed-decimal arithmetic shaped PL/I's rich data types, which included FIXED DECIMAL and PICTURE data aimed at commercial work.

Legacy

Although PL/I never displaced Fortran or COBOL, the System/360 implementations made it a major mainframe language, used heavily in commercial data processing, in the Multics operating system project (whose kernel was written in PL/I), and in later systems such as Prime Computer's PRIMOS.
