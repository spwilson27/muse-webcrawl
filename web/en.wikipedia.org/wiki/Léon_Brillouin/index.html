Born: 7 August 1889, Sèvres, France
Died: 4 October 1969, New York City
Field: Theoretical physics

Léon Brillouin

Léon Nicolas Brillouin (1889–1969) was a French physicist who made fundamental contributions to quantum mechanics, solid-state physics, and information theory. He was the son of physicist Marcel Brillouin and studied in Paris, where his teachers included Marie Curie and Paul Langevin.

Work

Brillouin introduced the concept of Brillouin zones, central to the theory of electron bands in solids, and independently developed the WKB (Brillouin–Wentzel–Kramers) approximation in quantum mechanics. He also described Brillouin scattering of light by acoustic waves, predicted theoretically and later widely used in optics and materials science. During the Second World War he emigrated to the United States, worked on radar, and later became a professor at Columbia University. His book Science and Information Theory (1956) linked information with entropy and physical measurement, coining the term "negentropy" and exploring Maxwell's demon.

Legacy

The Brillouin zone, Brillouin function, and Brillouin scattering commemorate his name. He was elected to the French Academy of Sciences and received numerous honours on both sides of the Atlantic.
