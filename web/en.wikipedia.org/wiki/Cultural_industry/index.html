Cultural industry


Mass entertainment as industry: the term's original target

The term culture industry (German: Kulturindustrie) was coined by the critical theorists Theodor Adorno (1903–1969) and Max Horkheimer (1895–1973), who argued in Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947) that popular culture is akin to a factory producing standardised cultural goods — films, radio programmes, magazines, etc. — that are used to manipulate mass society into passivity and to pacify and control consumers.

The argument

Adorno and Horkheimer deliberately used the singular "culture industry" rather than "mass culture" to stress that the phenomenon was not culture arising spontaneously from the masses but a planned, commercial production tailored to mass consumption. Standardisation is concealed by "pseudo-individuality" — superficial differences that make products seem unique. Amusement under late capitalism, they argued, becomes an extension of work: entertainment reconciles people to their labour and to the existing order, while authentic art's critical and negative power is neutralised.

Later usage

In the second half of the twentieth century the expression "cultural industries" (in the plural) came to be used in a neutral, policy-oriented sense to denote the sectors producing and distributing cultural goods and services — publishing, film, music, broadcasting, design, and later digital media — studied by researchers and promoted by bodies such as UNESCO. This descriptive usage, associated with writers such as Bernard Miège and David Hesmondhalgh, diverges sharply from the Frankfurt School's critical intent, a shift some later theorists have questioned.
