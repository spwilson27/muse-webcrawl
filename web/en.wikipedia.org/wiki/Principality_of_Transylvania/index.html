Principality of Transylvania

Placeholder image: Transylvania and the Partium in the 16th–17th centuries

The Principality of Transylvania was a semi-independent state ruled primarily by Hungarians, existing from 1570 until 1711. It emerged after the Ottoman conquest of central Hungary divided the medieval Kingdom of Hungary, and it survived as an Ottoman vassal state frequently caught between the Ottoman and Habsburg empires.

Origins

After the Battle of Mohács (1526) and the Ottoman capture of Buda (1541), eastern Hungary under John Zápolya and his son John Sigismund became a separate polity. The Treaty of Speyer (1570), by which John Sigismund renounced the royal title in favour of "Prince of Transylvania," is conventionally taken as the principality's founding. It included Transylvania proper and adjoining Hungarian counties known as the Partium.

Political and religious order

The principality was governed by an elected prince and a Diet representing the "three nations" — the Hungarian nobility, the Székely seats, and the Saxon towns. Its 1568 Edict of Torda was among Europe's earliest legal guarantees of religious freedom, recognising Catholic, Lutheran, Calvinist, and Unitarian worship.

Golden age and decline

Under Gabriel Bethlen (1613–1629) Transylvania prospered and intervened in the Thirty Years' War against the Habsburgs. George II Rákóczi's disastrous Polish campaign (1657) brought Ottoman retaliation. After the failed siege of Vienna (1683), Habsburg forces occupied Transylvania; the Diploma Leopoldinum (1691) confirmed its privileges under Habsburg rule. The last prince, Francis II Rákóczi, led a war of independence (1703–1711) that ended with the Peace of Szatmár, after which Transylvania was a Habsburg grand principality.
