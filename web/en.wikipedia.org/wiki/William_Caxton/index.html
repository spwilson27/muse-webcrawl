William Caxton (c. 1422 – c. 1491) was an English merchant, diplomat, and writer. He is thought to be the first person to introduce a printing press into England, in 1476, and as a printer was the first English retailer of printed books. His translation of the Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye, printed in Bruges around 1473–1474, was the first book printed in the English language.

Early life and career as a merchant

Caxton was born in the Weald of Kent, probably between 1415 and 1424. He was apprenticed to Robert Large, a wealthy London silk merchant (mercer) and Lord Mayor of London. After Large's death, Caxton moved to Bruges in the Low Countries, then the centre of the wool and cloth trade, where he prospered and became governor of the English merchant adventurers. His travels and diplomatic work for Margaret of York, sister of Edward IV, gave him access to the Burgundian court.

Printing

In Cologne in 1471–1472 Caxton learned the art of printing. Returning to Bruges, he set up a press with the Flemish scribe Colard Mansion and printed his own translation of Raoul Lefèvre's Recueil des histoires de Troie — the first book printed in English. In 1476 he established a press at Westminster, London. Among the first books printed in England were Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (c. 1477), the Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres (1477) — the first dated printed book in England — and editions of Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485) and works by Gower and Lydgate.

Translations and legacy

Caxton translated many works himself from French, Latin, and Dutch, including the Game and Playe of the Chesse and Aesop's Fables. He printed about one hundred books in total, of which around twenty were his own translations. His choices of texts helped standardise the English language, and he favoured the London dialect. He died in about 1491 and was buried at St Margaret's Church, Westminster; his press was taken over by his apprentice Wynkyn de Worde.
