Alkaloid

Alkaloids are a class of naturally occurring organic compounds that mostly contain basic nitrogen atoms. The name derives from "alkali-like", and the group includes many substances with marked pharmacological effects on humans and other animals. Alkaloids are produced by a wide variety of organisms — chiefly plants, but also bacteria, fungi and some animals — and are usually bitter-tasting secondary metabolites thought to serve as defences against herbivores and pathogens.

Definition and properties

There is no single universally accepted definition, but alkaloids are typically cyclic organic compounds containing nitrogen in a negative oxidation state, of limited distribution among living organisms. Most are weakly basic, sparingly soluble in water as free bases, and form water-soluble salts with acids. Many are optically active and crystalline. The German pharmacist Carl Friedrich Wilhelm Meißner coined the term in 1819, shortly after Friedrich Sertürner isolated morphine from opium (1804) — the first alkaloid obtained in pure form.

Distribution and function

Alkaloids occur in about 20% of flowering plant species, concentrated in families such as the Papaveraceae (poppies), Ranunculaceae, Solanaceae (nightshades) and Apocynaceae. In plants they are believed to deter feeding, inhibit competing seedlings, and regulate growth. Some animals, notably poison dart frogs, sequester alkaloids from dietary sources for their own defence.

Uses

Alkaloids have been used for millennia as medicines, poisons and ritual substances. Quinine treats malaria; morphine and codeine are analgesics; vinblastine and vincristine are anticancer drugs; atropine is an essential medicine. Others — caffeine, nicotine, cocaine — are widely used recreational stimulants, and several (strychnine, curare) were historically arrow and ordeal poisons.
