Animal locomotion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Animal locomotion, in ethology, is any of a variety of methods that animals use to move from one place to another. Some modes of locomotion are (initially) propelled by the animals themselves, e.g. running, swimming, jumping, flying, hopping, soaring and gliding. There are also many animal species that depend on their environment for transportation, a type of mobility called passive locomotion, e.g. sailing (some jellyfish), kiting (spiders), rolling (some beetles and spiders) or riding other animals (phoresis).

Terrestrial locomotion

On land, animals move by walking, running, hopping, crawling, and slithering. Legged locomotion is governed by gaits — patterns of footfall such as the walk, trot, canter, and gallop. The study of animal locomotion was pioneered by Eadweard Muybridge's sequential photography in the 1870s, which settled the question of whether a galloping horse ever has all four feet off the ground (it does).

Aquatic locomotion

Swimming ranges from the undulation of fish bodies and tails, to jet propulsion in cephalopods and jellyfish, to the rowing of limbs and flippers in turtles, penguins, and marine mammals. The fastest swimmer, the sailfish, has been estimated at speeds above 100 km/h in short bursts, though such figures are disputed.

Aerial locomotion

Powered flight evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats. Other animals glide, including flying squirrels, flying fish, and Draco lizards. Birds such as the peregrine falcon are among the fastest animals in a dive.

Energetics

Biomechanists quantify locomotion by cost of transport — the energy required to move a unit of body mass over a unit distance — which typically favours swimming for aquatic animals, flight for aerial animals of a given size, and running for large terrestrial animals.
