The Georgics (Latin: Georgica) is a poem of agricultural instruction by the Roman poet Virgil, completed and published in 29 BC. Written in some 2,188 lines of dactylic hexameter and divided into four books, it is ostensibly a manual on farming, but its digressions on nature, labour, politics and mortality make it one of the most admired works of Latin literature.

Background

Virgil composed the Georgics over about seven years following his Eclogues, at the urging of his patron Maecenas, to whom the poem is addressed; tradition holds that he read it to Octavian (the future Augustus) in 29 BC. It follows the didactic tradition of Hesiod's Works and Days, the Hellenistic poet Aratus, and Lucretius' De rerum natura, and it suited the Augustan programme of moral renewal and return to the Italian land after decades of civil war.

Contents

  • Book 1 — arable farming: ploughing, soils, the agricultural calendar, weather signs, and a dark closing passage on the portents at Julius Caesar's assassination and the civil wars.
  • Book 2 — trees and vines: viticulture and olive growing, ending with the famous "praises of Italy" (laudes Italiae) and an encomium of the farmer's life.
  • Book 3 — livestock: the breeding and care of cattle and horses, sheep and goats, closing with a harrowing account of a cattle plague in Noricum.
  • Book 4 — beekeeping: the society of the hive as a mirror of human society, concluding with the Aristaeus epyllion, which embeds the myth of Orpheus and Eurydice and explains the bugonia, the supposed generation of bees from an ox carcass.

Throughout, the poem balances practical instruction with its great themes: labor — Jupiter's decree that life on earth be hard, so that "work conquered all" (labor omnia vicit) — the power and indifference of nature, and the fragility of human order.

Influence

The Georgics shaped the whole later georgic tradition, including John Philips's Cyder, James Thomson's The Seasons and John Dyke's eidylls, and it was the model for agricultural poetry in Renaissance and Enlightenment Europe. John Dryden, who translated the poem in 1697, called it "the best poem by the best poet".
