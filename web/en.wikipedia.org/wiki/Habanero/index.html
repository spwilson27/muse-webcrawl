The habanero (Capsicum chinense) is a very hot variety of chili pepper. The name means "from Havana" (La Habana), Cuba, through which the peppers were once traded, although the plant itself originates in the Amazon basin and was domesticated in lowland South America before spreading to the Yucatán Peninsula, where it remains central to the cuisine. Unripe habaneros are green; ripe pods turn orange, red, white, brown ("chocolate") or pink, are typically 2–6 cm long, and have a distinctive fruity, floral aroma beneath intense heat.

Heat and cultivars

Habaneros rate 100,000 to 350,000 on the Scoville scale, making them among the hottest commonly eaten peppers; they were long considered the world's hottest before being surpassed by varieties such as the Red Savina (a selected habanero cultivar, ~577,000 SHU) and later the ghost pepper and Carolina Reaper. The Scotch bonnet is a close Caribbean relative. Heatless or mild habanero-type cultivars (such as 'Habanada' and 'NuMex Suave') have been bred for flavour without pungency.

Culinary use

Habaneros are essential to Yucatecan cooking — in salsas, cochinita pibil marinades and pickled onion accompaniments — and are widely used in hot sauces in Mexico, the Caribbean and the United States. Since 2010, "chile habanero de la península de Yucatán" has held a Mexican denomination of origin.
