Coupled human–environment system

A coupled human–environment system (also called a coupled human and natural system, socio-ecological system, or CHANS) is a system in which human and natural components interact in complex, reciprocal ways, so that the behavior of the whole cannot be understood by studying either component alone. The concept is central to sustainability science, human ecology, and land-change science.

Characteristics

Coupled human–environment systems display reciprocal feedback loops (for example, farming practices changing land cover, which changes local climate and water availability, which in turn changes farming), nonlinear dynamics with thresholds and surprises, time lags, heterogeneity across scales, and emergent properties. Research syntheses, notably a 2007 analysis by Jianguo Liu and colleagues in Science and the Annual Review of Environment and Resources, identified recurring features across study sites worldwide: complexity arising from interactions within each subsystem as well as between them, and the importance of flows of material, energy, and information linking distant places (telecoupling).

Research approaches

Because these systems cross disciplinary boundaries, their study combines methods from ecology, geography, economics, anthropology, and systems modeling, including agent-based models, integrated assessment, and long-term case studies maintained by networks such as the U.S. National Science Foundation's Long Term Ecological Research program and its coupled natural–human systems funding initiatives. Applications include watershed management, forest transitions, coastal vulnerability, agricultural intensification, and the management of common-pool resources studied in the tradition of Elinor Ostrom's work on social-ecological systems.
