Dutch Schultz (born Arthur Simon Flegenheimer; 6 August 1901 – 24 October 1935) was an American mobster of the Prohibition era. Based in New York City, he made his fortune bootlegging beer in the Bronx and later seized control of the Harlem numbers racket, becoming one of the most feared—and erratic—figures in organised crime.

Criminal career

Flegenheimer took the name Schultz from an early employer and "Dutch" from a notorious earlier gang. During Prohibition his gang supplied beer to speakeasies across the Bronx and Manhattan, fighting a violent turf war with rivals such as Vincent "Mad Dog" Coll. After repeal he muscled in on the Harlem policy (numbers) game run by operators including Stephanie St. Clair, imposing a "tax" on the bankers and fixing payouts.

Trials and death

Prosecuted for income tax evasion, Schultz was tried in Syracuse in 1935, where a charm offensive toward locals helped win an acquittal on some charges and a hung jury on others; at a second trial in Malone he was acquitted. Special prosecutor Thomas E. Dewey then targeted his rackets, and Schultz allegedly sought permission from the Mafia Commission to murder Dewey. Instead, fearing Schultz would act alone, the Commission ordered his death: on 23 October 1935 gunmen Charles Workman and Emanuel Weiss shot Schultz and three associates at the Palace Chophouse in Newark. He died the next day, aged 34, his rambling deathbed statements to police becoming famous.
