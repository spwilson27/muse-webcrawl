Splint (medicine)

A splint is a device used for support or immobilisation of a limb or the spine. It can be used in emergency care to stabilise a suspected fracture, sprain, or dislocation, restricting movement of the injured part to reduce pain, prevent further damage to soft tissues, nerves, and blood vessels, and facilitate transport. In longer-term treatment, splints hold joints or bones in a desired position during healing or to correct deformity.

Types and materials

Unlike a cast, a splint typically does not completely encircle the limb, so it can accommodate swelling; splints are therefore often applied in the acute phase of injury and later replaced by a cast. Common materials include plaster of Paris bandages moulded over padding, fibreglass, preformed aluminium or plastic, and vacuum or air splints. Named configurations include the sugar-tong splint, the volar (gutter) splint for hand and forearm injuries, the thumb spica, the posterior leg splint, and the ankle stirrup. A traction splint, such as the Hare or Kendrick device, applies longitudinal traction to a fractured femur. Rigid boards and improvised materials (rolled newspapers, sticks) serve in first aid.

Uses

Splints are used for fractures and dislocations; sprains and tendon injuries (for example, a resting splint for mallet finger); inflammatory conditions such as carpal tunnel syndrome, where a night wrist splint keeps the joint neutral; and in dentistry, where occlusal splints treat temporomandibular disorders and bruxism. Care must be taken to pad bony prominences, check neurovascular status, and leave room for swelling to avoid compartment syndrome.
