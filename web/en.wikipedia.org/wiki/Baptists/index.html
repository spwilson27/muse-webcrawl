Baptists are a family of Protestant Christian churches and denominations distinguished by baptising only professing believers ("believer's baptism", or credobaptism) and doing so by full immersion. Baptists also emphasise the autonomy of the local congregation, the authority of Scripture, salvation by faith alone, the priesthood of all believers, and the separation of church and state with liberty of conscience.

History

The Baptist movement originated among English Separatists in Amsterdam in 1609, when John Smyth baptised himself and his congregation; Thomas Helwys returned to England and founded the first Baptist church on English soil in 1612 and wrote the first English defence of universal religious liberty. Two streams emerged: General Baptists, holding Arminian views of general atonement, and Particular Baptists, who were Calvinistic. Baptists flourished in colonial America — Roger Williams founded the first Baptist church in America at Providence in 1638 — and through missionary work spread worldwide. In the United States, the Southern Baptist Convention became the country's largest Protestant denomination; Baptists were also central to African-American church life and the civil rights movement, notably Martin Luther King Jr., a Baptist minister.

Beliefs and practices

Baptist churches are congregational: each local church governs itself, though most cooperate in associations and conventions such as the Baptist World Alliance (founded 1905). Worship centres on preaching, prayer and congregational singing. Communion (the Lord's Supper) is observed as a memorial ordinance, often monthly. Because of the emphasis on local autonomy, beliefs vary widely, from fundamentalist to liberal; common distinctives are summarised in the acronym BAPTIST (Biblical authority, Autonomy, Priesthood of believers, Two ordinances, Individual soul liberty, Separation of church and state, Two offices — pastor and deacon).
