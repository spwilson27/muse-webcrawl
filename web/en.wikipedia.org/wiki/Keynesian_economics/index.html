Keynesian economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Keynesian economics is the macroeconomic theory associated with John Maynard Keynes, set out in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), holding that aggregate demand — total spending in the economy — strongly influences output and employment, especially in the short run, and that economies can settle below full employment with wages and prices too sticky to self-correct quickly. Its policy corollary is active demand management: government spending, tax and monetary measures to stabilise the cycle.

History of the doctrine

Forged in the Great Depression, Keynesianism became the post-war orthodoxy of the mixed economy (the "golden age" of growth with low unemployment), was formalised by Samuelson's neoclassical synthesis, then battered in the 1970s by stagflation, monetarism (Friedman's natural-rate hypothesis) and rational-expectations critiques (the Lucas critique) that relegated it in academic macro to the New Keynesian reconstruction (sticky-price DSGE models). Crises revive it: the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic produced textbook Keynesian responses (stimulus spending, deficit tolerance, liquidity provision) from governments of all parties, followed by renewed argument about inflation and debt. The doctrine's political economy — it arms treasuries with a theory of action — ensures its cycles follow the business cycle itself.
