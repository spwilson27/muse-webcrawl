Variety (universal algebra)

In universal algebra, a variety is a class of algebraic structures of the same signature that is closed under the formation of homomorphic images, subalgebras, and direct products. Equivalently, by Birkhoff's theorem (the HSP theorem, proved by Garrett Birkhoff in 1935), a class of algebras is a variety if and only if it can be defined by a set of identities — equations that must hold for all values of their variables.

Definition

A class K of algebras of a given type is called a variety if:

  • whenever A is in K and B is a homomorphic image of A, then B is in K;
  • whenever A is in K and B is a subalgebra of A, then B is in K;
  • whenever a family of algebras is in K, their direct product is in K.

Examples

Many familiar classes in abstract algebra are varieties: groups, abelian groups, rings, commutative rings, lattices, Boolean algebras, and vector spaces over a fixed field can all be axiomatized by identities. By contrast, fields do not form a variety, because the defining conditions (such as the existence of multiplicative inverses for nonzero elements) cannot be expressed purely as identities, and the class of fields is not closed under direct products or subalgebras in the required sense.

Related concepts

Varieties are central objects of study in universal algebra. Important associated notions include free algebras, congruences, subdirectly irreducible algebras, and the lattice of subvarieties of a given variety, ordered by inclusion. Weaker closure conditions give rise to quasivarieties (defined by quasi-identities) and pseudovarieties (used in the theory of finite semigroups).
