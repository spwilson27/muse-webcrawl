Smart mobs

A smart mob is a group whose coordination and communication abilities have been empowered by digital communication technologies, such as text messaging and mobile phones. Smart mobs are sometimes organised by people who know each other, but often form when strangers coordinate collective action — a protest, a gathering, or a shared response to an event — through mobile communication.

The term was popularised by Howard Rheingold in his 2002 book Smart Mobs: The Next Social Revolution. Rheingold argued that the combination of mobile communication, pervasive computing and reputation systems would enable new forms of cooperation, allowing groups to act together in ways never before possible.

Examples

Rheingold and later commentators pointed to early examples including:

  • The 2001 protests in the Philippines ("People Power II"), in which text-message chains helped bring more than a million people to Epifanio de los Santos Avenue, contributing to the fall of President Joseph Estrada.
  • The anti-globalisation protests of the late 1990s, such as the 1999 Seattle WTO protests, coordinated with mobile phones and websites.
  • Flash mobs from 2003 onward — playful gatherings summoned by email and text, such as crowds suddenly freezing in a station or applauding in a shop.
  • TxtMob, a service used to coordinate protests at the 2004 US political conventions.

Later phenomena such as social-media-organised protests during the Arab Spring are often discussed as heirs to the smart mob concept.

Concepts

Rheingold's analysis linked smart mobs to technologies of cooperation: communication media that lower the cost of coordinating, reputation systems (as in eBay seller ratings), and peer-to-peer networks. He warned the same tools could enable surveillance, manipulation and destructive mobs — "the mob" can be smart for good or ill. Related concepts include swarm behaviour, collective intelligence and the "mobile ad hoc social network".

Criticism

Critics argue the concept overstates the role of technology and understates existing social movements, and that "smart mob" events often rely on conventional organisations behind the scenes. Others note that authorities quickly learned to use the same channels for monitoring and counter-messaging.
