Cross-presentation

Cross-presentation is the ability of certain antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to take up, process and present extracellular antigens with MHC class I molecules to CD8 T cells (cytotoxic T cells). This process, first described in 1976 by Michael J. Bevan, is essential for the initiation of immune responses against many viruses and tumors that do not directly infect antigen-presenting cells.

Mechanism

Ordinarily, MHC class I molecules present peptides derived from endogenous (intracellular) proteins, whereas exogenous antigens are presented on MHC class II. In cross-presentation, professional APCs — particularly certain subsets of dendritic cells — route internalized exogenous material into the class I pathway. Two main routes have been proposed: the cytosolic pathway, in which antigen is exported from the phagosome to the cytosol for proteasomal degradation and TAP-dependent transport, and the vacuolar pathway, in which antigen is degraded within endocytic compartments and loaded onto class I molecules there.

Cross-priming

When cross-presentation leads to the activation of naive CD8 T cells, the process is termed cross-priming. Cross-priming is important for immunity against peripheral tissue-restricted viruses and for the anti-tumor immune response. Conversely, cross-presentation of self-antigens without co-stimulation can lead to peripheral tolerance, a phenomenon called cross-tolerance.

Cell types

In mice, the CD8α+ (and CD103+) conventional dendritic cell subset (cDC1) is specialized for cross-presentation, expressing receptors such as CLEC9A (DNGR-1) that recognize dead-cell antigens. The human equivalent is the CD141+ (BDCA3+) dendritic cell.
