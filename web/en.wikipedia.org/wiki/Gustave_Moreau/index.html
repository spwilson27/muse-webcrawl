Gustave Moreau

Gustave Moreau (French: [ɡystav mɔʁo]; 6 April 1826 – 18 April 1898) was a French artist and an important figure in the Symbolist movement. Jean Cassou called him "the Symbolist painter par excellence." He was a prolific artist who produced over 15,000 paintings, watercolors, and drawings, usually devoted to biblical and mythological figures drawn from literature.

Life and career

Moreau was born in Paris to an architect father. He studied under François-Édouard Picot at the École des Beaux-Arts, and was deeply influenced by Théodore Chassériau. A formative trip to Italy (1857–1859) exposed him to the Old Masters. His breakthrough came at the Salon of 1864 with Oedipus and the Sphinx. His mature work — jeweled, dreamlike scenes of Salome, Orpheus, and mythological heroes — departed from the naturalism of his contemporaries and anticipated Symbolism.

Teaching and legacy

Appointed professor at the École des Beaux-Arts in 1892, Moreau was an influential and liberal teacher; his pupils included Henri Matisse and Georges Rouault. On his death he bequeathed his Paris house and studio, with some 8,000 works, to the state; it became the Musée national Gustave Moreau, opened in 1903. The Surrealists later championed his visionary art, with André Breton calling him a precursor of the movement.
