Electronic music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A wall of modular synthesizer patched with cables in a 1970s studio, an engineer adjusting oscillators]

Electronic music broadly denotes music produced using electronic and electromechanical means — from the theremin (1920) and the musique concrète of post-war Paris studios, through the Moog synthesizer, to the laptop. As a popular genre it encompasses the synth pioneers (Wendy Carlos, Kraftwerk), disco's electronic turn, synth-pop, house, techno, trance, drum and bass, dubstep and the EDM festival era.

The prehistory is academic: Pierre Schaeffer's tape compositions (from 1948), Stockhausen's Cologne studio, the BBC Radiophonic Workshop (Delia Derbyshire's realisation of the Doctor Who theme, 1963). Carlos's Switched-On Bach (1968) proved the synthesizer could sell records; Kraftwerk's minimal machine-music from Düsseldorf supplied the blueprint that hip hop, techno and synth-pop all built on.

Club cultures

House (Chicago, named for the Warehouse club and Frankie Knuckles) and techno (Detroit's Belleville Three, synthesising Kraftwerk with P-Funk) crossed to Europe, where rave culture, acid house's 'second summer of love' (1988) and Berlin's post-Wall club scene made electronic dance music the youth culture of the continent. Drum and bass, garage, dubstep and EDM mark successive waves of a still-unfolding family tree.

Tools and aesthetics

The drum machine (TR-808, 1980), the sampler, MIDI (1983) and the DAW successively put the studio in the bedroom; electronic music's aesthetics — loops, timbre over melody, the producer as auteur — now pervade all genres. Its 'who is the artist' quarrels (DJs versus musicians) have quieted into canonisation: Kraftwerk, Aphex Twin and Daft Punk are museum subjects.
