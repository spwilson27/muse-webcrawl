Bibliography

Bibliography (from Ancient Greek βιβλίον, biblion, "book" and -γραφία, -graphia, "writing"), as a discipline, is the systematic study of books and other works—such as periodicals and newspapers—as physical objects, and the preparation of lists of such works. A bibliography, in the sense familiar to students, is an alphabetized list of sources used or cited in a work of research.

Branches

Enumerative bibliography

Enumerative (or systematic) bibliography produces lists of books—by author, subject, place, or period—such as national bibliographies and library catalogues. The modern discipline is closely tied to great library collections such as the Bibliothèque nationale de France and the British Library.

Analytical and descriptive bibliography

Analytical bibliography studies books as material objects—their printing, binding, paper, and transmission—to establish textual facts. Descriptive bibliography applies these findings to produce precise descriptions of editions and issues, recording format, collation, and variants. W. W. Greg and Fredson Bowers were leading theorists of this "new bibliography."

Textual bibliography

Textual bibliography applies bibliographical evidence to the editing of texts, distinguishing the author's intended readings from errors introduced in transmission.

Citation styles

Scholarly bibliographies follow standardized citation styles, including MLA, APA, Chicago/Turabian, and Harvard referencing, each prescribing the order and punctuation of author, title, publisher, and date elements.

References: Bowers, F. Principles of Bibliographical Description (1949); Harmon, R.B. Elements of Bibliography (1998).
