New York v. United States, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), is a United States Supreme Court case holding that Congress may not 'commandeer' state legislatures by compelling states to enact or administer a federal regulatory programme. The decision struck down the 'take-title' provision of the Low-Level Radioactive Waste Policy Amendments Act of 1985.

Background

Federal law gave states incentives to provide for disposal of low-level radioactive waste generated within their borders. Most incentives — monetary payments and escalating charges — were upheld, but the 'take-title' provision forced a state that failed to arrange disposal either to take ownership of the waste or to regulate according to federal instructions. New York challenged the Act after failing to site a disposal facility.

Decision

Writing for a 6–3 majority, Justice O'Connor held that while Congress may encourage state action through spending and preemption incentives, it may not compel states to enact or administer a federal regulatory programme, because such commandeering is inconsistent with the federal structure and political accountability established by the Constitution.

Legacy

Together with Printz v. United States (1997), which extended the rule to state executive officers, New York v. United States forms the anti-commandeering doctrine, a significant limit on federal power frequently invoked in disputes over immigration enforcement, gun regulation and marijuana policy.
