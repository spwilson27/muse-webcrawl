Dandy

Era: Late 18th–19th century (Britain and France)
Exemplar: Beau Brummell
Key traits: Meticulous dress, wit, studied nonchalance
Related: Dandyism

A dandy is a man who places particular importance upon refined language, physical appearance and leisurely hobbies, pursued with the appearance of nonchalance in a cult of self. The archetypal dandy, George "Beau" Brummell, set the standard of understated elegance in Regency London, favouring immaculate linen, perfectly tied cravats and dark tailored coats over aristocratic finery.

Dandyism

Dandyism became a cultural and literary stance as much as a fashion. In France, writers such as Charles Baudelaire celebrated the dandy as an aristocrat of the spirit in a democratising age; Jules Barbey d'Aurevilly wrote a famous essay on Brummell. Oscar Wilde embodied a flamboyant later version of the figure, and the dandy echoes in modern subcultures from the Congolese sapeurs to contemporary peacock tailoring.

In culture

Though the word can carry a note of mockery — a "fop" concerned only with dress — serious treatments of dandyism see it as a philosophy of self-creation and restraint, in which elegance results from simplicity and precision rather than ornament.
