Lough Corrib (Irish: Loch Coirib) is a lake in the west of Ireland, in counties Galway and Mayo. With a surface area of about 176 km² it is the largest lake in the Republic of Ireland and the second largest on the island of Ireland after Lough Neagh. It is connected to the sea at Galway by the short River Corrib.

Geography

The lough is roughly 47 km long. Its northern basin, in limestone and karst country, is deep, while the southern basin is shallower, with the two parts joined by a narrow channel. More than 300 islands dot the lake; the best known, Inchagoill, carries early Christian and Romanesque church remains and the grave said to be of Lugnad, nephew of St Patrick. Along its western shore rise the hills of Connemara and Joyce Country; on the east lie Ashford Castle and the village of Cong.

Ecology and fisheries

Lough Corrib is designated a Special Area of Conservation and Special Protection Area, supporting otters, whooper swans, and important limestone pavement and fen habitats. It is one of Ireland's great wild brown trout and salmon fisheries — the mayfly season in May is especially famous — though invasive species such as zebra mussels and curly waterweed (Lagarosiphon major) have threatened its ecology, and issues with algal blooms and the Cryptosporidium contamination of Galway's water supply (2007) drew attention to its water quality.

History

The lake takes its name, via the older Loch nOirbsean, from the sea god Orbsen (Manannán mac Lir). William Wilde — father of Oscar — wrote a classic topographical work, Lough Corrib (1867). The Galway-to-Clifden midland railway once crossed its outflow at Galway city.
