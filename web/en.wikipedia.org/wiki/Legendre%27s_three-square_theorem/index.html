Legendre's three-square theorem

In mathematics, Legendre's three-square theorem states that a natural number can be represented as the sum of three squares of integers if and only if it is not of the form n = 4a(8b + 7) for non-negative integers a and b. The first numbers that cannot be expressed as the sum of three squares are 7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71, and so on.

History

The theorem was first stated by Adrien-Marie Legendre in his Essai sur la théorie des nombres (1798), although his proof was incomplete. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the first complete proof in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), as part of his theory of ternary quadratic forms.

Related results

The theorem sits between Lagrange's four-square theorem, which says every natural number is a sum of four squares, and the two-square theorem characterising sums of two squares. It is also closely connected with the Legendre theorem on ternary quadratic forms and the Hasse–Minkowski theorem. A modern proof uses the theory of quadratic forms over the p-adic numbers, together with Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.
