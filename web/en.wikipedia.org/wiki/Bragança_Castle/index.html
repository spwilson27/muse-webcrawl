Bragança Castle (Portuguese: Castelo de Bragança) is a medieval castle in the city of Bragança, in the Trás-os-Montes region of north-eastern Portugal, close to the Spanish border. Occupying the hilltop of the old town (the cidadela), it is one of the best-preserved castles in the country and gave its name to the House of Braganza, the royal dynasty that ruled Portugal from 1640 until 1910 and Brazil until 1889. The castle's massive keep houses a military museum, and its walls enclose the Gothic church of Santa Maria, the Domus Municipalis, and the town pillory.

History

The site was fortified early: the settlement of Brigantia is traditionally linked to Celtic and Roman occupation, and a first castle was raised in the 12th century during the reigns of the first Portuguese kings, as the frontier with the kingdom of León was contested. King Sancho I granted Bragança a charter in 1187 and ordered its defences rebuilt; the works were destroyed in Leonese invasions and renewed under Afonso III, who granted a new charter and market rights in the 13th century. The great keep (Torre de Menagem), a square tower some 33 metres high, was built in the 15th century, when Bragança had become the seat of the Dukes of Braganza, created in 1442 for Afonso, Count of Barcelos. The castle retained military significance on the Spanish frontier through the Restoration War of the 17th century and the Peninsular War, when the region saw fighting and occupation.

Architecture

The fortress consists of an irregular oval enceinte of schist and granite walls, reinforced by fifteen towers and turrets, surrounding the citadel hill. Within the walls stand the keep, the Torre da Princesa (Princess's Tower)—associated in legend with a Moorish princess or a lady held prisoner—the church of Santa Maria, rebuilt in the Renaissance period over a Romanesque foundation, and the Domus Municipalis, a unique Romanesque civic building (c. 12th–13th century) of pentagonal plan that served as a cistern and council meeting place and is regarded as a singular survival of Romanesque civil architecture on the Iberian Peninsula. Outside the main gate stands the town pillory (pelourinho), mounted on a carved boar-like stone (a survival of the pre-Roman verraco tradition).

The keep now contains the Museu Militar (Military Museum), with displays on the Portuguese army from the Middle Ages to the colonial wars. The castle was classified as a National Monument in 1910 and remains the symbol of the city, appearing on its coat of arms.
