The roe deer (Capreolus capreolus), also known as the western roe deer or European roe, is a small species of deer native to Europe and western Asia. It is one of the most widespread and numerous deer in Europe, well adapted to woodland edge, farmland and increasingly suburban habitats.

Description

Roe deer are relatively small, with a reddish-brown coat in summer that turns greyish-brown in winter, a conspicuous white rump patch, and a very short tail. Males (bucks) carry small antlers, typically with three points, which are shed each autumn and regrown over winter in velvet. Females are called does and their young fawns. Roe deer are browsers and mixed feeders, eating leaves, shoots, berries, acorns and agricultural crops.

Behaviour and reproduction

Roe deer are largely crepuscular and may be solitary or live in small groups. Bucks hold territories in spring and summer. The rut takes place in July and August, but roe deer are unusual among ungulates in practising delayed implantation: the embryo does not implant until December, so fawns (often twins) are born in May or June, coinciding with abundant food. Fawns are left hidden in vegetation while the mother feeds nearby.

Distribution and status

The species ranges from the Atlantic coast of Europe eastward to the Caspian region; the larger Siberian roe deer is now often treated as a separate species, Capreolus pygargus. Extirpated from much of Britain by the 18th century except in Scotland, roe deer were reintroduced to England in the 19th century and have since spread widely. Numbers across Europe have grown with reforestation and milder winters, bringing deer into conflict with forestry, agriculture and road traffic.

In culture

The roe deer fawn Bambi, from Felix Salten's 1923 novel Bambi, a Life in the Woods (later a Disney film, in which the character became a white-tailed deer), is among the most famous fictional deer.
