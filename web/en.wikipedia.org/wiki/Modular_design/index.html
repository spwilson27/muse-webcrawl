Modular design

Modular design, or modularity in design, is a design principle that subdivides a system into smaller parts called modules, which can be independently created, modified, replaced, or exchanged between different systems. A modular system has well-defined interfaces between its modules, so that a module can be changed without redesigning the whole product.

Characteristics

Modular systems typically exhibit compartmentalisation (the system is divided into discrete, reusable components), loose coupling between modules through standardised interfaces, and explicit, well-documented connection points. Examples range from modular furniture and prefabricated buildings to computer hardware with interchangeable expansion cards, automotive platforms sharing components across models, and modular software architectures.

Advantages and disadvantages

Benefits include easier maintenance and repair, the ability to upgrade or customise a product by swapping modules, parallel development by separate teams, and economies from reusing modules across a product family. Drawbacks can include higher initial design effort, performance penalties from standardised interfaces, and extra size, weight, or cost devoted to connectors and housing.
