Lee wave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lee wave
Lenticular clouds formed at lee-wave crests

In meteorology, lee waves are atmospheric standing waves. The most common form is mountain waves, which occur when stable air flows over a mountain range or similar terrain feature and oscillates in the lee (downwind side) of the obstacle. The horizontal component of the wave typically has a wavelength of about 1 to 50 km and a vertical amplitude of 0.1 to 1 km or more. Because the wave is stationary relative to the terrain, clouds formed at its crests remain in a fixed position relative to the ground even though air continuously flows through them.

Formation

When stably stratified air encounters an obstacle such as a mountain ridge, it is displaced upward. Gravity acts as a restoring force and the air oscillates above and below its equilibrium level, generating a train of waves that propagates downwind and often extends through the troposphere into the stratosphere. Lee waves are strongest when the wind direction is roughly perpendicular to the ridge, wind speed increases with height, and stability is greatest near the surface. A temperature inversion near ridge-top level can trap wave energy in the lower atmosphere, producing particularly vigorous waves.

They were first systematically studied by German glider pilots in the 1930s in the Riesengebirge (Giant Mountains) of Silesia, where the phenomenon was called Moazagotl after a local shepherd said to have observed the associated clouds.

Clouds

Moisture condensing at the wave crests produces characteristic smooth, lens-shaped lenticular clouds (altocumulus standing lenticularis), which may stack vertically like plates. Below each crest, a turbulent rotary circulation can form a ragged rotor cloud. From the air, bands of lee-wave clouds are a classic sign of the phenomenon.

Aviation

Lee waves are prized by glider pilots: the rising branch of the wave provides strong, smooth lift used for long-distance and high-altitude soaring flights, including height records above 20 km in the Perlan project over the Andes. Conversely, the rotors and the descending branches of mountain waves produce severe turbulence hazardous to powered aircraft, and downslope windstorms in their lee (such as the Chinook of the Rockies or the foehn of the Alps) can bring damaging gusts and rapid warming.
