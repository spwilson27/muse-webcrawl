Nanny

A nanny is a person employed to provide childcare within a family's home. Unlike a babysitter, who typically works occasionally, a nanny usually works regularly and takes substantial responsibility for the children's care, development and daily routines. Nannies may live in or live out, and may be employed full-time or part-time.

Duties

Typical duties include preparing meals, dressing and bathing children, organising educational and play activities, school runs, supervising homework, and light housekeeping related to the children. Historically, as in the great houses of Victorian and Edwardian Britain, the nanny (or "nurse") headed the nursery staff and enjoyed considerable authority, remaining a fixture of upper-class family life; the fictional Mary Poppins and the real-life "Nanny" figures of the British royal household exemplify the role's cultural prominence.

Modern profession

Today professional nannies are found worldwide, often placed through agencies and sometimes holding qualifications such as those of Britain's Norland College, founded in 1892 to train nannies. Related arrangements include the au pair, who exchanges childcare for lodging and cultural experience, and the childminder, who cares for children in the caregiver's own home. Working conditions, pay and legal protections for nannies — overwhelmingly women, frequently migrants — vary widely and are a recurring subject of labour-rights advocacy.
