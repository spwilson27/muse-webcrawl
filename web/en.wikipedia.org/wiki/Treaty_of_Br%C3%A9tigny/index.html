Treaty of Brétigny

Treaty of Brétigny
Signed: 8 May 1360, Brétigny near Chartres
Parties: England (Edward III) and France (John II)
Context: Hundred Years' War, first phase

The Treaty of Brétigny was a treaty, drafted on 8 May 1360 and ratified at Calais in October, between King Edward III of England and King John II of France, temporarily ending the first phase of the Hundred Years' War.

Terms

Edward renounced his claim to the French throne in exchange for an enlarged Aquitaine — including Gascony, Poitou, Saintonge, the Limousin, Périgord and other territories — held in full sovereignty, plus Calais and Ponthieu. France agreed to pay a ransom of three million gold crowns for John, captured at Poitiers (1356), who was released with hostages (including his son Louis of Anjou) guaranteeing payment.

Breakdown

The renunciation clauses were never formally exchanged, giving a legal pretext for resumption. When Louis escaped from parole in 1363 John honourably returned to captivity in England, dying there in 1364. War resumed in 1369 and France under Charles V recovered most of the ceded land.
