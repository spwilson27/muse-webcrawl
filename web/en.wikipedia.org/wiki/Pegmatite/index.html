Pegmatite

Granitic pegmatite showing exceptionally coarse crystals.

A pegmatite is an igneous rock composed almost entirely of interlocking crystals usually larger than 2.5 centimetres (1 in) in size; the term most commonly refers to granitic pegmatites. Pegmatites form from the final, volatile-rich fraction of a crystallizing magma, and the abundance of water and other fluxes allows a small number of crystals to grow exceptionally large, sometimes to many metres.

Formation and structure

Pegmatite bodies typically occur as dikes, lenses, or irregular pods within or near granitic intrusions. Many show internal zoning: a wall zone, one or more intermediate zones, and a core, often of massive quartz. Because they crystallize from residual melts enriched in elements excluded from earlier crystals, pegmatites concentrate rare elements such as lithium, beryllium, tantalum, niobium, caesium, and boron.

Mineralogy and economic importance

Common minerals include quartz, feldspar (microcline and albite), and muscovite; rarer species include spodumene, beryl, tourmaline, lepidolite, and topaz. Pegmatites are the principal source of lithium (from spodumene and lepidolite), an important source of tantalum, caesium, and gem-quality crystals, and a source of industrial feldspar, mica, and quartz. Famous localities include the Black Hills of South Dakota, Minas Gerais in Brazil, and deposits in Western Australia.

Classification

The widely used classification of Černý and Ercit divides granitic pegmatites into classes such as LCT (lithium–caesium–tantalum) and NYF (niobium–yttrium–fluorine) families, reflecting distinct magma sources and geochemical signatures.
