Miranda v. Arizona

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), is a landmark decision of the US Supreme Court that established the Miranda warnings: a defendant taken into police custody and interrogated must be informed of the right to remain silent, that anything said can be used against them, the right to an attorney (appointed if indigent), and the right to waive these rights knowingly. Statements obtained without the warnings are generally inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief. The holding rested on the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination applied to the inherently coercive setting of custodial interrogation.

The consolidated cases' lead defendant, Ernesto Miranda, was retried without his confession and convicted again on other evidence. Miranda became a cultural fixture (the verb “to mirandize”; the warnings recited on every police procedural) and a political lightning rod: Congress attempted to overrule it in 1968 (§ 3501), an attempt the Court struck down in Dickerson v. United States (2000), holding Miranda a constitutional rule. Later cases trimmed its edges (public-safety exception, Quarles, 1984; impeachment use of unwarned statements; Vega v. Tekoh, 2022, holding a violation does not support a § 1983 damages suit) while the core warnings endure as a fixed feature of American policing.
