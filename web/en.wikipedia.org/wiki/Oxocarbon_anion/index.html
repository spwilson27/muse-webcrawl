Oxocarbon anion


General formula: CnOn2− (cyclic examples)

In chemistry, an oxocarbon anion is a negative ion consisting solely of carbon and oxygen atoms. The best-known examples are the cyclic, aromatic anions derived from the fully deprotonated forms of cyclic oxocarbon acids: the deltate, squarate, croconate, and rhodizonate dianions.

Cyclic oxocarbon anions

The series CnOn2− for n = 3 to 6 comprises deltate (C3O32−), squarate (C4O42−), croconate (C5O52−), and rhodizonate (C6O62−). These ions are planar and exhibit delocalized π bonding; squarate and croconate in particular are often cited as aromatic systems satisfying Hückel's rule with 2 π electrons delocalized over the ring, supplemented by lone-pair conjugation from oxygen.

Other examples

Acyclic oxocarbon anions include carbonate, oxalate, and the enediolate anions of reductones such as ascorbate. Salts of the cyclic anions have been studied for their crystal structures, vibrational spectra, and use as building blocks in coordination polymers and molecular materials.

History

Squaric acid and croconic acid were among the earliest known organic acids of unusual composition; croconic acid was described by Leopold Gmelin in 1825 from the action of potash on carbonaceous material, and rhodizonic acid was identified shortly afterward. Their striking symmetry made the anions classic subjects in discussions of aromaticity and charge delocalization.
