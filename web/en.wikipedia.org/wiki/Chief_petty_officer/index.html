Chief petty officer is a senior non-commissioned rank in many navies. In the United States Navy and United States Coast Guard, chief petty officer (CPO) is the seventh enlisted pay grade (E-7), above petty officer first class and below senior chief petty officer. Chiefs form the backbone of enlisted leadership in the US Navy, serving as both technical experts in their rating and as deckplate leaders and mentors of junior sailors. The rank was formally established on 1 April 1893.

United States

Promotion to chief petty officer is by selection board and is regarded as the most significant promotion in an enlisted naval career. Newly promoted chiefs undergo a period of training and initiation — traditionally called "chief season" — overseen by the Chiefs Mess. US Navy chiefs wear distinctive uniform items, including a fouled anchor insignia (an anchor wrapped in chain with the letters USN, topped by a star for senior and master chiefs) and combination cover in khaki uniforms distinct from those of junior enlisted sailors.

Above chief are the grades of senior chief petty officer (E-8) and master chief petty officer (E-9); the most senior enlisted leader of the Navy is the Master Chief Petty Officer of the Navy.

United Kingdom

In the Royal Navy, chief petty officer is the highest rate below warrant officer, equivalent to OR-7. Royal Navy chief petty officers are known informally as "chief" and wear three buttons on the cuff historically associated with the rank. The Royal Canadian Navy uses the same terminology, with chief petty officer 2nd class (OR-7) and chief petty officer 1st class (OR-8).

Role

Across navies, chief petty officers typically lead divisions or departments, manage training and discipline, advise commissioned officers, and preserve naval tradition and heritage.
