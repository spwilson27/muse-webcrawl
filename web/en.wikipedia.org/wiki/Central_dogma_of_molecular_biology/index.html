Central dogma of molecular biology

[Image: The central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein, with reverse transcription noted as a special transfer]

Overview

The central dogma of molecular biology is a framework for understanding the transfer of sequence information between informational biopolymers. Stated by Francis Crick in 1957 (and elaborated in 1970), it holds that information flows from DNA to RNA to protein, and that once information has passed into protein it cannot get out again — protein sequences cannot template nucleic-acid or protein sequences.

Transfers

Crick divided transfers into general (DNA→DNA replication, DNA→RNA transcription, RNA→protein translation), special (RNA→DNA reverse transcription, as in retroviruses — unknown in 1957 but incorporated by Crick as possible in special cases), and unknown (protein-templated transfers, which the dogma forbids). The dogma is frequently misquoted as "DNA makes RNA makes protein"; Crick later wrote that he had not fully appreciated "dogma" implied an unquestioned belief — he meant a grand, plausible hypothesis. Prions (protein-only inheritance of conformation, not sequence) do not violate the dogma as Crick framed it.
