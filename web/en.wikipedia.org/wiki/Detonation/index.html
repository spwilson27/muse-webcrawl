Detonation

A detonation is a type of combustion involving a supersonic exothermic front accelerating through a medium that eventually drives a shock front propagating directly in front of it. Detonations propagate supersonically through shock waves with speeds in the range 1,000–2,000 m/s in gases and up to 9,000 m/s in high-density solids.

Theory

The simplest theory, the Chapman–Jouguet condition, predicts detonation velocity from the conservation laws across the shock. The ZND model (Zeldovich, von Neumann, Döring) refines this by describing a leading shock followed by a reaction zone of finite thickness.

Occurrence

Detonations occur in most solid and liquid explosives and in fuel–air mixtures under the right conditions. In internal combustion engines, unintended detonation ("engine knock") can damage pistons. In astrophysics, Type Ia supernovae may begin with a deflagration-to-detonation transition in a white dwarf.

Applications

Controlled detonations are used in mining, demolition, explosive welding, pulse detonation engines and military ordnance. A detonating explosive is normally initiated by a detonator containing a small amount of sensitive primary explosive.
