Diploid

A diploid cell contains two complete sets of chromosomes, one from each parent.

Diploid describes a cell or organism that has two sets of chromosomes, one set inherited from each parent. The diploid chromosome number is written 2n; in humans 2n = 46. By contrast, gametes (sperm and eggs) are haploid, carrying a single set (n = 23 in humans), and some organisms or tissues are polyploid, with more than two sets.

Alternation of generations

Most animals are diploid throughout their lives, with the haploid stage limited to the gametes. Plants and many algae alternate between a diploid generation (the sporophyte) and a haploid generation (the gametophyte). Fungi are commonly haploid for most of their life cycle, the diploid stage being confined to a single cell formed by fusion of mating types.

Diploidy and reproduction

Diploidy arises at fertilisation, when two haploid gametes fuse to form a diploid zygote. Having two copies of each gene can mask harmful recessive mutations, one reason diploidy is thought to be advantageous. Meiosis, the specialised cell division that produces gametes, halves the diploid set back to haploid, ensuring the chromosome number is stable across generations.
