Teichmüller theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Teichmüller theory is a branch of mathematics that studies the moduli spaces of complex (conformal) structures on surfaces. It is named after the German mathematician Oswald Teichmüller, who developed the subject in the late 1930s and early 1940s, building on earlier work by Bernhard Riemann and others on moduli of Riemann surfaces.

Teichmüller space

The Teichmüller space of a closed oriented surface of genus g is the space of complex structures on the surface up to isotopy; it can be identified with a ball of real dimension 6g − 6 (for g ≥ 2). Points of Teichmüller space may also be described via hyperbolic metrics, measured laminations, or quadratic differentials. Teichmüller introduced a metric on this space defined using extremal quasiconformal maps, giving rise to Teichmüller geodesics and quasiconformal geometry.

Mapping class group

The mapping class group of the surface acts on Teichmüller space, and the quotient is the moduli space of Riemann surfaces. The action's dynamics connect Teichmüller theory to low-dimensional topology, hyperbolic geometry, and dynamical systems such as Teichmüller geodesic flow, studied extensively by Maryam Mirzakhani and others.

Inter-universal Teichmüller theory

A distinct development, unrelated in method, is Shinichi Mochizuki's inter-universal Teichmüller theory, announced in 2012 as part of a claimed proof of the abc conjecture; it remains a subject of scholarly discussion.
