Roland Barthes


Born: 12 November 1915, Cherbourg, France
Died: 26 March 1980, Paris, France
Known for: Structuralism, semiotics, post-structuralism, "The Death of the Author"

Roland Gérard Barthes (12 November 1915 – 26 March 1980) was a French literary theorist, essayist, philosopher, critic, and semiotician. His work explored a wide range of fields and influenced the development of many schools of theory, including structuralism, semiotics, social theory, design theory, anthropology, and post-structuralism. He is particularly known for developing the concepts of the "death of the author" and for his semiological analyses of contemporary culture.

Life

Barthes was born in Cherbourg, Normandy, the son of naval officer Louis Barthes, who was killed in a naval battle during the First World War when Roland was less than a year old. He was raised by his mother, Henriette Barthes, in Bayonne and later Paris. He studied classical literature at the Sorbonne, where he founded the Groupe de théâtre antique. His studies were interrupted by tuberculosis, which forced him into sanatoria and shaped much of his early life. After the war he taught in Romania and Egypt, where he met the linguist Algirdas Julien Greimas.

In the 1950s Barthes worked at the Centre national de la recherche scientifique, producing the essays that became Writing Degree Zero (1953) and the monthly mythologies for Les Lettres nouvelles, collected as Mythologies (1957). In 1976 he became the first holder of the chair of literary semiology at the Collège de France. In February 1980 he was struck by a laundry van while crossing a Paris street and died of his injuries a month later.

Work

Mythologies examined the myths of modern French culture — wrestling, soap powder, the face of Greta Garbo, the new Citroën DS — arguing that bourgeois culture naturalises its historically contingent values by turning history into nature. In the 1960s Barthes turned to structuralist narratology in works such as Elements of Semiology (1964), "Introduction to the Structural Analysis of Narratives" (1966), and S/Z (1970), a line-by-line reading of Balzac's "Sarrasine".

His 1967 essay "The Death of the Author" argued against reading a text through the intentions of its author, proposing instead that meaning is completed by the reader — "the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author". Later works such as The Pleasure of the Text (1973), A Lover's Discourse: Fragments (1977), and Camera Lucida (1980), a meditation on photography prompted by the death of his mother, are more personal and fragmentary in form.

Key concepts

  • Studium and punctum — in photography, the general cultural interest of an image versus the detail that "pricks" the viewer.
  • Writerly (scriptible) and readerly (lisible) texts — texts that make the reader a producer versus a mere consumer of meaning.
  • Myth as second-order signification — a sign of a first-order system becomes the signifier of an ideological second order.
