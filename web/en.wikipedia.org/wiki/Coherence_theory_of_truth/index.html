Coherence theory of truth

The coherence theory of truth holds that a proposition is true if and only if it coheres with a specified system of propositions — that is, if it is consistent with, and mutually explanatory of, the other propositions in the system. In its strongest historical form, associated with absolute idealists such as F. H. Bradley and Joachim Joachim, truth is a property of the whole system of beliefs, and individual propositions are true only to the degree that they belong to the maximally coherent whole.

Varieties and objections

Coherence theories differ over whether coherence is the nature of truth or merely a criterion or test for it. The view contrasts with the correspondence theory, on which truth consists in a relation to facts. Two classical objections are the isolation objection — a perfectly coherent system might float free of reality, as a consistent fairy tale does — and the plurality objection: there may be several mutually incompatible systems each internally coherent, yet they cannot all be true. Modern defenders reply by building empirical input and comprehensiveness into the requirements of coherence.
