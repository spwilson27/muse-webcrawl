Cultural depictions of the wolf

The wolf occupies a prominent and deeply ambivalent place in the folklore, mythology, and literature of the Northern Hemisphere. Across cultures the wolf appears as a symbol of wildness, danger, and predation, but also of loyalty, courage, family bonds, and spiritual power — a duality rooted in the animal's long and close relationship with human societies.

European tradition

In Greek myth, wolves were associated with Apollo and with Zeus Lycaeus, and Arcadian legends told of men transformed into wolves. Roman tradition celebrated the she-wolf (the Lupa Capitolina) that suckled Romulus and Remus, founders of Rome. In Norse mythology, the giant wolf Fenrir was prophesied to devour Odin at Ragnarök, while the god's own wolves, Geri and Freki, were loyal companions. Medieval and early modern Europe feared wolves as livestock killers and man-eaters — the Beast of Gévaudan being the most famous example — and the wolf became the villain of fairy tales such as Little Red Riding Hood, The Three Little Pigs, and Peter and the Wolf. The werewolf, a human transformed into a wolf, is one of folklore's most enduring monsters.

Asian and Indigenous traditions

In Mongolian and Turkic traditions, the wolf is a revered ancestor figure; the Ashina clan traced its descent from a she-wolf. In Japan, the now-extinct wolves were venerated as mountain guardians (ōkami) that protected crops. Many Indigenous North American cultures regard the wolf as a teacher, pathfinder, and emblem of family loyalty, and stories of children raised by wolves — from Kipling's Mowgli to ancient legend — appear worldwide.

Modern depictions

Twentieth-century conservation changed the wolf's image in much of the West: works such as Farley Mowat's Never Cry Wolf presented wolves as social, family-oriented animals, and the "lone wolf" and loyal pack have become stock figures in modern fiction, film, and heraldry.
