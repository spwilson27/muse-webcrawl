Early Scots was the emerging literary language of the Northern Middle English-speaking parts of Scotland in the period before 1450. The Northern forms of Middle English descended from Northumbrian Old English. During this period, speakers referred to the language as "English" (Inglis, Ynglis), while the term "Scottis" was reserved for Gaelic; only around 1500 did "Scots" begin to be applied to the Lowland tongue and "Erse" (Irish) to Gaelic.

History

Anglian speech had been established in south-eastern Scotland since the seventh century, within the kingdom and later earldom of Northumbria. Its great expansion came under David I (r. 1124–1153), whose reforms — the founding of royal burghs, feudal landholding and reformed monasteries — brought an influx of English-, Norman- and Flemish-speaking settlers and made the Anglic speech of the burghs the language of commerce and administration in the Lowlands. Gaelic, dominant under Malcolm III and Margaret, steadily retreated northwards and westwards in the documentary record during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

By the fourteenth century Early Scots had displaced Latin and French as the language of Scottish record. The earliest substantial texts include royal charters and burgh laws from the late thirteenth century, and John Barbour's epic The Brus (c. 1375), the earliest major work of Scottish literature, which celebrates Robert the Bruce. Acts of Parliament in Scots date from the reign of James I of Scotland in the early fifteenth century.

Characteristics

Early Scots differed from southern Middle English in its retention of Old English features mediated through Northumbrian, and in its distinctive sound changes. Characteristic features include:

  • The fronting of Old English /oː/ to /øː/, spelled ui — guid "good", schuin "shoes", muin "moon" (not fully complete in the Early Scots period).
  • Retention of /x/ spelled ch: richt, thocht, brocht.
  • Loss of final unstressed syllables later or differently than in the south, and the weak past tense ending in -it.
  • A large vocabulary from Old Norse (kirk, gait, lowp), Gaelic (clan, loch, glamour later), and Dutch and Low German trade terms.

Transition to Middle Scots

Around 1450 the language enters its Middle Scots phase, characterised by increasingly elaborate Latinised "aureate" diction in courtly poetry — the works of Robert Henryson, William Dunbar and Gavin Douglas, the so-called Scottish Chaucerians — and by spelling conventions that diverge more sharply from English practice.
