Pangaea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pangaea
Existed: Roughly 335–175 million years ago
Meaning: 'All lands' (Greek)
Proposed by: Alfred Wegener (1912), as part of continental drift

Pangaea was a supercontinent that existed during the late Palaeozoic and early Mesozoic eras, assembling by about 335 million years ago from the earlier continents and breaking apart from about 175 million years ago into Laurasia (north) and Gondwana (south), and ultimately into today's continents.

The concept was proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912 as the centrepiece of his theory of continental drift, supported by the jigsaw fit of South America and Africa, matching fossils (the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus, the plant Glossopteris) across oceans, and identical rock sequences on separate continents. Wegener lacked a mechanism and was ridiculed; the theory triumphed in the 1960s with plate tectonics (see plate tectonics and continental drift), the engine that builds mountain ranges.

Pangaea's single world ocean, Panthalassa, and its vast arid interior shaped evolution: the supercontinent's breakup isolated faunas and set the template of modern biogeography. Supercontinents appear to form and disperse in cycles of a few hundred million years; projections of the next one have been nicknamed Pangaea Proxima or Amasia.
