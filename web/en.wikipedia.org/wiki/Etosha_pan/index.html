The Etosha pan is a vast, flat salt pan in northern Namibia, the largest salt pan in Africa. Covering some 4,800 square kilometres — about a quarter of Etosha National Park — it is so large that it is visible from space. The pan is the bed of a dried-up lake: a blinding white expanse of salt and clay that is dry for most of the year and fills only briefly with a shallow sheet of water after good rains, when it attracts flamingos and other waterbirds in their thousands.

Geology and history

The pan occupies part of the Kalahari Basin. Millions of years ago it held a large lake fed by the Kunene River; when tectonic movements diverted the river and the climate dried, the lake shrank and evaporated, leaving thick salt deposits. In the traditions of the Ovambo and Hai||om peoples the pan is a place of ancestral stories; its name is commonly said to mean "the place of mirages" or "the great white place".

Ecology

For most of the year the pan itself is barren, fringed by sweet grasslands and mopane woodland that support large herds of zebra, springbok, wildebeest, elephant and lion around permanent waterholes. After heavy rain the flooded pan becomes a breeding ground for greater and lesser flamingos, whose numbers have been estimated in the hundreds of thousands in exceptional years, and it hosts breeding pelicans as well. The pan and its surrounding wetlands are protected as part of Etosha National Park, proclaimed in 1907, and the pan is listed as a wetland of international importance in the park's Ramsar designation.
