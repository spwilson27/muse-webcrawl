Temple name

A temple name is a posthumous honorific name given to a deceased monarch in the Sinosphere — China, Korea, and Vietnam — for use when the ruler's spirit tablet was enshrined in the imperial ancestral temple. Temple names consist of two Chinese characters in Chinese usage: a qualifier praising the ruler's virtues or achievements (such as Gao "high", Ta "grand", Wen "civil", or Wu "martial") followed by zu (祖, "ancestor/progenitor") for dynastic founders or zong (宗, "lineage") for later emperors.

Usage in China

The practice originated in the Shang dynasty and was systematised under the Han. In early periods temple names were awarded sparingly, only to especially meritorious emperors, but from the Tang dynasty onward nearly every emperor received one. Historians conventionally refer to Chinese emperors of the Tang, Song, Liao, Jin, Yuan, Ming, and Qing dynasties by their temple names — for example, Tang Taizong, Song Huizong, Ming Chengzu (the Yongle Emperor), and Qing Shengzu (the Kangxi Emperor). Earlier Han emperors are usually known by their posthumous names (such as Han Wudi, "the Martial Emperor of Han"), while other rulers are often called by their era names in the Ming and Qing.

Temple names are distinct from posthumous names (shi hao), which are longer descriptive phrases evaluating a reign, and from era names (nian hao), chosen by the living emperor to mark periods of his rule. A ruler could also be stripped of a temple name or refused one as a final disgrace; conversely, usurpers and founders' fathers sometimes received temple names retroactively to legitimise a line.

Korea and Vietnam

Korean kings of Goryeo and Joseon are usually known by their temple names, which end in -jo (조, 祖) for founders of a line or -jong (종, 宗) — for example, Taejo, Sejong the Great, and Jeongjo. Vietnamese emperors likewise received temple names ending in Tổ or Tông, such as Lý Thái Tổ and Lê Thánh Tông. In all three cultures the awarding of the temple name formed part of the funeral and succession rites by which a dead ruler joined the dynastic ancestors.
