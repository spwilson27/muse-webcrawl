Institutional racism


Disparities in outcomes can arise from institutional policies and practices

Institutional racism, also known as systemic racism, is a form of racism that is embedded in the laws and regulations of a society or an organisation. It manifests as discrimination in areas such as criminal justice, employment, housing, healthcare, education and political representation. Unlike racism perpetrated by individuals, institutional racism does not require the actions to be intentional, or for the perpetrators to hold overtly prejudiced views; it can arise from policies and practices that produce unequal outcomes for different racial groups.

The concept was popularised by Stokely Carmichael (later Kwame Ture) and Charles V. Hamilton in their 1967 book Black Power: The Politics of Liberation, which contrasted individual racism — overt acts by individuals — with institutional racism, described as originating in "the operation of established and respected forces in the society" and consequently receiving far less public condemnation.

Definition and theory

Institutional racism is generally understood as the collective failure of an organisation to provide an appropriate and professional service to people because of their colour, culture or ethnic origin, and as processes, attitudes and behaviour that amount to discrimination through prejudice, ignorance, thoughtlessness and racist stereotyping. This formulation was used in the United Kingdom's Macpherson Report (1999), the public inquiry into the Metropolitan Police's investigation of the murder of Stephen Lawrence, which concluded that the investigation had been affected by institutional racism.

Examples

Scholars and official reports have identified institutional racism in areas including:

  • Criminal justice: disparities in stop-and-search rates, sentencing and incarceration.
  • Housing: practices such as redlining in the United States, in which neighbourhoods with minority residents were systematically denied mortgages and investment.
  • Education: disparities in school funding, exclusion rates and attainment.
  • Employment: hiring practices and promotion processes that systematically disadvantage minority applicants.
  • Healthcare: unequal treatment and outcomes for patients of different ethnic backgrounds.

Related concepts

Institutional racism overlaps with concepts such as structural racism, which focuses on the historical and societal structures that reproduce inequality, and critical race theory, a legal and academic framework examining the relationship between race, law and power. The terms "institutional" and "systemic" racism are often used interchangeably, though some writers distinguish between discrimination embedded in a single institution and patterns spanning whole societies.

Criticism and debate

The concept has been debated. Critics argue that disparate outcomes alone do not prove discrimination, that the term can be applied too loosely, and that it may obscure individual agency. Supporters respond that the framework explains persistent inequalities that cannot be accounted for by individual prejudice alone and directs attention to reform of policies and procedures.
