Fuel fraction

In aerospace engineering, fuel fraction is the weight of fuel divided by the gross take-off weight of the aircraft: the proportion of the aircraft's starting mass that is usable fuel. It is a key parameter in aircraft design because range and endurance are governed by the fraction of weight that can be devoted to fuel, through relationships such as the Breguet range equation.

Mission fuel fractions

For design calculations, the mission is divided into segments—engine start and take-off, climb, cruise, descent, and landing—each with a characteristic ratio of end weight to start weight. Typical values are about 0.970 for take-off, 0.985 for climb, and 0.990 for landing; the cruise segment ratio follows from the Breguet equation using lift-to-drag ratio, specific fuel consumption and range. Multiplying the segment ratios gives the overall fuel fraction needed for the mission, excluding reserves.

Typical values

Long-range airliners have fuel fractions around 0.30–0.45 (a Boeing 777-200LR approaches the top of this range), fighters often 0.25–0.35 internally, and general aviation aircraft much less. For rockets the equivalent concept is the propellant mass fraction, which for orbital launch vehicles is far higher—around 0.85–0.95—because of the demands of the rocket equation.
