Kapu

Kapu is the ancient Hawaiian code of conduct, laws and regulations, cognate with the Polynesian concept of tapu (taboo). The kapu system regulated every aspect of traditional Hawaiian society — religion, diet, social rank and relations between men and women — and violations could be punished by death.

The system

Kapu derived from the religious order of the gods (akua) and chiefs (aliʻi), who possessed mana, spiritual power. Rules included the ʻai kapu, which forbade men and women from eating together and prohibited women from eating certain foods such as pork, bananas and some fish. Commoners had to prostrate themselves before high chiefs, and places, objects and seasons could be declared kapu (for example, fishing bans during spawning seasons, an early form of resource conservation).

Abolition

In 1819, shortly after the death of Kamehameha I, his successor Kamehameha II, together with the queen regent Kaʻahumanu and the king's mother Keōpūolani, publicly broke the eating kapu in the famous ʻAi Noa ("free eating") feast. The act abolished the kapu system and the state religion months before Christian missionaries arrived in 1820, transforming Hawaiian society.
