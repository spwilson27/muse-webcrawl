Louis Marie Turreau (4 July 1756 – 10 December 1816), full name Louis Marie Turreau de Garambouville, was a French general of the Revolutionary Wars, notorious for his brutal repression of the royalist revolt in the War in the Vendée through the so-called colonnes infernales ("infernal columns").

Career

Turreau was born at Évreux, the son of a tax official, and purchased a commission in the Conflans dragoons before the Revolution. A supporter of the Revolution, he rose rapidly: he served in the Vendée from 1793, became a general of brigade and then general of division, and in December 1793 was appointed commander-in-chief of the Army of the West.

The infernal columns

In January 1794 Turreau submitted to the Committee of Public Safety a plan to devastate the insurrectionary Vendée. Implementing his orders, twelve "infernal columns" marched across the region burning villages, destroying crops and killing inhabitants on a large scale, including many non-combatants. The campaign, carried out between January and May 1794, caused tens of thousands of deaths and remains one of the most controversial episodes of the French Revolution; later historians have debated whether it amounted to a form of genocide.

Later life

Recalled in May 1794, Turreau was arrested after the fall of Robespierre but released under the amnesty of 1795. He subsequently served in Switzerland and Piedmont and held commands under the Consulate and Empire, including as ambassador to the United States (1803–1804). He was created a baron of the Empire and his name, spelled TURREAU, is inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. He died in 1816 at Conches-en-Ouche.
