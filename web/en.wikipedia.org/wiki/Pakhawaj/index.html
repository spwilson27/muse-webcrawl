Pakhawaj

The pakhawaj is a barrel-shaped, two-headed drum, originating in the Indian subcontinent, which is the earliest version of the north Indian mridangam family of percussion instruments. It is the standard percussion accompaniment in the dhrupad style of Hindustani classical music, and is also used in devotional music, dance accompaniment (including Odissi), and in temple traditions.

Construction

The shell is traditionally carved from a single block of wood (often sheesham or neem), slightly bulged in the middle. The two heads are made of layered goat skin held by leather straps; the left head is loaded with a temporary paste of flour and water to produce bass tones, while the right head bears permanent black tuning spots (syahi) made of iron filings, rice paste and other materials, which give the drum its rich harmonic overtones.

Playing technique

The drum rests horizontally in the player's lap or on a stand and is played with the palms and fingers of both hands. Its rhythmic language of mnemonic syllables (bols) — such as dha, ge, na, ti, ka — is organised into cyclic rhythmic patterns (talas) such as chautal (12 beats) and dhamar (14 beats), characteristic of dhrupad performance.

History and lineages

The pakhawaj is regarded as older than the tabla and as the ancestor of the mridangam's northern form. Important historical lineages (gharanas) include the Kudau Singh tradition, the Nana Panse tradition, and the Nathdwara tradition. In the 20th century, dhrupad's revival brought renewed attention to pakhawaj masters such as Pagal Das and Raja Chhatrapati Singh.
