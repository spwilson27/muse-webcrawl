Siege tower

A siege tower (also called a belfry or movable tower) is a specialised siege engine constructed to protect attackers and their ladders as they approach the defensive walls of a fortification. The tower was usually rectangular, on four wheels and of a height roughly equal to that of the wall or sometimes higher, allowing archers to fire down on the defenders from the top. Once the tower was rolled up against the wall, a drawbridge or gangplank at its front was dropped so that soldiers could pour out onto the parapet.

Construction

Siege towers were timber structures, built on site from local wood near the besieged place. To resist fire they were often covered with iron plates or with non-flammable materials such as water-soaked hides, vinegar-soaked hides, earth or wet clay. The interior was divided into several storeys, the lowest sometimes housing a battering ram, the middle levels carrying assault troops, and the top platform mounting missile troops or light engines. Propulsion came from oxen, from men pushing from behind and inside, or from windlasses; the ground in front of the walls frequently had to be levelled or a causeway built across the ditch before the tower could advance.

History

Movable towers were used by the Assyrians in the ancient Near East, appearing in palace reliefs of the 9th century BC. They were developed by the Greeks and Carthaginians — Demetrius Poliorcetes deployed the enormous nine-storey Helepolis ("taker of cities") at the siege of Rhodes in 305–304 BC — and were a standard part of Roman siegecraft, as at Masada, where the great assault ramp and tower built by the Tenth Legion survive in outline to this day.

Siege towers remained common throughout the Middle Ages in Europe and Asia. Famous medieval uses include the Crusader towers at the siege of Jerusalem in 1099 and the towers employed at Acre and Constantinople. Defenders countered towers with fire arrows and incendiaries, by undermining their approach, by dropping stones and beams to smash them, and with defensive engines mounted on the walls. With the spread of effective gunpowder artillery from the fifteenth century, tall wooden towers became fatally vulnerable and fell out of use.
