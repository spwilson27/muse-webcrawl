Larry Doby

Lawrence Eugene Doby
Born December 13, 1923, Camden, South Carolina
Died June 18, 2003 (aged 79), Montclair, New Jersey
Position: Centre fielder
Teams: Newark Eagles; Cleveland Indians; Chicago White Sox; Detroit Tigers
Hall of Fame: inducted 1998

Lawrence Eugene Doby (December 13, 1923 – June 18, 2003) was an American professional baseball player. A centre fielder, he was the second African-American to break the modern Major League Baseball colour barrier and the first to do so in the American League, debuting with the Cleveland Indians on July 5, 1947—eleven weeks after Jackie Robinson broke the barrier with the Brooklyn Dodgers.

Career

Doby, raised in Paterson, New Jersey, starred in the Negro leagues with the Newark Eagles while still a teenager, served in the US Navy during the Second World War, and returned to help the Eagles win the 1946 Negro World Series. Cleveland owner Bill Veeck signed him in July 1947 and integrated the American League. After a difficult rookie season, Doby converted from second base to centre field and became a star: he was selected to seven consecutive All-Star Games (1949–1955), led the American League in home runs twice, and hit a decisive home run in Game 4 of the 1948 World Series, helping Cleveland to its championship. A famous photograph of Doby embracing winning pitcher Steve Gromek after that game became an enduring image of integration in American sport.

Doby later played for the Chicago White Sox and Detroit Tigers, and in 1962 became, with Don Newcombe, one of the first former major leaguers to play professionally in Japan, with the Chunichi Dragons. After retiring he worked in scouting and coaching, and in 1978 he succeeded Bob Lemon as manager of the White Sox, becoming the second African-American manager in major-league history after Frank Robinson.

Legacy

Despite his achievements, Doby long received less recognition than Robinson; he was frequently described as having endured the same abuse and isolation—he could not room or eat with teammates on the road—without the same public attention. He was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame by the Veterans Committee in 1998, and the Cleveland club retired his number 14. He died in Montclair, New Jersey, in 2003.
