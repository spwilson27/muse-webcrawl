Extended evolutionary synthesis

The extended evolutionary synthesis (EES) is a set of theoretical concepts argued to be more comprehensive than the earlier modern synthesis of genetics and evolution. The idea of an extended synthesis was discussed in the 1950s by C. H. Waddington, and was developed further in the 1980s and 2000s by researchers including Mary Jane West-Eberhard, Massimo Pigliucci, Eva Jablonka, and Kevin Laland.

Precursors and development

The modern synthesis, formed in the 1930s and 1940s, centred evolution on genetic variation and natural selection. Critics argued that it underplayed developmental processes, phenotypic plasticity, epigenetic inheritance, and the constructive role of organisms in shaping their environments. In 2014, a paper in Nature by Laland and colleagues, "Does evolutionary theory need a rethink?", brought the debate to wide attention.

Concepts

  • Developmental bias — development channels variation in non-random directions.
  • Phenotypic plasticity — environmentally induced variation can precede and guide genetic change.
  • Inclusive inheritance — epigenetic, behavioural, and symbolic inheritance alongside genetic inheritance.
  • Niche construction — organisms modify their environments, altering selection pressures.
  • Extra-genetic inheritance and reciprocal causation in evolutionary explanation.

Reception

The EES has been controversial: supporters argue it integrates established but marginalized findings into a richer causal framework, while detractors contend that its components are already accommodated by standard evolutionary theory and that the EES brings no fundamentally new predictions.
