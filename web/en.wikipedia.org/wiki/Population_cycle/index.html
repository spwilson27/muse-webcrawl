Population cycle

A population cycle in zoology is a phenomenon in which populations rise and fall over a predictable period of time, typically several years. Famous examples include the roughly ten-year cycle of the snowshoe hare and Canada lynx recorded in Hudson's Bay Company fur returns, and the three-to-five-year cycles of lemmings and voles in northern latitudes.

Examples

Cyclic dynamics have been documented in insects such as the larch budmoth (about nine years), in grouse and ptarmigan, in forest tent caterpillars, and in small rodents across the boreal zone. Predators specialising on cyclic prey, such as snowy owls and stoats, often track the same rhythm.

Causes

Explanations proposed include predator–prey interactions (formalised in the Lotka–Volterra equations), food limitation and induced plant defences, disease, and intrinsic mechanisms such as stress physiology and maternal effects. Modern research generally treats cycles as arising from interactions among these factors, with delayed density dependence at the core. The phenomenon is distinct from irregular outbreaks and from long-term population trends.
