The United States Census Bureau (officially the Bureau of the Census) is a principal agency of the U.S. federal statistical system, responsible for producing data about the American people and economy. It is part of the Department of Commerce. Its best-known task is the constitutionally mandated decennial census of population, which determines apportionment of seats in the House of Representatives and guides the distribution of federal funds.

Major programmes

Beyond the decennial census, the bureau conducts the American Community Survey (an annual rolling survey that replaced the census long form), the Economic Census every five years, the Census of Governments, and dozens of demographic and economic surveys, many sponsored by other agencies—among them the Current Population Survey with the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Data are disseminated principally through the data.census.gov platform.

History

The first census was taken in 1790 under Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, counting 3.9 million people. Census-taking was handled by temporary offices and U.S. marshals through the 19th century; Herman Hollerith's punched-card tabulating machines, first used in 1890, revolutionized processing and led to the founding of IBM's predecessors. A permanent Census Office was created in 1902 and became a bureau of the new Department of Commerce and Labor in 1903. The bureau adopted electronic computing early, receiving the first UNIVAC I in 1951 for the 1950 census tabulations.

Organization and confidentiality

The bureau operates 12 regional offices and processing centres in Jeffersonville, Indiana, and Tucson, Arizona. Individual responses are protected by Title 13 of the U.S. Code: data may be used only for statistical purposes, and identifiable records are kept confidential for 72 years, after which census schedules are released by the National Archives.
