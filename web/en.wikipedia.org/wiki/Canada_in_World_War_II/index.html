Canada in World War II
Date10 September 1939 – 1945
Prime MinisterWilliam Lyon Mackenzie King
PersonnelMore than 1.1 million served
CasualtiesAbout 45,000 killed; 54,000 wounded

The history of Canada in World War II begins with the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939. While the Canadian Armed Forces were eventually active in nearly every theatre of war, most combat was centred in Italy, Northwestern Europe, and the North Atlantic.

Canada declared war on Germany on 10 September 1939, a week after Britain and France, in a deliberate assertion of parliamentary sovereignty. During the war, more than 1.1 million Canadians and Newfoundlanders served in uniform, out of a population of about 11.5 million — one of the highest proportions of any Allied country.

Home front

The war transformed the Canadian economy. Under the British Commonwealth Air Training Plan, Canada hosted more than 100 airfields and trained over 130,000 Allied aircrew. Canadian industry produced aircraft, ships, vehicles, and munitions on a massive scale; by 1945 Canada had the world's fourth-largest air force and third-largest navy.

Major engagements

Canadian forces fought in the Battle of the Atlantic, the Dieppe Raid of 1942, the Italian Campaign (including Ortona), the Battle of Normandy and the subsequent advance through France, Belgium, and the Netherlands, and the liberation of the Netherlands, where Canadian troops are remembered with particular gratitude. The Royal Canadian Air Force contributed squadrons to Bomber Command and fighter operations over Europe.

Conscription

The question of conscription for overseas service divided the country, reviving tensions between English and French Canada. After a 1942 plebiscite released the government from its no-conscription pledge, Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King pursued a policy of "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary." In late 1944, some 16,000 home-defence conscripts were ordered overseas; only a fraction reached the front before the war in Europe ended.

Legacy

The war effort accelerated Canada's emergence as an industrial and middle power, expanded the federal government's role, and laid groundwork for the postwar welfare state.
