GW170817 was a gravitational wave (GW) signal observed by the LIGO and Virgo detectors on 17 August 2017, originating from the shell elliptical galaxy NGC 4993. Produced by the last minutes of the inspiral and merger of two neutron stars, it was the first gravitational-wave event observed to have electromagnetic counterparts — the short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A and the kilonova AT 2017gfo — and thus the first event studied through multi-messenger astronomy.

Detection

The signal lasted about 100 seconds in the detectors' sensitive band, sweeping upward in frequency as the two neutron stars, with masses estimated at roughly 1.2 and 1.6 solar masses, spiralled together. Some 1.7 seconds after the merger time inferred from the gravitational waves, the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope detected the short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A from a consistent region of sky.

Optical counterpart

Within about eleven hours, telescopes including the Swope Telescope in Chile located a new optical transient, AT 2017gfo, in NGC 4993 at a distance of about 40 megaparsecs. Over the following days and weeks it evolved from blue to red, the signature of a kilonova: radioactive decay of rapid-neutron-capture (r-process) elements forged in the merger debris. Spectra showed evidence of heavy elements such as strontium, supporting neutron-star mergers as a major cosmic source of gold, platinum and other heavy elements. Radio and X-ray emission from a relativistic jet viewed off-axis rose for months afterwards.

Scientific importance

The near-simultaneous arrival of the gravitational waves and gamma rays constrained the speed of gravity to equal the speed of light to within one part in 1015, ruling out many modified-gravity theories. The event provided an independent "standard siren" measurement of the Hubble constant and strong-field tests of general relativity. The discovery was announced on 16 October 2017 and was named Science magazine's Breakthrough of the Year.
