In astronomy, altitude (also called elevation) is one of the two coordinates of the horizontal coordinate system. It is the angle between the direction to an astronomical object and the observer's local horizon, measured along the vertical circle through the object. An object on the horizon has an altitude of 0°, an object directly overhead (at the zenith) has an altitude of +90°, and objects below the horizon have negative altitudes. Together with azimuth — the angle around the horizon, usually measured from north — altitude fixes an object's apparent position for a given observer at a given time.

Use and properties

Because the Earth rotates, the altitude and azimuth of a celestial object change continuously, unlike equatorial coordinates (right ascension and declination), which are effectively fixed for distant objects. Altitude therefore has to be computed for the observer's latitude and the local sidereal time. The system is the natural one for altazimuth telescope mounts and for naked-eye observing: air mass and atmospheric extinction depend mainly on altitude, and objects are best observed near their maximum altitude at meridian crossing (culmination). Atmospheric refraction raises the apparent altitude of objects near the horizon by about half a degree, which is why the Sun is still visible after it has geometrically set.
