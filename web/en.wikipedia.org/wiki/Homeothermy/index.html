Homeothermy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homeothermy, or homeothermism, is thermoregulation that maintains a stable internal body temperature regardless of external influences. An organism with this property is a homeotherm. Homeothermy is one aspect of the broader category of warm-bloodedness, and is contrasted with poikilothermy, in which internal temperature varies with the environment.

Mechanisms

Homeotherms maintain their temperature chiefly by regulating metabolic heat production and heat loss. In mammals and birds — the two groups of living homeotherms — the hypothalamus acts as a thermostat, triggering responses such as shivering and increased metabolic rate in the cold, and sweating, panting and vasodilation in the heat. Insulation in the form of fur, feathers or blubber slows heat exchange with the environment.

Advantages and disadvantages

A stable, high internal temperature allows enzymes to operate near their optimum and permits sustained activity independent of ambient temperature, including activity at night and in cold climates. The cost is high: homeotherms must eat far more than poikilotherms of similar size to fuel their metabolism, which is why endothermy typically requires a reliable, energy-rich food supply.

Variations

Homeothermy is not absolute. Many homeotherms allow controlled deviations: hibernators and daily torpor users let body temperature fall substantially to save energy (heterothermy), while some fish, such as tunas and lamnid sharks, maintain only parts of the body (notably swimming muscles and viscera) at elevated temperatures — regional homeothermy.
