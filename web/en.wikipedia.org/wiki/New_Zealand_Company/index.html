New Zealand Company

The New Zealand Company, chartered in 1839 as the New Zealand Land Company, was a British joint-stock company established to promote the systematic colonisation of New Zealand. Influenced by Edward Gibbon Wakefield's theories of systematic colonisation, it organised emigration and land sales to fund settlements.

Settlements

The company founded or promoted settlements at Wellington (Port Nicholson), New Plymouth, Nelson, Whanganui, and, through associated bodies, Otago and Canterbury. Its first emigrant ships reached Wellington in late 1839 and early 1840, shortly before the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi and Britain's proclamation of sovereignty.

Land disputes

The company's hurried and often dubious purchases of Māori land led to serious disputes, investigations by land claims commissioners, and conflict, including the Wairau Affray of 1843. Its finances were chronically troubled by the costs of surveys, emigration, and compensation.

Dissolution

Financially weakened, the company surrendered its charter in 1850 in return for a government loan and a guaranteed land allocation; it was finally dissolved in 1858. Despite its failures, it played a decisive role in the European settlement of New Zealand.
