Romanisation (cultural)

Romanisation

Romanisation (or Romanization, also Latinisation) is the process by which the peoples of the Roman Empire adopted Roman and Latin culture — language, law, dress, urban life, religion and material goods. Historians since the 19th century have debated whether it was driven by deliberate imperial policy or by local elites voluntarily emulating Roman ways to gain status.

Mechanisms

Key agents of Romanisation included the army (auxiliary veterans settled throughout the provinces), colonization by Roman citizens, grants of citizenship (universalized by Caracalla's edict of AD 212), urbanization around forums, baths and amphitheatres, and the spread of the villa economy. Latin became the language of administration in the West, evolving into the Romance languages, while the Greek-speaking East retained its own high culture under Roman rule — a major limit on the concept.

Debate

Older scholarship presented Romanisation as a one-way civilizing mission. Modern historians emphasize negotiation, resistance and hybridity: provincial identities persisted in local cults, languages such as Gaulish and Brittonic survived for centuries, and "becoming Roman" meant different things in different places. Some scholars prefer alternative terms such as "creolization" or "discrepant experience".
