Sinus rhythm

Sinus rhythm is the normal rhythm of the heart, in which each beat is initiated by the sinoatrial node (SA node), the heart's natural pacemaker, located in the right atrium. The electrical impulse spreads across the atria, pauses briefly at the atrioventricular node, and then travels through the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres to depolarise the ventricles in a coordinated contraction. A rhythm is described as sinus rhythm when impulses originate in the SA node and are conducted normally, regardless of rate; normal sinus rhythm (NSR) additionally requires a resting heart rate within the normal range, conventionally 60–100 beats per minute in adults.

Characteristics on the ECG

On an electrocardiogram (ECG), sinus rhythm is recognised by:

  • A P wave preceding every QRS complex, with a consistent morphology, upright in leads I and II and inverted in aVR (reflecting impulse spread from the SA node).
  • A constant PR interval, normally 120–200 ms.
  • A QRS complex following every P wave, usually of normal duration (less than 120 ms) unless bundle branch block or another conduction abnormality is present.
  • A regular rhythm, allowing for the slight phasic variation of sinus arrhythmia.

Variations

Sinus tachycardia

Sinus rhythm at a rate above 100 bpm. It is usually a physiological response to exercise, fever, pain, anxiety, hypovolemia or stimulants, though it can reflect underlying illness such as sepsis, anaemia or hyperthyroidism.

Sinus bradycardia

Sinus rhythm below 60 bpm. It is common and benign in athletes and during sleep, but may be pathological when caused by sinus node dysfunction, hypothyroidism, raised intracranial pressure or drugs such as beta blockers.

Sinus arrhythmia

A slight irregularity in sinus rhythm in which the rate increases with inspiration and decreases with expiration, mediated by vagal tone. It is a normal finding, especially in young people.

Clinical significance

Normal sinus rhythm is the reference rhythm against which arrhythmias — such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia and heart block — are defined. In resuscitation, "sinus rhythm" is also used to describe the return of an organised rhythm following defibrillation, and conversion of pathological rhythms to sinus rhythm (by drugs, cardioversion or ablation) is a common therapeutic goal.
