Suovetaurilia

The suovetaurilia was one of the most solemn sacrificial rites of ancient Roman religion, in which three animals — a pig (sus), a sheep (ovis), and a bull (taurus) — were offered together, usually to the god Mars. Its name is compounded from the Latin words for the three victims. The sacrifice served purposes of purification (lustratio) and blessing, for land, armies, and the whole Roman people.

The rite

In the classic form described by Cato the Elder in De agri cultura, the three animals were led in procession around the thing to be purified — a field, a city, or an assembled army — before being sacrificed with a formal prayer. Cato preserves the prayer to Mars asking the god to ward off disease, barrenness, and calamity, and to grant health and increase to crops, herds, and household. The procession around the boundary made the rite a lustrum: it blessed and enclosed the space within.

Public uses

The Roman censors closed each five-year census with a suovetaurilia on the Campus Martius, purifying the assembled citizen body in the lustrum conditum. Similar sacrifices purified armies before campaigns and the city after prodigies. A smaller-scale form using a pig, a ram, and a bull-calf, or only some of the animals, appears in related rites such as the Ambarvalia.

Depictions

The suovetaurilia procession was a stock subject of Roman state relief sculpture. Famous examples appear on the Louvre's altar reliefs of the reign of Domitian, on the Column of Marcus Aurelius, and on numerous triumphal and census monuments, where the three victims are shown led by attendants (victimarii) toward the altar, with the officiant's head veiled in the Roman manner (capite velato). Comparable triple-animal sacrifices existed among other Italic peoples and, in altered form, in Vedic India, a fact long noted by comparative religionists.
