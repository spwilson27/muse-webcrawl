The bank rate, also known as the discount rate in some systems, is the interest rate at which a nation's central bank lends money to domestic banks, and more broadly the central bank's principal policy rate. In the United Kingdom, "Bank Rate" is the official name of the rate set by the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee, at which the Bank lends to banks and other financial institutions and remunerates reserves.

Function

Changes in the bank rate influence interest rates throughout the economy: commercial banks adjust their own deposit and lending rates (such as mortgage "tracker" rates linked directly to Bank Rate), money-market rates move in step, and bond yields and the exchange rate respond. Raising the rate is used to restrain inflation; lowering it to stimulate borrowing, spending and growth. Historically, the Bank of England's rate — first published in 1833 and manipulated aggressively in the 19th century — was the pre-eminent signal of British monetary policy, and its 19th- and 20th-century changes were announced with considerable ceremony. In the United States, the analogous Federal Reserve discount rate applies to borrowing at the "discount window", distinct from the federal funds rate target; in Canada, the Bank Rate is defined as the rate on one-day advances to institutions, set slightly above the Bank of Canada's overnight rate target.
