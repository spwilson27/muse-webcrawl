Disco

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A discotheque dance floor in 1978 under a mirror ball: dancers in satin and sequins beneath coloured lights]

Disco is a genre of dance music and a subculture that emerged in the urban United States in the mid-1970s, in the clubs of New York and Philadelphia where Black, Latino and gay dancers built a utopia of rhythm: four-on-the-floor drums, orchestral strings and horns, and songs extended for the floor by a new priesthood of DJs and remixers.

From underground loft parties (David Mancuso's Loft, the Paradise Garage with DJ Larry Levan) disco conquered the mainstream with the Bee Gees' Saturday Night Fever soundtrack (1977, among the best-selling albums ever), Donna Summer and Giorgio Moroder's electronic 'I Feel Love' (1977), Chic, Sister Sledge and Gloria Gaynor's 'I Will Survive' (1978). A backlash — symbolised by Chicago's Disco Demolition Night in 1979 — drove it back underground, where it mutated into house music and garage, and thus into all modern club music. Song titles cited only.

The backlash and afterlife

Historians now read the anti-disco movement as entangled with racism and homophobia as much as with rock taste; its commercial collapse in 1980 hid disco's continuity in the clubs. The 12-inch single, the remix, the DJ as artist and the very idea of a records-led nightclub all descend from disco, and its periodic chart revivals (from Madonna to Dua Lipa's Future Nostalgia) confirm its permanence.
