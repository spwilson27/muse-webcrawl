The Bayh–Dole Act, formally the Patent and Trademark Law Amendments Act of 1980, is United States legislation that allows universities, small businesses and non-profit organisations to retain ownership of inventions made with federal research funding, and to license them — including exclusively — to private companies for commercial development. It is named for its Senate sponsors, Birch Bayh of Indiana and Bob Dole of Kansas, and is codified at 35 U.S.C. §§ 200–212.

Provisions

Before 1980, the federal government generally retained title to inventions arising from publicly funded research; of some 28,000 government-owned patents, fewer than 5 per cent had been licensed for use. Bayh–Dole created a uniform policy under which a federal contractor may elect to retain title to a "subject invention," provided it discloses the invention, files for patent protection, and acknowledges the government's support. The government retains a non-exclusive, royalty-free licence and "march-in rights" to license the invention itself if the owner fails to achieve practical application or in cases of public need — a power whose scope has been debated but which has never been exercised. Universities must share royalties with inventors and use their remaining income for research and education.

Effects and debate

The Act is widely credited with transforming American university technology transfer: university patenting and licensing rose dramatically after 1980, and university spin-out companies became a major channel for commercialising publicly funded research. Similar legislation was adopted in many other countries. Critics argue that patenting can restrict access to publicly funded discoveries — particularly medicines — and have periodically petitioned the government to use march-in rights to control prices; defenders reply that without the prospect of exclusivity, few companies would invest in developing early-stage university inventions. In 2023 the federal government proposed a framework that would allow pricing to be considered in march-in decisions, prompting renewed debate.
