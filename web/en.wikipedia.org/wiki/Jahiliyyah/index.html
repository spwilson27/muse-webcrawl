Jahiliyyah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jahiliyyah (Arabic: جاهلية, romanized: jāhiliyyah) is an Islamic term for the condition of the Arabs before the advent of Islam, often translated as the "Age of Ignorance". In Islamic discourse it denotes not simply a lack of knowledge but a state of moral and religious ignorance of divine guidance, characterised by idolatry, tribal violence and social practices that Islam sought to reform. In modern usage the concept has been extended by some thinkers to describe any society regarded as having turned away from divine guidance.

Etymology and meaning

The word derives from the Arabic root j-h-l, associated with jahl ("ignorance"), but scholars such as Toshihiko Izutsu have argued that in Qur'anic usage the term is better understood as the opposite of ḥilm — forbearance, self-control and civilised conduct — so that jahiliyyah connotes barbarism, impulsiveness and passionate tribal pride rather than mere absence of information.

In the Qur'an

The term appears four times in the Qur'an. In one passage, the believers are warned against the "judgement of jahiliyyah" (Q 5:50); elsewhere the text speaks of the "fierceness of jahiliyyah" (ḥamiyyat al-jāhiliyyah, Q 48:26), contrasted with the tranquillity (sakīnah) sent down by God. These usages frame jahiliyyah as a moral and social order opposed to that of Islam.

Historical usage

Traditionally, the period of jahiliyyah is taken to cover Arabia before the mission of Muhammad in the early 7th century CE. Pre-Islamic Arabian society was organised around tribes, with customary law, blood feuds, and a rich oral poetic tradition. The poetry of the period, including the famous Muʿallaqāt ("Suspended Odes"), is highly valued in Arabic literature even while the religious outlook of the age is criticised in Islamic sources.

Modern interpretations

In the 20th century, the concept was given new life by thinkers such as Abul A'la Maududi and Sayyid Qutb. Qutb, in works such as Milestones (1964), argued that jahiliyyah is not confined to a historical period but is a recurring condition: any social order in which sovereignty is attributed to human beings rather than to God is, in his formulation, jahili. This reinterpretation became influential in modern Islamist thought and has also been widely criticised by other Muslim scholars.

Historians of Arabia, meanwhile, have questioned the blanket portrayal of pre-Islamic Arabia as uniformly ignorant or barbaric, pointing to the sophisticated trading kingdoms of South Arabia, the Lakhmid and Ghassanid buffer states, and the presence of Jewish, Christian and monotheistic communities in the peninsula.
