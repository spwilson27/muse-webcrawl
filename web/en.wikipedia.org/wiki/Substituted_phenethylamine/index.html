Substituted phenethylamine

Substituted phenethylamines are a large and diverse chemical class of organic compounds derived from phenethylamine, a naturally occurring trace amine. The phenethylamine skeleton — a benzene ring separated from an amino group by a two-carbon chain — forms the structural backbone of many endogenous neurotransmitters, hormones, pharmaceutical drugs and recreational substances.

Endogenous compounds

The brain's catecholamine neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine, and the hormone epinephrine, are all substituted phenethylamines, as are the trace amines tyramine and phenethylamine itself. These compounds act at adrenergic and dopaminergic receptors and at trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs).

Pharmacological classes

Important subclasses include:

  • Substituted amphetamines — bearing an alpha-methyl group; stimulants and empathogens such as amphetamine and MDMA.
  • Substituted cathinones — beta-keto analogues, including the natural product cathinone from khat.
  • 2C compounds — 2,5-dimethoxy phenethylamines with a 4-position substituent, many of them psychedelic, described extensively by Alexander Shulgin in PiHKAL.
  • DOx compounds — the alpha-methyl (amphetamine) counterparts of the 2C series.
  • Mescaline and analogues — 3,4,5-trimethoxy substituted compounds found in peyote and other cacti.
  • NBOMe compounds — N-(2-methoxybenzyl) derivatives of the 2C series.

Structure–activity relationships

Substituents on the aromatic ring, side chain and nitrogen strongly modulate receptor binding and transporter activity. Methoxy groups at the 2- and 5-positions combined with a lipophilic 4-substituent favour 5-HT2A agonism and psychedelic activity, whereas alkyl substitution on nitrogen and the alpha carbon typically increases monoamine release and stimulant effects.
