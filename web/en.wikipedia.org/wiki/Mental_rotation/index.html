Mental rotation

Mental rotation is the ability to rotate mental representations of two-dimensional and three-dimensional objects as our brains perceive them to correspond to the way real physical objects rotate. It is a component of spatial ability and is studied within cognitive psychology. Mental rotation can be separated into the following cognitive stages: encoding the object, mentally rotating it until a comparison can be made, making the comparison, and deciding whether the objects match.

Shepard and Metzler experiments

The classic research was conducted by Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler in 1971. Subjects were shown pairs of three-dimensional block figures, one rotated relative to the other, and asked whether the figures were identical or mirror images. Reaction time increased linearly with the angular disparity between the two figures—up to about 180 degrees—suggesting that participants mentally rotated one figure into alignment with the other at a roughly constant rate, estimated at about 40–60 degrees per second.

Brain areas involved

Neuroimaging studies associate mental rotation with activity in the parietal lobes, particularly the intraparietal sulcus, and with motor and premotor areas. The task engages visuospatial processing in the right cerebral hemisphere more strongly in many studies.

Individual differences and training

Mental rotation tests show some of the largest average sex differences in cognitive research, with males on average scoring higher; the size and origin of this difference remain debated, with roles proposed for experience, strategy, and biology. Performance improves with practice, video-game experience, and spatial training, and tests of mental rotation are used in selection for fields such as engineering, aviation, and surgery.
