Roman Canon

The Roman Canon, officially known since 1970 as the Eucharistic Prayer I, is the oldest and most venerable of the eucharistic prayers used in the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church. It has been the central prayer of the Mass in the Latin Church for well over a millennium and was, until the liturgical reforms following the Second Vatican Council, the only anaphora of the Roman Rite.

The text of the canon reached substantially its present form by the time of Pope Gregory the Great (590–604). It is prayed by the celebrant at every Mass in which Eucharistic Prayer I is chosen, and it is especially recommended for Sundays and major feasts.

Structure

The canon begins with the Te igitur, in which the priest offers the sacrifice for the Church and its pastors, and continues through a series of intercessions and commemorations. Its principal parts include:

  • Te igitur — offering for the Church, the pope and the bishop
  • Memento, Domine — commemoration of the living
  • Communicantes — communion with Mary and the saints
  • Hanc igitur and Quam oblationem — prayers over the offerings
  • Qui pridie — the institution narrative and consecration
  • Unde et memores — the anamnesis
  • Supra quae and Supplices te rogamus — prayers of acceptance
  • Memento etiam — commemoration of the dead
  • Nobis quoque peccatoribus — prayer for the ministers
  • Per quem haec omnia and the concluding doxology Per ipsum

History

Scholars trace elements of the canon to the fourth century or earlier; passages are quoted by Saint Ambrose in his work De Sacramentis. Gregory the Great is traditionally credited with adding the phrase diesque nostros in tua pace disponas and with fixing the position of the Lord's Prayer after the canon. For centuries priests recited most of the canon silently or in a low voice, a practice retained today in the Extraordinary Form of the Roman Rite.

Use today

In the Mass of Paul VI the canon is one of four principal eucharistic prayers in the Roman Missal. Unlike the others, it contains variable insertions for particular feasts in its Communicantes and Hanc igitur, and it names an extensive list of saints in two litanies.
