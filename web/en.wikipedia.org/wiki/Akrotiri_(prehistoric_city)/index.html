Akrotiri (Greek: Ακρωτήρι) is a Cycladic Bronze Age settlement on the volcanic Greek island of Santorini (Thera). The settlement was destroyed in the Theran (Minoan) eruption around the middle of the 2nd millennium BCE and buried in volcanic ash, which preserved the buildings and their contents — much like the Roman ruins of Pompeii — earning the site the nickname "the Pompeii of the Aegean".

The settlement

Akrotiri was one of the most important Aegean ports of the Bronze Age, a prosperous trading town with multi-storey houses, paved streets, a sophisticated drainage and sanitation system, and large storage jars (pithoi) holding goods from across the eastern Mediterranean. The buildings are famous for their vivid frescoes — among them the "Flotilla" miniature frieze depicting a naval procession, the "Spring" fresco of lilies and swallows, the "Boxing Boys", and the "Fisherman" — now largely housed in museums in Athens and at the site. No human remains have been found, suggesting the inhabitants evacuated before the eruption, probably after warning earthquakes.

Eruption and chronology

The Minoan eruption, one of the largest volcanic events in recorded history, buried Akrotiri under metres of pumice and ash and reshaped Santorini into its present crescent form. Dating is contested: radiocarbon evidence points to the late 17th century BCE (around 1627–1600 BCE), while conventional archaeological synchronisms with Egypt have often placed it around 1500 BCE. The catastrophe has long been linked, speculatively, to Plato's legend of Atlantis.

Excavations

Systematic excavation began in 1967 under Spyridon Marinatos of the Archaeological Society of Athens and continued under Christos Doumas after Marinatos's death at the site in 1974. A protective shelter roof collapsed in 2005, killing a visitor; a replacement bioclimatic shelter opened in 2012, when the site reopened to the public.
