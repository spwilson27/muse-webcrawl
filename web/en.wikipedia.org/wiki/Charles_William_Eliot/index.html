Charles William Eliot (1834–1926) was an American academic who served as president of Harvard University from 1869 to 1909, the longest term in the university's history. A chemist by training, he transformed Harvard from a provincial college into a modern research university and reshaped American higher education as a whole.

Charles William Eliot, president of Harvard

Early life and career

Eliot was born in Boston into a wealthy and prominent family; his father, Samuel Atkins Eliot, was a congressman and mayor of Boston. He graduated from Harvard College in 1853, studied chemistry, and taught mathematics and chemistry at Harvard before a European sojourn studying educational systems and chemistry in France and Germany. He then taught at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. His 1869 Atlantic Monthly essays, "The New Education," attracted national attention and preceded his appointment at Harvard at age 35.

Presidency of Harvard

Eliot's reforms included the elective system, which allowed undergraduates wide freedom in choosing courses in place of a fixed classical curriculum; the elevation of Harvard's professional schools in law, medicine, and divinity to graduate status with rigorous standards; the growth of the Graduate School of Arts and Sciences; and a dramatic expansion of the faculty, library, and endowment. Under his leadership Harvard's enrollment and national standing grew enormously, and its model was emulated across the United States.

Later work

After retiring in 1909, Eliot remained a public intellectual, editing the Harvard Classics — the famous "Five Foot Shelf of Books" (1909), a 50-volume anthology meant to provide a liberal education through fifteen minutes of daily reading. He wrote widely on education, religion, and public affairs, and summered in Northeast Harbor, Maine, where he supported conservation efforts that fed into the creation of Acadia National Park. He died in 1926.
