Empyema

SpecialtyPulmonology, thoracic surgery, infectious disease
SymptomsFever, chest pain, cough, shortness of breath
Usual causeBacterial infection of the pleural space, usually complicating pneumonia
TreatmentAntibiotics, chest-tube drainage, fibrinolytics, surgery (VATS/decortication)

An empyema is a collection of pus in a naturally existing anatomical cavity of the body. Most commonly the term refers to pleural empyema (pyothorax), an accumulation of infected fluid in the pleural space between the lung and the chest wall. Empyemas can also occur in the gallbladder (gallbladder empyema), the pelvis, joints, and the subdural space.

Pleural empyema

Pleural empyema most often develops as a complication of bacterial pneumonia: a parapneumonic effusion becomes secondarily infected, classically passing through three stages — exudative, fibrinopurulent (with fibrin strands and loculations), and organising (a thick fibrous peel that traps the lung). Common organisms include Streptococcus pneumoniae, the Streptococcus anginosus group, Staphylococcus aureus, and anaerobes. Other causes include chest trauma, thoracic surgery, oesophageal perforation, and spread from subdiaphragmatic infection.

Signs and diagnosis

Patients typically present with persistent or recurrent fever, pleuritic chest pain, cough, breathlessness, and malaise, often after an apparently improving pneumonia. Diagnosis rests on imaging — chest X-ray, ultrasound, and CT — and on thoracentesis (pleural fluid sampling): frankly purulent fluid, or fluid with a pH below 7.2, positive Gram stain or culture, confirms the diagnosis and the need for drainage.

Treatment

Management combines prompt antibiotics with drainage of the infected space, usually by an intercostal chest drain. Loculated collections may require intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy (such as the tPA/DNase combination validated in the MIST2 trial) or surgery — video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) or open decortication to strip the fibrous peel and re-expand the lung. Despite modern treatment, pleural infection carries a mortality of roughly 10–20%, rising with age and comorbidity.

History

Empyema was described by Hippocrates, who recommended drainage by rib resection or cautery — one of the earliest recorded surgical procedures — a reminder that the twin principles of antibiotics plus drainage remain the cornerstones of treatment.
