Synod of Whitby

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Synod of Whitby was a Christian administrative gathering held in 664 at the double monastery of Streanæshalch, now known as Whitby Abbey, in the Kingdom of Northumbria. Northumbria's King Oswiu convened the synod to settle a dispute about the correct method for calculating the date of Easter, and about the proper style of monastic tonsure.

The synod marked the decision to follow Roman rather than Insular (Ionian or "Celtic") practice in Northumbria, signalling the ascendancy of Roman influence in the English church. Our chief source is Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People, supplemented by Stephen of Ripon's Life of St Wilfrid.

Background

Christianity in Britain and Ireland had developed distinct local customs after the end of Roman rule. The mission sent by Pope Gregory I under Augustine of Canterbury from 597 followed Roman practice, while the influential monastery of Iona, founded by Columba, and its daughter houses in Northumbria followed a different computus for Easter. In Oswiu's household the king, converted through the Ionian mission, celebrated Easter on one date while his Kentish queen Eanflæd, raised in the Roman tradition, sometimes was still fasting through Lent — so that, as Bede notes, Easter could be kept twice in one year at court.

The debate

Advocates of the Ionian practice were led by Colmán, Bishop of Lindisfarne, who appealed to the authority of St Columba and St John the Evangelist. The Roman case was pressed most forcefully by the young Wilfrid, abbot of Ripon, who argued that the Roman method was that used where the apostles Peter and Paul had taught and died, and was observed throughout the world. King Oswiu then declared that he would side with St Peter, the keeper of the keys of heaven, for he did not wish to risk finding the gates closed against him.

Outcome and consequences

Oswiu ruled in favour of the Roman Easter and tonsure. Colmán, unwilling to accept the decision, resigned his see and withdrew with his Irish monks and some English followers to Iona and then to Ireland. Tuda succeeded him as bishop. Although the synod's practical effects were local at first, it is traditionally seen as a landmark in the integration of the English church into mainstream Latin Christendom; the remaining British and Irish churches gradually adopted the Roman Easter over the following century and a half.
