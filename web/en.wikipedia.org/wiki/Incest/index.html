Incest is human sexual activity between family members or close relatives. This typically includes sexual activity between people in consanguinity (blood relations) and sometimes those related by affinity (marriage or stepfamily), adoption, or lineage. The incest taboo is one of the most widespread of all cultural taboos, found in nearly every human society, though the specific relations covered vary between cultures.

Definitions and law

Criminal laws differ considerably: most jurisdictions prohibit sexual relations between parents and children, siblings and other close kin, especially involving minors, where it is treated as child sexual abuse. Some jurisdictions also criminalise consensual relations between adult relatives, while others regulate only marriage. Many legal systems prohibit marriages within specified degrees of kinship even where first-cousin marriage — legal in much of the world — is permitted.

Explanations and exceptions

Anthropologists debate why the taboo is near-universal: proposed explanations include the Westermarck effect (an innate sexual aversion arising between children raised together), the genetic costs of inbreeding, and the social advantages of forming alliances through exogamy, as argued by Claude Lévi-Strauss. Historically, exceptions existed for royalty — brother–sister marriage occurred among pharaohs of Egypt (such as the Ptolemaic dynasty), Inca rulers and Hawaiian chiefs, where it was intended to preserve sacred bloodlines. Royal inbreeding in other dynasties, such as the Spanish Habsburgs, had well-documented genetic consequences.
