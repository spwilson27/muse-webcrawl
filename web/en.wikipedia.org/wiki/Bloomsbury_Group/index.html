The Bloomsbury Group—or Bloomsbury Set—was a group of associated English writers, intellectuals, philosophers and artists in the early 20th century. Its members included novelist Virginia Woolf, economist John Maynard Keynes, novelist E. M. Forster, biographer Lytton Strachey, art critic Clive Bell, painters Vanessa Bell and Duncan Grant, and art critic Roger Fry. Their works and outlook deeply influenced literature, aesthetics, economics, and attitudes to feminism, pacifism, and sexuality.

Origins

The group grew out of friendships formed at the University of Cambridge—many of the men belonged to the Cambridge Apostles—and the "Thursday evening" gatherings begun around 1905 at the Bloomsbury home of the Stephen siblings, Vanessa and Virginia, after the death of their father, Sir Leslie Stephen. Leonard Woolf famously summed up their civilized creed in the values of friendship, conversation, and the pursuit of truth and beauty, influenced by G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica.

Activities and influence

Roger Fry's Post-Impressionist exhibitions of 1910 and 1912, and the Omega Workshops (founded 1913), brought modern art to Britain. Virginia and Leonard Woolf founded the Hogarth Press in 1917. Members were largely conscientious objectors during the First World War. Though the "group" had dissolved as a formal circle by the 1930s, its members' individual achievements—in modernist fiction, Keynesian economics, and biography—kept the name in wide currency.

Criticism

The circle has been criticized as elitist and cliquish; Wyndham Lewis satirized it, and D. H. Lawrence attacked its intellectualism. Later scholarship has broadened its membership and reassessed its contributions, including those of women and less famous associates.
