His Majesty's Revenue and Customs (HMRC) is a non-ministerial department of the UK Government responsible for the collection of taxes, the payment of some forms of state support, the administration of other regulatory regimes including the national minimum wage, and the issuance of national insurance numbers. HMRC was formed by the merger of the Inland Revenue and HM Customs and Excise, which took effect on 18 April 2005 under the Commissioners for Revenue and Customs Act 2005.

Responsibilities

HMRC administers income tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax, inheritance tax, value added tax (VAT), excise duties, stamp duty land tax, customs duties and environmental levies such as the climate change levy and landfill tax. It operates the PAYE system by which most employees' income tax is collected, pays tax credits and Child Benefit, enforces the minimum wage, and — through its customs functions and Border Force partners — polices trade at the border. It is the UK's largest revenue authority, collecting more than £800 billion annually in recent years.

Organisation and criticism

The department is headed by a board and an executive committee; its First Permanent Secretary serves as chief executive, and its officials are styled Commissioners for His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, exercising powers derived from the Crown. HMRC has faced recurring criticism over customer service — long telephone waiting times and the closure of local tax offices — over the tax gap between tax owed and collected, and over settlement deals with large companies. Its "Making Tax Digital" programme, requiring businesses and landlords to keep digital records and file quarterly, has been repeatedly delayed. In 2010 the department's handling of PAYE errors affecting millions of taxpayers drew public controversy, and in 2007 the loss of discs containing the Child Benefit records of 25 million people — by the predecessor systems — led to the resignation of its chairman and major reforms in data security.
