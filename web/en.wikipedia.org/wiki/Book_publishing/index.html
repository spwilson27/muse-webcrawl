Book publishing is the industry concerned with the creation, production, and distribution of books — fiction, nonfiction, scholarly, educational, and children's titles — in print and digital form. A publisher typically acquires rights in an author's manuscript, finances its editing, design, manufacture, and marketing, and sells the finished book through retailers, wholesalers, libraries, and direct channels, paying the author royalties on sales.

Process

Editors (often guided by literary agents' submissions) acquire manuscripts and shepherd them through developmental editing, copyediting, and proofreading. Designers produce the cover and interior layout; the book is assigned an ISBN and catalogued. Printing may use offset lithography for large runs or digital print-on-demand for small ones. Publicity, marketing, and sales departments pitch the title to bookstores and review media, usually months in advance. Royalties for print books commonly run 10–15% of list price on hardcovers, with advances paid against them.

History

Commercial publishing followed the spread of movable-type printing after Gutenberg (c. 1440). Copyright law — beginning with the Statute of Anne (1710) — created the modern author–publisher relationship. The 19th century brought industrial printing, cheap paper, and mass literacy; the 20th added paperback revolutions, conglomerate consolidation, and, after 2000, e-books, audiobooks, and self-publishing platforms such as Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing.

Segments

The industry divides into trade (consumer) publishing, educational and academic publishing, and professional/scholarly publishing. Self-publishing and hybrid models now account for a substantial share of new titles, while the largest trade houses remain concentrated in the "Big Five" groups.
