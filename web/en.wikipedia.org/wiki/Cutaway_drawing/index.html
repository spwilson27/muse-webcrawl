Cutaway drawing

A cutaway drawing, also called a cutaway diagram, is a 3D graphics, drawing, diagram and or illustration, in which surface elements of a three-dimensional model are selectively removed, to make internal features visible, but without sacrificing the outer context entirely.

Purpose

Cutaways are used where the internal structure of an object cannot be shown by ordinary external views and where an exploded view would lose the spatial relationships between parts. Classic subjects include engines, aircraft, buildings, ships, geological formations, and the human body. By removing a portion of the outer shell — often as a clean quarter-section or an irregular "window" — the illustrator reveals mechanisms in place, preserving the viewer's sense of how the whole object fits together.

Technique

Traditionally, technical illustrators constructed cutaways on the drawing board using perspective geometry, often working from engineering blueprints. The cut surfaces are typically shown with hatching or a contrasting tone so the eye can distinguish solid material from empty space. Modern cutaways are produced with CAD models and 3D rendering software, which can clip geometry along arbitrary planes, but skilled renderers still art-direct the removal so that important internal details are not hidden behind the cut edge.

History

Sectional and cutaway representations appear in the notebooks of Leonardo da Vinci, whose anatomical and machine studies peeled away layers to expose inner workings. The genre flourished in the 20th century in popular-science magazines, aircraft and motoring publications, and museum exhibits; artists such as the illustrators of Flight magazine and the famous cutaways of ships and aircraft in British annuals made the form widely familiar. Physical cutaway models — real engines or airframes with sections machined away — serve the same teaching purpose in museums and technical schools.
