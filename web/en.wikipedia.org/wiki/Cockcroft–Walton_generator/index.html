Cockcroft–Walton generator

A Cockcroft–Walton generator used to accelerate particles.

The Cockcroft–Walton generator, or Greinacher multiplier, is an electric circuit that generates a high DC voltage from a low-voltage AC or pulsing DC input. It is a type of voltage multiplier consisting of a ladder network of capacitors and diodes. The circuit was developed from Heinrich Greinacher's work in 1919 and was made famous by John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton, who used it in 1932 to power the particle accelerator with which they first split the atomic nucleus, an achievement for which they received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1951.

Operation

The multiplier is driven by an alternating input voltage. As the input swings, the diodes steer charge between the capacitors so that charge accumulates stage by stage: in the idealized no-load condition, the output voltage equals twice the peak input voltage multiplied by the number of stages. Unlike transformer-based supplies, the multiplier can reach very high voltages using components that each only have to withstand a modest fraction of the output, and it requires no large high-voltage insulation. However, the output voltage falls (and ripple grows) as more stages are added and as load current increases, which limits the current the generator can deliver.

Applications

Cockcroft–Walton generators were the standard high-voltage supply for early particle accelerators, and they remain in use as the pre-injector voltage source at some accelerator laboratories, where they have gradually been replaced by radio-frequency quadrupoles. Small multipliers are ubiquitous in electronic equipment needing a high voltage at low current: photocopiers and laser printers, cathode-ray tube displays, X-ray systems, photomultiplier tubes, night-vision image intensifiers, ionizers, and microwave ovens, which commonly use a half-wave doubler variant.
