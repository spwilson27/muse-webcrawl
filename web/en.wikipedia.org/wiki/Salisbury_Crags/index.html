The Salisbury Crags are a series of cliffs in Holyrood Park, Edinburgh, on the western flank of Arthur's Seat. The approximately 46-metre (151 ft) line of crags forms a dramatic backdrop to the city centre, visible from Edinburgh Castle and the Royal Mile. They are part of an intrusive igneous sill within the ancient Arthur's Seat volcano system.

Geology

The crags consist of dolerite (teschenite) intruded as a sill into Carboniferous sedimentary rocks about 340 million years ago, during the activity of the Arthur's Seat volcano. Differential erosion later stripped away the softer sediments, exposing the resistant igneous rock as a steep escarpment; glaciation further shaped the hill. The crags are celebrated in the history of geology: in the 18th century James Hutton studied exposures here, including the locality known as Hutton's Section on the southern side, where he found evidence that the igneous rock had been forced, molten, between sedimentary layers — a key argument for his theory that the Earth was shaped by heat and immense time. The exposure demonstrates columnar jointing formed as the magma cooled.

History

The crags were extensively quarried from the 16th century onward for paving stone, and quarrying left scars that became the subject of public controversy in the 19th century, when campaigns led by local figures helped end extraction and preserve the park's appearance. The path along the base and top of the crags, the Radical Road, was built in 1820 by unemployed weavers from the west of Scotland employed under a relief scheme promoted by Sir Walter Scott after the Radical War of 1820 — hence its name. The crags form part of the Royal Park managed by Historic Environment Scotland, and the path is occasionally closed for safety following rockfalls. Rock climbing on the crags has been restricted in modern times, though their quarry bays were once popular training ground for Scottish mountaineers.

The name is traditionally said to derive from the Earls of Salisbury who accompanied Edward I of England into Scotland, though the origin is uncertain and other derivations, including from "sallow" willows or a landowner called Salisbury, have been proposed.
