Silent mutation

A silent mutation is a form of mutation that does not result in a change in the amino acid sequence of the protein produced by a gene. Silent mutations occur when a nucleotide substitution alters a codon so that it still codes for the same amino acid — possible because the genetic code is degenerate, with most amino acids specified by several codons differing mainly at the third ("wobble") position. Such a mutation is also called a synonymous substitution, and the changed sequence is said to be a synonymous variant. Mutations that do change the encoded amino acid are called nonsynonymous (missense) or nonsense mutations, by contrast.

Consequences

Because the protein sequence is unchanged, silent mutations were long assumed to be selectively neutral and without phenotypic effect — hence "silent". Research since the early 2000s has shown this is not always so:

  • Codon usage bias: synonymous codons are not used equally, and the corresponding tRNAs differ in abundance; swapping in a rare codon can slow or stall translation, altering co-translational protein folding and thus protein function.
  • Splicing and mRNA structure: a synonymous change can create or destroy exonic splicing enhancers/silencers, change mRNA secondary structure or stability, and thereby alter the amount or form of protein made.
  • Disease associations: synonymous variants have been implicated in human diseases — for example, a silent change in the ABCB1 (MDR1) transporter was shown to alter substrate specificity through changed folding; synonymous mutations in genes such as CFTR and various cancer genes can affect splicing and expression.

Evolutionary significance

Rates of synonymous substitution (dS, or Ks) are used in molecular evolution as an approximately neutral baseline against which the rate of nonsynonymous substitution (dN) is compared; dN/dS ratios reveal positive or purifying selection. Evidence of selection on codon usage in many organisms shows that even this baseline is not perfectly neutral.
