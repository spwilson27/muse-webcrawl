An omphalos (Ancient Greek: ὀμφαλός, "navel") is a religious stone artefact in ancient Greek religion. In Greek tradition the most famous omphalos stood at the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi, marking the centre — the "navel" — of the world.

Mythology

According to myth, Zeus wished to determine the centre of the earth and released two eagles (or ravens) from opposite ends of the world; the birds met at Delphi, and a stone was placed there to mark the point of their meeting. Another tradition held that the stone was the very one that Cronus had swallowed in the belief that it was his infant son Zeus, and which he later vomited up; Zeus set it up at Delphi beneath Mount Parnassus as a sign to mortals.

The Delphi omphalos

The omphalos stood in the adyton, the inner sanctum of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, near the seat of the Pythia, the oracle. Surviving sculpted copies, including a Roman-era example preserved in the Delphi Archaeological Museum, depict a rounded conical stone covered with a carved net-like pattern (the agrenon, a woollen net in which the original was wrapped), with two birds perched on top in some representations.

Other omphaloi

Stones identified as omphaloi were kept at other Greek sanctuaries, and the idea of a sacred "navel of the world" recurs in many cultures. The concept is often compared with the axis mundi, the world centre or cosmic axis in comparative religion, and with sacred stones such as the Black Stone of the Kaaba and the foundation stone in Jerusalem tradition.

Later usage

The word has been applied metaphorically to anything regarded as a central point. The "omphalos hypothesis" of the 19th-century naturalist Philip Henry Gosse proposed that the world was created with the appearance of age, as Adam was created with a navel; the term is still used in discussions of creationism. In modern Greece, Omphalos has also been used as a place name. Artists and writers, including James Joyce in Ulysses, have used the Delphic omphalos as an image of the centre of a world.
