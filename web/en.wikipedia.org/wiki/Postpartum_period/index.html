Postpartum period

A mother with her newborn shortly after childbirth.

The postpartum period, also known as the puerperium, begins after childbirth as the mother's body, including hormone levels and uterus size, returns to a non-pregnant state. The terms are usually understood to refer to the first six weeks following delivery, though physiological and psychological changes can extend for months.

Phases

The postpartum period is often divided into three phases: the acute phase (the first 6–12 hours after delivery, when complications such as postpartum haemorrhage are most likely), the early phase (the following two to six weeks, during which the uterus involutes and lochia is discharged), and the delayed phase (up to six months, with gradual recovery of muscle tone and connective tissue).

Physical changes and care

Typical experiences include uterine cramping ("afterpains"), vaginal discharge (lochia), breast engorgement as lactation begins, perineal soreness, and fatigue. Postpartum care involves monitoring for haemorrhage and infection, support with breastfeeding, pain management, and screening for complications such as venous thromboembolism.

Mental health

Many mothers experience transient "baby blues" in the first days. More serious conditions include postpartum depression, which affects a significant minority of mothers, and the rare but severe postpartum psychosis, a psychiatric emergency. Screening and support during this period are emphasised in modern guidelines.

Cultural practices

Many cultures observe a traditional confinement period of rest for new mothers — for example "doing the month" (zuò yuèzi) in Chinese culture and the forty-day cuarentena in parts of Latin America.
