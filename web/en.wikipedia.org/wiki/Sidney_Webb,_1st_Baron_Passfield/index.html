Sidney Webb, 1st Baron Passfield

Sidney James Webb, 1st Baron Passfield, PC (13 July 1859 – 13 October 1947) was a British socialist, economist, and reformer, who co-founded the London School of Economics. He was an early member of the Fabian Society in 1884, joining, like George Bernard Shaw, three months after its inception. Along with his wife Beatrice Webb and with Annie Besant, Graham Wallas, Edward R. Pease, Hubert Bland and Sydney Olivier, Shaw and Webb turned the Fabian Society into the pre-eminent politico-intellectual society in England during the Edwardian era and beyond.

Background and education

Webb was born in Milford Lane, off the Strand in London, to a professional family. He studied law at the Birkbeck Literary and Scientific Institution for a degree and studied at King's College London, graduating from the University of London in 1886. He also worked in the civil service, holding posts in the War Office and the Colonial Office.

Career

Webb co-wrote, with Beatrice, influential works including The History of Trade Unionism (1894) and Industrial Democracy (1897). Together they founded the London School of Economics in 1895 with a bequest left to the Fabian Society, and in 1913 they founded the political magazine New Statesman. Webb was elected to the London County Council in 1892 and served as its chairman. He was appointed to the Privy Council and served in the first Labour government of Ramsay MacDonald as President of the Board of Trade in 1924. In 1929 he was raised to the peerage as Baron Passfield and served as Secretary of State for the Colonies and Secretary of State for Dominion Affairs.

The Webbs' later admiration for the Soviet Union, expressed in their book Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? (1935), proved controversial. Webb died in 1947; his ashes, and later Beatrice's, were interred in Westminster Abbey.
