A t-test is a type of statistical hypothesis test in which the test statistic follows a Student's t-distribution under the null hypothesis. It is most commonly used to determine whether the means of one or two populations differ, when the population standard deviation is unknown and the sample size is small. A t-test can be performed as a one-sample test, an independent two-sample test, or a paired test on matched observations.

History

The t-statistic was introduced in 1908 by the chemist William Sealy Gosset, who worked at the Guinness brewery in Dublin and needed a method for monitoring the quality of stout from small samples. Because Guinness prohibited employees from publishing research, he published under the pseudonym "Student", giving the test and the distribution their enduring names. The modern theory was developed by Ronald Fisher in the 1920s.

Types

  • One-sample t-test: tests whether the mean of a sample differs from a known or hypothesised value.
  • Two-sample t-test: compares the means of two independent groups; the classical form assumes equal population variances, while Welch's t-test does not.
  • Paired t-test: compares two measurements on the same subjects, such as before-and-after observations, by testing the mean of the differences.

Assumptions and interpretation

The test assumes that the data are continuous, that observations are independent, and that the sample means are approximately normally distributed — exactly so when the underlying populations are normal. The result is summarised by a p-value: the probability, under the null hypothesis, of obtaining a t-statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. Results are conventionally described as statistically significant when the p-value falls below a chosen threshold such as 0.05.
