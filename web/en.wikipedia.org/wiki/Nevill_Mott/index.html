Sir Nevill Francis Mott (30 September 1905 – 8 August 1996) was a British theoretical physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1977 for his work on the electronic structure of magnetic and disordered systems, especially amorphous semiconductors.

Early life and education

Mott was born in Leeds to Charles Francis Mott and Lilian Mary Reynolds. He was educated at Clifton College in Bristol and at St John's College, Cambridge, where he read mathematics and theoretical physics.

As a young researcher he worked on collision theory and scattering, including what became known as Mott scattering of electrons by nuclei.

Career

Mott spent much of his career at the University of Bristol, where he built a leading school of theoretical physics, and later at Cambridge, where he was head of the Cavendish Laboratory from 1954 to 1971.

He made foundational contributions to the theory of metals and insulators, the physics of photographic emulsions, and the electronic properties of disordered materials. The metal–insulator transition associated with electron correlations is named the Mott transition after him.

Nobel Prize and later life

In 1977 Mott shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Philip W. Anderson and John H. van Vleck for fundamental theoretical investigations of the electronic structure of magnetic and disordered systems.

Mott also wrote on science and religion and was involved in debates about the relationship between the two. He died in Milton Keynes in 1996.
