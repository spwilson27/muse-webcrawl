A chariot is a type of carriage, usually fast and light, drawn by horses and used in ancient warfare, racing, and ceremony. The first chariots — with spoked wheels — appeared around 2000 BC in the Sintashta culture of the Eurasian steppe and spread across the Near East, Egypt, Greece, Rome, India, and China, becoming the prestige weapon of Bronze Age and early Iron Age armies.

Warfare

Chariots served as mobile archery platforms and shock weapons. Hittite and Egyptian chariot forces clashed at the Battle of Kadesh (1274 BC), one of the largest chariot battles ever fought. Crews typically consisted of a driver and a warrior armed with a composite bow, javelins, or spear. In China, chariots were the core of Shang and Zhou armies; in India they feature prominently in the Mahabharata. Celtic and Roman-era British forces used light chariots, and Rome retained chariots chiefly for racing in the circus.

Racing and ceremony

Chariot racing was among the most popular sports of the ancient world: the four-horse quadriga race was an Olympic event from 680 BC, and Roman circus racing with its faction teams (Blues, Greens, Reds, Whites) drew enormous crowds at the Circus Maximus.

Decline

As cavalry riding improved and infantry tactics evolved, the war chariot was superseded by mounted troops, surviving in ceremonial and sporting roles and in religious symbolism (e.g., the chariot of the sun god Surya or Helios).
