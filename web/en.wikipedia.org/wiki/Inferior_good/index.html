Inferior good
FieldMicroeconomics
Income elasticityNegative
Contrasts withNormal good

In economics, an inferior good is a good whose demand decreases when consumer income rises (and increases when income falls) — the opposite of a normal good. Its income elasticity of demand is negative. "Inferior" describes this demand behaviour, not necessarily low quality.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include staple starches such as instant noodles and basic rice, margarine relative to butter, second-hand goods, budget supermarket own-brands, and intercity bus travel relative to flying. As households grow richer they substitute toward preferred alternatives — fresh foods, name brands, or more comfortable transport — so demand for the inferior alternative falls.

Income and substitution effects

When the price of a good changes, the total effect decomposes into a substitution effect and an income effect. For inferior goods the income effect works against the substitution effect; in the extreme case of a Giffen good, the income effect dominates, and demand rises when the price rises. Inferiority is also relative to income levels and consumer preferences: a good may be inferior for high-income consumers but normal for low-income ones, which is reflected in Engel curves that bend back as income grows.
