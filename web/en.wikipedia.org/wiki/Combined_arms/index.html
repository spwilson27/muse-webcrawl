Combined arms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Combined arms is an approach to warfare that seeks to integrate different combat arms of a military to achieve mutually complementary effects — for example, using infantry and armour in an urban environment, where each counters the vulnerabilities of the other. The classic illustration is the dilemma imposed on an opponent: to defend against one arm, a force must expose itself to another — troops who take cover from artillery in the open become vulnerable to infantry, while those who shelter in buildings from infantry become targets for tanks and artillery.

History

Ancient and early modern armies combined infantry, cavalry and missile troops, but modern combined-arms doctrine coalesced in the First World War, in response to trench stalemate: the German stormtrooper tactics of 1917–18 and the Allied Hundred Days offensive of 1918 integrated artillery creeping barrages, infantry infiltration, tanks, engineers and ground-attack aircraft. In the Second World War the German Blitzkrieg — itself built on ordinary combined-arms principles with massed armour and air support — and the Red Army's deep operations demonstrated the concept at operational scale.

Practice

Effective combined arms requires the arms to be integrated at a low tactical level — typically battalion or brigade "combat teams" or battlegroups that permanently or habitually combine tanks, mechanised infantry, engineers, air defence and artillery observers, with supporting aviation and logistics. Modern doctrine extends the principle to "joint" operations across services and, in NATO usage, to the synchronisation of fires, intelligence, cyber and information effects. The failures of forces that operate arms in isolation — armour without infantry in cities, infantry without fire support in the open — are a recurrent lesson of recent conflicts.
