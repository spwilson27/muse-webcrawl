Deaf history

Deaf history is the history of the Deaf community and of attitudes toward deaf people, from ancient references to deafness in law and religion, through the founding of deaf schools and sign languages, to the modern Deaf rights movement.

Antiquity to the Renaissance

Early legal codes often denied deaf people full civil rights, assuming inability to reason without speech. Individual teachers appear from the 16th century: the Spanish Benedictine monk Pedro Ponce de León taught deaf children of the aristocracy, and in 1620 Juan Pablo Bonet published the first manual alphabet and method for teaching the deaf.

Schools and sign languages

In 1760 the Abbé Charles-Michel de l'Épée founded the first public school for the deaf in Paris, systematising French Sign Language; his successor Roch-Ambroise Sicard spread the method. Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet brought teacher Laurent Clerc to the United States, founding the American School for the Deaf in Hartford in 1817 — an origin of American Sign Language (ASL). Gallaudet University, chartered in 1864, remains the world's only liberal-arts university for deaf students.

Oralism and suppression

The Milan Congress of 1880 declared oral education superior and drove sign language out of classrooms in much of Europe and America for nearly a century, a policy now widely regarded as having harmed deaf education. Manual methods persisted in deaf clubs and communities.

Modern era

Linguistic recognition of sign languages began with William Stokoe's 1960 analysis of ASL. The Deaf President Now protest at Gallaudet University in 1988 secured the university's first deaf president, I. King Jordan, a landmark of Deaf civil rights. Historians such as Harlan Lane and Jack Gannon have written major histories of the community, while organisations such as the World Federation of the Deaf advocate internationally.
