Pseudosphere

In geometry, a pseudosphere is a surface with constant negative Gaussian curvature. It is generated by revolving a tractrix about its asymptote. A pseudosphere of radius R has curvature −1/R² at each point, mirroring the sphere of radius R, which has curvature +1/R² — hence the name, from the Greek pseudos ("false") and sphaira.

History

The surface was studied in connection with the tractrix by Christiaan Huygens and others in the 17th century. Eugenio Beltrami showed in 1868 that the pseudosphere provides a local model of hyperbolic geometry: geodesics on the pseudosphere behave like lines in the hyperbolic plane, giving a concrete demonstration that hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is. The surface thus played a key role in resolving the centuries-old question of the parallel postulate.

Properties

Despite extending infinitely in length, the pseudosphere has finite surface area (4πR², the same as a sphere of radius R) and finite volume (⅔πR³, half that of the sphere). The rim where the tractrix meets its axis is a singular edge: the surface cannot be smoothly extended past it. By Hilbert's theorem, no complete surface of constant negative curvature can be isometrically embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space, so the pseudosphere can model only part of the hyperbolic plane.

Related constructions

Other surfaces of constant negative curvature include Dini's surface and the Kuen surface. Rotating other curves gives the broader family of surfaces of revolution studied in classical differential geometry.
