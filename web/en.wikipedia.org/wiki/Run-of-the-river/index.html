Run-of-the-river hydroelectricity

Run-of-the-river hydroelectricity (ROR) is a type of hydroelectric generation in which electricity is produced principally from the natural flow and elevation drop of a river, with little or no impoundment of water. Unlike conventional hydroelectric dams, which store water in large reservoirs, run-of-the-river projects channel a portion of the river's flow through a canal or penstock to a powerhouse, where it drives turbines before being returned to the river.

Design

A typical installation consists of a low diversion weir or barrage, an intake, a conveyance channel or pipe, a powerhouse containing turbines and generators, and a tailrace returning water downstream. Some facilities include a small amount of "pondage" — storage sufficient for a few hours — allowing limited adjustment of output to demand, but they cannot sustain generation through seasonal changes in flow the way large reservoir projects can. Schemes range from micro-hydro installations serving single buildings to large projects on major rivers exceeding 100 megawatts.

Advantages

  • Little flooding of land upstream, avoiding displacement of communities and loss of agricultural or forest land.
  • Reduced alteration of natural river flow regimes and sediment transport compared with large storage dams.
  • No large reservoir, so little of the methane emissions associated with decaying submerged vegetation.
  • Often lower construction cost and shorter construction time than equivalent storage schemes.

Disadvantages

  • Output depends on the river's flow, which varies seasonally and during droughts; generation is therefore intermittent and largely non-dispatchable.
  • Without storage, the plant cannot be used to meet peak demand reliably or provide large-scale grid balancing.
  • Diversion of flow can still affect aquatic ecosystems, fish migration and downstream water users if poorly designed.

Environmental impact

Because they avoid large reservoirs, run-of-the-river schemes are often described as environmentally preferable to conventional hydroelectricity. Impacts nonetheless include barrier effects of diversion weirs on migrating fish, changes to flow in the bypassed reach, and construction disturbance. Modern designs mitigate these with fish passes, minimum environmental flows and run-of-river pondage operated to mimic natural discharge patterns.
