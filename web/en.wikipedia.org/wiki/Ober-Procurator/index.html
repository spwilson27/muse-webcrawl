Ober-Procurator

The Ober-Procurator (Russian: обер-прокурор, also rendered Chief Procurator) was the official who headed the Most Holy Synod in the Russian Empire from 1722 to 1917. A lay official appointed by the emperor, the Ober-Procurator was effectively the tsar's minister for Orthodox Church affairs and the most powerful figure in the administration of the Russian Orthodox Church.

Origins

The office was created by Peter the Great in 1722, a year after he replaced the Patriarchate of Moscow with the Holy Synod, a collegial state body governing the Church. The Ober-Procurator was charged with supervising the Synod's work on the emperor's behalf — described in his instructions as "the sovereign's eye" — ensuring that its decisions conformed to state law and policy. Initially a watchdog post, the office steadily accumulated executive power: by the nineteenth century the Ober-Procurator controlled the Synod's agenda, finances, appointments, and correspondence with the throne.

Notable holders

The office's most famous holder was Konstantin Pobedonostsev (in office 1880–1905), tutor and adviser to Alexander III and Nicholas II, whose conservative influence over church schools, censorship, and religious policy made him one of the most powerful men in Russia. The office was abolished after the February Revolution of 1917, when the Provisional Government replaced it briefly with a Minister of Confessions before the Church restored the patriarchate later that year.
