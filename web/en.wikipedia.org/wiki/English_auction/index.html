English auction

An English auction is the best-known type of open ascending-price auction. The auctioneer begins with a low opening (reserve) price and progressively raises it, often announcing each new bid, until only one bidder remains willing to continue. The last remaining bidder wins the item and pays the final bid price. It is the traditional format of auction houses such as Sotheby's and Christie's, of livestock and antiques sales, and — in a timed, proxy-bid variant — of online marketplaces such as eBay.

Mechanics

The auctioneer announces a starting price, often below the expected market value, and calls for bids in set increments. Bidders signal acceptance publicly, so all participants can observe rivals' willingness to pay. The process ends when no participant will exceed the current bid; the item is then "knocked down" to the standing bidder at that price, subject to any reserve price below which the seller will not sell. Variants include the Japanese auction, in which the price rises continuously on a clock and bidders must signal continued participation or drop out irreversibly, and online "English" auctions where automated proxy bidding raises a participant's bid by the smallest increment needed to stay ahead, up to their secret maximum.

Strategy and theory

With private values, the dominant strategy in an English auction is to remain in the bidding until the price reaches one's own valuation, then drop out — a logic that makes the English auction strategically equivalent to the second-price (Vickrey) auction, since the winner effectively pays the second-highest valuation (plus one increment). Under the revenue equivalence theorem, under standard assumptions the seller can expect the same revenue as from other standard auction forms. With common or affiliated values, the open format reveals information as rivals drop out, which mitigates the winner's curse and can raise expected revenue relative to sealed-bid formats — one reason spectrum regulators and art sellers favour open auctions.

History and variations

Ascending auctions are ancient — described by Herodotus in Babylon and used in Roman sales of war booty ("sub hasta"). In England the format became standard for estates, ships and cargoes in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, including "auction by inch of candle", in which bidding continued until a short candle burned out. Modern developments include multi-round simultaneous ascending auctions for radio spectrum, pioneered by the US Federal Communications Commission in 1994 and credited with raising billions in revenue while allocating licences efficiently.
