Uniform boundedness principle

In mathematics, specifically functional analysis, the uniform boundedness principle (also called the Banach–Steinhaus theorem) states that for a family of continuous linear operators whose domain is a Banach space, pointwise boundedness implies uniform boundedness in the operator norm. It was published in 1927 by Stefan Banach and Hugo Steinhaus, building on work of Hans Hahn, and is one of the cornerstones of functional analysis, alongside the Hahn–Banach theorem and the open mapping theorem.

Statement

Let X be a Banach space, Y a normed space, and F a collection of continuous linear operators from X to Y. If for every x in X the values are bounded — that is, sup over T in F of ‖Tx‖ is finite — then the operators are uniformly bounded: sup over T in F of ‖T‖ is finite. The proof relies on the Baire category theorem, and the result fails for incomplete spaces, where counterexamples exist.

Applications

The principle underlies convergence results throughout analysis: it implies that weakly convergent sequences are bounded, and it is used to prove that there exist continuous functions whose Fourier series diverge at a point (by showing that the partial-sum operators cannot be uniformly bounded for the sup norm), as well as to establish the principle of condensation of singularities. A standard corollary is that a weak limit of bounded linear operators is bounded, and that a family of operators converging pointwise on a Banach space has bounded norms.
