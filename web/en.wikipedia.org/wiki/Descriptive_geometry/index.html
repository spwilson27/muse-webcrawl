Descriptive geometry

Descriptive geometry is the branch of geometry concerned with representing three-dimensional objects in two dimensions by means of projections, and with solving spatial problems — intersections, true lengths, angles, developments — by constructions in the plane. It is the theoretical foundation of technical drawing and engineering graphics.

History

The discipline was founded by the French mathematician Gaspard Monge, whose lectures at the École Normale in 1795, published as Géométrie descriptive (1799), systematized the method of double orthogonal projection. Monge had developed the technique for designing fortifications; for a time it was treated as a French military secret. Practical projection methods had earlier precedents in stonecutting treatises and in the work of Albrecht Dürer and Gérard Desargues, but Monge turned them into a general engineering science.

Methods

In Monge's system an object is projected orthogonally onto a horizontal plane (plan) and a vertical plane (elevation), the two views being unfolded onto a single sheet about a ground line. From these coordinated views the draftsman can recover distances, find intersections of lines, planes, and surfaces, determine shadows, and develop (unfold) surfaces such as cones and cylinders into patterns for fabrication.

Applications

Descriptive geometry underlies architecture, mechanical and civil engineering drawing, shipbuilding, and stonemasonry (stereotomy). It was a required subject in engineering curricula worldwide for nearly two centuries.

Relation to modern graphics

Computer-aided design performs Monge's constructions automatically by projective mathematics, but the theory remains the conceptual basis of multiview drawing standards and of projective geometry in computer graphics.
