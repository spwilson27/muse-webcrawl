Powder River Basin

Location: Northeastern Wyoming and southeastern Montana, United States

Area: Approximately 120 miles east-west by 200 miles north-south

Named for: Powder River

The Powder River Basin is a geologic structural basin in southeast Montana and northeast Wyoming, about 120 miles (190 km) east to west and 200 miles (320 km) north to south, known for its extensive coal reserves. The basin is drained by the Powder River, the Cheyenne River, and their tributaries, and lies between the Bighorn Mountains to the west and the Black Hills to the east.

Coal production

The region supplies more than 40 percent of the coal produced in the United States, mined from thick seams of subbituminous coal in the Paleocene Fort Union Formation, principally the Wyodak-Anderson seam, which can exceed 70 feet in thickness. Because the coal lies close to the surface, it is extracted by large surface mines such as the North Antelope Rochelle Mine, the largest coal mine in the world by reserves and production. The low sulfur content of Powder River Basin coal made it especially attractive after the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990 increased demand for low-sulfur fuel.

Other resources

The basin also produces oil and natural gas, including coalbed methane, and contains significant uranium deposits. The Gillette, Wyoming area serves as the hub of mining activity.

Geology

The basin formed during the Laramide orogeny. Its sedimentary fill includes Cretaceous marine shales and sandstones overlain by the fluvial deposits of the Fort Union and Wasatch formations, which contain the coal seams and abundant fossil remains.
