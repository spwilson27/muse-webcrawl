Weather modification

Weather modification is the act of intentionally manipulating or altering the weather. The most common form is cloud seeding, which increases rainfall (or snowfall) by dispersing substances such as silver iodide or dry ice into clouds to serve as condensation or ice nuclei. Other aims include hail suppression, fog dispersal at airports, and, historically, attempts to weaken tropical cyclones.

History

Modern weather modification began in 1946, when Vincent Schaefer and Irving Langmuir at General Electric demonstrated that dry ice could induce precipitation, and Bernard Vonnegut discovered silver iodide seeding. Military applications followed: Operation Popeye (1967–1972) attempted to extend the monsoon over the Ho Chi Minh Trail during the Vietnam War, leading to the 1977 ENMOD Convention, which prohibits hostile environmental modification. Project Stormfury (1962–1983) tested seeding hurricanes with silver iodide but was abandoned as inconclusive.

Practice and evidence

Operational programs exist in dozens of countries; China's Weather Modification Office is the largest, and the United Arab Emirates runs extensive rain-enhancement research. Evaluations by the U.S. National Research Council have found no conclusive scientific proof that seeding produces statistically significant increases in rainfall under most conditions, though later experiments using radar measurements in Idaho (2017) demonstrated measurable snowfall from seeding in specific orographic clouds. Critics raise environmental questions about silver iodide accumulation and disputes over rainfall "stolen" from downwind regions.
