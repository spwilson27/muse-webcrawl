Mesoamerican calendar

The Mesoamerican calendar is a family of related calendar systems developed in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica by cultures including the Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, and Aztec (Mexica). Despite local variations, these calendars share a core structure combining a 260-day ritual cycle with a 365-day solar cycle, interlocking in a 52-year Calendar Round.

The 260-day cycle

The sacred almanac — the tzolk'in among the Maya, tonalpohualli among the Aztecs — combines 13 numbers with 20 named days to give 260 unique days. It was used for divination, naming children, and scheduling rituals and markets. Its origin is debated: hypotheses cite the human gestation period, the interval between zenith passages of the sun at southern Mesoamerican latitudes, agricultural cycles, and the synodic period of Venus (averaging about 263 days in visibility as morning star).

The 365-day cycle

The vague solar year — Maya haab', Aztec xiuhpohualli — consists of 18 months of 20 days (360 days) plus a final period of 5 unnamed or unlucky days (wayeb' / nemontemi). Because no leap correction was applied, the calendar drifted against the tropical year.

The Calendar Round and Long Count

Because 260 and 365 share factors, a paired date (260-day + 365-day) recurs every 18,980 days — 52 solar years or 73 ritual cycles — the Calendar Round, marked among the Aztecs by the New Fire ceremony. The Maya additionally used the Long Count, a vigesimal (base-20) count of days from a mythic creation date in 3114 BC, allowing events to be fixed in deep time on carved monuments.

Legacy

Highland Maya communities in Guatemala, notably K'iche' daykeepers, still maintain the 260-day count, one of the longest continuously used calendars in the world. Popular interest spiked around the completion of a Long Count b'ak'tun cycle on 21 December 2012.
