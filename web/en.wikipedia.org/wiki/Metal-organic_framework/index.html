Metal–organic framework

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are a class of porous coordination polymers consisting of metal ions or clusters coordinated to organic ligands to form one-, two-, or three-dimensional structures. They are a subclass of coordination polymers characterised by exceptionally high porosity and internal surface area.

Structure and synthesis

In a MOF, inorganic secondary building units — metal-containing nodes — are joined by organic linkers through strong bonds, producing crystalline materials with precisely defined pores. Well-known examples include MOF-5, HKUST-1 (Cu-BTC), ZIF-8 and UiO-66. Some MOFs have surface areas exceeding 7,000 m²/g, greater than any other porous material.

Applications

MOFs are studied for gas storage (hydrogen and methane), carbon dioxide capture, gas separation, catalysis, sensing, drug delivery and water harvesting from air. Their modular chemistry allows pore size and functionality to be tuned for a target application. The field was pioneered by Omar Yaghi, who coined the term "metal–organic framework" in the 1990s; Yaghi shared the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of MOFs.
