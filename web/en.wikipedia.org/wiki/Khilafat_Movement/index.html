The Khilafat Movement (1919–1924) was a pan-Islamist political protest campaign launched by Muslims of British India, led by Shaukat Ali, Maulana Muhammad Ali Jauhar, Hakim Ajmal Khan and Abul Kalam Azad, to restore the status of the Ottoman caliph — the symbolic head of Sunni Islam — after the defeat of the Ottoman Empire in the First World War and the harsh terms anticipated from the peace settlement.

Background

During the First World War, Britain had assured Indian Muslims that the Ottoman caliph's position and the holy places of Islam would be respected, in return for wartime loyalty. After the Armistice of Mudros (1918) and the Treaty of Sèvres (1920), the empire was partitioned and the caliph reduced to a figurehead under Allied control, provoking widespread anger. In November 1919, an All-India Khilafat Conference met in Delhi and called for protest, including a boycott of British goods.

Alliance with Gandhi

Mahatma Gandhi, leader of the Indian National Congress, supported the Khilafat cause as an opportunity to unite Hindus and Muslims against British rule. The Khilafat leaders in turn endorsed his programme, and in 1920 the movement merged with the Non-cooperation movement: Indians resigned government posts, boycotted foreign cloth, schools and courts, and promoted swadeshi (home industry). For a time, the alliance produced unprecedented Hindu–Muslim unity.

Decline

The movement lost momentum after Gandhi suspended non-cooperation following the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922, in which a mob killed 22 policemen. Communal riots, notably the Moplah rebellion in Malabar (1921), strained the alliance. The cause itself disappeared when Turkish leader Mustafa Kemal Atatürk abolished the caliphate in March 1924, removing the very institution the movement had sought to defend.

Legacy

Although it failed in its stated aim, the Khilafat Movement was the first all-India mass mobilisation of Muslims and brought religious leaders into nationalist politics. It trained a generation of activists, deepened mass participation in the independence struggle, and demonstrated both the possibilities and the fragility of Hindu–Muslim political unity.
