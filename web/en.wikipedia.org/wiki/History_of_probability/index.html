History of probability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Probability theory was born from correspondence about gambling problems in 1654.

The history of probability is the development of the mathematical treatment of uncertainty, from ancient games of chance and medieval insurance to the correspondence of Pascal and Fermat in 1654, the classical theory of Laplace, the statistical mechanics and inferential statistics of the 19th and early 20th centuries, and Kolmogorov's axiomatisation of 1933.

Origins

Although dice and astragali are ancient, and Gerolamo Cardano wrote on games of chance in his Liber de Ludo Aleae (written c. 1564, published 1663), a mathematical theory began only when the Chevalier de Méré posed the problem of points to Blaise Pascal, whose letters with Pierre de Fermat in 1654 introduced expected value and combinatorial methods. Christiaan Huygens published the first book on the subject, De Ratiociniis in Ludo Aleae (1657). Jacob Bernoulli's posthumous Ars Conjectandi (1713) gave the first law of large numbers, and Abraham de Moivre's Doctrine of Chances (1718) introduced the normal approximation.

Classical and statistical developments

Thomas Bayes's essay (published 1763) founded inverse probability; Pierre-Simon Laplace's Théorie analytique des probabilités (1812) crowned the classical theory, applying it to astronomy, demography and jurisprudence. In the 19th century probability underpinned statistical mechanics (Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs) and the biometric school of Galton, Pearson and Fisher, which created modern statistics, while actuaries and insurers had long used mortality tables in practice.

Modern foundations

Twentieth-century debates between frequentist (von Mises, Neyman), Bayesian, and logical (Keynes, Ramsey) interpretations accompanied formalisation: Émile Borel and measure theory led to Andrey Kolmogorov's Foundations of the Theory of Probability (1933), which made probability a branch of measure theory. Kolmogorov and Khinchin developed stochastic processes, also central to Wiener's Brownian motion and Itô calculus, later indispensable in mathematical finance.
