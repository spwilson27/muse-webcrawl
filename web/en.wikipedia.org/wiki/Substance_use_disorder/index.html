A substance use disorder (SUD) is a condition in which the use of one or more substances — such as alcohol, opioids, stimulants, cannabis or tobacco — leads to clinically significant impairment or distress, characterised by impaired control, social problems, risky use, tolerance and withdrawal. The DSM-5 grades severity by the number of criteria met (mild, moderate, severe); severe SUD roughly corresponds to the older concept of addiction. Globally, alcohol use disorders are the most common, and drug use disorders affect an estimated 39–64 million people by conservative counts, with far higher numbers using drugs problematically.

Causes and mechanisms

SUD arises from interacting genetic vulnerability (heritability around 40–60%), developmental and environmental factors (adversity, peer use, availability), and the pharmacology of the substance. Repeated use produces neuroadaptations in reward, stress and executive-control circuits: dopamine-mediated incentive salience makes drug cues compulsive triggers, while tolerance and withdrawal drive negative reinforcement. Comorbidity with depression, anxiety, PTSD and other mental disorders is common ("dual diagnosis").

Treatment

Effective treatments include motivational interviewing, cognitive-behavioural therapy, contingency management and mutual-help groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous. Medications are first-line for several disorders: methadone, buprenorphine and naltrexone for opioid use disorder; naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram for alcohol use disorder; and varenicline and nicotine replacement for tobacco. Harm-reduction measures — naloxone distribution, syringe services, supervised consumption — reduce deaths and disease without requiring abstinence. Untreated SUD causes major morbidity, including alcoholic liver disease and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.
