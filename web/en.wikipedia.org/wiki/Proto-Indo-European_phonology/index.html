The phonology of Proto-Indo-European (PIE), the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European languages, has been inferred by the comparative method from its daughter languages. Although no writing of PIE survives, its sound system can be reconstructed in considerable detail because sound changes in the daughter languages are largely regular.

Consonants

Traditional reconstructions give three series of stops at five places of articulation: voiceless (*p *t *ḱ *k *kʷ), voiced (*b *d *ǵ *g *gʷ) and voiced aspirated (*bʰ *dʰ *ǵʰ *gʰ *gʷʰ). The stop *b is conspicuously rare, and the glottalic theory reinterprets the traditional voiced series as ejectives. Other consonants include *s, the nasals *m and *n, liquids *l and *r, the glide *y (and *w), and three "laryngeals" *h₁, *h₂, *h₃ — consonants of uncertain articulation, proposed by Ferdinand de Saussure in 1879 and partly confirmed by Hittite ḫ.

Vowels and resonants

PIE had the vowels *e and *o, which frequently alternated under ablaut (e-grade, o-grade, zero-grade), plus long vowels largely arising from vowel + laryngeal sequences. Resonants *m̥ *n̥ *l̥ *r̥ *i *u could function as syllable nuclei in the zero grade, a feature reflected in forms such as Sanskrit syllabic ṛ.

Accent and syllable structure

PIE had a mobile pitch accent: one syllable of each word bore a high tone, and accent position could distinguish grammatical forms, as preserved most faithfully in Vedic Sanskrit and Ancient Greek. Roots were typically of the shape (s)CeC, and syllabification followed sonority principles; sequences of consonants were simplified by rules such as the loss of laryngeals between consonants.

Sound changes in daughter languages

Characteristic developments include Grimm's law in Germanic (voiceless stops becoming fricatives, etc.), the centum–satem split (palatovelars merging with plain velars in centum languages, or assibilating in satem languages), Grassmann's law in Greek and Indic, and the vocalization and loss of laryngeals almost everywhere except Anatolian.
