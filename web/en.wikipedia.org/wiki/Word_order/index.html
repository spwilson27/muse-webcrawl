In linguistics, word order is the order of syntactic constituents in a language. The most commonly studied pattern is the relative order of the subject (S), verb (V) and object (O) in a declarative clause, which yields six logically possible basic orders. Word order typology, pioneered by Joseph Greenberg in 1963, correlates constituent order with many other features of grammar, such as the placement of adpositions, adjectives and relative clauses.

The six possible orders of subject, verb and object

Basic orders

Subject–object–verb (SOV) is the most frequent order worldwide, found in languages such as Japanese, Turkish, Hindi and Basque. Subject–verb–object (SVO), the order of English, Mandarin and most European languages, is a close second; together the two account for the large majority of languages. Verb-initial orders (VSO and VOS) occur in languages such as Irish, Classical Arabic, Tagalog and Malagasy, while object-initial orders (OVS and OSV) are rare, found mainly in some Amazonian languages such as Hixkaryana.

Correlations and flexibility

Greenberg's universals showed that word order correlates with other features: verb–object languages tend to use prepositions and place relative clauses after nouns, while object–verb languages tend to use postpositions. Some languages, such as Latin, Russian or Warlpiri, have flexible word order because grammatical relations are marked by case inflection instead; in such languages ordering often encodes information structure — topic and focus — rather than basic grammatical roles.
