Microglia

Microglia are a type of glial cell located throughout the brain and spinal cord. They are the resident macrophage cells of the central nervous system and act as its first and main line of immune defence, constantly surveying their surroundings for damaged neurons, plaques, infectious agents, and cellular debris, which they remove by phagocytosis. Microglia account for roughly 10 percent of the cells in the brain. They were first described by Pío del Río Hortega in the early twentieth century.

Development and identity

Unlike neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes, which arise from the neuroectoderm, microglia derive from primitive macrophages of the yolk sac that enter the brain early in development and thereafter maintain themselves by self-renewal. This distinct origin separates true microglia from other brain macrophages and from monocytes that may enter the brain during disease.

Functions

In the healthy brain, microglia are far from idle. Their fine processes continually extend and retract, sampling the extracellular space. They prune excess synapses during development — helping sculpt neural circuits — clear away apoptotic cells, and support neurons with growth factors. Upon detecting injury or pathogens, microglia change shape, proliferate, migrate to the site, release cytokines and other signals, and phagocytose debris; this "activated" state may be protective or, if prolonged, damaging.

Role in disease

Microglial dysfunction is implicated in many neurological conditions. Genetic studies of Alzheimer's disease have identified numerous risk variants in genes expressed mainly in microglia, such as TREM2. Microglia are also studied in Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke, chronic pain, and psychiatric conditions including schizophrenia and depression, making them an active target of drug development.
