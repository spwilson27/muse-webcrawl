The Great Storm of 1987 was a violent extratropical cyclone that devastated parts of the United Kingdom and northern France on the night of 15–16 October 1987. A deep depression formed in the Bay of Biscay and underwent explosive deepening as it crossed the English Channel, bringing hurricane-force gusts to southern England — the strongest storm to hit the region since the Great Storm of 1703.

Impact

Winds gusted to over 100 mph across a wide area, peaking around 115 mph in England and over 120 mph on the Brittany coast. An estimated 15 million trees were blown down in England, including six of the famous Seven Oaks at Sevenoaks; roads and railways were blocked, 18 people were killed in Britain and several more in France, and buildings suffered widespread damage — most famously at Shanklin on the Isle of Wight. Electricity supplies were cut to hundreds of thousands of homes, some for more than a week. The storm also contributed to chaos in financial markets on 16 October, the Friday before the Black Monday stock-market crash.

The forecast controversy

The storm became notorious in Britain for the BBC weather forecast by Michael Fish, who hours before had told viewers that a woman had rung in about a hurricane, and that there was "no hurricane" on the way — though he had also warned of very windy weather, and his "hurricane" remark referred (as he later explained) to a system in Florida. A subsequent inquiry led to reforms in Met Office storm forecasting, including better use of ship, buoy and satellite observations; analysis has since shown the storm was a rare sting-jet event, with a distinctive descending jet of damaging air.
