Information economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Asymmetric information — as in Akerlof's "market for lemons" — is the field's founding problem.

Information economics or the economics of information is the branch of microeconomic theory that studies how information and information systems affect an economy and economic decisions. Information has special characteristics: it is easy to create but hard to trust, easy to spread but hard to control. Unlike a material good, one person's use of information need not deplete it, and its value often depends on context and exclusivity.

Asymmetric information

The field's core insight is that markets malfunction when one side knows more than the other. George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for Lemons" showed how uncertainty about used-car quality can unravel a market entirely. Two canonical pathologies follow: adverse selection (hidden information before a transaction — bad risks drive out good ones, as in insurance) and moral hazard (hidden actions after a transaction — insured parties take greater risks).

Responses

Market participants respond with signalling (Spence's model of education as a costly signal of ability), screening (menus of contracts that induce self-selection), warranties, reputations, and regulation such as mandatory disclosure. Contract theory and mechanism design — recognised by the 2007 and 2016 Nobel Prizes — formalise the design of transactions under private information.

Information goods

A second strand studies goods that are costly to produce but cheap to reproduce — software, media, data — and the pricing strategies (versioning, bundling) they demand, popularised by Shapiro and Varian's Information Rules (1999).
