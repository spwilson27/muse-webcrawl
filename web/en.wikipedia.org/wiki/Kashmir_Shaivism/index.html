Kashmir Shaivism

Kashmir Shaivism is a tradition of nondual Hindu philosophy and practice that flourished in Kashmir between roughly the 9th and 11th centuries CE. Rooted in the Shaiva Agamas, it teaches that all reality is the self-expression of a single universal consciousness identified with Shiva, and that liberation consists in recognising (pratyabhijñā) one's identity with that consciousness. Its best-known school is the Trika system.

History

The tradition traces its revealed texts to the Shiva Sutras, said to have been revealed to the sage Vasugupta in the 8th or 9th century. Somānanda systematised the doctrine in the Śivadr̥ṣṭi, Utpaladeva developed the "Recognition" (Pratyabhijñā) philosophy, and the polymath Abhinavagupta (c. 950–1016) produced its grand synthesis in works such as the Tantrāloka and Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī. His disciple Kṣemarāja wrote the influential Pratyabhijñāhr̥dayam. The tradition survived among Kashmiri Pandit lineages and attracted wide modern interest in the 20th century.

Philosophy

Kashmir Shaivism is an idealist monism: the universe is not an illusion separate from God (as in Advaita Vedanta's strict reading) but a real manifestation of consciousness's own dynamism (spanda, "vibration"). Shiva, endowed with śakti, freely projects the world through a process described by the 36 tattvas, from pure consciousness down to earth. The individual soul is Shiva contracted by three impurities (malas) — the sense of separateness, of difference, and of limited agency. Liberation is achieved not by acquiring something new but by recognising what one already is, aided by grace (śaktipāta), a guru, and practices drawn from the allied Kaula and Krama streams, including mantra, ritual and the aesthetic contemplation Abhinavagupta also applied in his celebrated theory of rasa.
