The Marker Wadden is an artificial archipelago in the Markermeer, a lake in the central Netherlands, built to restore the deteriorating ecology of the Markermeer and to create new habitat for fish and birds. The project is one of the largest nature-restoration projects in Western Europe. The islands are constructed from sand, clay and silt dredged from the lake bed itself.

Background

The Markermeer was separated from the IJsselmeer by the completion of the Houtribdijk in 1975. Cut off from currents and sediment supply, the lake became increasingly turbid with fine silt, smothering the lake bed and sharply reducing populations of fish and waterbirds. The Marker Wadden project was initiated by the Dutch nature-preservation organisation Natuurmonumenten, funded in part by a €15 million grant from the National Postcode Lottery's Dream Fund, alongside government and private contributions.

Construction and design

Construction began in 2016, carried out by Boskalis. The first phase created a cluster of islands with sheltered lagoons, marshes, mudflats, reed beds and sandy beaches, using ring dikes of sand filled with silt from the lake. The main island, with a small harbour, visitor facilities, walking trails and bird hides, opened to the public in September 2018. Further islands are planned, with the full design envisaging around 1,000 hectares of new land.

Ecology

The shallow, sheltered waters and varied habitats have rapidly attracted breeding and migrating birds, including avocets, spoonbills, terns and plovers, as well as improving spawning and nursery conditions for fish. The islands act as a silt trap, gradually improving water clarity in the wider Markermeer. The Marker Wadden forms part of the Nieuw Land National Park, established in 2018.
