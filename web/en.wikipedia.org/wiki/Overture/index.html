Overture

An overture (from French ouverture, "opening") is an instrumental composition that serves as an introduction to an opera, oratorio, ballet, play or other larger work. The term is also used for independent orchestral pieces in similar style, known as concert overtures. The overture sets the mood for the performance, often previewing musical themes that will appear in the work itself, and historically served the practical purpose of quieting the audience and signalling that the performance was about to begin.

History

Baroque origins

The operatic overture emerged in the 17th century. In France, Jean-Baptiste Lully established the French overture: a majestic slow opening in dotted rhythms, followed by a fast fugal section, sometimes with a return of the slow music. This pattern was widely imitated across Europe and also detached itself from opera, becoming the opening movement of orchestral suites, as in Handel's Music for the Royal Fireworks. In Italy, Alessandro Scarlatti popularised the Italian overture or sinfonia: three short movements, fast–slow–fast, a scheme that fed directly into the early symphony.

Classical era

Reformers such as Christoph Willibald Gluck argued that the overture should prepare the audience for the drama to come rather than serve as unrelated entertainment. Mozart's overtures — to The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni and The Magic Flute — became models of economy and dramatic aptness, typically in sonata form, often written at the last moment before the premiere.

Romantic era

Romantic composers deepened the overture's connection to the drama. Weber wove themes from the opera into the overture to Der Freischütz; Beethoven wrote four overtures for his single opera Fidelio (the three Leonore overtures and the final, lighter Fidelio overture), the Leonore No. 3 being so dramatically complete that it is often played as a concert piece — sometimes, controversially, within the opera itself. Rossini's brilliant, crescendo-driven overtures to The Barber of Seville and William Tell became concert staples in their own right, though Rossini cheerfully recycled material between operas. Wagner replaced the detachable overture with the Vorspiel (prelude), an introduction woven continuously into the first scene, as in Lohengrin and Tristan und Isolde.

Concert overture

Detached from any stage work, the concert overture is a one-movement programmatic orchestral piece, usually in sonata form. Mendelssohn pioneered the genre with his overture to A Midsummer Night's Dream (1826) and The Hebrides ("Fingal's Cave"). Notable examples include Berlioz's Roman Carnival, Brahms's Academic Festival and Tragic overtures, Tchaikovsky's Romeo and Juliet and the 1812 Overture — with its famous cannon fire — and Dvořák's Carnival overture.

Musical theatre and film

The overture survives in Broadway and West End musicals as an instrumental medley of the show's songs played before the curtain rises, in operetta, and vestigially in the main-title sequences of films. In modern operatic practice full overtures are less common; many contemporary productions follow Wagner's lead with brief preludes or begin the drama immediately.
