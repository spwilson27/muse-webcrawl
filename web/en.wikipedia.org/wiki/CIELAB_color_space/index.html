CIELAB color space

The CIELAB color space, also referred to as L*a*b*, is a color space defined by the International Commission on Illumination (abbreviated CIE) in 1976. It expresses color as three values: L* for perceptual lightness, and a* and b* for the four unique colors of human vision — red, green, blue, and yellow. CIELAB was intended as a perceptually uniform space, where a given numerical change corresponds to a similar perceived change in color.

Coordinates

The three coordinates of CIELAB represent the lightness of the color (L* = 0 yields black and L* = 100 indicates diffuse white; specular white may be higher), its position between red and green (a*, negative values indicate green while positive values indicate red), and its position between yellow and blue (b*, negative values indicate blue and positive values indicate yellow). The asterisk (*) after L, a and b are pronounced star and are part of the full name to distinguish L*a*b* from Hunter's Lab, described below.

CIELAB values are computed from CIE XYZ tristimulus values relative to a reference white point using a cube-root nonlinearity that mimics the eye's response. Color differences are measured with the Delta E formulas, from the simple Euclidean ΔE*ab (1976) to the more perceptually refined CIEDE2000.

Uses

CIELAB is ubiquitous in color management: it serves as the profile connection space in ICC profiles, and is used throughout printing, textile dyeing, paint formulation, plastics, and food quality inspection wherever device-independent color specification and tolerancing are required. Software such as Adobe Photoshop offers a Lab mode for image editing.

Related spaces

The CIELUV space, also defined in 1976, is its sister space for additive mixtures and self-luminous displays. Richard Hunter's earlier Hunter Lab (1948) uses square-root instead of cube-root functions. The newer Oklab space (2020) was designed to improve on CIELAB's uniformity, especially in the blue region.
