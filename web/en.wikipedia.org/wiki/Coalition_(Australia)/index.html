Coalition (Australia)

The Coalition is a political alliance of centre-right political parties in Australia, chiefly the Liberal Party of Australia and the National Party of Australia (commonly simply "the Nationals"). The two parties cooperate in a formal coalition in federal politics and in most states, presenting a united front against the Australian Labor Party, and are often described collectively as the Liberal–National Coalition.

Structure

Under the coalition arrangement, the leader of the Liberal Party serves as leader of the Coalition (and, in government, as prime minister), while the leader of the Nationals becomes deputy leader (and deputy prime minister in government). Cabinet positions in coalition governments are allocated between the parties roughly in proportion to their parliamentary numbers, and the Nationals traditionally hold portfolios such as trade, transport, and agriculture.

History

The alliance traces its origin to 1923, when Stanley Bruce's Nationalist Party formed government with Earle Page's Country Party after an election fought partly over opposition to the Labor Party. Following the formation of the modern Liberal Party by Robert Menzies in 1944, the Liberals and the Country Party (renamed the National Party in 1982) governed Australia for most of the period from 1949 to 1972 and again from 1975 to 1983, 1996 to 2007, and 2013 to 2022.

State level

Coalition arrangements vary by state. In Queensland the two parties merged in 2008 to form the Liberal National Party, and in the Northern Territory the Country Liberal Party represents both. In Western Australia the parties have at times contested elections separately and sat apart, while in New South Wales and Victoria the coalition has been the standard conservative alliance.
