Renal calycies

The renal calycies (singular calyx) are cup-shaped chambers of the kidney through which urine passes on its way from the renal papillae to the renal pelvis and ureter. The calyceal system is part of the urinary collecting system and is visible on imaging studies such as intravenous pyelograms and CT urograms.

Structure

Minor calyces surround the apex of each renal pyramid (papilla); urine released at the area cribrosa enters the minor calyx directly. Two or three minor calyces converge to form a major calyx, and two or three major calyces converge to form the renal pelvis, the funnel-shaped proximal end of the ureter. The number of calyces varies, typically around seven to fourteen minor calyces per kidney.

Function

Peristaltic activity of the smooth muscle in the calyceal walls — paced by atypical smooth muscle cells near the papilla — helps propel urine from the papilla toward the pelvis and ureter. The calyces themselves store no urine; they are conduits.

Clinical significance

Dilation of the calyces (calyceal dilatation or hydronephrosis when the pelvis is also involved) can result from obstruction by kidney stones, tumors, or congenital narrowing. Stones may form within a calyx, and a calyceal diverticulum can harbor stagnant urine prone to infection or stone formation.
