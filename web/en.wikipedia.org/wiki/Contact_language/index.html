Contact language

A contact language is a language variety that arises from sustained contact between speakers of different languages, typically where groups without a shared language need to communicate for trade, work, or administration. The term is used especially for pidgins and creoles, but it can also describe mixed languages and other varieties shaped by intense language contact.

TypesPidgins, creoles, mixed languages, lingua francas
Typical settingsTrade, colonisation, slavery, migration
FieldContact linguistics

Formation

When speakers of mutually unintelligible languages interact regularly, they may develop a simplified auxiliary language—a pidgin—drawing most of its vocabulary from a dominant "lexifier" language and simplified grammar influenced by the speakers' native languages. If children grow up speaking a pidgin as their first language, it expands into a full creole. Some contact languages, such as Michif (French and Cree) or Media Lengua (Spanish and Quechua), combine components of two languages in a stable, systematic way and are classed as mixed languages.

Examples

Historical and contemporary contact languages include Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, Haitian Creole, Nigerian Pidgin, Chinook Jargon of the Pacific Northwest, and the Mediterranean Lingua Franca. Their study informs debates about language universals, second-language acquisition, and the social history of colonisation and trade.
