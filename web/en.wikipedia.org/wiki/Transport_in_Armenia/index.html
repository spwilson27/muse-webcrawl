Transport in Armenia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transport in Armenia is shaped by the country's landlocked position in the mountainous South Caucasus and by the closure of its borders with Turkey and Azerbaijan since the early 1990s. Trade and travel therefore route through Georgia to the north and Iran to the south. Roads carry the overwhelming majority of passenger and freight traffic.

Roads

Armenia has roughly 7,700 km of paved public roads. The main north–south artery runs from the Georgian border through Vanadzor and Yerevan to Meghri on the Iranian border; the North–South Road Corridor investment program has been upgrading this route to expressway standard in sections. Marshrutkas (minibuses) and buses provide intercity public transport, and private cars account for a growing share of travel. Road conditions vary, with mountain passes subject to winter closures.

Railways

The Armenian railway network totals about 850 km of broad-gauge (1,520 mm) electrified line. Since 2008 it has been operated by the South Caucasus Railway, a subsidiary of Russian Railways, under a long-term concession. The main line connects Yerevan with Gyumri and the Georgian border at Ayrum, continuing to Tbilisi and the Black Sea ports. Lines toward Azerbaijan and Turkey (through Nakhchivan and Kars) have been closed since the early 1990s. Domestic passenger services are limited, with a popular seasonal train to Lake Sevan and international services to Tbilisi and (seasonally) Batumi. The Yerevan Metro, opened in 1981, has a single line of about 12 km and ten stations.

Air

Zvartnots International Airport near Yerevan handles nearly all international air traffic, serving several million passengers a year, with routes concentrated on Russia, the European Union, and the Middle East. Shirak Airport at Gyumri provides a secondary facility. Armenia liberalized its aviation market in 2013 ("open skies"), and low-cost carriers have since expanded service.

Other

As a landlocked country Armenia has no seaports; its trade moves through the Georgian ports of Poti and Batumi. Pipelines import natural gas from Russia via Georgia and from Iran. Aerial tramways include the Wings of Tatev, opened in 2010 as the world's longest reversible aerial tramway, serving Tatev Monastery. Border crossings with Georgia (Bagratashen, Bavra, Gogavan) and Iran (Agarak/Norduz) are the country's lifelines, and any regional settlement reopening east–west routes is expected to transform Armenian transport planning.
