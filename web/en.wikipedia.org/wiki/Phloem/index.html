Phloem (from Greek phloios, "bark") is the living vascular tissue in plants that transports soluble organic compounds — principally the sugars made in photosynthesis — from the leaves and other source tissues to the parts of the plant that need them, a process called translocation. Together with the xylem, which carries water and minerals upward from the roots, phloem forms the plant's vascular system.

Structure

Phloem consists of sieve elements (sieve tube members in flowering plants, connected end-to-end into sieve tubes, and sieve cells in gymnosperms), companion cells that load sugars and sustain the enucleate sieve elements, phloem parenchyma, and fibres. Sieve tube sap is rich in sucrose — up to 20–30% by weight — and also carries amino acids, hormones, RNA and other signalling molecules throughout the plant.

Mechanism

Translocation is explained by the pressure-flow (mass flow) hypothesis proposed by Ernst Münch in 1930: active loading of sugar into the phloem at a source draws in water by osmosis, generating high turgor pressure that pushes sap toward a sink (root, fruit, growing tip), where sugar is unloaded and pressure falls. Flow in different sieve tubes can run in opposite directions at the same time. Aphids, which tap directly into sieve tubes, have been used both by researchers to sample phloem sap and, unfortunately for farmers, as vectors of plant viruses that travel in the phloem.

In woody plants the secondary phloem forms the inner layer of the bark; removing a complete ring of it ("girdling" or ring-barking) starves the roots and kills the tree.
