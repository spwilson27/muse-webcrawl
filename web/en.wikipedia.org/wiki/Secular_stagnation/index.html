Secular stagnation

Placeholder image: long-run decline in real interest rates in advanced economies.

Secular stagnation is a concept in economics describing a condition in which an economy experiences little or no growth over an extended period, typically accompanied by chronically low interest rates, persistent excess saving over investment, weak demand, and below-target inflation. In such a condition, a market-driven economy may not return to full employment through interest rate cuts alone, because the neutral real interest rate needed to balance saving and investment may be negative.

Origins of the term

The term was coined by the economist Alvin Hansen in the late 1930s, when he worried that the United States, facing slowing population growth and the closing of the frontier, would suffer a long period of inadequate investment demand after the Great Depression. The idea fell out of fashion during the postwar boom but was revived by former U.S. Treasury Secretary Lawrence Summers in a widely discussed 2013 speech at the International Monetary Fund, in which he argued that advanced economies might have entered a new era of persistently deficient demand.

Proposed causes

Explanations commonly offered for secular stagnation include slowing population growth and aging populations, rising income inequality (which raises aggregate saving because the wealthy save a larger share of income), declining prices of capital goods, increased demand for safe assets, deleveraging after financial crises, and a global "saving glut." Weak demand in turn discourages investment, which lowers productivity growth in a self-reinforcing process sometimes called hysteresis.

Policy implications

If the natural rate of interest is very low or negative, conventional monetary policy may be unable to stimulate the economy, since nominal interest rates cannot fall far below zero. Summers and others therefore argued for greater reliance on fiscal policy, especially public investment in infrastructure, and on structural reforms to raise demand. Critics countered that slow growth reflected supply-side problems such as weak productivity growth and mismeasurement, or that low rates were a temporary consequence of the financial crisis rather than a permanent condition. The surge of inflation after the COVID-19 pandemic led some economists to declare the secular stagnation era over, while others argued the underlying forces would reassert themselves.
