Hydroelectricity in Norway supplies the overwhelming majority of the country's electricity: Norway is the world's seventh-largest producer of hydropower and the largest in Europe, generating roughly nine-tenths of its electricity from water power. Steep terrain, high precipitation and glacial meltwater, combined with large seasonal reservoirs, make Norway's power system almost entirely renewable and give it a central balancing role in the northern European grid.

History

Norway's first hydroelectric plants were built in the 1880s and 1890s to power sawmills, mines and early industry; the Hammeren plant in Oslo (1900) was among the first municipally owned stations. The founding of Norsk Hydro in 1905, using waterfalls such as Rjukanfossen to fix nitrogen for fertiliser, made hydropower the engine of Norwegian industrialisation and prompted the "concession laws" — the so-called panic laws of 1906–1909 — which reserved control of waterfalls to the Norwegian state and required installations to revert to public ownership. Major state-led expansion followed the Second World War, and large regulated river systems were developed through the 1950s–1980s.

Production and storage

Norway has roughly 34 GW of installed hydropower capacity spread over more than 1,700 plants, from micro-hydro schemes to the 1,240 MW Kvilldal station in Rogaland. Annual hydro generation averages around 130–140 TWh out of a total national production of roughly 155 TWh. Crucially, Norwegian reservoirs can store about 87 TWh — close to 70% of annual consumption and about half of all reservoir capacity in Europe — allowing water to be held in wet seasons and released in dry winters, and making Norway a natural "battery" for wind and solar power in neighbouring countries via interconnectors to Sweden, Denmark, the Netherlands, Germany and the United Kingdom (the North Sea Link, opened 2021).

Environmental debate

Although hydropower has low carbon emissions, damming Norwegian rivers has long been controversial. The Alta controversy of 1979–1981, in which Sámi and environmental campaigners protested the Alta–Kautokeino dam, became a landmark in Norwegian environmental and Indigenous politics. Since the 1980s a series of master plans and protection plans (Verneplan) have permanently protected many rivers, and most new development consists of small-scale plants and upgrades of existing stations.

Role today

Because reservoir hydro can be dispatched in seconds, Norway trades power actively on the Nord Pool market, exporting in wet years and importing in dry ones. Hydropower also underpins energy-intensive industries — aluminium smelters, silicon and ferroalloy plants — sited near fjord-side power stations. Wind power has grown to supply most of the remaining generation, with small contributions from thermal and solar sources.
