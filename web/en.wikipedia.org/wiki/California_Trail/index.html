The California Trail was an emigrant trail of about 3,000 miles (4,800 km) across the western half of North America from Missouri River towns to what is now the state of California. After it was established, the first half of the California Trail followed the same corridor of networked river valley trails as the Oregon Trail and the Mormon Trail, namely the valleys of the Platte, North Platte and Sweetwater rivers in present-day Nebraska, Wyoming and Utah region before diverging in western Wyoming and crossing the Great Basin and the Sierra Nevada.

History

The trail was pioneered in stages by fur traders and early emigrant parties, including the Bidwell–Bartleson Party of 1841. It shared its eastern portion with the Oregon Trail as far as Fort Hall (in modern Idaho) or the Raft River, where California-bound travellers turned southwest to the Humboldt River, followed it across present-day Nevada, and crossed the Sierra Nevada by passes such as Donner Pass and Carson Pass. Use exploded during the California Gold Rush after 1848; an estimated 200,000–250,000 emigrants and gold seekers used the trail before the completion of the first transcontinental railroad in 1869 made the wagon journey obsolete.

Notable episodes

The most famous episode on the trail was the ordeal of the Donner Party in the winter of 1846–47, trapped by snow near Truckee (Donner) Lake and reduced to cannibalism. Other landmarks and hazards included the crossing of the Forty Mile Desert, cholera on the Platte road, and conflicts with Native American peoples along the Humboldt.
