Duck and cover

"Duck and cover" is a method of personal protection against the effects of a nuclear explosion that was taught to generations of United States school children during the Cold War. On seeing the flash of a detonation, or on hearing an alert, people were instructed to drop to the ground, crouch under a desk or other cover, protect the head and neck, and stay down until the blast wave had passed.

The film

The phrase is inseparable from the 1951 civil defense film Duck and Cover, produced by the Federal Civil Defense Administration and shown in schools across the country. The film mixed live action with animation featuring Bert the Turtle, who survived by retreating into his shell, accompanied by the song "Duck and Cover." Millions of children rehearsed the drills in school corridors and classrooms.

Effectiveness and criticism

Ducking and covering can genuinely reduce injury from flying glass, debris, thermal flash, and the blast wave at distances where survival is plausible, and remains part of modern guidance for those outside the zone of total destruction. However, the drills were widely satirised, especially from the 1960s onward, as a futile or propagandistic ritual in the age of thermonuclear weapons and megaton yields, symbolising official underestimation of nuclear war. The practice receded with the end of the Cold War, though "drop, cover, and hold on" survives in earthquake preparedness.
