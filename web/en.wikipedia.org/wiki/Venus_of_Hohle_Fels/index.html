The Venus of Hohle Fels (also known as the Venus of Schelklingen) is an Upper Palaeolithic Venus figurine made of mammoth ivory that was unearthed in 2008 in the Hohle Fels cave near Schelklingen in the Swabian Jura of southwestern Germany. It is dated to between 40,000 and 35,000 years ago, belonging to the early Aurignacian culture, and is among the oldest known examples of figurative art and the oldest known depiction of a human being.

Discovery

The figurine was found in September 2008 by a team from the University of Tübingen led by archaeologist Nicholas Conard, in six fragments a few metres from the cave entrance at the base of the Aurignacian deposits. Its discovery was announced in the journal Nature in May 2009. The same cave system has produced other very early art and music, including bone and ivory flutes considered among the oldest musical instruments, and the nearby caves yielded the Lion-man figurine.

Description

The figurine is about 6 cm (2.4 in) tall and carved from a single piece of mammoth ivory. It depicts a female figure with greatly exaggerated sexual characteristics: very large breasts, belly and vulva, with small arms and legs. In place of a head there is a perforated knob, suggesting the figure was worn as a pendant. Traces of red ochre were reported on the surface.

Interpretation

Like other Venus figurines, its meaning is debated: interpretations include a fertility symbol, a depiction of womanhood or sexuality, or a self-representation by a woman. Its age pushes the origin of figurative sculpture back to the very beginning of the Aurignacian, shortly after modern humans arrived in Europe.
