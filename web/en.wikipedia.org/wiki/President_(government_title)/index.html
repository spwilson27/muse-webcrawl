President (government title)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A president is a common title for the head of state in most republics. The title can also be held by the leader of a political party or other organisation, a corporation or a university. In government, presidents range from powerful chief executives (presidential and semi-presidential systems) to ceremonial figureheads in parliamentary republics (Germany, India, Ireland, Italy), where their functions — appointing governments, dissolving parliaments, signing laws, representing the state — are mostly exercised on advice, with reserve powers held for crises.

Selection and terms

Presidents are chosen by direct popular election (often two-round), by electoral colleges (the United States; India; Germany's Federal Convention), or by parliament (South Africa, Israel), for terms typically of four to seven years, usually with a two-term limit where the office is powerful (term limits have themselves become battlegrounds, with several countries amending them amid controversy). Acting, interim and collective presidencies handle vacancies and special cases (Switzerland's Federal Council presidency rotates annually among seven members).

History

The governmental title was established by the United States (1789), whose presidency became the model republics copied — with the modern strong presidency reshaped by war, mass media and party polarisation into what critics and admirers alike call the "imperial" or administrative presidency, checked by legislatures, courts and federalism in varying degrees.
