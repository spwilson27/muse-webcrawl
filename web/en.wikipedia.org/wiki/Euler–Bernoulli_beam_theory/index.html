Euler–Bernoulli beam theory

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (also known as engineer's beam theory or classical beam theory) is a simplification of the linear theory of elasticity which provides a means of calculating the load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams. It covers the bending of beams that are long compared with their cross-sectional dimensions, under loads applied laterally to the beam's axis.

Assumptions and equation

The theory assumes that cross-sections of the beam, initially plane and perpendicular to the beam's neutral axis, remain plane and perpendicular after bending; shear deformation and rotary inertia are neglected. Combining this kinematic assumption with Hooke's law gives the central relation between bending moment M and curvature, and differentiating with respect to the position x along the beam yields the fourth-order governing equation for the deflection w(x):

d²/dx² ( EI d²w/dx² ) = q(x),

where E is Young's modulus, I is the second moment of area of the cross-section, and q is the distributed load. For a uniform beam this reduces to EI w'''' = q. Standard solutions give, for example, the tip deflection of a cantilever under an end load P: δ = PL³/(3EI).

History and limitations

The theory grew out of the work of Jacob Bernoulli and Daniel Bernoulli and was given its classical form through the investigations of Leonhard Euler in the 18th century; Navier later placed it on a firm basis within elasticity theory. It is accurate for slender beams (length-to-depth ratios above roughly 10) but underestimates deflections of short, deep beams, for which the Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beam theory, which includes shear deformation, is preferred.
