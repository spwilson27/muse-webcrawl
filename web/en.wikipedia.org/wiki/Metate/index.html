Metate

A metate (from Nahuatl metlatl) or mealing stone is a type of ground stone tool used for grinding grain and seeds, most characteristically in Mesoamerica and the American Southwest. It consists of a large, flat or trough-shaped stone slab on which material is ground with a smaller hand-held stone called a mano, pushed back and forth across the surface. The word is used in Spanish and in English-language archaeology of the Americas.


A stone metate with its mano, used for grinding maize

History and use

Metates have been used in Mesoamerica for thousands of years, principally to grind nixtamalised maize into masa dough for tortillas and tamales, but also for cacao, chillies, and other foods. Traditional metates are carved from a single piece of volcanic stone such as basalt, often raised on three short legs. In many households the metate was — and in rural areas remains — a central tool of daily food preparation, and ethnographers have documented the considerable time and labour involved in grinding maize by hand.

Archaeological significance

Because they are durable and abundant, metates are important artefacts for archaeologists studying ancient diet, household organisation, and trade in stone raw materials. Elaborately carved metates from Costa Rica's Greater Nicoya and Atlantic Watershed regions — some with sculpted animal effigies and intricate openwork — are regarded as prestige objects and are prominent in museum collections, suggesting ceremonial as well as practical functions.

Related tools

The metate is functionally related to the quern of the Old World and to the molcajete, a smaller three-legged mortar used for grinding spices and making salsas. The Aztec and Maya used metates extensively; the Maya term is ka' in some Mayan languages.
