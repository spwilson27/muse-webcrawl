Geodesic dome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geodesic dome
Type: Shell structure / space frame
Based on: Icosahedral geometry
Popularised by: R. Buckminster Fuller
Famous examples: Montreal Biosphere; Spaceship Earth, Epcot

A geodesic dome is a hemispherical or partial-spherical thin-shell structure (a lattice shell) based on a geodesic polyhedron: a network of triangles whose edges approximate great circles (geodesics) on a sphere. The triangles form a rigid, self-bracing framework that distributes structural stress across the whole surface, yielding a structure that is strong in proportion to its weight and encloses the maximum volume for a given surface area.

History

The first geodesic dome was built after World War I by Walther Bauersfeld, chief engineer of the Carl Zeiss optical company, as a planetarium in Jena, Germany. The form was independently reinvented and popularised by the American designer R. Buckminster Fuller, who coined the term "geodesic", received a US patent in 1954, and promoted domes as efficient housing and shelter. Thousands were built: military radomes along the DEW Line, the gold dome of the American exhibition in Moscow (1959), Fuller's United States Pavilion at Expo 67 in Montreal (now the Biosphere), and the giant Spaceship Earth attraction at Epcot (1982), a complete geodesic sphere. The Eden Project in Cornwall uses geodesic biomes.

Design and properties

A dome's geometry is specified by its frequency — the number of subdivisions of the base icosahedron's edges: higher frequencies approach a sphere more closely but require more distinct strut lengths. Domes are efficient to heat and cool because of their low surface-to-volume ratio and lack of stagnant corners in air circulation, and their members work mainly in tension and compression. Practical drawbacks include awkward interior space near the walls, difficulty sealing the many joints against water, limited standard door and window fittings, and — for homes — problems with building codes, financing, and resale.

Related forms

Fuller's geometry also inspired the naming of the carbon molecules called fullerenes (such as buckminsterfullerene, C60), whose structure of pentagons and hexagons resembles a geodesic sphere, and geodesic principles appear in virus capsid geometry and in the car and aircraft design principle of tensegrity.
