Substitution effect

The substitution effect is the change in the quantity demanded of a good that results from a change in its relative price, holding the consumer's utility (in the Hicksian formulation) or purchasing power (in the Slutsky formulation) constant. When the price of a good falls, consumers tend to substitute toward that good and away from relatively more expensive alternatives; when it rises, they substitute away from it.

Decomposition of a price change

In consumer theory, the total effect of a price change on demand is decomposed into the substitution effect and the income effect. The substitution effect always moves demand in the opposite direction to the price change for the good itself: a price fall, by itself, increases quantity demanded. The income effect, by contrast, may reinforce or oppose it depending on whether the good is normal or inferior. For a Giffen good, a strongly negative income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand rises when price rises.

Hicks and Slutsky

John Hicks defined the substitution effect by adjusting income so the consumer can reach the same indifference curve as before the price change. Eugen Slutsky instead adjusted income so the consumer can just afford the original bundle of goods. The two measures differ slightly for discrete price changes but coincide for infinitesimal ones, a relationship expressed in the Slutsky equation, which decomposes the price derivative of Marshallian demand into a substitution term and an income term.

Labour supply

The concept also explains the shape of the labour supply curve: a wage rise increases the opportunity cost of leisure (substitution effect, encouraging more work) while also making the worker richer (income effect, encouraging more leisure). The net result may produce a backward-bending supply curve at high wages.
