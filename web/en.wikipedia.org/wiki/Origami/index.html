Origami

Origami (from Japanese ori, "folding," and kami, "paper") is the Japanese art of paper folding. In modern usage, the word "origami" is used as an inclusive term for all folding practices, regardless of their culture of origin. The goal is to transform a flat square sheet of paper into a finished sculpture through folding and sculpting techniques. Modern origami practitioners generally discourage the use of cuts, glue, or markings on the paper; folders that use these techniques often employ the related term kirigami for cut work.

History

Paper was introduced to Japan in the sixth century, and folding became part of ceremonial and gift-giving customs; folded paper figures appear in printed instructions by the late eighteenth century. The oldest known book on paper folding, the Senbazuru Orikata (1797), showed how to fold strings of connected cranes. In the twentieth century Akira Yoshizawa is credited with elevating origami to a creative art form, devising thousands of models and, with Samuel Randlett, the standard system of diagramming arrows and symbols now used worldwide.

Techniques

Most designs begin from a square and proceed through a repertoire of base forms — the preliminary, waterbomb, bird, and frog bases — built from valley folds, mountain folds, reverse folds, sinks, and squash folds. Advanced "technical origami" designs its models mathematically: the folder plans the flap layout on the sheet using circle-packing methods pioneered by Robert J. Lang and others, then collapses the crease pattern into the finished form. Wet-folding, in which the paper is lightly dampened, allows smooth curves and a sculpted finish.

Mathematics and applications

The mathematics of paper folding studies flat-foldability (Maekawa's and Kawasaki's theorems), rigid origami, and the Huzita–Hatori axioms, which can solve cubic equations and trisect angles — constructions impossible with compass and straightedge alone. Origami principles now inform engineering, from deployable solar arrays and airbag folding to medical stents and self-assembling robots. The paper crane (orizuru) has become an international symbol of peace, associated with the story of Sadako Sasaki and the Children's Peace Monument in Hiroshima.
