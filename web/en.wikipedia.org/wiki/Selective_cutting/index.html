Selective cutting

Selective cutting, also called selective logging or selection cutting, is a silvicultural practice in which only certain trees in a forest stand are harvested — typically those of a chosen species, size, or quality — while the remainder of the stand is left standing. It contrasts with clearcutting, in which nearly all trees in an area are removed at once.

Methods

Under a selection system, individual trees or small groups are removed at regular intervals (the cutting cycle), creating or maintaining an uneven-aged forest. Variants include single-tree selection and group selection. High-grading — removing only the most valuable trees and leaving inferior ones — is sometimes confused with selective cutting but is generally considered a poor practice because it degrades the genetic quality and structure of the remaining stand.

Effects

Properly applied, selective cutting can maintain continuous forest cover, reduce soil erosion and habitat disruption, and sustain a flow of timber over time. However, it requires repeated entry into the stand, which can damage remaining trees and soils, and in tropical forests selective logging can still cause substantial collateral damage. It is a central practice in close-to-nature and continuous-cover forestry.
