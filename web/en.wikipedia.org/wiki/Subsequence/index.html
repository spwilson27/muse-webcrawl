Subsequence

In mathematics, a subsequence of a given sequence is a sequence that can be derived from the given sequence by deleting some or no elements without changing the order of the remaining elements. For example, the sequence ⟨A, B, D⟩ is a subsequence of ⟨A, B, C, D, E, F⟩ obtained after removal of elements C and E. The relation of one sequence being the subsequence of another is a preorder.

Subsequences should not be confused with substrings or contiguous subsequences (sometimes called factors): a substring must consist of consecutive elements, whereas a subsequence may skip elements freely. For instance, ⟨A, D, F⟩ is a subsequence but not a substring of the example above.

Properties and theorems

Subsequences are fundamental in analysis and combinatorics. The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem states that every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence; this underlies the concept of sequential compactness. In the theory of formal languages and combinatorics on words, Higman's lemma states that the subsequence ordering on finite sequences over a finite alphabet is a well-quasi-ordering — there are no infinite antichains or infinite strictly decreasing sequences of words.

Applications

Finding the longest common subsequence of two sequences is a classic problem in computer science, used in diff utilities, version control, spell-checking, and computational biology for comparing DNA and protein sequences. The longest increasing subsequence problem has applications in patience sorting and the analysis of permutations, connected by the Erdős–Szekeres theorem, which guarantees that any sequence of (r−1)(s−1)+1 distinct numbers contains a monotonic subsequence of length r or s.
