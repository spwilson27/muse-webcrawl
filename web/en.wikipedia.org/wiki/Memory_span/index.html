Memory span

In psychology and neuroscience, memory span is the longest list of items — digits, letters, words or spatial locations — that a person can repeat back immediately in the correct order after a single presentation. It is measured with span tasks such as forward digit span and is widely used as an index of short-term memory and working memory capacity.

Typical values

For healthy adults, forward digit span averages about seven items. In his famous 1956 paper "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", George Miller proposed that short-term memory holds about 7 ± 2 chunks of information. Later research, notably by Nelson Cowan, argued that when rehearsal and chunking are controlled, the core capacity is closer to four chunks. Word span depends on the spoken duration of the words (the word-length effect): span is roughly the number of items that can be spoken in about two seconds.

Forward and backward span

In forward span the sequence is repeated in the order presented; in backward span it must be reversed, which adds a manipulation demand and so loads working memory more heavily. Both subtests appear in intelligence batteries such as the Wechsler scales. Spatial analogues include the Corsi block-tapping test, in which a sequence of blocks is tapped in order.

Development and clinical use

Memory span grows through childhood, from about two items at age 2–3 to adult levels in adolescence, and declines in older age and in various neurological and psychiatric conditions. Reduced span is a feature of attention-deficit disorders, learning disabilities, and dementias, making span tasks staples of neuropsychological assessment.
