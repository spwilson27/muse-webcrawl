The 1955 Le Mans disaster was a major crash during the 24 Hours of Le Mans motor race at the Circuit de la Sarthe in Le Mans, France, on 11 June 1955. A Mercedes-Benz 300 SLR driven by Pierre Levegh was launched into the crowd after colliding with another car, killing Levegh and 83 spectators, and injuring around 120 more. It remains the deadliest accident in motor racing history.

The crash

About two hours into the race, Mike Hawthorn's Jaguar D-Type cut across to the pits, slowing sharply in front of Lance Macklin's Austin-Healey. Macklin swerved left into the path of Levegh's Mercedes, which was travelling at roughly 200 km/h (125 mph). The Mercedes struck the rear of the Austin-Healey and was launched into the air, sailing over a protective embankment onto a packed spectator area opposite the pits. The car disintegrated; its engine block, bonnet and front axle scythed through the crowd, and the magnesium-alloy bodywork ignited, burning with such intensity that water used by firefighters reacted with the magnesium and intensified the fire.

Aftermath

The race was controversially continued, officials reasoning that stopping it would cause the huge crowd to leave at once and block ambulances. Hawthorn and Ivor Bueb went on to win for Jaguar. Mercedes-Benz withdrew its remaining cars during the night out of respect for the victims, and at the end of the 1955 season the company withdrew from motor racing entirely; it did not return as a works team for more than three decades.

The disaster prompted sweeping safety reforms at circuits worldwide and led several countries to ban motor racing; Switzerland's ban on circuit racing, imposed in the wake of the crash, lasted for decades. An official inquiry attributed the crash to no single driver and placed responsibility on the circuit's inadequate safety standards, including the narrow pit straight and lack of separation between spectators and the track. The Circuit de la Sarthe was subsequently redesigned with a wider pit area and greater spectator setbacks.
