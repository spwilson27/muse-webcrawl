Grafting

Grafting is a horticultural technique whereby tissues of plants are joined so as to continue their growth together. The upper part of the combined plant is called the scion and the lower part is called the rootstock. The success of this joining requires that the vascular tissues grow together, and such joining is called inosculation. The technique is most commonly used in asexual propagation of commercially grown plants for the horticultural and agricultural trades, and in particular allows desirable fruit, flower or growth characteristics of the scion (which is genetically identical to the parent plant, i.e., a clone) to be combined with advantageous root traits of the rootstock, such as disease resistance, hardiness or dwarfing.

Biology of the graft union

A successful graft requires the cambium of scion and stock to be placed in close contact; the cut surfaces first form callus tissue from proliferating parenchyma cells, which interlocks and then differentiates into new xylem and phloem, restoring the flow of water and nutrients. Grafting generally works only within closely related plants (usually the same genus, sometimes the same family) because of graft compatibility; incompatible combinations fail immediately or break down after months or years. Unlike cross-breeding, grafting does not normally mix the genetics of the two partners — the scion remains a clone of its parent — although rare "graft hybrids" (chimeras, such as +Laburnocytisus 'Adamii') can arise from tissue mixing at the union, and modern research has shown that small RNAs and even genetic material can cross the graft junction.

Reasons for grafting

  • Clonal propagation: many fruit cultivars (e.g., apple varieties) do not come true from seed, so grafting preserves the variety.
  • Disease and pest resistance: European wine grapes (Vitis vinifera) are grafted onto American rootstocks resistant to the phylloxera aphid, a practice that saved the wine industry in the late 19th century.
  • Size control: dwarfing and semi-dwarfing apple rootstocks (such as the Malling series) allow high-density orchards.
  • Precocity and repair: grafted trees bear fruit sooner than seedlings; bridge grafts can save girdled trees.
  • Changing varieties: established trees can be "top-worked" to a new cultivar.
  • Multiple varieties on one plant: "fruit salad" trees bear several cultivars on a single rootstock.
  • Vegetable production: tomatoes, cucumbers, melons and eggplants are widely grafted onto disease-resistant rootstocks in commercial horticulture.

Techniques

Principal graft types include the whip (splice) and whip-and-tongue grafts for small material of equal diameter; cleft grafts for joining a small scion to a large stock; bark grafts made in spring when the bark slips; side-veneer grafts used for conifers and ornamentals; and approach grafts, in which both partners remain on their own roots until the union forms. Budding — inserting a single bud under the bark of the stock (T-budding or shield budding, and chip budding) — is the standard method for roses and many fruit trees because it is fast and economical of scion wood. After joining, unions are typically sealed with grafting wax or tape to prevent desiccation.

History

Grafting is ancient: evidence suggests it was practiced in China before 2000 BC and in Mesopotamia, and it was described in detail by Roman authors including Cato the Elder, Varro, Columella and Pliny the Elder. The technique spread with fruit cultivation through the Islamic world and medieval Europe; by the Renaissance, grafting collections and treatises were common. The 19th-century phylloxera crisis made grafting indispensable to viticulture worldwide. In the 20th and 21st centuries, grafting extended into commercial vegetable production, especially in East Asia and Europe, and is a standard research tool in plant physiology for studying long-distance signalling.

Natural grafting

Grafting also occurs in nature: adjacent tree trunks or roots may fuse (root grafts are common and can transmit diseases such as oak wilt), and parasitic plants unite their vascular systems with their hosts. Inosculation of branches is sometimes exploited in "tree shaping" and living architecture.
