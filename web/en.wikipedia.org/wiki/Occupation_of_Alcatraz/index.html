The Occupation of Alcatraz was a 19-month-long occupation of Alcatraz Island and its abandoned federal prison by Native American activists from 20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971. Led by a group calling itself Indians of All Tribes (IOAT), the occupation began with 89 activists, including students from the San Francisco Bay Area, and drew national attention to the grievances of Native Americans and the failures of the federal termination policy.

Background

Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary closed in 1963 and the island was declared surplus federal property. The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie provided that surplus federal land should be returned to the tribes from whom it was taken. A brief symbolic occupation by Sioux activists in 1964 preceded the main action. Activists proposed turning the island into a Native American cultural and educational centre.

The occupation

On 20 November 1969, 89 activists set out by boat; 14 reached and held the island, and their numbers soon grew into the hundreds. The occupiers organised a community with a school, kitchen and elected council, and broadcast "Radio Free Alcatraz". Prominent participants included Richard Oakes, LaNada War Jack, Adam Fortunate Eagle, John Trudell and Grace Thorpe. Supplies arrived by boat from sympathetic supporters in San Francisco.

Proclamation

IOAT issued a satirical proclamation offering to purchase Alcatraz for "$24 in glass beads and red cloth" — a pointed reference to the legendary purchase of Manhattan — and pledging to treat the federal government as the government had treated Native peoples, including by establishing a "Bureau of Caucasian Affairs".

Decline and removal

After Richard Oakes left following the death of his stepdaughter in a fall on the island in January 1970, cohesion declined; utilities were cut, a fire in June 1970 destroyed several buildings (each side blamed the other), and numbers dwindled. On 11 June 1971 a force of federal marshals, FBI agents and special forces removed the remaining 15 occupiers without serious resistance.

Legacy

Although the island was not transferred, the occupation is widely credited with galvanising the Red Power movement and shifting federal policy: while it continued, President Nixon repudiated termination and increased tribal self-determination, and dozens of pieces of legislation and the return of lands such as Taos Pueblo's Blue Lake followed. Alcatraz later became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area, and an annual Indigenous Peoples' sunrise ceremony commemorates the occupation.
