Research stations in Antarctica

Some 30 countries operate research stations in Antarctica under the Antarctic Treaty System, which reserves the continent for peaceful scientific purposes. There are roughly 70 stations in total, of which about 40 are occupied year-round; the summer population of researchers and support staff reaches about 4,000–5,000, falling to around 1,000 in winter.

Major stations

The largest station is the United States' McMurdo Station on Ross Island, which can support over 1,000 people in summer and serves as the logistics hub for the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station at the geographic South Pole. Russia operates Vostok Station in the interior near the pole of cold, where the lowest natural temperature on Earth (−89.2 °C) was recorded in 1983, and drilling there penetrated to subglacial Lake Vostok. Other notable stations include Argentina's Esperanza Base — where the first person born in Antarctica, Emilio Palma, was delivered in 1978 — Chile's Eduardo Frei Base, Australia's Mawson, Davis and Casey stations, Britain's Rothera and the mobile Halley VI, France and Italy's Concordia Station on Dome C, China's Great Wall and Zhongshan stations, and India's Maitri and Bharati.

Purposes

Station science covers glaciology and ice-core climate records, atmospheric physics including monitoring of the ozone hole, astronomy and neutrino detection (IceCube at the South Pole), biology of extreme environments, and geodesy and space weather. Stations cluster on the Antarctic Peninsula and coast where resupply by ship is easiest; inland plateau stations depend on aircraft and tractor traverses.

Governance

Under the 1959 Antarctic Treaty and its 1991 Environmental Protocol, stations are subject to international inspection, military activity is prohibited, and mining is banned. Station operators coordinate through COMNAP (the Council of Managers of National Antarctic Programs).
