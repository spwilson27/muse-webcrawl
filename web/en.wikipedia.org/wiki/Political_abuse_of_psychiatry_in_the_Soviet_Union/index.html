Political abuse of psychiatry in the Soviet Union

There was systematic political abuse of psychiatry in the Soviet Union, based on the interpretation of political opposition or dissent as a psychiatric problem. During the leadership of General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev, psychiatry was used as a tool to eliminate political dissidents from society; people who openly challenged the authority of the state were labelled as having mental disorders and confined in psychiatric hospitals, known as psikhushkas, without legitimate clinical justification.

Sluggish schizophrenia

The practice was underpinned by the diagnostic concept of "sluggish schizophrenia" (vyalotekushchaya shizofreniya), developed by the psychiatrist Andrei Snezhnevsky and the Moscow school of psychiatry. Sluggish schizophrenia was held to progress very slowly, with symptoms so mild that a person could appear outwardly sane while harbouring delusions. In practice, behaviours such as "reformist delusions", "heightened sense of justice", and stubborn insistence on human rights were read as symptoms of the condition, allowing almost any determined dissenter to be diagnosed.

Victims and methods

Prominent victims of punitive psychiatry included the biologist Zhores Medvedev, the mathematician Leonid Plyushch, the human rights activist Pyotr Grigorenko, the writer Viktor Fainberg, and the poet Natalya Gorbanevskaya. Vladimir Bukovsky smuggled documents documenting the abuse to the West in 1971.

Confinement in special psychiatric hospitals run by the Ministry of Internal Affairs could last for years. Inmates were subjected to forcible "treatments" including insulin coma therapy, electroconvulsive therapy, and powerful antipsychotic drugs such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine, as well as painful procedures such as wet packs, in which patients were wrapped tightly in wet canvas that contracted as it dried.

International condemnation

The abuse drew growing international criticism during the 1970s. At the 1977 World Psychiatric Association congress in Honolulu, a resolution condemning the Soviet practice was passed narrowly. Facing likely expulsion, the Soviet All-Union Society of Neurologists and Psychiatrists resigned from the World Psychiatric Association in 1983. The case of political abuse of psychiatry in the USSR became a major human rights issue in Soviet-Western relations. Systematic punitive psychiatry largely ended during the glasnost reforms of Mikhail Gorbachev in the late 1980s.
