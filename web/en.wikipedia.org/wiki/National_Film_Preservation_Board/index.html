National Film Preservation Board

The National Film Preservation Board is a body of the United States federal government, associated with the Library of Congress, composed of representatives of the American film community — including filmmakers, archivists, scholars, industry organisations and crafts unions. Its best-known function is to advise the Librarian of Congress in selecting films each year for the National Film Registry, which preserves works judged "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".

The Board works with the Library of Congress in Washington, D.C.

History

The Board was created by the National Film Preservation Act of 1988, legislation driven by congressional concern over practices such as the colourisation of black-and-white films and by the rapid deterioration of nitrate film stock. The Act established both the Board and the National Film Registry. Subsequent reauthorisations, including the National Film Preservation Acts of 1992, 1996 and 2005, renewed the programme and also chartered the related but separate National Film Preservation Foundation, a non-profit that raises private funds for archive preservation projects.

Functions

Each year the public nominates films for the Registry; members of the Board review nominations and recommend a final slate, from which the Librarian of Congress selects 25 films. Films become eligible ten years after their initial release. The Board also advises the Librarian on the continuing development and implementation of a national film preservation plan, and its members represent organisations such as the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences, the Directors Guild of America, the American Film Institute, university film programmes and major archives.
