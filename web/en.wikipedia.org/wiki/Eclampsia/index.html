Eclampsia

Eclampsia
SpecialtyObstetrics
SymptomsSeizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia; headache, visual disturbance, high blood pressure
OnsetUsually after 20 weeks of pregnancy; may occur during labour or postpartum
TreatmentMagnesium sulfate, blood pressure control, delivery of the baby
PrognosisSerious; a leading cause of maternal death worldwide

Eclampsia is the onset of seizures (convulsions) in a pregnant woman with pre-eclampsia — the pregnancy disorder characterised by high blood pressure and signs of damage to other organs, most often the kidneys and liver. The seizures are not attributable to a pre-existing brain condition. Eclampsia is an obstetric emergency that endangers both mother and baby and remains a major cause of maternal mortality, particularly in low-resource settings.

Signs and presentation

Most cases follow recognised pre-eclampsia with hypertension and proteinuria, often with warning symptoms such as severe headache, visual changes (blurring, flashing lights), and upper abdominal pain; however, seizures occasionally occur without preceding classic signs. Eclamptic fits are usually tonic–clonic and self-limiting but may recur, and complications include cerebral haemorrhage, pulmonary oedema, kidney failure, HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, and fetal distress.

Treatment and prevention

Management priorities are to protect the airway, stop and prevent further seizures — magnesium sulfate is the drug of choice, shown in the Magpie trial (2002) to halve the risk of eclampsia in women with pre-eclampsia — control severe hypertension with agents such as labetalol or hydralazine, and expedite delivery, which is the definitive treatment, once the mother is stabilised. Preventive care includes antenatal blood pressure monitoring, low-dose aspirin for women at high risk, and calcium supplementation where dietary intake is low.
