Ancient Egyptian creation myths

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ancient Egyptian creation myths are the accounts by which ancient Egyptians explained the origin of the world. Rather than a single canonical story, several theological centers — Heliopolis, Memphis, Hermopolis, and Thebes — developed their own cosmogonies, which coexisted and overlapped. Common to all is the emergence of order from the primordial waters of chaos, called Nu or Nun, and the appearance of the first land, the primeval mound, recalling the fertile earth emerging from the Nile flood.

The Heliopolitan cosmogony

The priesthood of Heliopolis taught that the self-created sun god Atum emerged from Nun upon the first mound. Alone, he generated the twins Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture). Shu and Tefnut produced Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), who in turn bore Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys. These nine deities form the Ennead, the company of gods that structured much of Egyptian theology. In one famous episode, the wandering Eye of the sun god had to be coaxed back to its master, an exchange that in some tellings gave rise to humankind from the god's tears.

The Memphite theology

The Shabaka Stone preserves the Memphite doctrine in which Ptah, patron god of Memphis, creates the world through his heart (thought) and tongue (speech): by conceiving the gods and all things in his heart and naming them, he brings them into being. This emphasis on creation by the spoken word is one of the earliest known philosophical treatments of the logos concept.

The Hermopolitan Ogdoad

At Hermopolis, creation was ascribed to the Ogdoad, four pairs of primordial deities — Nun and Naunet (water), Heh and Hauhet (infinity), Kek and Kauket (darkness), and Amun and Amaunet (hiddenness) — who personified the state of the world before creation. Their interaction produced the cosmic egg or lotus from which the sun god was born, bringing light and order.

Theban and later traditions

With the rise of Thebes, Amun was elevated as a self-created, hidden creator, eventually merged with Ra as Amun-Ra, "king of the gods". Egyptian cosmogonies were not dogmas but complementary frameworks, continually retold in temple texts, hymns, and funerary literature such as the Pyramid Texts and the Book of the Dead.
