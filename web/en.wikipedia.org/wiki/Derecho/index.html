A derecho (from Spanish derecho, "straight") is a widespread, long-lived wind storm associated with a band of rapidly moving showers or thunderstorms variously known as a squall line, bow echo, or quasi-linear convective system. Although a derecho can produce destruction similar in strength to tornadoes, the damage typically occurs in one direction along a relatively straight path, hence the term "straight-line winds".

Characteristics

By definition, a derecho's wind damage swath must extend more than about 400 km (250 miles) and include wind gusts of at least 93 km/h (58 mph) along most of its length, with several gusts exceeding 121 km/h (75 mph). Derechos develop within mesoscale convective systems, usually in warm, humid air masses, and can persist for many hours, causing power outages, forest blowdowns and structural damage over several states.

Notable events

The term was coined by Iowa physicist Gustavus Hinrichs in 1888, as a counterpart to "tornado". Major modern events include the June 2012 North American derecho, which knocked out power to millions from the Midwest to the Mid-Atlantic, and the August 2020 Midwest derecho, one of the costliest thunderstorm events in U.S. history, with winds estimated up to 140 mph in Iowa.
