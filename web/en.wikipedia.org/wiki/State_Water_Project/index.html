The California State Water Project (SWP) is a state water management project in the U.S. state of California under the supervision of the California Department of Water Resources. It is the world's largest publicly built and operated water and power development and conveyance system, providing water to more than 27 million Californians and about 750,000 acres of irrigated farmland.

History

The project was authorized by the Burns-Porter Act, approved by voters in November 1960, which authorized $1.75 billion in general obligation bonds. It grew out of a 1957 feasibility report championed by Governor Edmund G. "Pat" Brown. Construction began in the 1960s, with the Oroville Dam on the Feather River completed in 1968 and the California Aqueduct delivering water to Southern California by the early 1970s.

Facilities

Key facilities include Lake Oroville, the tallest earthfill dam in the United States at 770 feet; the California Aqueduct, which carries water 444 miles from the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta south through the San Joaquin Valley; the Edmonston Pumping Plant, which lifts water 1,926 feet over the Tehachapi Mountains — the highest water lift in the world; San Luis Reservoir, an off-stream storage facility shared with the federal Central Valley Project; and Castaic Lake and Pyramid Lake in Southern California. The project also includes the South Bay Aqueduct and the North Bay Aqueduct.

Operations and water supply

The SWP delivers water to 29 contracting agencies, including the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California, the Kern County Water Agency, and the Santa Clara Valley Water District. Contractors pay the full capital and operating costs of the project. Deliveries vary widely with hydrology, and environmental restrictions on pumping in the Delta — intended to protect endangered fish such as the delta smelt — have reduced exports in many years.

Power generation

The project is both a major consumer and producer of electricity. Hydroelectric plants at Oroville and along the aqueduct generate power, but the energy required to pump water over the Tehachapis makes the SWP one of California's largest single electricity users.

Criticism and environment

Critics have argued the project promised more water than the system reliably yields, harmed the Delta ecosystem, and benefited large agribusiness. Proposed additions, including the Peripheral Canal (defeated by voters in 1982) and the later California WaterFix tunnels proposal, have been controversial for decades.
