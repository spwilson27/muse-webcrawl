Hyaluronic acid

Hyaluronic acid (also called hyaluronan or hyaluronate) is a naturally occurring glycosaminoglycan found in connective, epithelial, and neural tissues throughout the body. It is a linear polysaccharide composed of repeating disaccharide units of D-glucuronic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, and can reach several million daltons in molecular weight, forming some of the longest polymer chains in biology.

Structure and properties

Hyaluronic acid was first isolated from the vitreous body of cattle eyes by Karl Meyer in 1934. Unlike other glycosaminoglycans, it is not sulfated and is not attached to a core protein. In aqueous solution it adopts an expanded random-coil conformation that traps large volumes of water — a single gram can bind up to six litres of water — giving the molecule exceptional viscosity and lubricating properties.

Biological role

Hyaluronan is a major component of the extracellular matrix and is abundant in skin, cartilage, synovial fluid, and the vitreous humor. In joints it acts as a lubricant and shock absorber; in skin it contributes to hydration and elasticity. About half of the body's hyaluronic acid is present in the skin. It also plays roles in wound healing, cell migration, and inflammation, interacting with cell-surface receptors such as CD44.

Medical and cosmetic uses

Hyaluronic acid is widely used in medicine and cosmetics: as dermal fillers and moisturizers in skincare, as viscosupplementation injections for osteoarthritis of the knee, in eye surgery as a viscoelastic, and in wound dressings. Because it is identical across species, it is generally non-immunogenic, although products are now mostly produced by bacterial fermentation rather than extraction from rooster combs.
