Telegram

A telegram (from Greek tele, "far", and gramma, "something written") is a message transmitted by electrical telegraph and delivered in written or printed form. For more than a century, telegrams were the fastest means of long-distance communication and were used for urgent personal, commercial and official news.

History

Electrical telegraphy was commercialised in the 1840s by Samuel Morse in the United States and by Cooke and Wheatstone in Britain. Messages were sent in Morse code over wires, transcribed by operators, and hand-delivered. Telegram style — charged by the word — produced the terse "telegraphic" prose of "STOP" punctuation. Telegram services proliferated with standard forms, greetings telegrams and international cable networks.

Decline

Telephony, telex, fax and finally email and mobile messaging displaced the telegram. Western Union sent its last telegram in 2006; India's state service, among the last large systems, closed in 2013. A few countries retain ceremonial or niche services.

Cultural significance

Telegrams carried defining news of births, marriages, deaths and wartime casualties, and the arrival of the telegram boy became a cultural shorthand for momentous tidings. The medium also gave rise to hoaxes, singing telegrams and the famous Zimmermann Telegram of 1917, which helped draw the United States into the First World War.
