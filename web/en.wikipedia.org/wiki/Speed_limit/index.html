Speed limit

Road speed limits are used in most countries to set the legal maximum speed at which vehicles may travel on a given stretch of road. Speed limits are normally indicated on a traffic sign reflecting the maximum permitted speed, expressed in kilometres per hour (km/h) or miles per hour (mph), and are enforced by national or regional traffic police through methods such as speed cameras, lidar and radar guns, and average-speed systems.

History

The first numeric speed limit was the 10 mph limit in the UK's Locomotive Act 1861; the notorious "Red Flag Act" of 1865 cut limits to 4 mph in the country and 2 mph in towns. The UK abolished blanket limits in 1930, then restored them progressively: a permanent 70 mph motorway limit arrived in 1967. The United States imposed a nationwide 55 mph National Maximum Speed Law in 1974 in response to the oil crisis, repealed in 1995. Germany's autobahns famously retain stretches with no general limit for cars, only an advisory 130 km/h ("Richtgeschwindigkeit"), though many sections are signed with limits.

Justification and effects

Limits are set for road safety, environmental reasons (noise, emissions), and traffic management. Crash severity rises steeply with impact speed — kinetic energy grows with the square of speed — and pedestrian fatality risk climbs sharply above 30 km/h, motivating 20 mph/30 km/h urban zones promoted by Vision Zero policies. Fuel consumption and emissions also rise at high cruising speeds.

Variable and minimum limits

Variable speed limits change with weather, congestion, or time of day, as on smart motorways. Some roads post minimum speeds to keep traffic flowing. Advisory speed limits (e.g., on curves) are recommendations rather than enforceable maxima in some jurisdictions.

Enforcement and debate

Debate continues over whether limits should follow design speed and the 85th-percentile rule, the role of automated enforcement, and the balance between mobility and safety. Studies consistently find that mean speeds and fatalities rise when limits are raised and fall when they are lowered.
