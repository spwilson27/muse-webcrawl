Amaryllis is a genus of flowering plants in the family Amaryllidaceae containing two species of bulbous perennials native to the Western Cape region of South Africa. The best known is Amaryllis belladonna, the belladonna lily or Jersey lily, widely cultivated in warm climates around the world. The genus name is from a shepherdess in classical pastoral poetry; Linnaeus adopted it for the plant in 1753.

Description

Amaryllis belladonna produces large, showy, trumpet-shaped flowers in shades of pink and white on a stout leafless stem that appears in late summer, after the strap-shaped leaves have died back — a habit that gives it the name "naked lady". The bulbs are poisonous, containing alkaloids such as lycorine. The second species, A. paradisicola, was described in 1998 from the Richtersveld.

Confusion with Hippeastrum

The name "amaryllis" is popularly but wrongly applied to the large-flowered bulbs sold for indoor winter blooming, which belong to the South American genus Hippeastrum. A long-standing taxonomic dispute over which genus should bear the name Amaryllis was settled at the 14th International Botanical Congress in 1987, which conserved the name for the South African genus; true Amaryllis differs from Hippeastrum in its solid flower stem, among other characters.

Cultivation

A. belladonna thrives in Mediterranean climates with dry summers, including South Africa, California, Australia, and the island of Jersey, where it is a familiar garden escape. Bulbs are planted with their necks at the soil surface and flower best undisturbed in full sun.
