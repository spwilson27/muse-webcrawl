Rayonnant

Rayonnant (French: [ʁɛjɔnɑ̃], "radiant") is a term for the second phase of Gothic architecture, current in France from about 1240 to 1350. It is characterised by a shift in emphasis from the lofty scale and structural audacity of High Gothic toward ever larger windows, walls increasingly dissolved into stained glass, and tracery whose slender bars radiate like spokes from the centres of rose windows — the feature from which the style takes its name.

Characteristics

Rayonnant architects reduced the thickness of walls and supports, enlarged window openings, and unified interior elevations so that the triforium was glazed and merged with the clerestory. Bar tracery grew more elaborate, with thin moulded stone mullions forming patterns of circles, trefoils and wheel-like ("rayonnant") designs. Surfaces were enriched with gables over portals and windows, pinnacles, and delicate stone canopies over statues.

Notable examples

  • Sainte-Chapelle in Paris (consecrated 1248), built for Louis IX to house relics of the Passion, whose upper chapel is a cage of stained glass.
  • The rebuilt nave and transepts of Notre-Dame de Paris, with its great rose windows.
  • Amiens Cathedral nave and later work.
  • Saint-Denis rebuilding under Abbot Eudes Clement.
  • Troyes, Tours and Beauvais cathedrals, and much of Cologne Cathedral in the Empire, begun in 1248 on French models.

Spread and succession

Rayonnant was the first pan-European Gothic style, exported through drawings, travelling masons and the prestige of the French court. In England it corresponds broadly to the Decorated style. From about 1350 it gave way in France to the more flamboyant curves of Flamboyant Gothic.
