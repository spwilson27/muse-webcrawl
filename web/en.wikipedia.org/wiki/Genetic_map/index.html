Genetic map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A genetic map (also called a linkage map) is a type of chromosome map that shows the relative positions of genes and other important features, based on the frequencies of recombination between markers during crossover of homologous chromosomes. The greater the frequency of recombination between two genetic markers, the farther apart they are assumed to be. Conversely, markers close together on the chromosome tend to be inherited together and show low recombination frequencies.

History

The first genetic map was constructed in 1913 by Alfred Sturtevant, then an undergraduate in Thomas Hunt Morgan's laboratory at Columbia University, who mapped the relative positions of genes on the X chromosome of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster. Sturtevant reasoned that the frequency of crossing over between two genes could serve as a measure of their distance apart.

Map distance

Distances on a genetic map are measured in centimorgans (cM), named in honor of Morgan. Two markers are 1 cM apart if they recombine in approximately 1 percent of meioses. Because recombination probability saturates at 50 percent — the value expected for unlinked loci — mapping functions such as the Haldane and Kosambi functions are used to convert observed recombination fractions into additive map distances.

Uses

Genetic maps are essential tools for identifying the chromosomal location of genes responsible for inherited traits and diseases, for quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis in breeding, and for anchoring and ordering sequence contigs in genome assembly projects. They complement physical maps, which give distances in base pairs: in humans, 1 cM corresponds on average to roughly one million base pairs, though the correspondence varies greatly along the genome because recombination rates vary.
