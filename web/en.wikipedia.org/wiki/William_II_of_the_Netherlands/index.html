William II (Dutch: Willem Frederik George Lodewijk; 6 December 1792 – 17 March 1849) was King of the Netherlands, Grand Duke of Luxembourg, and Duke of Limburg from 1840 until his death in 1849. Before his accession he had a distinguished military career, fighting in the Peninsular War and at the Battle of Waterloo, where he was wounded. His reign is best remembered for the constitutional revision of 1848, which transformed the Netherlands into a parliamentary constitutional monarchy.

Early life and military career

William was born in The Hague, the eldest son of the future King William I and Wilhelmine of Prussia. When French revolutionary forces invaded the Netherlands in 1795, the family went into exile. William was educated in Berlin and at the University of Oxford, and entered the British Army, serving as aide-de-camp to the Duke of Wellington during the Peninsular War.

As commander of the I Netherlands Corps in 1815, William fought at the Battle of Quatre Bras and at Waterloo, where he was wounded in the shoulder by a musket ball. His conduct made him a popular hero in the Netherlands, and he became known as the "Hero of Waterloo".

Marriage and the Belgian Revolution

In 1816 William married Grand Duchess Anna Pavlovna, sister of Tsar Alexander I of Russia. During the Belgian Revolution of 1830 he attempted to mediate between his father and the Belgian rebels but failed, and relations between father and son became strained.

Reign

William II succeeded his father on his abdication on 7 October 1840. Economically the reign was marked by the continuing costs of the Belgian secession and the controversial cultuurstelsel (cultivation system) in the Dutch East Indies.

Constitution of 1848

Frightened by the revolutionary wave sweeping Europe in 1848, William famously remarked that he had changed "in one night from extremely conservative to extremely liberal". He invited the liberal statesman Johan Rudolph Thorbecke to draft a new constitution. The Constitution of 1848 established ministerial responsibility, direct elections to the House of Representatives, and freedom of the press, education and religion, and it remains, much amended, the basis of the Dutch state today.

Death

William II died suddenly in Tilburg on 17 March 1849, before the new order had fully taken shape. He was succeeded by his son, William III.
