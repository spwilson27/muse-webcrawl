Consignment

Consignment is a commercial arrangement in which goods are left in the possession of an authorized third party (the consignee) to sell, while the owner of the goods (the consignor) retains legal title until the goods are sold. The consignee sells the goods on the consignor's behalf, typically taking a commission, and remits the remaining proceeds to the consignor. Unsold goods are normally returned to the consignor.

How it works

In a typical consignment arrangement, the consignor delivers inventory to the consignee — a retailer, shop, agent or auction house — under terms set out in a consignment agreement. The agreement specifies the selling period, pricing (and any permitted markdowns), the commission split, responsibility for insurance and loss, and what happens to unsold items. Because title does not pass until sale, consigned stock generally does not appear as inventory owned by the consignee in its accounts, and consignors bear the risk that the goods may not sell.

Consignment shops

The term is most familiar from consignment shops, second-hand retail stores — common in North America — that sell clothing, furniture, antiques and other goods on behalf of their owners, in contrast to thrift stores, which typically take donated goods. Consignment shops commonly pay the consignor a percentage (often 40–60%) of the selling price once the item sells, within a fixed consignment period of 30–90 days.

Other uses

Consignment is widely used in international trade and distribution: manufacturers supply goods to distributors or retailers who pay only for what they sell, easing retailers' working-capital needs. In publishing, books may be supplied on a sale-or-return (consignment) basis. "Vendor-managed inventory" in modern supply chains is a related model, in which a supplier maintains stock at a customer's premises until it is used.

Legal aspects

At law, consignment creates a bailment and agency relationship; creditors of the consignee generally cannot claim consigned goods, since the consignee does not own them — although rules such as Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States may require consignors to file financing statements to protect their interests against the consignee's secured creditors.
