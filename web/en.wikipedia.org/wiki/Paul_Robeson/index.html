Paul Robeson

Born9 April 1898, Princeton, New Jersey
Died23 January 1976, Philadelphia
OccupationsSinger, actor, athlete, activist
EducationRutgers University; Columbia Law School

Paul Leroy Robeson (9 April 1898 – 23 January 1976) was an American bass-baritone concert artist, stage and film actor, professional athlete and political activist. A figure of extraordinary versatility — an All-American football player, a law graduate, and then one of the most famous performers in the world — he became a hero of the international labour and anti-colonial movements and, for his criticism of United States racism and his sympathy toward the Soviet Union, a target of McCarthy-era persecution that destroyed his career at home.

Early life

Robeson was born in Princeton, New Jersey, the son of the Reverend William Drew Robeson, who had escaped slavery as a teenager, and Maria Louisa Bustill, of a prominent free Black abolitionist family. At Rutgers University he was one of very few Black students, excelled academically (Phi Beta Kappa, class valedictorian) and was twice named a football All-American at end. He earned a law degree from Columbia while playing professional football in the early NFL, but left legal practice after encountering racism at his firm.

Performance career

Encouraged by his wife Eslanda ("Essie") Goode Robeson, he turned to the stage, starring in Eugene O'Neill's All God's Chillun Got Wings and The Emperor Jones. Concerts of spirituals with pianist Lawrence Brown made his deep bass-baritone famous on both sides of the Atlantic. In Jerome Kern's Show Boat (London 1928; film 1936) his rendition of "Ol' Man River" became his signature. He played Othello in London in 1930 and on Broadway in 1943–44 — the longest-running Shakespeare production in Broadway history to that date — and starred in British films including Sanders of the River (1935) and Song of Freedom (1936), though he grew disillusioned with Hollywood's portrayals of Black characters.

Activism and persecution

Travel in Europe in the 1930s — including to Republican Spain, where he sang for International Brigades troops, and to the Soviet Union, where he felt free of racial discrimination — shaped his politics: he championed African independence, labour rights, Welsh miners, and civil rights at home. During the Cold War his refusal to disavow his views brought disaster. After remarks attributed to him at the 1949 Paris Peace Conference sparked outrage, riots by anti-communist mobs attacked his concert at Peekskill, New York. In 1950 the State Department revoked his passport, cutting off his foreign income; in 1956 he was interrogated by the House Un-American Activities Committee, memorably defying its members. His health and career broken, he returned to international stages only after the Supreme Court's 1958 ruling in Kent v. Dulles restored his right to travel.

Death and legacy

Robeson spent his final years in seclusion in Philadelphia and died there in 1976. Posthumous honours have included induction into the College Football Hall of Fame, a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, and commemoration on a US postage stamp; Rutgers named its cultural centre and library for him. His life is now widely taught as a defining case of artistry, race and political freedom in 20th-century America.
