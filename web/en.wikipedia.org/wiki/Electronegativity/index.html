Electronegativity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electronegativity
Field: Chemistry
Topic: Bonding
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

Electronegativity is a measure of the tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared electrons of a covalent bond toward itself. On the Pauling scale, fluorine — the most electronegative element — is assigned 3.98, and caesium about 0.79.

Electronegativity increases across a period (toward fluorine) and decreases down a group. The difference between two bonded atoms' values indicates bond character: near-equal values give nonpolar covalent bonds, intermediate differences polar covalent bonds, and large differences (roughly > 1.7–2.0) predominantly ionic bonds.

[Image: A periodic table heat-map of electronegativity rising toward fluorine]

Scales

Linus Pauling's 1932 thermochemical scale is the most used; Mulliken, Allred–Rochow and Allen scales define electronegativity differently (ionisation energy/electron affinity averages, effective nuclear charge, valence-electron energies) and broadly agree in trend.

Consequences

Polar bonds from electronegativity differences explain water's hydrogen bonding, the acidity trends of oxoacids, and why carbon (2.55) and hydrogen (2.20) form near-nonpolar C–H bonds while O–H bonds are strongly polar.
