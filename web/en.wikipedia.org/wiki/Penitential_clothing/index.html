Penitential clothing

A hairshirt (cilice) of coarse animal hair.

Penitential clothing refers to garments worn as an act of penance, self-mortification, or public humiliation for sin, in various religious and cultural traditions. The best-known examples are sackcloth and the hairshirt; penitential garments also include the robes worn by penitents in inquisitorial proceedings and in surviving Holy Week processions.

Sackcloth and ashes

In the Hebrew Bible and the ancient Near East, mourning and repentance were expressed by wearing sackcloth, a rough cloth of goat's hair, and sitting in or sprinkling ashes. Figures from Jacob to the king of Nineveh in the Book of Jonah adopt the practice, and sackcloth remained a symbol of contrition in Judaism and early Christianity, echoed in the liturgy of Ash Wednesday.

Hairshirt and cilice

The hairshirt, in Latin cilicium (from Cilicia, source of goat-hair cloth), is a garment of coarse animal hair worn against the skin to cause discomfort and constant reminder of penance. It was worn by ascetics, monks, and saints, and by lay penitents; modern forms of the cilice, such as a spiked metal chain worn around the thigh, are used by some members of Catholic movements. Historical wearers associated with the hairshirt include Thomas Becket, whose hairshirt was discovered after his murder in 1170, and Thomas More.

Public penitents

In medieval and early modern Europe, public penitents processed in distinctive robes. The Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions dressed reconciled or condemned heretics in the sanbenito, a yellow or black penitential tunic painted with crosses, flames, or devils, worn at the auto-da-fé. In southern Spain and elsewhere, confraternities of penitents (nazarenos) still process during Holy Week wearing long robes and tall conical hoods (capirotes), garments of medieval penitential origin signifying anonymity and humility.
