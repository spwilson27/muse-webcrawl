Khaen

The khaen (Lao: ແຄນ; Thai: แคน) is a mouth organ of Lao origin used in the traditional music of Laos and of the Isan region of northeastern Thailand. It consists of a set of bamboo pipes of graduated length, each fitted with a free reed, bound into a wooden windchest; the player blows and draws air through a mouthpiece while stopping holes in the pipes with the fingers. In 2017 UNESCO inscribed "the khaen music of the Lao people" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Construction

A khaen typically has 16 pipes arranged in two rows of eight, though instruments with 6, 8 or 18 pipes also exist. The pipes are made from a slender bamboo (hiah) and pass through a carved hardwood air chamber; each pipe contains a small free reed of bronze or silver alloy and a finger hole. Because the reeds sound on both inhalation and exhalation, the player can sustain an unbroken drone and intricate rhythmic polyphony.

Music and cultural role

The khaen is the principal accompanying instrument of lam, the sung poetry of Laos and Isan, and of mor lam performance, in which singers improvise verses over the khaen's cyclical patterns. Its sound is emblematic of Lao identity, and the instrument also plays solo and ensemble roles in courtship songs, festivals and healing rituals. Legend attributes its invention to a Lao woman who imitated the call of the phoenix-like nok karavek bird for her king.

Spread

Related free-reed mouth organs are found throughout East and Southeast Asia, including the Chinese sheng, Japanese shō and the instruments of the Hmong people. In recent decades the khaen has appeared in world music, jazz and experimental contexts outside its homeland.
