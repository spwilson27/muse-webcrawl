Nart saga

The Nart sagas (Abkhaz: Нарҭаа ражәабжьқәа; Circassian: Нарт пшыналъэхэр; Ossetian: Нарты кадджытæ) are a cycle of epic tales of the peoples of the North Caucasus, including the Ossetians, Circassians (Adyghe), Abkhazians, Chechens and Ingush. They tell of the Narts, a race of mighty heroes, giants and demigods who feasted, fought and quested in a mythic age. The sagas preserve some of the oldest strata of Iranian and Caucasian mythology and have been compared in importance to the Greek myths and the Norse Eddas.

The world of the Narts

The Narts live in a world of mountains and pastures, dividing their time between feasting in their great hall, raiding, and single combat. Central figures include the wise matriarch Satanaya, mother of the Narts, whose counsel the heroes seek; the hero Sosruko (Soslan in Ossetian), born from a stone, trickster and slayer of giants; Batraz, the steel-bodied hero who was tempered in water; Syrdon, the wily harpist; and the smith god Kurdalagon, who forges weapons for the heroes in his heavenly smithy. Many tales turn on pride, hospitality, vengeance and the winning of magical treasures.

Origins and scholarship

The sagas have roots in the cultures of the Scythians, Sarmatians and Alans — Iranian steppe peoples ancestral to the Ossetians — overlaid with the traditions of the indigenous peoples of the Caucasus. Scholars such as Georges Dumézil noted parallels between the three "clans" of the Narts and the trifunctional structure he proposed for Indo-European society, and between Nart figures and deities of other traditions: the one-eyed giant echoes the Cyclops, and the hero's quest for fire and the cup of heroes has counterparts in Greek and Indic myth. Similarities have also been drawn between elements of the sagas and the Arthurian legend, including a hero's sword thrown into water and a magical cup.

Collection and legacy

The tales were transmitted orally for centuries and were first systematically recorded in the 19th and 20th centuries by collectors working in Ossetian, Circassian, Abkhaz and other languages. Today they remain a living part of Caucasian cultural identity, retold in literature, film and the names of sports teams and public monuments.
