Étale cohomology

The étale topology lets algebraic varieties behave cohomologically like topological spaces

In mathematics, the étale cohomology groups of an algebraic variety or scheme are algebraic analogues of the usual singular cohomology groups with finite coefficients for a topological space. They were introduced by Alexander Grothendieck, together with Jean-Pierre Serre and others, around 1960, in order to prove the Weil conjectures relating the zeta functions of varieties over finite fields to their topology.

Motivation

For varieties over the complex numbers, classical algebraic topology assigns cohomology groups that detect the shape of the underlying analytic space. For varieties over finite fields or number fields, no such topological space exists in the usual sense. Grothendieck's insight was that cohomology need not come from a space itself: it suffices to specify a site — a category equipped with a notion of covering — and the étale site, whose covers are families of étale morphisms, provides exactly the required structure.

Definition

Let X be a scheme. Its étale site has as objects the étale morphisms U → X, with coverings given by jointly surjective families of étale maps. The étale cohomology of X with coefficients in a sheaf F on this site is defined as the derived functors of the global sections functor, written Hi(X, F).

Of greatest arithmetic interest are the ℓ-adic cohomology groups, obtained by taking inverse limits of étale cohomology with coefficients in Z/ℓn. These carry an action of the absolute Galois group of the base field, making them fundamental to modern number theory and the Langlands program.

Applications

Pierre Deligne used étale cohomology in his proof of the last and hardest of the Weil conjectures in 1974. ℓ-adic cohomology also underlies the proof of the Mordell conjecture by Gerd Faltings and provides the setting for the cohomological realisations of motives.
