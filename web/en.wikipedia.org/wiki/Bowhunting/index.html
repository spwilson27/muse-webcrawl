Bowhunting

[Image: A bowhunter with a compound bow]

Bowhunting (or bow hunting) is the practice of hunting game animals by archery. Many indigenous peoples have employed the method as their primary means of hunting for thousands of years, and it has survived into modern times as a sport and hunting method. Unlike hunting with a firearm, bowhunting places the hunter in much closer proximity to the prey — typically within 20 to 45 metres — demanding greater skill in stalking, concealment, and reading animal behaviour.

History

Archery hunting dates to the Stone Age; the bow and arrow spread to every inhabited continent except Australia. In the modern era, figures such as Saxton Pope and Arthur Young, who studied bowhunting with Ishi, the last of the Yahi, and Fred Bear, founder of Bear Archery, revived interest in the practice in the early 20th century. The Pope and Young Club, founded in 1961, keeps records for North American big game taken with bow and arrow.

Equipment

Bowhunters use longbows, recurve bows, or compound bows. Regulations in many jurisdictions require a minimum draw weight for hunting large game. Arrows for big game are fitted with broadheads — sharpened steel points with two or more blades designed to produce rapid blood loss. Crossbows are permitted during archery seasons in some jurisdictions and prohibited in others.

Methods

Common techniques include still-hunting (slow stalking on foot), hunting from tree stands and ground blinds overlooking trails, feeding areas, or water sources, spot-and-stalk hunting in open terrain, and calling animals within range. Because of the short effective range, scent control, camouflage, and wind awareness are central skills.

Regulation

Many regions set separate, often longer, archery seasons preceding firearm seasons, reflecting the lower success rates of bowhunters. Some European countries, historically including the United Kingdom, prohibit bowhunting entirely, while others license it under strict conditions.
