Weaire–Phelan structure

The Weaire–Phelan structure is a three-dimensional structure representing an idealised foam of equal-sized bubbles. Proposed in 1993 by Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan of Trinity College Dublin, it partitions space into equal-volume cells with less total surface area than the structure proposed by Lord Kelvin in 1887, which had stood as the conjectured best solution for more than a century.

Geometry

The Kelvin problem asks how space can be divided into cells of equal volume with the minimal surface area, as in a dry soap foam. Kelvin's solution used truncated octahedra with slightly curved faces. The Weaire–Phelan structure uses two kinds of cells: an irregular pentagonal dodecahedron and a polyhedron with 14 faces (two hexagons and twelve pentagons), arranged with eight cells per repeating unit, achieving about 0.3 percent less surface area than Kelvin's structure. Whether it is the true optimum remains unproved.

Applications

The structure inspired the design of the Beijing National Aquatics Centre (the "Water Cube"), built for the 2008 Summer Olympics, whose façade and roof are based on a random-looking slice of Weaire–Phelan foam geometry. The structure also occurs in clathrate hydrate crystal chemistry, where related polyhedral cages enclose gas molecules.
