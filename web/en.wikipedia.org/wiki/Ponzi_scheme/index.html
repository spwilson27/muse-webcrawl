A Ponzi scheme is a form of fraud that lures investors and pays profits to earlier investors with funds from more recent investors. Victims are led to believe that the profits come from legitimate business activity — for example, the sale of a product or successful investments — when in fact they come from the deposits of newer participants. A Ponzi scheme can maintain the illusion of a sustainable business as long as new investors contribute enough funds; when recruitment falters or too many investors withdraw at once, the scheme collapses.

Characteristics

Regulators such as the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission identify common warning signs: high investment returns with little or no risk; overly consistent returns; unregistered investments and unlicensed sellers; secretive, complex or opaque strategies; problems with paperwork; and difficulty receiving payments or cashing out.

History

The scheme is named after Charles Ponzi, an Italian immigrant to the United States who in 1919–1920 promised investors a 50% profit within a few weeks by exploiting price differences in international reply coupons used for postage. Money poured in — at its peak his operation took in millions of dollars — but press scrutiny and an official investigation exposed that few coupons had ever been bought. Ponzi was arrested in August 1920 and convicted of mail fraud. Earlier precedents include the 1890s schemes of Adele Spitzeder in Munich and Sarah Howe in Boston, and the fictional swindles described in Charles Dickens's novels Martin Chuzzlewit (1844) and Little Dorrit (1857).

Famous cases

  • Bernie Madoff — the largest known Ponzi scheme. Madoff's investment advisory business collapsed in December 2008; the amount missing from client accounts, including fabricated gains, was estimated at nearly $65 billion, with actual principal losses of roughly $18 billion. Madoff pleaded guilty in 2009 and was sentenced to 150 years in prison.
  • Allen Stanford — convicted in 2012 of running a roughly $7 billion scheme based on fraudulent certificates of deposit sold through his offshore bank in Antigua.
  • MMM — Sergei Mavrodi's scheme in 1990s Russia, which attracted millions of participants.

Related schemes

A Ponzi scheme differs from a pyramid scheme in that, in a pyramid scheme, participants themselves recruit new members and are rewarded for doing so, whereas a Ponzi scheme is typically run by a central operator who solicits investments directly. Cryptocurrency frauds and "high-yield investment programs" (HYIPs) frequently follow the Ponzi model.
