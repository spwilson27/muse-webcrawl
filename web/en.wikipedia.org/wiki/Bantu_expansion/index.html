Bantu expansion

The Bantu expansion was a series of migrations of Bantu-speaking peoples from their homeland in West-Central Africa — in the region of modern Nigeria and Cameroon — across much of sub-Saharan Africa. Beginning around 3000–2000 BCE and continuing for millennia, the expansion carried Bantu languages, farming (notably yam, oil palm and later sorghum, millet, bananas and ironworking) and pottery traditions into Central, East and Southern Africa, where Bantu-speaking communities largely replaced, displaced or absorbed earlier hunter-gatherer populations such as the Pygmies and the Khoisan peoples.

Routes

Scholars distinguish an early western stream, which moved south along the Atlantic coast and through the Congo basin rainforest, and an eastern stream, which passed north of the forest through the Great Lakes region (associated with the Urewe pottery culture) and then spread southward through East and Southern Africa, reaching the far south by the early centuries CE. A later savanna phase connected the two streams across the southern savannas.

Evidence

The reconstruction rests on linguistics (the distribution and divergence of some 500 related Bantu languages), archaeology (pottery styles, ironworking and settlement patterns) and, increasingly, population genetics, which shows admixture between incoming farmers and indigenous forager groups along the routes.

Consequences

The expansion transformed the demography and ecology of the continent: agriculture and iron metallurgy spread widely, and Bantu languages — including Swahili, Zulu, Xhosa, Shona, Kongo and Lingala — became the mother tongues of hundreds of millions of people.
