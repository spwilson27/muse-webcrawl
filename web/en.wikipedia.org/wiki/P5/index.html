P5

The P5 are the five permanent members of the United Nations Security Council: China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States. Under the UN Charter, adopted in 1945, each permanent member holds a veto over substantive Security Council resolutions, and the composition of the group reflects the victorious great powers of the Second World War as recognised at the time of the UN's founding.

Composition and history

The five seats were allocated in 1945 to the United States, the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, France and the Republic of China. Two seats have changed hands by succession: in 1971 UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 transferred the Chinese seat from the Republic of China (Taiwan) to the People's Republic of China, and in 1991, on the dissolution of the USSR, the Russian Federation took over the Soviet seat by notification rather than by any formal amendment. All five are also the five nuclear-weapon states recognised by the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) — a coincidence that reflects their status at the time that regime was negotiated.

Role and criticism

As a group the P5 consult on Council business, draft most resolutions in informal "penholder" arrangements, and issue joint statements as the NPT nuclear-weapon states. Their privileged status is the chief target of Security Council reform proposals — from the G4 nations (Brazil, Germany, India, Japan) seeking permanent seats of their own, to campaigns to restrain use of the veto in cases of mass atrocity. Because Charter amendment requires ratification by all five, the P5 can block any change to their own position.

P5+1

The P5+1 — the five permanent members plus Germany (with the European Union participating as facilitator) — was the format that negotiated with Iran over its nuclear programme from 2006 onward, culminating in the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA) of 2015.
