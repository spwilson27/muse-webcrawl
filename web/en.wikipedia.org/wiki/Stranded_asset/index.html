Stranded asset

Early-retired fossil-fuel infrastructure can become a stranded asset

Stranded assets are assets that have suffered from unanticipated or premature write-downs, devaluations or conversion to liabilities. The term is most often used in connection with fossil-fuel reserves and infrastructure whose value could be destroyed by the transition to a low-carbon economy, by climate policy, or by the physical impacts of climate change.

Concept

The idea gained prominence in the early 2010s through the work of the Carbon Tracker Initiative, which pointed out that burning all proven fossil-fuel reserves would exceed any plausible "carbon budget" consistent with limiting global warming to 2 °C. If governments act to stay within such a budget, a substantial fraction of reserves—termed "unburnable carbon"—could never be produced, and the power plants, pipelines and refineries built to handle them would be stranded.

Causes

Assets can be stranded by environmental or social change (such as new regulation or shifting demand), technological change (cheap renewables or electric vehicles displacing incumbents), legal developments, or physical risks such as flooding of low-lying infrastructure. Central banks and financial regulators, including the Bank of England under Mark Carney, have drawn attention to stranded-asset risk as a financial-stability issue, since pension funds and insurers hold large exposures to carbon-intensive companies.

Debate

Estimates of the value at risk vary widely, from hundreds of billions to several trillion dollars. Critics argue that reserves are long-lived and demand will persist, while others note that stranding may arrive abruptly if policy, technology and market sentiment shift together.
