The skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis) is a medium-sized perciform fish in the tuna family, Scombridae. It is otherwise known as the balaya, bonito, cakalang, arctic bonito, mushmouth, oceanic bonito, striped tuna or victor fish. It grows up to 1 m (3 ft) in length. It is a streamlined, fast-swimming pelagic fish common in tropical waters throughout the world, where it inhabits surface waters in large shoals (up to 50,000 fish), feeding on fish, crustaceans, cephalopods, and molluscs.

Skipjack tuna is an important commercial and game fish, usually caught using purse seine nets, and is used in Japanese cuisine as katsuo no tataki, as well as being an ingredient in dashi through the production of katsuobushi (bonito flakes). It is the most important species of tuna caught worldwide and accounts for a large share of the canned tuna market.

Description

The skipjack tuna has a fusiform, elongated, rounded body. It lacks scales except on the corselet (a band of large, thick scales forming a circle around the body behind the head) and a small patch of scales between the pelvic fins. It has 41–45 vertebrae. The back is dark purplish blue and the lower parts and belly are silver with four to six dark, broken horizontal stripes, which distinguish it from other tunas. It can reach about 108 cm (43 in) in length and 32.5 kg (72 lb) in weight.

Distribution and habitat

Skipjack tuna are cosmopolitan pelagic fish found in tropical and warm-temperate waters of the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans, generally between about 45°N and 43°S. They are usually found in surface waters where temperatures exceed 15 °C, often gathering beneath floating objects and around fish aggregating devices (FADs).

Ecology

Skipjack are opportunistic predators, feeding mainly on small fish such as anchovies and sardines, as well as squid and crustaceans. They spawn throughout the year in equatorial waters, with females producing large numbers of eggs. Skipjack are preyed upon by larger tunas, billfish, sharks, and seabirds. The species grows quickly and matures early, which makes its populations comparatively resilient to fishing pressure, although stock assessments differ between ocean basins.

Fisheries

Skipjack tuna is one of the world's most important commercial fish species, with annual catches frequently exceeding 2.5 million tonnes. Major fisheries operate in the western and central Pacific, the eastern Pacific, the Indian Ocean, and the Atlantic. Fish are caught principally with purse seines and pole-and-line gear. The catch is marketed fresh, frozen, dried, smoked, and canned; much of the canned tuna sold as "light tuna" in the United States is skipjack.

Human consumption

In Japanese cuisine, skipjack is known as katsuo and is eaten raw as sashimi and lightly seared as katsuo no tataki. Dried, fermented, and smoked skipjack, shaved into katsuobushi, is a key ingredient of dashi stock. In Indonesia the fish is known as cakalang and is grilled or smoked; in the Maldives it is a dietary staple (Maldive fish).
