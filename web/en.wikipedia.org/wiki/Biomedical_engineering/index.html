Biomedical engineering

Hero image: development and testing of a prosthetic device, a classic application of biomedical engineering.

Biomedical engineering (also known as medical engineering) is the application of engineering principles and design concepts to medicine and biology for healthcare applications, including diagnostics, monitoring, therapy, and prosthetics. The field bridges the gap between engineering and medicine, combining design and problem-solving skills with medical and biological sciences to advance healthcare treatment.

Subdisciplines

Biomedical engineering encompasses many subfields. Biomechanics applies mechanics to biological systems, from blood flow to joint replacement. Biomedical instrumentation and imaging produced technologies such as the electrocardiograph, MRI, CT scanning, and ultrasound. Rehabilitation engineering develops prostheses, orthoses, and assistive devices. Tissue engineering and biomaterials research aims to grow or scaffold replacement tissues, while neural engineering interfaces electronics with the nervous system, as in cochlear implants and brain–computer interfaces. Clinical engineers manage medical technology within hospitals.

History

Early landmarks include Willem Einthoven's electrocardiograph (1903), the iron lung, the first successful kidney dialysis machine built by Willem Kolff in the 1940s, and the implantable pacemaker (1958). John Charnley's low-friction hip replacement in the 1960s revolutionized orthopedics, and the development of CT scanning by Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan Cormack earned a Nobel Prize in 1979. The field was institutionalized with dedicated university departments from the 1970s onward; pioneers such as Robert Langer extended it into drug delivery and tissue engineering.

Regulation and practice

Because medical devices directly affect patient safety, biomedical products are tightly regulated — by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the European Union's Medical Device Regulation, and standards bodies — through premarket approval processes and risk classification. Biomedical engineers work in industry, hospitals, research laboratories, and regulatory agencies.
