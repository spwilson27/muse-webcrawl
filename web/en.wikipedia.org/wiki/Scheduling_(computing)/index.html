Scheduling (computing)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In computing, scheduling is the action of assigning resources to perform tasks. The resources may be processors, network links, or expansion cards; the tasks may be threads, processes, or data flows. The component responsible is called a scheduler. Scheduling is a fundamental part of operating system design, enabling multiprogramming and multitasking by deciding which ready process runs on a CPU at any moment.

Types of schedulers

Operating systems commonly distinguish three scheduler types. The long-term scheduler (or admission scheduler) controls the degree of multiprogramming by deciding which jobs enter the system. The medium-term scheduler temporarily removes processes from memory, swapping them out to reduce contention. The short-term scheduler, or CPU scheduler, selects from processes in the ready queue and allocates the CPU to one of them; it runs very frequently, often every few milliseconds.

Scheduling criteria

Schedulers are judged by criteria such as CPU utilization, throughput (jobs completed per unit time), turnaround time, waiting time, response time, and fairness. Preemptive schedulers can interrupt a running process before it completes its time slice, while non-preemptive (cooperative) schedulers let a process run until it finishes or blocks.

Common algorithms

Well-known scheduling algorithms include first-come, first-served (FCFS); shortest job next (SJN); round-robin scheduling, which gives each process a fixed time quantum; priority scheduling; multilevel queue and multilevel feedback queue scheduling; and completely fair scheduling as used in the Linux kernel. Real-time systems use algorithms such as rate-monotonic scheduling and earliest deadline first to meet timing constraints.
