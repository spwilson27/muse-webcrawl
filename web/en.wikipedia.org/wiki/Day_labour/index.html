Day labour (in Commonwealth spelling; US: day labor) is work performed by labourers who are hired and paid one day at a time, with no promise of continuing employment. Day labourers find work through informal street-corner gathering points, temporary staffing agencies, day-labour centres, or online platforms, and are employed heavily in construction, agriculture, landscaping, moving and domestic service.

History

Hiring labour by the day is ancient — the parable of the workers in the vineyard in the Gospel of Matthew turns on labourers hired at successive hours of the day — and it was the normal form of urban casual work through the 19th century, when dockers, building labourers and harvest hands assembled at pubs, docksides and market squares for the morning "call-on". Trade unions long campaigned for the decasualisation of such work, arguing that the daily scramble degraded wages and safety; Britain's National Dock Labour Scheme (1947) and similar arrangements elsewhere replaced the casual call-on with registered pools of dock labour.

Modern day labour

In the contemporary United States, day labour is closely associated with immigrant workers. A landmark national study (2006) estimated roughly 117,000 day labourers seeking work on any given day, most of them Latino migrants, hired mainly by homeowners and small contractors. Workers gather at "shape-up" sites — often near home-improvement stores — where community-run worker centres in cities such as Los Angeles, Seattle and New York have organised hiring halls to set minimum rates and combat wage theft, which surveys show to be endemic: late payment, underpayment and non-payment are common, and injured day labourers often lack access to workers' compensation.

Economics and law

Day labour sits at the most flexible margin of the labour market: it gives employers instant, commitment-free labour and gives workers — including those excluded from formal employment by language, credentials or immigration status — immediate income, but at the price of insecurity, irregular earnings and minimal legal protection. In most jurisdictions day labourers are legally entitled to minimum wage and overtime regardless of status, though enforcement is difficult. Temporary staffing agencies formalise the same model on a larger scale, while app-based gig platforms have been described as a digital descendant of the street-corner shape-up.
