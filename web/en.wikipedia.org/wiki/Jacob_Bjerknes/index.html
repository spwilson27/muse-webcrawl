Jacob Aall Bonnevie Bjerknes (2 November 1897 – 7 July 1975) was a Norwegian-American meteorologist. He was a son of the pioneering meteorologist and geophysicist Vilhelm Bjerknes, founder of the Bergen School of Meteorology.

Polar front theory

Working as a young assistant in Bergen during and after the First World War, Jacob Bjerknes developed, at age 21, the polar front model of the extratropical cyclone (1919), describing cyclones as disturbances along the boundary — the polar front — between polar and tropical air masses, with warm and cold fronts and a characteristic life cycle ending in occlusion. The Bergen cyclone model remains the textbook foundation for analysing mid-latitude weather systems.

Later career

Bjerknes became professor at the Geophysical Institute in Bergen in 1931. During the Second World War he emigrated to the United States, where he helped establish the meteorology program at the University of California, Los Angeles, training forecasters for the U.S. Army Air Forces, and remained at UCLA for the rest of his career.

In the 1960s Bjerknes made fundamental contributions to understanding ocean–atmosphere interaction in the tropical Pacific: he proposed the positive feedback (now called the Bjerknes feedback) linking Pacific trade winds, sea-surface temperature gradients and upwelling, and recognised the connection between El Niño and the Southern Oscillation, laying groundwork for the modern theory of ENSO. He received the Carl-Gustaf Rossby Research Medal of the American Meteorological Society, among other honours.
