Dimensionless quantity

A dimensionless quantity is a quantity to which no physical dimension is assigned, also known as a bare quantity, a pure number, or a scalar in the appropriate context. Dimensionless quantities are defined as the ratio of two quantities with the same dimensions, so their units cancel out. They are widely used in mathematics, physics, engineering and economics to characterise systems independently of the particular units chosen.

Properties

Dimensionless quantities have the same value regardless of the system of units used to measure them, which makes them essential for comparing experiments performed at different scales. The Buckingham π theorem states that any physically meaningful equation involving a set of variables can be rewritten in terms of a smaller set of independent dimensionless groups — the π groups — formed from those variables. This underlies the method of dimensional analysis and the use of scaled models in wind tunnels and towing tanks.

Examples

  • Counts — the number of objects in a sample is a dimensionless integer.
  • Angles — measured in radians as the ratio of arc length to radius.
  • Strain — the ratio of a change in length to the original length.
  • Reynolds number — the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in a fluid flow.
  • Mach number — the ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound.
  • Fine-structure constant — a dimensionless fundamental physical constant, approximately 1/137, characterising the strength of electromagnetism.
  • Proportions and percentages — mass fractions, mole fractions and concentrations expressed as ratios.

Dimensionless units

Some dimensionless quantities are given special unit names for convenience. The radian and steradian are the SI units of angle and solid angle; the decibel expresses logarithmic ratios; and units such as parts per million (ppm) denote small dimensionless ratios. Although these carry names, the quantities themselves remain dimensionless.
