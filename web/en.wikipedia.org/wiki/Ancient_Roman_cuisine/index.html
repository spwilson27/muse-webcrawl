Ancient Roman cuisine
Region: Roman Republic and Empire
Staples: Wheat, olive oil, wine
Key source: Apicius, De re coquinaria
Signature seasoning: Garum (fermented fish sauce)

Ancient Roman cuisine changed over the long duration of Roman civilisation, from simple porridge and bread in the early Republic to elaborate imperial banquets drawing ingredients from across the empire. Food rested on the Mediterranean triad of grain, olive oil, and wine.

Meals

Romans typically ate a light breakfast (ientaculum), a small midday meal (prandium), and the main meal, cena, in the late afternoon. Wealthy diners reclined on couches in the triclinium, eating with fingers and spoons; courses ran from eggs and appetisers (gustatio) through meats and fish to fruit and sweets (secundae mensae) — hence the saying "from egg to apples".

Ingredients and flavours

Garum, a fermented fish sauce, seasoned nearly everything, alongside honey, vinegar, wine reductions, and spices such as pepper, cumin, and silphium (later asafoetida). Dormice, sea urchins, stuffed sow's udder, and flamingo tongues feature in accounts of elite excess, satirised in Petronius' Satyricon (Trimalchio's dinner). Ordinary people ate bread, cheese, olives, lentils, and food from street cookshops (thermopolia), as excavated at Pompeii.

Sources

The recipe collection attributed to Apicius, De re coquinaria, along with works by Cato, Columella, and Pliny the Elder, preserves detailed knowledge of Roman foodways, farming, and preservation.
