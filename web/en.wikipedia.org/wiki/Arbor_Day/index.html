Arbor Day (from Latin arbor, "tree") is a holiday in which individuals and groups are encouraged to plant trees. It originated in Nebraska City, Nebraska, in 1872 and is now observed in many countries, on dates suited to local planting seasons.

History

The first American Arbor Day was organised by J. Sterling Morton, a newspaper editor and later U.S. Secretary of Agriculture, who had moved to the largely treeless Nebraska Territory and promoted tree planting for shade, windbreaks, fuel, and building material. On 10 April 1872, an estimated one million trees were planted in Nebraska; prizes were offered to counties and individuals planting the most trees. Nebraska made Arbor Day a state holiday in 1885, choosing 22 April, Morton's birthday. The conservationist Birdsey Northrop carried the idea abroad, and in 1970 President Richard Nixon proclaimed the last Friday in April as National Arbor Day in the United States. The Arbor Day Foundation, founded in 1972, promotes the observance and runs programmes such as Tree City USA.

International observance

Many countries hold analogous tree-planting days: China's Arbor Day (12 March), India's week-long Van Mahotsav festival in July, Israel's Tu BiShvat, Australia's National Tree Day in late July, and Germany's Tag des Baumes (25 April). An earlier Spanish precedent is often cited: the village of Mondoñedo held a tree-planting festival in 1594, and Villanueva de la Sierra celebrated what is sometimes called the first modern Arbor Day in 1805.
