The 1910 Rogers Pass avalanche killed 62 men clearing a Canadian Pacific Railway line at Rogers Pass in British Columbia's Selkirk Mountains on the night of 4 March 1910. It remains Canada's deadliest avalanche.

Background

Rogers Pass, at 1,330 metres (4,360 ft), lay on the Canadian Pacific main line through some of the heaviest snowfall terrain in North America. Dozens of snow sheds protected the line, but slides repeatedly blocked open sections. In the winter of 1909–10, an exceptional storm cycle dropped metres of snow on the pass.

The disaster

On 4 March an avalanche from Mount Cheops buried the line west of the summit, and a work train with a rotary snowplow, a locomotive, and a crew of some 63 men was sent from Revelstoke to clear it. Shortly before midnight, as the men worked by lantern light, a second, far larger slide swept down from Mount Avalanche on the opposite side of the valley. The locomotive was thrown across the tracks, the plow buried, and the clearing party overwhelmed. Only one man, fireman Billy LaChance, who had been knocked clear, survived. The bodies of 62 men — many of them Japanese and other immigrant labourers — were recovered over the following days.

Consequences

The disaster, following earlier fatal slides at the pass, strengthened the case for avoiding the surface crossing. The Canadian Pacific drove the Connaught Tunnel beneath the pass, opening in 1916 and removing the main line from the worst slide paths. Rogers Pass is now commemorated at the Rogers Pass National Historic Site, and the 1910 avalanche remains the worst in Canadian history; the same storm week saw the even deadlier Wellington avalanche in Washington State, which killed 96.
