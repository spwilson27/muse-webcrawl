Dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway

The dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway (DCML), also known as the posterior column–medial lemniscus pathway, is a sensory pathway of the central nervous system that conveys sensations of fine touch, vibration, two-point discrimination and conscious proprioception (position and movement sense) from the skin and joints to the cerebral cortex.

Course

First-order neurons have their cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglia. Their central axons enter the spinal cord and ascend without synapsing in the dorsal (posterior) columns: fibres from the lower body travel in the medial fasciculus gracilis, and fibres from the upper body (added at about the T6 level and above) in the lateral fasciculus cuneatus.

In the caudal medulla these axons synapse on second-order neurons in the nucleus gracilis and nucleus cuneatus. Their axons sweep ventromedially as internal arcuate fibres, cross the midline (the sensory decussation) and ascend through the brainstem as the medial lemniscus, passing through the medulla, pons and midbrain.

The second-order fibres terminate in the ventral posterolateral nucleus (VPL) of the thalamus. Third-order neurons project from the thalamus through the posterior limb of the internal capsule and corona radiata to the primary somatosensory cortex of the postcentral gyrus, where the sensation is consciously perceived.

Clinical significance

Lesions of the dorsal columns cause loss of fine touch, vibration and proprioception below the lesion on the same side (before the decussation), producing sensory ataxia — an unsteady gait that worsens in the dark or with the eyes closed (a positive Romberg sign). Classic causes include tabes dorsalis (tertiary syphilis), vitamin B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration), multiple sclerosis and dorsal compression of the cord. This pattern contrasts with the anterolateral (spinothalamic) system, which carries pain and temperature and crosses within the spinal cord.
