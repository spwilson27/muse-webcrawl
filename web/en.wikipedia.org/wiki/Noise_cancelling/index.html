Noise cancelling

Noise cancelling most commonly refers to active noise control (ANC), a method for reducing unwanted sound by adding a second sound specifically designed to cancel the first through destructive interference. It is distinguished from passive noise isolation, which uses physical barriers and materials to block sound.

Principle

A noise-cancelling system uses microphones to sample ambient noise, then generates an "anti-noise" waveform of equal amplitude but opposite phase (180° out of phase). When the two waves meet, they interfere destructively and the perceived sound is reduced. The inventor Paul Lueg first described the concept in a patent filed in the 1930s; practical systems awaited fast digital signal processing.

Applications

ANC is widely used in headphones — popularized for air travellers by Bose from 1989 onward — as well as in aviation headsets, automobile cabins, and some industrial and medical settings. Feedforward systems place the microphone outside the ear cup; feedback systems place it inside; hybrid designs combine both and generally perform best.

Limitations

Cancellation works best on low-frequency, steady sounds such as engine drone. High-frequency and sudden, irregular sounds are harder to cancel because of their short wavelengths and unpredictability. ANC circuits also consume power and can introduce hiss or distortion.
