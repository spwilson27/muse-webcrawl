Mnemonic

A mnemonic device (named after Mnemosyne, the Greek goddess of memory), or memory device, is any learning technique that aids information retention or retrieval in human memory, often by associating the target information with something more easily remembered, such as a phrase, image, rhyme or spatial layout.

Types

Acronyms and acrostics

An acronym forms a pronounceable word from the initial letters of a list, while an acrostic forms a memorable sentence. Examples include "ROYGBIV" for the colours of the rainbow, and "My Very Excellent Mother Just Served Us Noodles" for the order of the planets from the Sun.

Rhymes and songs

Rhythm, rhyme and melody make verbal material stick: children learn the alphabet through song, and rules such as "i before e, except after c" are carried in rhyme.

Method of loci

The method of loci — the "memory palace" — dates to ancient Greek and Roman rhetoric, and is attributed in legend to the poet Simonides. The learner places vivid images of items to be remembered at locations along a familiar route or building, then mentally walks the route to retrieve them in order. Competitive memory athletes use this technique to memorise shuffled decks of cards and long strings of digits.

Chunking and imagery

Chunking groups items into larger meaningful units, reducing the load on working memory, whose capacity is commonly estimated at about four to seven chunks. Vivid, bizarre or interacting mental images are remembered better than abstract words, a phenomenon related to the picture superiority effect.

History

Mnemonic techniques were cultivated in classical antiquity as part of the art of memory, taught alongside rhetoric in works such as the Rhetorica ad Herennium, Cicero's De oratore and Quintilian's Institutio oratoria. In the Middle Ages and Renaissance the art of memory was elaborated by figures such as Ramon Llull, Giordano Bruno and Robert Fludd, sometimes with occult or philosophical ambitions. Modern experimental psychology studies the same techniques under the headings of elaborative encoding, dual coding and retrieval practice.

Effectiveness

Laboratory studies consistently find large benefits of imagery-based mnemonics for ordered recall and vocabulary learning (the keyword method), though benefits for comprehension and transfer are more modest. Mnemonics are widely used in education, medicine — where students memorise vast lists of symptoms, nerves and drugs — and by performers of memory feats.
