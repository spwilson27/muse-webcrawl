Twice-cooked pork

Twice-cooked pork
Alternative namesDouble-cooked pork, Hui guo rou
CourseMain course
Place of originChina
RegionSichuan
Main ingredientsPork belly, doubanjiang, douchi, garlic sprouts or leeks

Twice-cooked pork or double-cooked pork (Chinese: 回鍋肉; pinyin: huíguōròu; lit. 'meat returned to the pot') is a Chinese dish in Sichuan cuisine. It is often considered one of the most iconic dishes of Sichuan cooking. The process of cooking involves first simmering pork belly in water with spices, slicing it, and then stir-frying it in oil, typically with spicy seasonings.

Origin

The origin of twice-cooked pork is unknown. One legend holds that the dish was created in the Song dynasty by the poet Su Shi (Su Dongpo), though this is also the claimed origin of Dongpo pork. Another account traces it to the Qing dynasty, when pork offered in ancestral sacrifices was later taken home and stir-fried. The dish became firmly associated with Sichuan province and the city of Chengdu, where it is a staple of home cooking as well as restaurant menus.

Preparation

The dish is prepared in two stages, which give it its name. First, a piece of pork belly (often with the skin on) is simmered in water with ginger, scallions, and sometimes Shaoxing wine until just cooked through. After cooling, the pork is cut into thin slices. The slices are then returned to a hot wok and stir-fried until the fat renders and the edges curl slightly — a stage described as the "lamp nest" shape. Seasonings are added, classically Pixian doubanjiang (fermented chili bean paste), fermented black soybeans (douchi), and sweet flour sauce or sugar, together with garlic sprouts (leaf garlic), young leeks, or green peppers.
