Small-cell carcinoma is a type of highly malignant cancer that most commonly arises in the lung, where it accounts for roughly 15 percent of lung cancers, but can also occur at extrapulmonary sites such as the cervix, prostate and gastrointestinal tract. Under the microscope the tumour consists of small cells with scant cytoplasm, ill-defined borders and finely granular "salt-and-pepper" chromatin; the historical name "oat-cell carcinoma" refers to their appearance.

Small-cell lung cancer

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is strongly associated with cigarette smoking. It grows rapidly, metastasises early, and is initially very sensitive to chemotherapy and radiation, though it usually relapses. It is associated with paraneoplastic syndromes including the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) and ectopic Cushing syndrome, and with Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome.

Staging and treatment

SCLC is classically divided into limited stage (confined to one hemithorax and a single radiation field) and extensive stage. Limited-stage disease is treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy; extensive-stage disease with platinum-based chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy such as atezolizumab or durvalumab. Despite initial response rates, long-term survival remains poor, with five-year survival below 10 percent overall.
