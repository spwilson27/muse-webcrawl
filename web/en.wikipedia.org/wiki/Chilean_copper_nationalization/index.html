Chilean copper nationalization

The Chilean nationalization of copper refers to the process by which the Chilean government acquired control of the country's large-scale copper mining industry, culminating in the constitutional reform of July 11, 1971, under President Salvador Allende.

Background

During the 20th century, Chile's largest copper mines — including Chuquicamata, El Teniente, and El Salvador — were owned mainly by the U.S. companies Anaconda and Kennecott. Under President Eduardo Frei Montalva in the 1960s, Chile pursued "Chileanization," acquiring majority stakes in the mines.

Nationalization

In 1971, Allende's Popular Unity government proposed a constitutional amendment for full nationalization, which Congress approved unanimously. Compensation to the companies was reduced by deductions for "excess profits" earned since 1955, a calculation that led the government to claim the companies owed Chile money rather than the reverse — a move that sharply increased tensions with the United States.

Aftermath

The state copper corporation CODELCO was formally created in 1976 under the military government of Augusto Pinochet, which retained state ownership of the large mines even while pursuing free-market policies elsewhere. Copper remains central to Chile's economy, and July 11 is commemorated as the Day of National Dignity.
