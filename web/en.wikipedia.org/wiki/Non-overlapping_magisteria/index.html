Non-overlapping magisteria

Non-overlapping magisteria (NOMA) is the view, proposed by palaeontologist Stephen Jay Gould in 1997, that science and religion each represent a distinct "magisterium" — a domain of teaching authority — and that the two domains do not overlap: science covers the empirical realm of fact and theory about the natural world, while religion (in Gould's formulation) deals with questions of ultimate meaning and moral value.

Argument

Gould set out the idea in the essay "Nonoverlapping Magisteria" (1997) and the book Rocks of Ages (1999). Properly respected, he argued, the principle "cuts both ways": it forbids religion from dictating empirical conclusions, and forbids scientists from claiming that their findings settle moral or spiritual questions. Gould presented it as a basis for respectful coexistence that he said had been endorsed by figures in both camps.

Criticism

Critics such as Richard Dawkins have objected that religions in practice do make empirical claims — about miracles, origins and answered prayer — which cannot be quarantined from science, and that NOMA is a diplomatic fiction rather than a description of actual belief. Others have argued the boundary it assumes between facts and values is itself contested.
