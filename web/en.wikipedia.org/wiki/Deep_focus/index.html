Deep focus

Deep focus is a photographic and cinematographic technique using a large depth of field, so that foreground, middle ground and background are all in sharp focus. It is the opposite of shallow focus and often combined with deep-space staging, in which significant elements are placed far apart in depth.

Technique

Deep focus is achieved with a small aperture, wide-angle lens, bright lighting and (in cinema) fast film stocks or sensitive sensors. Cinematographer Gregg Toland pioneered celebrated deep-focus work with coated lenses, powerful arc lights and techniques such as the split-diopter shot, which keeps two planes sharp with a partial close-up lens.

History and theory

Jean Renoir used depth staging in the 1930s; Orson Welles and Toland made it famous in Citizen Kane (1941) and William Wyler's films such as The Best Years of Our Lives (1946). Critic André Bazin championed deep focus and the long take as more realistic and democratic than montage, letting viewers choose where to look within the frame. The style later influenced directors from Akira Kurosawa to modern filmmakers using digital cameras' inherently greater depth of field.
