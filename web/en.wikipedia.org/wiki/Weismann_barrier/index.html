Weismann barrier


Weismann's germ plasm theory: hereditary material passes only through the germ line.

The Weismann barrier, also known as Weismannism, is the idea that information cannot pass from somatic cells (the cells of the body) to germ cells (sperm and egg), and hence that characteristics acquired during an organism's lifetime cannot be inherited by its offspring. It is named after the German evolutionary biologist August Weismann (1834–1914), who articulated the concept in his germ plasm theory in the late nineteenth century.

History

Weismann distinguished between the germ plasm, the hereditary material contained in the germ cells, and the somatoplasm, which makes up the rest of the body. Because the germ line is sequestered early in development, changes to somatic cells — such as the docking of mice's tails, which Weismann performed experimentally over several generations — could not affect heredity. The theory was an important step toward rejecting the inheritance of acquired characteristics associated with Lamarckism.

Significance

The Weismann barrier provided a conceptual foundation for the modern synthesis of evolutionary biology and is related to the central dogma of molecular biology, under which information flows from DNA to protein but not in reverse. Modern research in epigenetics has shown that some acquired states can be transmitted across generations through chemical modifications of DNA and other mechanisms, but these findings are generally seen as qualifications rather than a wholesale refutation of the barrier.
