Chattel

In common law, chattel (from Middle English catel, from Old North French catel, akin to "cattle") is any tangible movable property — as distinct from real property (land and things fixed to it). Examples include furniture, vehicles, livestock, and machinery. The phrase "goods and chattels" traditionally expresses the entirety of one's movable possessions.

Chattel personal and chattel real

The law historically divided chattels into chattel personal (movable goods proper) and chattel real (an interest in land that was nevertheless treated as personal property, such as a leasehold estate). This distinction allowed the common law to keep leases out of the feudal land system while still protecting the termor's interest.

Chattel mortgage and chattel paper

A chattel mortgage is a security interest taken over movable property, now largely superseded in England and Wales by bills of sale and in the United States by Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, which governs secured transactions in personal property. Chattel paper — a record evidencing both a monetary obligation and a security interest in specific goods (as in hire-purchase and equipment leasing) — is defined and perfected under the same framework.

Chattel slavery

The term is most notorious in slavery: chattel slavery denotes a system in which enslaved people are legally classified as chattels, i.e. the moveable property of their owners, to be bought, sold, inherited and disposed of at will. It distinguished the legal basis of plantation slavery in the Americas from many earlier forms of bound labour.
