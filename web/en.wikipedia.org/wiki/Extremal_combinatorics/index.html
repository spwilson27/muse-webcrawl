Extremal combinatorics

Extremal combinatorics is a field of combinatorics which studies how large or how small a collection of finite objects (numbers, graphs, vectors, sets, etc.) can be, if it has to satisfy certain restrictions. Much of extremal combinatorics concerns classes of sets; this is called extremal set theory. For instance, in an n-element set, how many subsets of k elements can pairwise intersect one another? How many subsets can avoid containing a full arithmetic progression?

Examples

Famous results in the field include Mantel's theorem (1907), which states that a triangle-free graph on n vertices has at most ⌊n²/4⌋ edges, generalized by Turán's theorem to graphs avoiding complete subgraphs of a given size. The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem determines the largest families of k-subsets of an n-set that pairwise intersect, and Sperner's theorem gives the largest antichain of subsets of a set. The Erdős–Szekeres theorem and Ramsey theory address unavoidable order within large structures.

Methods

Extremal problems are often attacked by induction, double counting, the probabilistic method, shifting (compression) arguments, and more recently the flag algebra method developed by Alexander Razborov. Stability results describe the structure of near-extremal configurations, and supersaturation results show that exceeding an extremal threshold forces many copies of the forbidden structure.
