Monoplane

[Image placeholder: a monoplane]

A monoplane is a fixed-wing aircraft configuration with a single main plane, in contrast to a biplane or triplane, which have two or three planes stacked vertically. By the 1930s the cantilever monoplane had become the conventional configuration for nearly all fixed-wing aircraft, owing to its lower drag and structural efficiency at high speeds.

History

Some of the earliest aircraft were monoplanes: Louis Blériot crossed the English Channel in 1909 in the mid-wing Blériot XI monoplane, and the Fokker Eindecker of 1915 was the first successful monoplane fighter. Nevertheless, braced biplanes dominated the First World War because their box-like trusses gave greater strength for a given weight with the materials of the day, while early monoplane wings suffered structural failures. Advances in all-metal stressed-skin construction and thick cantilever airfoils — pioneered by Hugo Junkers, whose J 1 flew in 1915 — removed the monoplane's disadvantages, and by the mid-1930s types such as the Boeing 247 and Douglas DC-3 had made the biplane obsolete for most purposes.

Wing position

Monoplanes are classified by the position of the wing on the fuselage: low-wing, mounted at or below the fuselage bottom (typical of airliners and fighters); mid-wing, mounted near the vertical middle; shoulder-wing, mounted above the fuselage centreline; high-wing, mounted on or above the top of the fuselage (common in cargo aircraft); and parasol wing, held above the fuselage on struts.
