Marquetry

Marquetry (also spelled as marqueterie) is the art and craft of applying pieces of veneer to a structure to form decorative patterns, designs, or pictures. The technique may be applied to case furniture or even to decorative objects with smooth, veneerable surfaces. The veneers used are primarily wood varieties, but may also include materials such as turtle shell, mother of pearl, brass, and ivory.

Technique

The marquetry worker cuts the design from sheets of veneer, traditionally with a fine fret saw or knife, assembling the pieces like a jigsaw puzzle on a paper backing before gluing the whole sheet to the carcass. Sand shading — dipping pieces in hot sand to scorch the edges — gives pictorial marquetry its sense of depth. The finished surface is scraped, polished, and often finished with French polish.

History

Wood inlay was practised in ancient Egypt, and the art revived in Renaissance Italy as intarsia. Marquetry reached its height in 17th- and 18th-century France, where ébénistes such as André-Charles Boulle combined wood with tortoiseshell and brass (Boulle work) for the court of Louis XIV at Versailles. Dutch floral marquetry and English work of the Chippendale period are also celebrated.

Parquetry

Parquetry is a related technique using geometric block patterns, most familiar in parquet flooring; unlike pictorial marquetry, parquetry patterns are built from repeated geometric pieces.
