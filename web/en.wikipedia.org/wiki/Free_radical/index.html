Free radical

In chemistry, a free radical (often simply radical) is an atom, molecule, or ion that has at least one unpaired valence electron. Such species are usually highly reactive: the unpaired electron readily forms chemical bonds with other substances, or the radicals dimerise with one another. Most molecules have all electrons paired, so radicals are typically short-lived intermediates — though some, such as the triphenylmethyl radical first described by Moses Gomberg in 1900, oxygen (a diradical in its ground state), and nitric oxide, are comparatively stable.

Formation and reactions

Radicals form by homolysis of a covalent bond, by single-electron transfer, or by photolysis and thermolysis of weak bonds such as peroxides. Once formed, radicals propagate chain reactions: a radical abstracts an atom from a stable molecule, generating a new radical. This mechanism underlies free-radical polymerisation (used to make polyethylene and polystyrene), combustion, and the cracking of hydrocarbons. Radical reactions terminate when two radicals combine.

In biology

Reactive oxygen species such as the superoxide anion and hydroxyl radical are produced in aerobic metabolism. They can damage DNA, proteins, and lipids, and accumulating oxidative damage has been implicated in ageing and diseases including cancer and atherosclerosis. Cells defend themselves with enzymes such as superoxide dismutase and with antioxidants. Conversely, immune cells deliberately generate radicals to kill pathogens, and radicals serve as signalling molecules.

In the atmosphere

Radical chemistry drives much of atmospheric chemistry: hydroxyl radicals are the atmosphere's principal "detergent", removing many pollutants, while chlorine radicals from chlorofluorocarbons catalyse the destruction of stratospheric ozone.
