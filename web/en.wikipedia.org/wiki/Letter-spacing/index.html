Letter-spacing

Letter-spacing, also called tracking in typography, is the adjustment of the horizontal space between a group of letters (glyphs) in a block of text. Unlike kerning, which adjusts the space between individual pairs of letters, letter-spacing applies a uniform amount of additional or reduced space to every character in a word, line, or passage. Positive letter-spacing increases the space between characters ("tracking out"); negative letter-spacing decreases it ("tracking in" or "tight" setting).

Usage

Letter-spacing is commonly used to:

  • Improve the appearance of small capitals and of headlines set in all capitals, which look better slightly tracked out.
  • Emphasise words in German typographic tradition (Sperrsatz), where letterspaced type historically replaced italics or bold for emphasis, particularly in blackletter texts.
  • Adjust the colour and density of a text block, for example tightening display type at large sizes, where default spacing looks loose, and loosening spacing at very small sizes to aid legibility.
  • Create distinctive logotypes and mastheads.

Excessive letter-spacing in body text harms readability because it breaks up the word shapes readers recognise. Typographic guidance generally restricts letterspacing of lowercase text, and letterspacing of lowercase German emphasis fell out of use in the twentieth century.

In metal type and digital type

In hand-set metal type, letterspacing was achieved by inserting thin strips of material ("leads" and spacing material) between the sorts; the term Sperren in German reflects the insertion of these spaces. Phototypesetting and digital typesetting made the adjustment continuous and trivially easy. In CSS, the letter-spacing property controls tracking, specified in units such as em or pixels; professional layout software such as Adobe InDesign expresses tracking in thousandths of an em.

Letter-spacing and legibility

Research on reading indicates that moderate letterspacing can benefit some readers, notably some children and readers with dyslexia, by reducing visual crowding, while very wide or very tight spacing slows fluent readers. Accessibility guidelines therefore recommend that users be able to adjust letter-spacing (along with line height and paragraph spacing) without loss of content — a requirement reflected in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (criterion 1.4.12, Text Spacing).
