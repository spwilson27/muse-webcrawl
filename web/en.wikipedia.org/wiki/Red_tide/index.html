Red tide

Red tide is a common term used for a harmful algal bloom (HAB). It occurs when colonies of algae — simple plants that live in the sea and freshwater — grow out of control while producing toxic or harmful effects on people, fish, shellfish, marine mammals, and birds. The human illnesses caused by HABs, though rare, can be debilitating or even fatal. Many red tides are caused by dinoflagellates, whose pigmentation may colour the water red, brown, green or golden; not all algal blooms are red, and not all red-coloured water is toxic.

Causes

Blooms develop when temperature, light, salinity and nutrient conditions favour rapid growth of a species; nutrient enrichment from agricultural runoff and sewage can intensify them, though many blooms occur in apparently unpolluted waters and offshore. Cyst-forming dinoflagellates rest on the seabed and germinate when conditions suit. Wind, currents and upwelling concentrate cells along coasts.

Notable species and syndromes

  • Karenia brevis — causes Florida red tides in the Gulf of Mexico; its brevetoxins kill fish and manatees and aerosolise in surf, irritating human airways. Paralytic shellfish poisoning is linked instead to Alexandrium species producing saxitoxin.
  • Pseudo-nitzschia — diatoms producing domoic acid, causing amnesic shellfish poisoning; a 1987 outbreak in Canada killed three people, and blooms on the U.S. West Coast have closed crab fisheries.
  • Dinophysis and Prorocentrum — produce okadaic acid, responsible for diarrhetic shellfish poisoning.

Impacts and monitoring

Impacts include mass mortality of fish and invertebrates, deaths of seabirds, sea turtles, dolphins and manatees, contamination of shellfish beds (monitored by regulatory closure programmes in many countries), losses to tourism and fisheries, and respiratory irritation on affected beaches. Monitoring combines cell counts by microscopy, satellite ocean-colour imagery, automated imaging flow cytometry and toxin assays. Whether climate change and coastal eutrophication are increasing the global frequency of HABs remains an active research question.

History and culture

Red water and associated fish kills have been reported since antiquity — one of the plagues of Egypt in Exodus turns the Nile "to blood" — and Spanish explorers recorded events in the Gulf of Mexico in the 1500s. The term "red tide" is now discouraged by many scientists in favour of "harmful algal bloom", because tides play no necessary role.
