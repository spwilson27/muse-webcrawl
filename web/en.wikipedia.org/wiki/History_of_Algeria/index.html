History of Algeria

The history of Algeria spans from the ancient Berber (Amazigh) kingdoms of Numidia, through Roman, Vandal, Byzantine and Arab rule, the Ottoman Regency of Algiers, more than a century of French colonisation, and the independent People's Democratic Republic of Algeria proclaimed in 1962 after a long and bloody war of liberation.

Algeria, the largest country in Africa, stretches from the Mediterranean Sea deep into the Sahara.

Ancient Algeria

The region was inhabited by Berber peoples from deep antiquity. In the 3rd century BC the kingdoms of Numidia emerged, ruled by kings such as Massinissa, who unified the Massylii and Masaesyli and allied with Rome against Carthage. Under Jugurtha, Numidia fought Rome in the Jugurthine War (112–105 BC). The territory was eventually absorbed into the Roman Empire, becoming the provinces of Numidia and Mauretania Caesariensis. Roman cities such as Timgad, Tipaza and Hippo Regius (where Saint Augustine was bishop) flourished, exporting grain and olive oil.

Medieval period

The Vandals crossed into North Africa in 429 AD, followed by Byzantine reconquest under Belisarius in the 530s. Arab armies arrived in the 7th century, bringing Islam and the Arabic language. Successive Berber dynasties — the Rustamids, Zirids, Hammadids, Almoravids, Almohads and Zayyanids — ruled the region, with Tlemcen and Béjaïa becoming major centres of trade and learning.

Ottoman Regency (1516–1830)

In 1516 the corsair brothers Aruj and Hayreddin Barbarossa seized Algiers and placed it under the Ottoman Empire. The Regency of Algiers became a powerful maritime state, notorious in Europe for Barbary piracy. Algiers was bombarded by European and American fleets repeatedly, including the Anglo-Dutch attack of 1816.

French Algeria (1830–1962)

France invaded in 1830, ostensibly over the "fly whisk incident", and conquered the coast. Resistance led by Emir Abdelkader lasted until 1847. Algeria was annexed and administered as part of France itself, attracting large numbers of European settlers (the pieds-noirs), while the indigenous Muslim population was dispossessed of land and denied full citizenship. The nationalist movement grew after the Sétif massacre of 1945.

War of Independence (1954–1962)

The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched the Algerian War on 1 November 1954. The conflict, marked by guerrilla warfare, the Battle of Algiers (1956–57) and the use of torture, destabilised the French Fourth Republic. The Évian Accords of March 1962 led to a referendum, and independence was proclaimed on 5 July 1962.

Independent Algeria

Ahmed Ben Bella became the first president, succeeded by Houari Boumédiène, who pursued socialist industrialisation financed by oil and gas revenues. After riots in 1988, multiparty reforms were introduced, but the cancellation of elections won by the Islamic Salvation Front in 1991 triggered a brutal civil war (the "Black Decade") that killed an estimated 150,000–200,000 people. Abdelaziz Bouteflika, president from 1999, resigned in 2019 amid the Hirak protest movement; Abdelmadjid Tebboune was then elected president.
