A siege is a military blockade of a city or fortress with the intent of conquering by attrition or a well-prepared assault. Siege warfare is one of the oldest forms of warfare, dating to the fortified cities of the ancient Near East, and it remained a central feature of conflict until the development of modern artillery, air power, and mechanised armies.

Methods

The besieger typically encircles the target with lines of circumvallation (facing the city) and contravallation (facing outward, against relief forces). Attackers may simply starve the defenders out, or actively reduce the fortifications using siege engines — battering rams, siege towers, catapults and trebuchets — and by mining beneath walls to collapse them. Defenders countered with sorties, counter-mines, and ever more elaborate fortifications, culminating in the star forts of the gunpowder age designed by engineers such as Vauban.

Historical development

Gunpowder artillery in the 15th century made medieval curtain walls vulnerable, as at Constantinople in 1453, but trace italienne fortifications restored the balance for centuries. In the 20th century, sieges such as Leningrad (1941–1944) and Stalingrad showed that entire cities could still be encircled, though aircraft and long-range artillery changed their character. Modern sieges remain governed in part by international humanitarian law, which prohibits starvation of civilians as a method of warfare.

Siege in culture

The legendary ten-year siege of Troy, recounted in the Iliad, is among the most famous stories of the ancient world; other celebrated sieges include Alesia, Masada, Malta (1565), and Sarajevo (1992–1996).
