Pleiades (Greek mythology)

The Pleiades (Ancient Greek: Πλειάδες) were a group of seven sister nymphs in Greek mythology, the companions of Artemis and daughters of the Titan Atlas and the Oceanid nymph Pleione. They were born on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia and were the sisters of the Hyades, Hyas, and the Hesperides. Together with the seven Hyades, they were called the Atlantides, Dodonides, or Nysiades, nursemaids and teachers of the infant Dionysus.

Mythology

After Atlas was forced to carry the heavens on his shoulders, Orion began to pursue all of the Pleiades, and Zeus transformed them first into doves, and then into stars to comfort their father. The constellation of the Pleiades is said to be still pursued across the night sky by the constellation Orion.

One of the most memorable myths involving the Pleiades is the story of their sisterhood to the Hyades. When their brother Hyas was killed by a lion (or, in some accounts, a wild boar or snake), the sisters died of grief and were placed among the stars.

Merope, the youngest of the seven Pleiades, was the only one of the sisters to marry a mortal, Sisyphus, the king of Corinth. As a result, she is sometimes called the "lost Pleiad" or "invisible Pleiad" because her star is the faintest of the seven: in some accounts she hides her light in shame at having taken a mortal husband, in others because her star dimmed when Troy fell or after the death of Sisyphus.

In astronomy

The sisters gave their names to the seven brightest stars of the open star cluster known today as the Pleiades (Messier 45), in the constellation Taurus. In reality the cluster contains hundreds of stars, and under ideal conditions more than seven — sometimes nine, including the "parents" Atlas and Pleione — can be seen with the naked eye.
