Karl Popper

Sir Karl Raimund Popper (28 July 1902 – 17 September 1994) was an Austrian-British philosopher of science and political philosopher, widely regarded as one of the greatest philosophers of science of the 20th century. He is best known for his doctrine of falsifiability, his defence of the "open society", and his critique of historicism and of the classical inductivist views on the scientific method.

Life

Popper was born in Vienna into a family of Jewish origin that had converted to Lutheranism. He studied at the University of Vienna, where he took a PhD in psychology in 1928, and worked for a time as a schoolteacher. In 1934 he published Logik der Forschung (The Logic of Scientific Discovery), which brought him to the attention of the Vienna Circle, although he was critical of their verificationism.

Anticipating the Nazi threat, Popper emigrated to New Zealand in 1937, lecturing at Canterbury University College in Christchurch. There he wrote The Poverty of Historicism and The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), attacks on the idea that history follows discoverable laws and on the authoritarian thought of Plato, Hegel and Marx. In 1946 he moved to the London School of Economics, where he became professor of logic and scientific method in 1949. He was knighted in 1965.

Philosophy of science

Popper proposed falsifiability as the criterion demarcating science from non-science: a theory is scientific only if it forbids some possible observations and can in principle be refuted by them. Against induction, he argued that scientific theories are bold conjectures that can never be verified but only corroborated by surviving severe attempts at falsification. Theories that survive testing are not thereby true, but are the best approximations available, giving science its objective, progressive character.

Political philosophy

In The Open Society and Its Enemies, Popper defended liberal democracy against totalitarian ideologies, advocating "piecemeal social engineering" — gradual, reversible reforms subject to critical scrutiny — over utopian blueprints. He asked, as the fundamental question of politics, not "who should rule?" but "how can we organize institutions so that bad rulers can be removed without bloodshed?"

Legacy

Popper's students and associates at the LSE included Imre Lakatos and Paul Feyerabend, who developed his ideas in opposed directions. His work shaped debates in evolutionary biology, economics and social science, and he is frequently cited by scientists such as Peter Medawar, who called him "incomparably the greatest philosopher of science that has ever been".
