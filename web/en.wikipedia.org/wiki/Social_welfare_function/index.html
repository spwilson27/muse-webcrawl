Social welfare function

In welfare economics and social choice theory, a social welfare function—also called a social ordering, ranking, utility, or choice function—is a function that ranks a set of social states by their desirability. A social welfare function is a "process" or "aggregation" of individual "utility functions" into a societal utility function.

The notion was developed by Abram Bergson (1938) and Paul Samuelson, and is closely related to Kenneth Arrow's work on social choice.

Types

  • Utilitarian (Benthamite): W = Σ ui — the sum of individual utilities.
  • Rawlsian (maximin): W = min ui — welfare is determined by the worst-off individual.
  • Nash: W = Π ui — the product of utilities.
  • Isoelastic (Atkinson): a one-parameter family interpolating between utilitarian and Rawlsian forms, with the parameter representing inequality aversion.

Arrow's impossibility theorem

Kenneth Arrow proved in 1951 that no social welfare function can simultaneously satisfy a small set of reasonable conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship) when there are three or more alternatives — a result that shaped the field of social choice theory.

Applications

Social welfare functions underlie optimal income taxation theory (Mirrlees), cost-benefit analysis, and the construction of social indicators such as measures of inequality and poverty.
