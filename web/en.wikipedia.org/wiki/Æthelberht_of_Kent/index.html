Æthelberht (Old English: Æðelberht; c. 550 – 24 February 616) was King of Kent from about 589 until his death. The eighth-century chronicler Bede lists him as the third king in Britain to hold imperium — overlordship — over the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms south of the Humber, and he is remembered above all as the first English king to convert to Christianity, and as the issuer of the earliest surviving code of laws written in English.

Reign and overlordship

Æthelberht succeeded his father Eormenric and, according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, fought a major battle against Ceawlin of Wessex. His marriage to Bertha, a Christian Frankish princess, linked Kent to Merovingian Gaul and its trade; Kentish wealth was built on cross-Channel commerce through ports such as Fordwich and Sarre. Bede credits him with military predominance as far as the Humber, though the practical extent of his overlordship is debated by historians.

Conversion

In 597 the mission sent by Pope Gregory the Great under Augustine landed in Kent. Æthelberht — whose wife already worshipped at the church of St Martin in Canterbury — received the missionaries in the open air, by tradition beneath an oak on the Isle of Thanet, permitted them to preach, and was himself baptised, traditionally on or around Pentecost 597 (some accounts say 601). He gave Augustine land at Canterbury for what became Canterbury Cathedral and St Augustine's Abbey, and founded churches including St Paul's in London. Large numbers of his subjects were baptised, though he refrained from compelling conversion.

Law code

Around 602–603 Æthelberht issued his domboc: some ninety clauses in Old English setting compensation tariffs for injuries and offences — including the earliest English-language protections for the Church — and notable for beginning with graded fines for theft of church property. The code survives only in the 12th-century Textus Roffensis, preserved at Rochester, but is the earliest written law code of any Germanic kingdom in its vernacular.

Death and succession

Æthelberht died on 24 February 616 and was succeeded by his son Eadbald, who initially rejected Christianity before converting himself. Æthelberht was buried at St Augustine's Abbey and was later venerated as a saint, with a feast on 25 February.
