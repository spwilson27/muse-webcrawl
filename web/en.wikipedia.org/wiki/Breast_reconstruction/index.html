Breast reconstruction

Breast reconstruction is the surgical process of rebuilding the shape and appearance of a breast, most commonly in women who have undergone mastectomy as treatment for breast cancer, or prophylactically in women at high genetic risk. It involves several plastic surgery techniques that attempt to restore a breast to near-normal shape, size, and appearance. Reconstruction may be performed at the time of mastectomy (immediate) or months or years later (delayed), and it may be combined with procedures on the opposite breast to achieve symmetry.

Types

Implant-based reconstruction

The most common approach places a saline or silicone gel implant beneath the chest skin or pectoralis muscle. It is often performed in two stages: a tissue expander is inserted at mastectomy and gradually inflated with saline over weeks to stretch the skin, then exchanged for a permanent implant. Prepectoral placement, supported by acellular dermal matrix "mesh", has become increasingly common. Implant reconstruction offers shorter operations and recovery and no donor-site scar, but implants may require replacement over a lifetime and carry risks of capsular contracture, rupture, infection, and, rarely, an association with anaplastic large-cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) linked to textured devices.

Autologous (flap) reconstruction

Flap techniques rebuild the breast with the patient's own tissue, usually from the abdomen, back, buttock, or thigh. The pedicled TRAM flap transfers abdominal skin and fat on the rectus abdominis muscle; the free DIEP flap — now the most favoured abdominal technique — transfers skin and fat on perforating blood vessels while sparing the muscle, reconnected by microsurgery. The latissimus dorsi flap from the back is often combined with an implant. Autologous reconstruction tends to look and age more naturally and tolerates radiotherapy better, at the cost of longer surgery and a donor-site scar.

Nipple reconstruction and finishing procedures

Reconstruction of the nipple–areola complex is usually a final, minor stage: the nipple is formed from local skin flaps or grafts and the areola restored with tattooing (micropigmentation). Fat grafting is widely used to refine contour. Symmetry operations — reduction, lift (mastopexy), or augmentation of the healthy breast — are frequently offered.

Considerations and outcomes

The choice of technique depends on the patient's body, cancer treatment plan (particularly radiotherapy), prior surgery, and preference. Studies generally find that reconstruction is associated with improved body image and quality of life after mastectomy, though satisfaction varies by technique and expectations. In the United States, the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act of 1998 requires health plans that cover mastectomy to also cover reconstruction, including surgery on the other breast for symmetry.
