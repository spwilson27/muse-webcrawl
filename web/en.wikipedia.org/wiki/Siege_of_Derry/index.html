Siege of Derry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Siege of Derry (April – August 1689) was the first major event in the Williamite War in Ireland, in which the Protestant defenders of the city of Derry (Londonderry) withstood a siege by the Jacobite army of the deposed King James II for 105 days, under the rallying cry "No surrender."

Background

In December 1688, thirteen apprentice boys shut the gates of Derry against a Jacobite regiment, an act commemorated to this day. When James II's forces under Richard Hamilton, and later Conrad von Rosen and the king himself, advanced on the city in April 1689, the governor Robert Lundy favoured surrender and was deposed; command passed to the clergyman George Walker and Major Henry Baker, and later to John Mitchelburne.

The siege

Some 30,000 Protestant refugees crowded into the small walled city. The Jacobites blockaded the River Foyle with a boom and reduced the population by bombardment, disease and starvation; defenders subsisted on dogs, rats and tallow. Several thousand died. Relief expeditions initially failed to break the boom, but on 28 July 1689 (Old Style) the ships Mountjoy, Phoenix and Jerusalem broke through under fire and reached the city with supplies. The Jacobites burned their camp and withdrew on 1 August.

Legacy

The siege became a foundational episode in Ulster Protestant unionist tradition, commemorated annually by the Apprentice Boys of Derry. The city's walls, among the best-preserved in Europe, remain its defining landmark.
