Developmental biology

Developmental biology is the study of the process by which animals and plants grow and develop. Developmental biology also encompasses the biology of regeneration, asexual reproduction, metamorphosis, and the growth and differentiation of stem cells in the adult organism. Its central questions are how a single cell — the fertilised egg — gives rise to the ordered complexity of a multicellular organism, through cell differentiation, morphogenesis and growth.

Key concepts

Development is controlled by gene regulatory networks in which signalling molecules (morphogens) such as Wnt, Hedgehog, BMP and retinoic acid pattern the embryo, and transcription factors switch cell programmes on and off. Pattern-formation mechanisms include induction between cell groups and gradient interpretation. The discovery of conserved homeobox (Hox) genes, which pattern the body axis in animals from flies to humans, revealed that development uses a shared genetic toolkit, giving rise to the field of evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo").

Model organisms

Developmental biologists rely on model organisms including the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, the frog Xenopus, the zebrafish, the chick, the mouse, and the plant Arabidopsis thaliana.

History

The field descends from embryology: Aristotle's observations, the preformation–epigenesis debate, Spemann and Mangold's organiser experiment (1924), and the molecular revolution of the late 20th century, recognised by the 1995 Nobel Prize to Lewis, Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus for genetic control of early embryonic development.
