Load factor (electrical)

Image placeholder: a daily load curve

In electrical engineering, the load factor is defined as the average load divided by the peak load in a specified time period. It is a measure of the utilization rate, or efficiency of usage, of an electrical system. A high load factor describes loads that are more constant, while a low load factor describes loads that have a large peak relative to average consumption.

Load factor = Average load ÷ Maximum load (in a given period)

Because the average can never exceed the peak, the load factor always lies between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). An equivalent formulation uses energy consumed: load factor equals the total energy used during a period divided by the energy that would have been used had the peak demand persisted for the whole period.

Example

A commercial building with a peak demand of 100 kW that consumes 43,200 kWh in a 30-day month (720 hours) has an average load of 60 kW and therefore a load factor of 60 ÷ 100 = 0.60, or 60%. Customers with higher load factors generally pay less per unit of energy, because the fixed costs of the supply infrastructure are spread over more kilowatt-hours, and many tariffs include a demand charge based on peak load.

Significance for utilities

Electricity generation, transmission and distribution plant must be sized to meet peak demand, yet much of that capacity sits idle outside peak hours. A low system load factor therefore implies expensive, under-used assets. Utilities encourage higher load factors through time-of-use pricing, demand response programmes and off-peak tariffs, and they serve flat industrial loads preferentially. Baseload generating plant — historically coal and nuclear stations — is most economical when run continuously at a high load factor, while peaking plant handles short-lived demand spikes.

Related concepts

Load factor is related to, but distinct from, the capacity factor of a generator (actual output divided by maximum possible output) and the demand factor (maximum demand divided by total connected load). It should also not be confused with the load factor used in aviation (passenger occupancy) or structural engineering.
