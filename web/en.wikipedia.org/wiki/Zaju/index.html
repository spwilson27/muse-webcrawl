Zaju

Zaju (Chinese: 雜务, "variety plays") was the dominant form of Chinese opera drama during the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368). A zaju play typically consists of four acts (zhe) and sometimes an introductory "wedge" scene (xiezi), combining sung verse, spoken dialogue and dance to music drawn from a single mode and rhyme throughout each act.

Form

Unlike the many-voiced dramas of later Chinese theatre, in a classical Yuan zaju only one character — the male lead (zhengmo) or the female lead (zhengdan) — sings through the whole play, while the other roles speak. Each act is a suite of songs (qu) in a single musical mode, all rhyming on the same rhyme. Plots, many adapted from history, legend and courtroom stories, unfold across the four acts in a tightly compressed form. Performance took place in the entertainment quarters of Yuan cities, with troupes playing in open theatres.

Playwrights and famous plays

The Yuan era produced China's first great professional dramatists, many of them scholar-officials displaced by Mongol rule who turned to the commercial theatre. Guan Hanqing, the most prolific, wrote The Injustice to Dou E and The Orphan of Zhao is attributed to Ji Junxiang; Wang Shifu wrote the celebrated romance The Romance of the Western Chamber; Ma Zhiyuan wrote Autumn in the Han Palace; and Bai Pu wrote Rain on the Paulownia Tree. These four are traditionally grouped as the Four Great Yuan Playwrights. The Ming scholar Zang Maoxun's anthology Selection of Yuan Plays (1615–1616) preserved one hundred zaju texts, the principal surviving corpus.

Legacy

Zaju established the sung-drama conventions — role types, act structure, and the blend of aria and dialogue — from which the later chuanqi of the Ming dynasty, Kunqu, and ultimately Peking opera developed. Its plays remain central to the Chinese dramatic canon and are still staged and studied worldwide.
