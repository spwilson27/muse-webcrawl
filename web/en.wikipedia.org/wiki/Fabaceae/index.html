Fabaceae (/fəˈbeɪsi.iː/), commonly known as the legume, pea, or bean family, is a large and economically important family of flowering plants. It includes trees, shrubs and herbaceous plants, and is characterised by its fruit — a pod called a legume — and typically compound leaves. With roughly 19,500 species, it is the third-largest family of land plants after the orchids and the aster family.

Characteristics

Most members bear pods that split along two seams to release the seeds. Many species form root nodules housing nitrogen-fixing bacteria of the genus Rhizobium, which convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms the plant can use; this symbiosis makes legume crops valuable in rotations for enriching soil. Flowers range from the familiar pea-like (papilionaceous) blossoms of the Faboideae to the radial flowers of the mimosoid clade.

Classification

The APG system places Fabaceae in the order Fabales. A 2017 classification by the Legume Phylogeny Working Group recognises six subfamilies: Caesalpinioideae (which now includes the former mimosoids), Cercidoideae, Detarioideae, Dialioideae, Duparquetioideae, and Faboideae (Papilionoideae), the largest of them.

Economic importance

Legumes are among humanity's most important food plants: soybeans, common beans, peas, lentils, chickpeas, peanuts, and lupins supply a large share of global plant protein. Forage legumes such as alfalfa (lucerne) and clover underpin livestock agriculture, and the family also yields timber (rosewood), gums, dyes (indigo), and ornamentals such as wisteria and laburnum.

Distribution

The family is cosmopolitan, occurring on every continent except Antarctica, in habitats from rainforest to desert and tundra-edge. Its diversity is greatest in seasonally dry tropics and temperate regions.
