Chinese poetry

Calligraphy of a classical Chinese poem

Chinese poetry is poetry written, spoken, or chanted in the Chinese language, with a tradition extending more than two thousand years. Traditional Chinese poetry forms include shi (詩), ci (詞), and qu (曲), alongside folk traditions such as the songs collected in the Classic of Poetry (Shijing), the earliest existing anthology, compiled from works of the 11th to 7th centuries BCE.

Development

The Chu Ci ("Songs of Chu"), associated with Qu Yuan, introduced a more personal, lyrical voice. In the Han dynasty the ballad-like yuefu flourished. Poetry reached what is often considered its golden age in the Tang dynasty, with masters such as Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei perfecting regulated verse (lüshi) and quatrains (jueju), characterised by strict tonal patterns and parallelism. In the Song dynasty, the ci lyric, written to pre-existing tunes, rose to prominence with poets such as Su Shi and Li Qingzhao.

Features

Classical Chinese poetry is written in Classical Chinese, uses tonal prosody, and frequently relies on allusion, imagery drawn from nature, and compression of expression. Ability to compose poetry was for centuries a requirement of the imperial civil service examinations and a mark of the cultivated scholar-official.
