Laches (Greek: Λάχης) is an early Socratic dialogue by Plato on the nature of courage (andreia), named after the Athenian general Laches, one of Socrates' interlocutors. Like most of Plato's early dialogues, it ends in aporia — the participants discover that they cannot define the virtue they set out to explain.

Summary

Two fathers, Lysimachus (son of Aristides the Just) and Melesias (son of Thucydides), are considering whether their sons should be trained in fighting in armour (hoplomachia), and consult the generals Nicias and Laches, who give opposing verdicts and agree to abide by Socrates' judgement. Socrates redirects the discussion: the real question is care of the soul, and what the fathers need is knowledge of virtue — in particular courage, the virtue the training is supposed to instil.

The definitional search follows. Laches first defines courage as a sort of endurance of the soul, but concedes that foolish endurance is harmful and so not courageous. Nicias, drawing on Socratic teaching, proposes that courage is knowledge — knowledge of things to be feared and dared, in effect of future goods and evils. This provokes a famous difficulty: if courage is knowledge of all good and evil (past, present and future), it appears identical with the whole of virtue, not a part of it; and Laches objects that animals and children called courageous plainly lack such knowledge. The generals, unable to answer, invite Socrates to join them in seeking a teacher — the dialogue closes with the resolve that all of them, fathers included, need education.

Themes

The dialogue dramatises the contrast between the practical soldier Laches and the intellectual general Nicias — both of whom had fought with Socrates at Delium, where Socrates' own courage in retreat was renowned — and is a principal source for Socrates' ethical intellectualism and for the "unity of virtue" problem. It is also read for its critique of technical skill-teaching and its portrait of Socratic midwifery applied to education.
