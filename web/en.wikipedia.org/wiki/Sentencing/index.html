Sentencing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sentencing is the stage of criminal proceedings at which a convicted defendant is given a punishment by the court — fines, community orders, probation, imprisonment or, in retaining jurisdictions, death. Sentencing aims mix retribution (just deserts), deterrence (general and specific), incapacitation, rehabilitation and restoration, in proportions that define each era's penal politics; the "sentencing decision" allocates among them within statutory ranges, guidelines systems and appellate review.

Structures and controversies

Systems vary from fully discretionary (bounded only by statutory maxima) through guideline grids (the US federal Sentencing Guidelines, advisory since Booker (2005); England and Wales's Sentencing Council guidelines) to mandatory minima and automatic life terms — the latter two recurrently criticised for transferring discretion to prosecutors and producing gross disparities (the 100:1 crack/powder cocaine ratio, reformed to 18:1 in 2010, is the textbook US case). Racial, ethnic and class disparities in sentencing are documented across jurisdictions and drive research and reform; so do wrongful disparities by geography and judge. Victim impact statements, pre-sentence reports, guilty-plea discounts and restorative options complicate the picture further. Appeals against sentence and sentence-review bodies institutionalise the search for consistency without eliminating its tensions with individualised justice.
