Endorphin

Endorphins (contracted from "endogenous morphine") are endogenous opioid neuropeptides produced by the central nervous system and the pituitary gland. The term "endorphin" implies a pharmacological activity analogous to that of the corticosteroids or other compounds, as opposed to the anatomically or functionally specific terms used for particular compounds such as enkephalins and dynorphins. The principal endorphin, β-endorphin, is a 31-amino-acid peptide cleaved from proopiomelanocortin (POMC).

History

Opioid receptors were identified in the early 1970s, prompting the search for the body's own ligands for them. In 1975, two teams independently isolated endogenous opioid peptides: John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz reported the enkephalins in Aberdeen, Scotland, while researchers elsewhere isolated the longer peptide β-endorphin from pituitary extracts.

Physiology

Endorphins are released in response to pain, stress and vigorous exercise, and act on opioid receptors — principally the μ-opioid receptor — to inhibit the transmission of pain signals and produce feelings of euphoria. The surge of well-being following prolonged aerobic exercise, popularly called the "runner's high", was long attributed to endorphins, although more recent research also implicates endocannabinoids. Endorphin release also accompanies the consumption of certain foods, laughter, and social bonding, and endorphins are involved in the placebo analgesia response.

Types

  • α-Endorphin and γ-endorphin – shorter peptides derived from β-endorphin.
  • β-Endorphin – the most studied, produced in the pituitary and hypothalamus.
