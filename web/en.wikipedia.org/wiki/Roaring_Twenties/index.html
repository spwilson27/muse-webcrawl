Roaring Twenties

The Roaring Twenties, sometimes stylised as the Roaring '20s, refers to the decade of the 1920s in Western society and culture. It was a period of sustained economic prosperity with a distinctive cultural edge in the United States and Europe, particularly in major cities such as New York, Chicago, Paris, Berlin and London. The era is associated with jazz music, the flapper, Art Deco, cinema, radio, automobiles and a break with traditional values — and it ended abruptly with the Wall Street Crash of October 1929.

Economic prosperity

Post–First World War recovery, mass production (notably Ford's assembly lines), electrification, consumer credit and advertising fuelled a boom in automobiles, radios and household appliances. Stock markets climbed steeply, drawing in many small investors. Farmers and some industrial workers, however, shared little in the prosperity, and wealth inequality widened.

Culture and society

The Jazz Age brought syncopated music and dances such as the Charleston to wide audiences; the Harlem Renaissance flowered in New York. Women won the vote in the US (1920) and Britain (1918/1928), and the flapper embodied new freedoms in dress and behaviour. Prohibition (1920–1933) banned alcohol in the United States but gave rise to speakeasies and organised crime figures such as Al Capone. Radio broadcasting, "talking pictures" (beginning with The Jazz Singer, 1927), and mass-circulation magazines created a shared mass culture, while Charles Lindbergh's 1927 solo transatlantic flight made him an international hero.

Elsewhere in the world

Germany's "Golden Twenties" (1924–1929) saw stabilisation after hyperinflation and a flowering of Weimar culture. France called the period the années folles. Elsewhere the decade included the consolidation of the Soviet Union, rising fascism in Italy, and anti-colonial movements across Asia and Africa.

End of the era

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the ensuing Great Depression ended the boom; the phrase "Roaring Twenties" became firmly attached to the decade in retrospect, symbolising both exuberance and excess.
