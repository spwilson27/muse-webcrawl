Curtain raiser

A curtain raiser is a short play, sketch, or other performance given at the start of a theatrical programme before the main production. The term is also used metaphorically for any preliminary event — such as an undercard match in sport or an opening fixture — that precedes and builds anticipation for a principal event.

In theatre

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, an evening at the theatre typically consisted of several items: a curtain raiser, the main play, musical interludes, and an afterpiece. As audiences arrived over time, the short opening piece was considered expendable and was chosen to be light and entertaining. Playwrights often began their careers writing curtain raisers; George Bernard Shaw's early one-act works and several of Arthur Wing Pinero's short plays served this purpose. Famous examples include J. M. Barrie's The Twelve Pound Look and the early works of Noël Coward.

The custom declined in the twentieth century as theatre programmes shortened to a single play, but one-act curtain raisers are still sometimes staged before short main pieces, especially in opera and at festivals.

In sport

In sports such as rugby league, Gaelic games, and boxing, preliminary matches played before the main game at the same venue are also called curtain raisers. They commonly feature youth, reserve, or women's teams and give spectators a full programme of entertainment.
