Computer cooling

Computer cooling is required to remove the waste heat produced by computer components, to keep components within permissible operating temperature limits. Components that are susceptible to temporary malfunction or permanent failure if overheated include integrated circuits such as central processing units (CPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs), chipsets, and storage devices.

Air cooling

The most common method uses heat sinks—metal structures with fins that increase surface area—often combined with fans that move air across them. Thermal interface materials, such as thermal paste, improve contact between the chip and the heat sink. Case fans establish airflow through the enclosure.

Liquid cooling

Liquid cooling circulates a coolant through water blocks attached to hot components, carrying heat to a radiator where it is dissipated. It is used in high-performance desktops, workstations, and data centers, and can offer lower temperatures and quieter operation at the cost of greater complexity.

Other methods

Other approaches include heat pipes and vapor chambers, thermoelectric (Peltier) cooling, immersion cooling in dielectric fluids, and phase-change systems for extreme overclocking. Modern processors also use dynamic frequency scaling and thermal throttling to reduce heat generation under load.
