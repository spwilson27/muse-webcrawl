Laogai

Laogai (Chinese: 劳改; pinyin: Láogǎi), short for 改 造 ("reform through labour"), was a system of criminal justice in the People's Republic of China involving penal labour and prison farms. Modelled in part on the Soviet Gulag, the system was established under Mao Zedong after 1949 and used to detain both criminal offenders and political dissidents, who were subjected to forced labour and ideological reform.

History

Camps were set up across China in the 1950s, and during campaigns such as the Anti-Rightist Movement (1957) and the Cultural Revolution (1966–1976), large numbers of intellectuals, former officials, and alleged class enemies were sent to them, often without trial. Conditions varied but frequently involved long hours of agricultural, mining, or industrial work, inadequate food, and political study sessions. Estimates of the total number of people who passed through the system over the decades vary widely, running into the tens of millions; the researcher Harry Wu, himself a survivor of nineteen years in the camps, brought the term to international attention in the 1990s.

Distinctions

Laogai was formally distinct from laojiao ("re-education through labour"), an administrative detention system that allowed police to impose terms of up to four years without court proceedings. Laojiao was abolished in 2013. The term "laogai" itself was officially dropped from prison names in the 1990s, when camps were renamed simply as prisons, though human rights organizations continued to document forced labour in Chinese penal institutions.

Economics and criticism

Prison enterprises produced goods ranging from tea and coal to manufactured products, some of which entered export markets, prompting foreign legislation such as U.S. restrictions on importing prison-labour goods. The Chinese government maintained that labour formed part of lawful punishment and rehabilitation. Critics, including the Laogai Research Foundation founded by Harry Wu, argued the system served political repression and profit.
