Insulator (electricity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electrical insulator
Common materialsGlass, porcelain, polymers (silicone rubber, EPDM), ceramics
PropertyVery high electrical resistivity; dielectric strength typically 10–40 kV/mm
UsesOverhead power lines, substations, bushings, electronics

An electrical insulator is a material in which electric current does not flow freely. The atoms of the insulator have tightly bound electrons which cannot move easily through the material; with a resistivity orders of magnitude higher than conductors such as copper, insulators are used to support and separate electrical conductors without allowing current through themselves.

Insulators are used in electrical equipment to support and separate conductors. A true insulator's resistance breaks down at high voltages through dielectric breakdown, a limit specified by its dielectric strength. Glass, porcelain, and composite polymer materials are the most common choices for high-voltage work.

Physics of conduction in solids

In band theory, a material insulates when its valence band is completely filled and separated from an empty conduction band by a large band gap: thermal energy cannot promote electrons across the gap, so no mobile charge carriers exist. In conductors the bands overlap, and in semiconductors the gap is small enough for useful carrier generation. Ionic conduction can still occur slowly in real insulators, especially at elevated temperatures.

Applications

High-voltage insulators

Overhead transmission lines are supported on towers by strings of disc-shaped (suspension) insulators. The distinctive shed profile lengthens the surface "creepage" path so rain and contamination do not create a conductive track. Longer strings are used as voltage rises; a 765 kV line may carry strings of 30 or more discs. Polymer composite insulators, with a fiberglass core and silicone rubber sheds, are lighter and more pollution-resistant than porcelain or glass.

Other uses

  • Bushings — insulated feed-throughs that carry a conductor through a grounded barrier such as a transformer tank wall.
  • Coaxial cable and wiring — polymer insulation separates conductor from shield and protects against short circuits.
  • Electronics — dielectrics in capacitors, gate oxides in transistors, and insulating substrates.
  • Telegraph-era insulators — the small glass "CD" insulators of 19th-century lines are now widely collected.

Materials

Porcelain, made from clay, quartz (or alumina), and feldspar, has been the traditional material for high-voltage insulators: strong, weather-resistant, and cheap. Toughened glass offers visible failure indication because damaged units shatter. Since the 1960s, polymer insulators have gained share for their light weight and hydrophobic surfaces.
