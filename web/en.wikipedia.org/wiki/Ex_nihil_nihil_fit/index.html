Ex nihil nihil fit

Ex nihil nihil fit (Latin for "nothing comes from nothing") is a philosophical maxim expressing the principle that nothing can come into being out of nothing. The idea is most famously associated with the pre-Socratic philosopher Parmenides of Elea (5th century BC) and was later restated by the Roman poet and philosopher Lucretius in his didactic poem De rerum natura (On the Nature of Things), where it appears as nil posse creari de nilo — "nothing can be created from nothing."

History

Parmenides argued that what-is cannot arise from what-is-not, since non-being is unthinkable and unspeakable; from this he concluded that reality is eternal, ungenerated, and unchanging. The principle passed into later Greek philosophy and was taken up by the atomists Leucippus and Democritus, who held that the universe consists of atoms and void, and that apparent generation and destruction are merely rearrangements of what already exists.

Lucretius opened De rerum natura with the argument that if things could arise from nothing, then anything could be born from anything, without seed, season, or parent — which observation contradicts. The maxim consequently became a cornerstone of ancient natural philosophy and of the Epicurean doctrine that the totality of things is eternal.

Relation to modern physics

The principle is often compared with the modern law of conservation of energy and the conservation of mass-energy, according to which energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only transformed. Commentators note, however, that the physical vacuum of quantum field theory is not "nothing" in the philosophical sense, so modern cosmology does not straightforwardly violate the maxim.
