The Cypro-Minoan script (also called Cypro-Minoan syllabary) is an undeciphered syllabic script used on the island of Cyprus and at Ugarit on the Syrian coast during the Late Bronze Age, approximately 1550–1050 BC. The term was coined by Sir Arthur Evans because of the script's apparent derivation from the Minoan Linear A of Crete.

Corpus

The corpus is small — a few hundred inscriptions — including clay tablets from Enkomi and Ugarit, inscribed clay balls, cylinder seals, and marks on pottery and metal objects. The longest texts are tablets from Enkomi. Scholars conventionally distinguish up to three related variants (CM1, CM2, CM3) reflecting the script's development over time and place.

Status

The script remains undeciphered: the language or languages it records are unknown, though it is a leading candidate for writing Eteocypriot, a pre-Greek language of Cyprus known from later inscriptions. Its descendant, the Cypriot syllabary of the Iron Age, was used to write both Eteocypriot and Greek, and can be read — but the surviving Cypro-Minoan corpus is too small and formulaic for that relationship alone to crack it.
