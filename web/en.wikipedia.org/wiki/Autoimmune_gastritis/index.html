Autoimmune gastritis

Autoimmune gastritis
Gastric corpus mucosa with parietal cell loss
SpecialtyGastroenterology
Key featureImmune destruction of gastric parietal cells
ConsequencesAchlorhydria, vitamin B12 malabsorption, pernicious anemia, iron deficiency
MarkersAnti-parietal cell and anti-intrinsic factor antibodies

Autoimmune gastritis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the stomach in which the immune system attacks the parietal cells of the gastric corpus and fundus. Progressive loss of these acid- and intrinsic-factor-producing cells leads to mucosal atrophy, reduced or absent acid secretion (achlorhydria), and impaired absorption of vitamin B12 and iron. It is also called autoimmune atrophic gastritis or, in older literature, type A gastritis — contrasting with the antral-predominant type B gastritis caused by Helicobacter pylori.

Mechanism

The principal autoantigen is the gastric H+/K+-ATPase, the proton pump of the parietal cell. Autoreactive T cells and anti-parietal cell antibodies drive destruction of the oxyntic glands; loss of acid feedback causes hypergastrinaemia, which in turn drives enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cell hyperplasia. Intrinsic factor deficiency produces vitamin B12 malabsorption manifesting as pernicious anemia, historically the classic presentation. Iron malabsorption often appears earlier, particularly in younger patients and women.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on serology (anti-parietal cell antibodies, raised gastrin, low pepsinogen I), blood tests showing B12 or iron deficiency, and gastric biopsy showing corpus-predominant atrophy with ECL-cell hyperplasia. There is no curative therapy; treatment consists of parenteral or high-dose oral vitamin B12, iron replacement, and endoscopic surveillance because of increased risks of gastric adenocarcinoma and type 1 gastric neuroendocrine tumours.
