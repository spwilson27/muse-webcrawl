The Aṣṭādhyāyī (Devanagari: अष्टाध्यायी, "Eight Chapters"), also known as the Aṣṭādhyāyī of Pāṇini, is a foundational work of Sanskrit grammar composed by the ancient Indian grammarian Pāṇini, generally dated to the 5th or 4th century BCE. It is the earliest surviving complete grammatical description of a language, and it remains one of the most remarkable intellectual achievements of the ancient world.

The text consists of approximately 4,000 aphoristic rules called sūtras, distributed across eight chapters (adhyāya), each divided into four quarters (pāda). Together the rules generate, by derivation, the whole of Classical Sanskrit morphology and a large part of its phonology and syntax. The Aṣṭādhyāyī did not merely describe Sanskrit: in the Brahmanical tradition it effectively defined correct Sanskrit, and its study became obligatory for scholars for more than two millennia.

Structure and method

Pāṇini's grammar is organised as a tightly integrated system of rules that apply in a specified order. Fourteen introductory aphorisms, the Śivasūtras, arrange the sounds of Sanskrit into classes identified by marker consonants (anubandha), allowing sounds to be referred to by compact abbreviations called pratyāhāra. Rules make extensive use of inheritance (anuvṛtti), in which elements of an earlier sūtra are understood to carry over into later ones, giving the text its famously compressed, almost algebraic character.

The grammar distinguishes primitive roots (dhātu), from which verb forms are derived, and nominal stems (prātipadika), to which case endings are attached. Derivational processes include affixation, vowel gradation (guṇa and vṛddhi), reduplication, and the euphonic combination rules known as sandhi. Pāṇini also handles compounding, accent, and the syntax of case relations (kāraka theory), making the work a comprehensive generative system.

Commentary tradition

The Aṣṭādhyāyī attracted an immense commentarial literature. The aphorisms and critical notes (vārttika) of Kātyāyana (c. 3rd century BCE) and the great commentary (Mahābhāṣya) of Patañjali (c. 2nd century BCE) together with Pāṇini's text form the "three sages" (trimuni) of the grammatical tradition. Later works such as the Kāśikā (7th century CE) reorganised the sūtras topically, and Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita's Siddhānta Kaumudī (17th century) became the standard teaching arrangement.

Influence

When the Aṣṭādhyāyī became known in Europe in the 19th century it profoundly influenced the emerging science of linguistics. Its methods anticipated features of modern formal language theory: rules written in a metalanguage, ordered rule application, and recursive derivation. Scholars such as Leonard Bloomfield praised Pāṇini's grammar as "one of the greatest monuments of human intelligence," and its structure has been compared to that of a computer program. The American linguist Paul Kiparsky and others have drawn explicit parallels between Pāṇinian grammar and modern generative grammar.
