Westward expansion

Westward expansion of the United States
Period:Roughly 1803–1912
Key events:Louisiana Purchase (1803); Mexican Cession (1848); California Gold Rush (1848–55); Homestead Act (1862); transcontinental railroad (1869)
Ideology:Manifest destiny
Consequences:Dispossession of Native Americans; slavery debates; new states

Westward expansion was the 19th-century movement of settlers across North America that carried the United States from the Atlantic to the Pacific. Between the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 and the admission of Arizona in 1912, the nation acquired and organised the vast territories of the West, transforming its economy, politics and demographics.

Territorial growth

The Louisiana Purchase doubled the country's size for $15 million. Florida followed (1819), then Texas annexation (1845), the Oregon Country settlement with Britain (1846), and the Mexican Cession after the Mexican–American War (1848), which added California and the Southwest. The Gadsden Purchase (1853) and Alaska (1867) completed the mainland outline.

Migration and settlement

Hundreds of thousands travelled the Oregon, California and Mormon trails. The California Gold Rush drew "forty-niners" from around the world. The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres to settlers who farmed them, and the First Transcontinental Railroad (1869) bound the coasts together, collapsing the cross-country journey from months to days.

Native American displacement

Expansion came at catastrophic cost to Native peoples: the Indian Removal Act (1830) forced eastern nations west on the Trail of Tears; plains wars, broken treaties, reservation confinement and the near-extermination of the bison destroyed Indigenous ways of life. The 1890 Wounded Knee massacre and the census declaration of a "closed" frontier mark the conventional end of the era.

Legacy

The question of whether new states would permit slavery drove the sectional crisis leading to the Civil War (Missouri Compromise, Kansas–Nebraska Act). Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 "frontier thesis" argued the frontier had shaped American democracy and character — an interpretation historians continue to revise.
