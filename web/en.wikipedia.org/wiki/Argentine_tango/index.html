Argentine tango

Argentine tango is a partner dance and social dance that originated in the late 19th century in the working-class port neighbourhoods of Buenos Aires, Argentina, and Montevideo, Uruguay, along the Río de la Plata. It developed from a fusion of African rhythms, European partner dances, and local forms such as the milonga and habanera, danced by immigrant and working-class communities. In 2009 UNESCO added tango to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The dance

Unlike the standardised ballroom tango, Argentine tango is largely improvisational: partners dance in an embrace — close or open — and the leader guides the follower through walking steps, turns (giros), pivots (ochos), ganchos, and pauses, responding to the phrasing of the music. Social tango is danced at milongas (tango dance events), where customs such as the cabeceo (an invitation by eye contact and a nod) and the tanda (a set of songs danced with one partner) structure the evening.

Music

Tango music is typically played by an orquesta típica built around the bandoneón (a large concertina-like instrument), violins, piano, and double bass. The "Golden Age" of tango in the 1930s and 1940s produced legendary orchestras led by Juan D'Arienzo, Carlos Di Sarli, Aníbal Troilo, and Osvaldo Pugliese, whose recordings still dominate milonga playlists. Singer Carlos Gardel became tango's most famous voice, and Astor Piazzolla later revolutionised the genre with his nuevo tango compositions.

Styles

Social styles include the close-embrace milonguero style of the crowded downtown dance halls, tango salón, and the more open tango nuevo influenced by late-20th-century analysis of tango movement. Show tango (tango fantasia) is a theatrical, choreographed form with lifts and dramatic figures, distinct from the social dance floor.
