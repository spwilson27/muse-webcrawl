The FTSE 100 Index, nicknamed the "Footsie", is a share index of the 100 companies listed on the London Stock Exchange with the highest market capitalisation. It is maintained by FTSE Russell, a subsidiary of the London Stock Exchange Group, and is the most widely used barometer of the performance of the UK's largest listed companies — although its constituents earn the majority of their revenues outside the United Kingdom, making it a global as much as a British indicator.

History

The index began on 3 January 1984 with a base level of 1,000 points, replacing the narrower FT 30 index as the standard market measure. It first closed above 7,000 in 2015 and crossed 8,000 in early 2023. Notable falls include Black Monday (1987), the bursting of the dot-com bubble (2000–2003), the financial crisis of 2008, the Brexit referendum shock (2016) and the COVID-19 crash (2020).

Methodology

Constituents are reviewed quarterly, in March, June, September and December. Companies ranking in the top 90 by market value are automatically included and those falling below 110th are removed, buffering churn. The index is weighted by free-float-adjusted market capitalisation and calculated in real time during London trading hours. The 100 companies together represent roughly 80 per cent of the total market capitalisation of the London Stock Exchange; the next tier forms the FTSE 250 Index of mid-cap companies.

Composition

The index has long been dominated by oil and gas (Shell, BP), banking (HSBC, Barclays, Lloyds), pharmaceuticals (AstraZeneca, GSK), mining (Rio Tinto, Anglo American) and consumer goods (Unilever). A persistent observation in financial commentary is the FTSE 100's relative scarcity of large technology companies compared with US indices such as the S&P 500, and its consequently higher dividend yield — it has historically yielded around 3.5–4 per cent. Because most constituent earnings are in foreign currency, the index level often moves inversely to sterling: a weaker pound tends to lift the index.
