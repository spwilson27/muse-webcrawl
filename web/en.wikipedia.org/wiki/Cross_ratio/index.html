The cross ratio, also called the anharmonic ratio or double ratio, is a number associated with an ordered quadruple of points on a line (or on a circle or conic). In coordinates, for four points with positions a, b, c, d on the real line, the cross ratio is

(A, B; C, D) = (CA / CB) / (DA / DB)

using signed lengths. Equivalently, in coordinates, (c−a)(d−b) / ((c−b)(d−a)).

Invariance

The cross ratio is the fundamental invariant of projective geometry: it is preserved by projective transformations of the line and, in the complex plane, by Möbius transformations. This invariance, already exploited by Pappus of Alexandria and revived by 19th-century geometers, makes the cross ratio the basic tool for defining projective coordinates.

Special values

Permuting the four points yields six generally distinct values: λ, 1/λ, 1−λ, 1/(1−λ), λ/(λ−1), and (λ−1)/λ. A quadruple with cross ratio −1 is called harmonic. The cross ratio is real if and only if the four points are concyclic or collinear (in the complex setting). It also defines a metric in hyperbolic geometry via the logarithm of the cross ratio (the Cayley–Klein model).

Applications

Cross ratios appear in computer vision (projective invariants for recognition), in the theory of elliptic integrals and modular functions, and in one-dimensional conformal field theory, where four-point correlation functions depend on a cross ratio.
