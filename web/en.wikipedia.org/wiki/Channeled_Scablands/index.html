The Channeled Scablands are a relatively barren and soil-free region of interconnected rock-cut basins and coulees in eastern Washington state, United States. They were scoured by a series of cataclysmic floods — the Missoula Floods — during the last ice age, roughly 18,000 to 13,000 years ago, when the ice dam holding back Glacial Lake Missoula repeatedly failed and released enormous volumes of water across the Columbia Plateau.

Geology

The floodwaters stripped away the loess soil covering the Columbia River Basalt and carved an anastomosing network of channels, dry cataracts such as Dry Falls, giant current ripples, gravel bars and rock basins. Underlying flood deposits and erratic boulders rafted in icebergs are found throughout the region. The floods drained through Wallula Gap and down the Columbia River Gorge to the Pacific Ocean.

History of research

Geologist J Harlen Bretz proposed in the 1920s that the scablands were carved by a single gigantic flood, a hypothesis that contradicted the uniformitarian orthodoxy of the day and was resisted for decades. Evidence assembled by Bretz, J. T. Pardee and others — including the giant ripples of Glacial Lake Missoula's shoreline — eventually vindicated the flood theory, and Bretz received the Penrose Medal in 1979. The scablands have since served as an analogue for flood-carved channels observed on Mars.

Features

  • Dry Falls — a 3.5-mile-wide, 400-foot-high fossil cataract.
  • Grand Coulee — a 50-mile-long coulee now partly occupied by Banks Lake.
  • Palouse Falls — where the Palouse River was diverted by the floods.
  • Giant current ripples — gravel dunes up to 15 m high formed by the flood currents.
