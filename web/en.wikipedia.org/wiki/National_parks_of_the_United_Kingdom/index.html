National parks of the United Kingdom are areas of relatively undeveloped and scenic landscape designated under the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 (in England and Wales) and the National Parks (Scotland) Act 2000. Unlike national parks in many other countries, most land within UK parks is privately owned and farmed, and the parks contain towns and villages; they are classified by the IUCN as Category V protected landscapes rather than wilderness areas.

Purposes

In England and Wales, national park authorities pursue two statutory purposes: conserving and enhancing the natural beauty, wildlife, and cultural heritage of the parks, and promoting opportunities for public understanding and enjoyment of their special qualities. Where these conflict, conservation takes precedence under the Sandford Principle. Scottish parks have four aims, additionally including promoting sustainable use of natural resources and sustainable economic and social development of local communities.

The Cairngorms is the largest UK national park at about 4,528 km², and the Lake District — a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2017 — receives the most visitors, roughly 19 million a year. There are no national parks in Northern Ireland, though the Mourne Mountains have long been proposed. Access campaigns by groups such as the Ramblers, tracing back to the 1932 Kinder Scout mass trespass in what became the Peak District, were instrumental in creating the parks and later the right to roam under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000.
