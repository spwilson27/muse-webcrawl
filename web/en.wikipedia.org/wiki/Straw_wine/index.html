Straw wine

Straw wine, or raisin wine, is a wine made from grapes that have been dried to concentrate their juice. The classic method, known in French as passerillage, involves spreading harvested bunches on mats of straw or reeds — or hanging them from rafters — for weeks or months in a well-ventilated room. As the grapes lose water, their sugars, acids and flavours concentrate, producing a sweet, high-alcohol dessert wine after slow fermentation.

History

Drying grapes for wine is ancient: raisin wines were made around the Mediterranean in classical antiquity, including on Cyprus and the Aegean islands, and the technique survived in many European regions. Writers of Roman agriculture described wines made from grapes dried on mats in the sun.

Regional examples

  • France: vin de paille from the Jura, made mainly from Chardonnay, Savagnin and Poulsard; also produced in small quantities in Hermitage and elsewhere.
  • Italy: passito wines such as Vin Santo of Tuscany, Recioto della Valpolicella and Recioto di Soave, Sciacchetrà in the Cinque Terre, and the passitos of Pantelleria.
  • Austria: Strohwein or Schilfwein, a prädikat category.
  • Greece and Cyprus: Commandaria of Cyprus, one of the oldest named wines, and passito wines of Santorini and Samos.
  • Spain: raisin wines from Málaga and Alicante, including Fondillón made from overripe Monastrell.

Production

Grapes are picked slightly underripe to retain acidity, then dried for periods ranging from a few weeks to over six months. Water loss of 30–40% is typical. Fermentation of the resulting dense must is slow and often stops naturally, leaving residual sugar.
