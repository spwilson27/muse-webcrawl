Political participation

[Image: Citizens attending a public demonstration, one form of political participation]

Political participation refers to the voluntary activities by which citizens seek to influence government, politics and public policy. It is a central concept in political science, studied both as an indicator of democratic health and as a channel through which inequalities of income, education and status can translate into unequal political influence.

Forms

Conventional participation includes voting — the most common act in democracies — campaigning, donating money, joining parties and interest groups, contacting officials, and standing for office. Unconventional participation includes protests, demonstrations, boycotts, petitions, strikes and occupations; political scientists once treated these as marginal, but they are now widespread and often used by the same engaged citizens who vote. Digital participation — discussing politics online, signing e-petitions, crowdfunding campaigns and sharing political content — has blurred the boundary between expression and action, prompting debate about low-effort engagement ("slacktivism") and its real-world effects.

Determinants

Research consistently finds participation rises with education, income and age (voting turnout being lowest among the young), and is shaped by mobilisation: parties, unions, churches and civic groups recruit participants, so participation tracks organisational life. Institutional rules matter too — compulsory voting raises turnout dramatically, registration requirements lower it (see Voter registration), and proportional electoral systems tend to produce higher turnout than plurality ones. The influential "civic voluntarism model" of Verba, Schlozman and Brady explains participation through resources (time, money, civic skills), psychological engagement, and recruitment networks.

Inequality and consequences

Because participants differ systematically from non-participants, governments hear disproportionately from the affluent and educated — a pattern scholars call participatory inequality. Low and falling turnout in many established democracies since the late 20th century has prompted concern about legitimacy, alongside research suggesting that declining party membership has partly been offset by growth in protest and issue-based activism.
