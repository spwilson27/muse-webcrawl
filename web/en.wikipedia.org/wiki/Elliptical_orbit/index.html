Elliptical orbit

In astrodynamics or celestial mechanics, an elliptical orbit or eccentric orbit is an orbit with an eccentricity greater than 0 and less than 1. In a gravitational two-body problem with negative specific orbital energy, both bodies follow similar elliptical orbits with the same orbital period around their common barycenter. Also, one body may be in an elliptical orbit around the other if the system's geometry allows it.

In the special case of a Keplerian ellipse, an object in an elliptical orbit travels in a plane, with one of the foci occupied by the central body. The Kepler's laws of planetary motion describe the motion of planets around the Sun, all of which follow nearly elliptical paths.

Characteristics

Under standard assumptions, the specific orbital energy of an elliptical orbit is negative. The semi-major axis a of the ellipse and the eccentricity e define the orbit's size and shape. The distance from the central body varies between the periapsis (closest approach) and apoapsis (farthest point).

The orbital speed is given by the vis-viva equation:

v2 = μ(2/r − 1/a)

where μ is the standard gravitational parameter, r is the current radial distance, and a is the semi-major axis.

Examples

Most artificial satellites around Earth are in elliptical orbits, including Molniya orbits used for communications over high northern latitudes. The planets of the Solar System have low-eccentricity elliptical orbits; Mercury has the most eccentric planetary orbit at about 0.206.
