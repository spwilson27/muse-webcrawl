Robert Siodmak

Robert Siodmak (8 August 1900 – 10 March 1973) was a German film director who also worked in France and Hollywood. An émigré who fled the Nazis, he became one of the masters of film noir in 1940s Hollywood, directing classics such as Phantom Lady (1944), The Spiral Staircase (1946), The Killers (1946) and Criss Cross (1949), before returning to Europe in the 1950s.

Early career in Germany and France

Born in Dresden into a Jewish family (he was born during his parents' stay in the United States, a fact that later eased his emigration), Siodmak worked as a bank clerk and editor before co-directing the experimental silent film People on Sunday (1930) with Edgar G. Ulmer, from a script by his brother Curt and Billy Wilder — a landmark of late Weimar cinema. After the Nazi takeover in 1933 he moved to Paris, directing French films including Le Sexe faible and Pièges (1939), then fled to Hollywood in 1939.

Hollywood and film noir

At Universal, Siodmak moved from B-pictures such as Son of Dracula (1943) to a run of noirs defined by German Expressionist lighting, fatalism and psychological dread. The Killers, adapted from Ernest Hemingway's short story, made stars of Burt Lancaster and Ava Gardner and earned Siodmak his only Academy Award nomination for Best Director. His other films of the period include Christmas Holiday (1944), The Suspect (1944), The Strange Affair of Uncle Harry (1945) and the swashbuckler The Crimson Pirate (1952).

Return to Europe

Frustrated with Hollywood, Siodmak returned to Europe in the 1950s, winning the Golden Bear at Berlin for Die Ratten (1955) and directing in Germany, France and Britain; his later work included the Karl May western Old Surehand and the peplum The Battle of Rome (1968). He died in Locarno in 1973. Critics now rank him, with Fritz Lang, as the émigré director who most shaped the visual style of film noir.
