Anna Karenina

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Anna Karenina in a black ball dress at the Moscow ball, in an early illustration: the moment Vronsky's admiration becomes scandal]

Anna Karenina (Russian: Анна Каренина) is a novel by Leo Tolstoy, published in instalments between 1875 and 1877 and in book form in 1878, and widely acclaimed as one of the greatest novels ever written — Tolstoy himself called it his first true novel. Set against a panorama of imperial Russian society, it interweaves the adulterous passion of Anna Karenina and Count Vronsky with the courtship and marriage of Konstantin Levin, Tolstoy's most autobiographical character.

The novel opens with its celebrated observation that happy families are all alike while every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way — a summary (in the standard English rendering) of the two household stories it will tell in counterpoint.

Plot in outline

Anna, married to the stiff statesman Karenin, meets the cavalry officer Vronsky at a Moscow station shadowed by a railway death; their affair scandalises Petersburg, breaks her marriage and isolates her, and as Vronsky's ardour is hemmed in by society's rules and her own jealousy, Anna is driven toward despair and the railway line. Levin, rejected then accepted by Kitty Shcherbatskaya, meanwhile works his land, loses and finds faith, and arrives at a private, hard-won peace — the novel's second centre of gravity.

Reception

Dostoevsky hailed it in his lifetime as flawless; Matthew Arnold's essay helped found Tolstoy's English reputation. Nabokov called it 'the flawless magic of Tolstoy's style' at its peak. Its scale — some 800 pages and dozens of fully realised characters, from ballrooms to hayfields — made it the standard against which the European novel is measured, and its ending among the most debated in fiction.
