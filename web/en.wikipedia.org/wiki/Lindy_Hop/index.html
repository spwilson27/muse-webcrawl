The Lindy Hop is an American dance that was born in the African-American communities of Harlem, New York City, in 1928 and has evolved since then. It was very popular during the swing era of the late 1930s and early 1940s. Lindy is a fusion of many dances that preceded it or were popular during its development, but is mainly based on jazz, tap, breakaway, and Charleston. It is frequently described as a jazz or swing dance and is a member of the swing dance family.

History

The dance developed at the Savoy Ballroom, an integrated ballroom in Harlem, where dancers competed to big bands led by musicians such as Chick Webb. According to tradition, the name commemorates Charles Lindbergh's solo transatlantic flight of 1927: dancer "Shorty" George Snowden is said to have told a reporter that he was doing the "Lindy Hop". The dance's basic step, the swing-out, separates the partners into an open position — the "breakaway" that distinguished Lindy from earlier closed-position dances — and its most spectacular development was the aerial (or air step), pioneered by Frankie Manning in 1935, in which the follower is thrown or lifted through the air. Manning's troupe, Whitey's Lindy Hoppers, performed in films such as Hellzapoppin' (1941) and toured internationally.

Decline and revival

Lindy's popularity waned after the Second World War as musical tastes changed, and related dances such as jitterbug, rock and roll and West Coast Swing carried parts of its vocabulary forward. Beginning in the 1980s, dancers in the United States, Sweden and Britain sought out surviving Savoy dancers, including Manning and Norma Miller, sparking a worldwide revival; the Herräng Dance Camp in Sweden became a major centre of the renewed scene, and Lindy Hop is now danced socially and competitively around the world.

Characteristics

Lindy Hop is danced in 6- and 8-count patterns to swung rhythms, emphasising improvisation, connection between partners, and musicality. Core figures include the swing-out, Lindy circle, Charleston variations, Texas Tommy and various turns and dips; experienced dancers add aerials in performance and competition but not usually on social floors.
