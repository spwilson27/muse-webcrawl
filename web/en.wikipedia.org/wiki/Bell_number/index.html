In combinatorics, the Bell numbers count the possible partitions of a set: the number of ways a set of n labelled elements can be divided into non-empty, unlabelled subsets. The nth Bell number, written Bn, also equals the number of equivalence relations on an n-element set. The sequence begins 1, 1, 2, 5, 15, 52, 203, 877, 4140, 21147 (with B0 = 1 for the single partition of the empty set).

Examples

A set of one element has only one partition, so B1 = 1. A three-element set {a, b, c} can be partitioned in five ways: all together; a alone with {b, c}; b alone with {a, c}; c alone with {a, b}; or all separate — hence B3 = 5. The numbers grow rapidly: B10 = 115,975 and B15 is over 1.3 billion.

Formulas

Bell numbers can be computed by summing the Stirling numbers of the second kind, which count partitions into a fixed number of blocks: Bn = Σk S(n, k). They satisfy the recurrence Bn+1 = Σk=0..n C(n, k) Bk, which underlies the simple "Bell triangle" (also called the Aitken array) construction. Dobiński's formula gives Bn = (1/e) Σk≥0 kn/k!, which also shows that the Bell numbers are the moments of a Poisson distribution with mean 1.

History

Set partitions were studied in medieval Japan in connection with The Tale of Genji, and the numbers were investigated by Srinivasa Ramanujan before being popularised and named after the mathematician and science-fiction writer Eric Temple Bell, who wrote about them in the 1930s. Bell numbers appear in problems ranging from the classification of rhyme schemes — a poem of n lines can rhyme in Bn ways — to the counting of ancestral recombination patterns in genetics.
