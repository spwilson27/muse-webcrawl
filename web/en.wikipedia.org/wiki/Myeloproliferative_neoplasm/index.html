Myeloproliferative neoplasm

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs), formerly called myeloproliferative diseases or disorders, are a group of clonal blood cancers in which the bone marrow produces excess mature blood cells of the myeloid lineages. The classic Philadelphia chromosome–negative MPNs are polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, and primary myelofibrosis; chronic myeloid leukaemia, driven by the BCR-ABL fusion gene, is classified within the same family in modern WHO terminology.

Classification

William Dameshek proposed the concept of "myeloproliferative disorders" in 1951, grouping conditions that share proliferation of bone marrow elements. The World Health Organization classifies MPNs among myeloid neoplasms and includes:

  • Chronic myeloid leukaemia (BCR-ABL1–positive)
  • Polycythemia vera — overproduction mainly of red blood cells
  • Essential thrombocythemia — overproduction mainly of platelets
  • Primary myelofibrosis — proliferation with progressive bone marrow scarring
  • Rarer entities such as chronic neutrophilic leukaemia, chronic eosinophilic leukaemia, and MPN, unclassifiable

Genetics

The discovery in 2005 of the JAK2 V617F mutation — present in nearly all patients with polycythemia vera and roughly half of those with essential thrombocythemia and primary myelofibrosis — transformed the field. Most JAK2-negative patients carry mutations in CALR (calreticulin) or MPL (the thrombopoietin receptor). These "driver" mutations constitutively activate JAK-STAT signalling, the pathway targeted by JAK inhibitor drugs such as ruxolitinib.

Symptoms and complications

Many patients are asymptomatic and diagnosed on routine blood counts. Others develop fatigue, itching (especially after hot showers, in polycythemia vera), night sweats, weight loss, gout, and enlargement of the spleen. The main dangers are blood clots (including unusual sites such as the hepatic veins — Budd–Chiari syndrome), bleeding, and, over years, transformation to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukaemia.

Treatment

Treatment is usually aimed at preventing thrombosis and controlling symptoms rather than cure: low-dose aspirin, venesection (phlebotomy) to keep haematocrit below 45% in polycythemia vera, cytoreductive agents such as hydroxycarbamide or interferon alfa, and JAK inhibitors for myelofibrosis and resistant cases. Allogeneic stem cell transplantation is the only curative option, reserved for suitable higher-risk patients.
