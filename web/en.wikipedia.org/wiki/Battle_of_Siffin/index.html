Battle of Siffin

The Battle of Siffin (Arabic: وقعة صفين) was fought in 657 CE between the forces of the fourth caliph, Ali ibn Abi Talib, and Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, the governor of Syria, on the banks of the Euphrates at Siffin, near modern Raqqa in Syria. It was a central episode of the First Fitna (the first Muslim civil war) and a foundational event in the eventual Sunni–Shia split.

Background

After the assassination of the caliph Uthman in 656, Ali succeeded him but was challenged by Mu'awiya, Uthman's kinsman, who demanded that the killers be punished before he would pledge allegiance. Ali had already defeated a rebellion at the Battle of the Camel when he marched north to confront Mu'awiya in Syria.

The battle

The two armies skirmished for weeks before full battle was joined in July 657. Among those killed on Ali's side was Ammar ibn Yasir, a revered companion of Muhammad, whose death was widely read as confirmation that Mu'awiya's party was in the wrong. As Ali's forces appeared to gain the upper hand — tradition credits the ferocity of al-Ashtar's attacks — Mu'awiya's soldiers, on the advice of Amr ibn al-As, raised copies or leaves of the Qur'an on their lances and called for the dispute to be settled by the Book of God rather than the sword.

Arbitration and consequences

Pressed by his own troops, especially the Qur'an-readers (qurra), Ali accepted arbitration. The arbiters, Abu Musa al-Ash'ari for Ali and Amr ibn al-As for Mu'awiya, met in 658 but failed to restore unity; the arbitration instead weakened Ali's position. A faction of Ali's supporters, outraged that he had submitted God's judgment to men, seceded and became the Kharijites, whose slogan "judgment belongs to God alone" defined their movement. The inconclusive outcome at Siffin thus entrenched the division of the Muslim community and set the stage for Mu'awiya's eventual establishment of the Umayyad Caliphate after Ali's assassination in 661.
