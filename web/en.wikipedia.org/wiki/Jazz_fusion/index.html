Jazz fusion (also fusion or jazz-rock fusion) is a music genre that emerged in the late 1960s in the United States, combining the improvisation and harmonic sophistication of jazz with the amplified instruments, backbeats, and energy of rock, and drawing elements from funk, rhythm and blues, Latin music, and later electronic music. Pioneers included Miles Davis, whose albums In a Silent Way (1969) and Bitches Brew (1970) crystallised the style, and Davis alumni who formed the leading fusion bands.

History

The genre was born when jazz musicians began using electric instruments: Tony Williams's Lifetime, Larry Coryell's Eleventh House, and Miles Davis's electric bands. Key ensembles of the 1970s included Weather Report (Joe Zawinul, Wayne Shorter, Jaco Pastorius), Return to Forever (Chick Corea), the Mahavishnu Orchestra (John McLaughlin), and Herbie Hancock's Headhunters, whose self-titled 1973 album fused jazz with funk. In Britain, Soft Machine, Brand X, and Ian Carr's Nucleus developed a European variant.

By the early 1980s, critics noted a commercial, smoother offshoot — smooth jazz and "fuzak" — and many originals disowned the label. Yet fusion persisted: jazz-funk, acid jazz, and the jazz-rock revivals of later decades all descend from it, and musicians such as Pat Metheny, John Scofield, and the Snarky Puppy collective have kept the hybrid tradition prominent.

Characteristics

Fusion typically features extended compositions, odd time signatures, complex chord changes, and long improvised solos played on electric instruments — often with prominent bass guitar (Jaco Pastorius being the archetype) and synthesisers. Drumming blends jazz ride-cymbal patterns with rock backbeats. Later developments absorbed African, Latin, and South Asian musics, and "fusion" became an umbrella term for any jazz hybrid.
