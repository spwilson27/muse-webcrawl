Unitary state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A unitary state is a sovereign state governed as a single entity in which the central government is the supreme authority. Any administrative divisions (subnational units) exercise only those powers that the central government chooses to delegate, and their existence and competences can in law be altered or abolished by it — in contrast to a federation, where the division of powers is constitutionally entrenched.

Characteristics and examples

Most of the world's states are unitary, including the United Kingdom, France, Japan, China, Italy, Spain and most smaller countries. Unitary systems range from highly centralised (France's historic prefectoral tradition) to strongly devolved (the UK since 1998, where Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own legislatures — though parliamentary sovereignty formally preserves Westminster's supremacy, a tension explored in constitutional debate). Spain's "State of Autonomies" is sometimes called quasi-federal for the depth of its regional powers within a unitary constitution.

Arguments

Advocates cite uniform standards, simpler accountability and economies of scale; critics cite insensitivity to regional diversity and distance from citizens — pressures that have driven devolution and decentralisation reforms in many unitary states, and federalisation in some (Belgium moved from unitary to federal between 1970 and 1993).
