Haematopoiesis (also spelled hematopoiesis; from Greek haima, "blood", and poiesis, "to make") is the formation of blood cellular components. All cellular blood components are derived from haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). In a healthy adult human, roughly 1011–1012 new blood cells are produced daily in order to maintain steady-state levels in the peripheral circulation — replacing short-lived cells, particularly neutrophils and red blood cells, which survive on average only about a day and about 120 days respectively.

Sites of haematopoiesis

In developing embryos, blood formation occurs first in aggregates called blood islands in the yolk sac. As development progresses, it moves to the spleen, liver and lymph nodes, and when bone develops, the bone marrow takes over most haematopoiesis. In adults, production of red blood cells and platelets occurs in the red marrow of the axial skeleton — the pelvis, sternum, cranium, ribs, vertebrae and scapulae — and proximal ends of the long bones; production in other bones declines with age as marrow becomes fatty (yellow). In disease states, the liver and spleen may resume production (extramedullary haematopoiesis).

Lineages

Pluripotent HSCs divide into two main progenitor families:

  • Common myeloid progenitors, giving rise to erythrocytes (red blood cells), megakaryocytes (which shed platelets), granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), monocytes/macrophages, mast cells and dendritic cells.
  • Common lymphoid progenitors, giving rise to T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes and natural killer cells. T-cell precursors migrate to the thymus to mature; B cells mature in the bone marrow in mammals.

Differentiation is directed by growth factors and cytokines — erythropoietin for red cells, thrombopoietin for platelets, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) for neutrophils — and by transcription factors (e.g. GATA1, PU.1) that progressively restrict progenitors to single lineages and remodel their chromatin.

Stem cells and the niche

Long-term HSCs sit at the apex of the hierarchy and can self-renew for the life of the organism, sustaining blood formation. They reside in specialised bone-marrow niches, interacting with osteoblasts, endothelial and perivascular stromal cells that supply factors such as stem cell factor and CXCL12. HSC transplantation (bone marrow transplantation) is the oldest successful stem-cell therapy and is used to treat leukaemias, lymphomas, aplastic anaemia and inherited blood and immune disorders.

Clinical significance

Disorders of haematopoiesis include anaemias (too few red cells), cytopenias, myeloproliferative neoplasms (such as polycythaemia vera), myelodysplastic syndromes, and the leukaemias, in which malignant clones of haematopoietic precursors proliferate at the expense of normal blood formation. Clonal haematopoiesis of indeterminate potential — the age-related expansion of a mutant HSC clone — is increasingly recognised as a risk factor for haematological cancers and cardiovascular disease.
