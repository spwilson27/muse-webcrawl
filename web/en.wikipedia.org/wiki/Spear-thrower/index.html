Spear-thrower

A spear-thrower, spear-throwing lever, or throwing board is a tool that uses leverage to throw a long, flexible dart or light spear farther and harder than would be possible by hand alone. It consists of a shaft, held in one hand, with a hook or socket that engages the butt of the dart; a swinging motion of the arm effectively lengthens the thrower's arm, greatly increasing the projectile's velocity. Well-known forms include the atlatl of the Americas and the woomera of Aboriginal Australia.

History

The earliest secure evidence comes from the Upper Paleolithic of Europe, where spear-throwers of reindeer antler — some elaborately carved, such as the famous "leaping horse" example from Bruniquel — appear in Magdalenian contexts roughly 17,000 years ago. Spear-throwers were used worldwide: by the Aztecs and other Mesoamerican peoples (whose atlatl gave its name to the Nahuatl-derived term), by Inuit hunters as the throwing board for harpoons, in New Guinea, and throughout Aboriginal Australia, where the woomera is a multipurpose tool also used as a cutting implement, fire saw, and shield. In Europe the device largely disappeared with the spread of the bow, but it survived in the Americas until and even after European contact.

Modern use

Today the atlatl is used in sport throwing, with competitions governed by the World Atlatl Association, and several U.S. states permit atlatl hunting of deer during special seasons. Experimental archaeology has shown that darts thrown with a spear-thrower can exceed 150 km/h and carry effectively to ranges far beyond a hand-thrown javelin.
