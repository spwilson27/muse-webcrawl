Hobelar

A hobelar was a type of light cavalryman or mounted infantryman used in Western Europe during the late Middle Ages, particularly in Ireland and in the Anglo-Scottish border wars of the 13th and 14th centuries. The name derives from the hobby, a small, hardy Irish horse on which they rode.

Origins and equipment

Hobelars originated in Ireland, where the terrain and style of warfare favoured mobile light horsemen over heavily armoured knights. They typically wore light armour such as a padded jack or mail shirt, carried a sword, lance or javelins, and sometimes a shield. Their mounts were unarmoured and prized for stamina and sure-footedness over boggy and broken ground.

Military use

Edward I of England employed Irish hobelars in his Scottish wars, and the type was adopted in the Scottish Marches and northern England. Records of musters specify their required equipment: a horse worth a set minimum price, an aketon or haubergeon, helmet, sword, knife, and lance. Valued for raiding, reconnaissance, and pursuit, hobelars are often cited as early examples of the mounted-infantry and light-horse roles that later developed into dragoons and other light cavalry.

By the later 14th century the distinction between hobelars and other light mounted troops such as mounted archers blurred, and the term fell out of use.
