Sunday Bloody Sunday (film)

Sunday Bloody Sunday is a 1971 British drama film directed by John Schlesinger, written by Penelope Gilliatt, and starring Glenda Jackson, Peter Finch and Murray Head. The film tells the story of a love triangle in contemporary London involving a young bisexual artist who divides his time between two older lovers.

Plot

Alex Greville (Glenda Jackson), a divorced employment counsellor, and Dr Daniel Hirsh (Peter Finch), a middle-aged gay Jewish physician, are both involved with the same young man, the sculptor Bob Elkin (Murray Head). Each knows of the other's existence and tolerates the arrangement, though neither is entirely content. Bob drifts between the two, unable to commit to either, and eventually leaves London for New York to pursue his career, leaving both lovers to reflect on what the relationship gave them. Daniel's closing monologue to the camera — in which he concludes that what he and Bob had was worthwhile even if imperfect — is one of the film's most celebrated moments.

Production

Schlesinger drew partly on his own experience in crafting the story, and Gilliatt's screenplay was widely praised for its intelligence and restraint. The film is notable for its matter-of-fact depiction of homosexuality and bisexuality at a time when such portrayals were rare in mainstream cinema; the kiss between Finch and Head was handled without sensationalism. A recurring musical motif is the trio "Soave sia il vento" from Mozart's opera Così fan tutte.

Reception and awards

The film was a critical success and is frequently listed among the finest British films of its era. It won several BAFTA Awards, including Best Direction, Best Actor (Finch) and Best Actress (Jackson), and was nominated for four Academy Awards, including Best Director, Best Actor, Best Actress and Best Original Screenplay. The British Film Institute ranked it among the top British films of the 20th century.
