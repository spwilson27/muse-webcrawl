Phoebe is an irregular satellite of Saturn, about 213 km in diameter, named after the Titaness Phoebe of Greek mythology. It was discovered by William Henry Pickering in 1899 from photographic plates taken in Peru — the first satellite ever discovered photographically — and was the first moon found to move in a retrograde orbit.

Orbit and origin

Phoebe orbits Saturn at a distance of about 12.9 million km in 545 days, retrograde and highly inclined to Saturn's equator. It is the largest member of the Norse group of irregular satellites, most of which probably originated in collisions involving Phoebe-like bodies. Phoebe's dark surface, its orbit, and spectra showing water ice and carbon dioxide have led scientists to conclude it is a captured planetesimal from the Kuiper Belt rather than a moon formed with Saturn. Its impacts eject dust that feeds the enormous, tenuous Phoebe ring discovered by the Spitzer Space Telescope in 2009 — the largest planetary ring known — and material spiralling inward darkens the leading hemisphere of the moon Iapetus, explaining that moon's famous two-tone colouring.

Exploration

Voyager 2 imaged Phoebe distantly in 1981. On 11 June 2004, the Cassini spacecraft made a close flyby — its only targeted encounter with an outer irregular moon — passing within about 2,000 km and revealing a heavily cratered, icy body with craters up to 100 km across and evidence of layered deposits of ice and darker material.
