Photogram

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A photogram of plant specimens.

A photogram is a photographic image made without a camera by placing objects directly onto the surface of a light-sensitive material, such as photographic paper, and then exposing it to light. The usual result is a negative shadow image that shows variations in tone depending on the transparency of the objects used. Areas of the paper that received no light appear white; areas exposed for a shorter time or through transparent or semi-transparent objects appear grey, and fully exposed areas are black.

History

The technique was among the earliest photographic processes: in the 1830s and 1840s William Henry Fox Talbot made "photogenic drawings" of botanical specimens, and Anna Atkins used the cyanotype variant to produce her celebrated books of algae illustrations, among the first books illustrated with photographs. The technique was revived in the twentieth century by avant-garde artists — Man Ray, who called his works "rayographs", László Moholy-Nagy, and Christian Schad ("schadographs") — and remains a staple of experimental photography and art education.

Technique

Working under safelight, the artist arranges objects on photographic paper, exposes the arrangement to an enlarger or other light source for a few seconds, then develops, stops, and fixes the print in the normal way. Translucent objects such as fabric, glass, liquids, and living tissue produce tonal gradations; opaque objects give crisp white silhouettes.
