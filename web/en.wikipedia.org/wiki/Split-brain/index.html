Split-brain

Split-brain
CauseCorpus callosotomy (surgical severing of the corpus callosum), usually for severe epilepsy
Key researchersRoger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga, Joseph Bogen
EffectsHemispheres unable to share most information; lateralised perception and responses

Split-brain or callosal syndrome is a type of disconnection syndrome resulting from the surgical severing of the corpus callosum, the great bundle of fibres joining the two cerebral hemispheres. The operation, called a corpus callosotomy, is performed as a last resort to limit the spread of epileptic seizures. Research on split-brain patients by Roger Sperry and his students in the 1960s revealed striking specialisation of the two hemispheres and earned Sperry a share of the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Effects

Because each hemisphere primarily receives sensory input from the opposite side of the body and visual field, information presented to one side can be processed without reaching the other. In a typical experiment, a word flashed to the left visual field (right hemisphere) cannot be named by most patients, because speech is usually controlled by the left hemisphere — yet the left hand, guided by the right hemisphere, can select the named object by touch. Conversely, an object shown to the right visual field is named easily but the right hemisphere remains unaware of it.

Patients nevertheless appear strikingly normal in everyday life: the hemispheres learn to share cues through behaviour ("cross-cueing"), and the eyes scan freely so both sides sample the world. Occasionally the hands act at cross-purposes, as in intermanual conflict, and the left hemisphere tends to confabulate explanations for actions initiated by the right hemisphere — a phenomenon Gazzaniga dubbed the "interpreter."

Implications

Split-brain research demonstrated that the hemispheres are specialised — language and analytic processing predominantly on the left, visuospatial and holistic processing on the right — and raised enduring questions about the unity of consciousness: whether a split-brain patient houses one mind or two.

History

The first callosotomies for epilepsy were performed by William Van Wagenen in the 1940s, with puzzlingly few obvious effects. In the 1960s Joseph Bogen revived the operation in California, and Sperry's group at Caltech, including Michael Gazzaniga, devised the lateralised tests that uncovered the syndrome. Partial callosotomy, severing only part of the callosum, is now more common.
