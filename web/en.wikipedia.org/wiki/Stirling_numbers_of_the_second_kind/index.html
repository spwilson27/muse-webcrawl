Stirling numbers of the second kind

In mathematics, particularly in combinatorics, a Stirling number of the second kind (or Stirling partition number) is the number of ways to partition a set of n labelled objects into k non-empty unlabelled subsets. It is denoted S(n, k) or by the bracket notation {n ; k}. Stirling numbers of the second kind occur in many counting problems and in the expansion of falling factorials.

Definition and recurrence

The numbers satisfy the boundary conditions S(n, 0) = 0 for n > 0, S(0, 0) = 1, and S(n, k) = 0 for k > n. They obey the recurrence

S(n + 1, k) = k · S(n, k) + S(n, k − 1),

because the (n+1)-th element either forms a singleton block in a partition counted by S(n, k−1), or joins one of the k existing blocks of a partition counted by S(n, k).

Examples

For example, S(4, 2) = 7: the set {1, 2, 3, 4} has seven partitions into two blocks — 123|4, 124|3, 134|2, 12|34, 13|24, 14|23, and 1|234. The first few rows of the triangle are: n=1: 1; n=2: 1, 1; n=3: 1, 3, 1; n=4: 1, 7, 6, 1; n=5: 1, 15, 25, 10, 1. Summing a row gives the Bell number Bn, the total number of partitions of an n-set: B5 = 52.

Explicit formula

By inclusion–exclusion, the number of onto functions from an n-set to a k-set is k!S(n, k), giving the explicit formula

S(n, k) = (1/k!) Σj=0k (−1)j C(k, j) (k − j)n.

Applications

Stirling numbers of the second kind convert between ordinary powers and falling factorials: xn = Σk S(n, k) (x)k, where (x)k = x(x−1)…(x−k+1). This identity underlies computations of power sums, the moments of the Poisson distribution, and finite-difference calculus. They are named after the Scottish mathematician James Stirling, who introduced them in his Methodus Differentialis (1730). Closely related are the Stirling numbers of the first kind, which count permutations by number of cycles.
