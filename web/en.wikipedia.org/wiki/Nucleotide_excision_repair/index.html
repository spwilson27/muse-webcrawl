Nucleotide excision repair

Nucleotide excision repair (NER) is a DNA repair mechanism that removes bulky, helix-distorting lesions from DNA, including ultraviolet-induced pyrimidine dimers and chemical adducts produced by agents such as cigarette smoke and some chemotherapy drugs. It is one of the cell's principal defences against mutagenic damage.

Mechanism

NER proceeds through recognition of the lesion, unwinding of the DNA around it, incision on both sides of the damage, excision of the damaged single-stranded segment (about 24–32 nucleotides in eukaryotes), and gap-filling synthesis using the undamaged strand as template, followed by ligation. Two sub-pathways exist: global genome NER, which surveys the whole genome using damage sensors such as XPC, and transcription-coupled NER, initiated when RNA polymerase II stalls at a lesion in a transcribed strand. In eukaryotes, the TFIIH complex's helicases (XPB and XPD) open the DNA, and the endonucleases XPF–ERCC1 and XPG make the cuts.

Clinical significance

Inherited defects in NER cause the disorders xeroderma pigmentosum, Cockayne syndrome and trichothiodystrophy, characterised by extreme sun sensitivity, greatly increased skin-cancer risk, and in some cases neurological degeneration and premature ageing. Tumour resistance to platinum chemotherapy can also arise from enhanced NER capacity.
