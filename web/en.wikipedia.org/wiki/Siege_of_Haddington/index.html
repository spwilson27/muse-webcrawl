Siege of Haddington

The sieges of Haddington were a series of engagements during the war of the Rough Wooing (1543–1551), in which England sought to force a marriage between Edward VI and the infant Mary, Queen of Scots. The East Lothian town of Haddington — strongly fortified and garrisoned by the English from the spring of 1548 — was repeatedly besieged by Scottish and French forces but held out until the English withdrew for strategic reasons in September 1549.

Background

After the crushing Scottish defeat at the Battle of Pinkie in September 1547, the English commander, the Duke of Somerset, planted a chain of garrisons in southern Scotland to compel the marriage alliance. Haddington, walled and bastioned under the direction of the engineer Sir Thomas Palmer, became the most important of these, an English foothold some eighteen miles from Edinburgh.

The sieges

From mid-1548 the garrison — at its height some two thousand men under commanders including Sir James Wilford — was besieged by the Scots under the Regent Arran, reinforced by several thousand French troops sent to Scotland under the terms of the Auld Alliance. French engineers pressed formal siege approaches, and fighting around the town was heavy; Wilford himself was captured in an ill-judged sortie against the French camp. Plague and supply difficulties afflicted besiegers and defenders alike — famously, the besieging army was struck by disease during the summer of 1549. Attempts to storm the town failed, and the garrison was repeatedly relieved and revictualled by English columns from Berwick.

Withdrawal and aftermath

By the autumn of 1549 the English position in Scotland had become untenable and ruinously expensive. Somerset ordered Haddington evacuated; the garrison withdrew in good order in September 1549, spiking guns and destroying stores. Mary, Queen of Scots had meanwhile been sent to France in August 1548 to marry the Dauphin, ending English hopes and the war's central aim.
