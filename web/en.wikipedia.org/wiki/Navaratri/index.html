Navaratri

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Navaratri is an annual Hindu festival observed in honour of the goddess Durga, an aspect of Adi Shakti, the supreme goddess. Navaratri literally means "nine nights" in Sanskrit. The festival spans nine nights (and ten days) and is celebrated every year in the autumn during the lunar month of Ashvin (September–October in the Gregorian calendar). It is observed for different reasons and celebrated differently in various parts of the Hindu Indian cultural sphere.

Theoretically, there are four seasonal Navaratrīs. However, in practice, it is the post-monsoon autumn festival called Sharada Navaratri which is the most observed, in honour of the divine feminine Devi. The festival is celebrated in the bright half of the Hindu calendar month of Ashvin, which typically falls in the Gregorian months of September and October.

Significance

Navaratri celebrates the victory of good over evil, most famously the goddess Durga's nine-night battle against the buffalo demon Mahishasura, culminating in his defeat on the tenth day, Vijayadashami (also celebrated as Dussehra). In eastern India the festival coincides with Durga Puja; in the north it intertwines with the Ramayana tradition and the burning of effigies of Ravana; in the south it is marked by the Golu display of dolls and figurines.

Each of the nine nights is associated with one of the nine forms of the goddess, the Navadurga: Shailaputri, Brahmacharini, Chandraghanta, Kushmanda, Skandamata, Katyayani, Kalaratri, Mahagauri and Siddhidatri. In many traditions the nine nights are also grouped in threes dedicated to Durga, Lakshmi and Saraswati.

Celebrations

  • Gujarat and western India: devotees perform the circular garba and dandiya raas dances around a central lamp or image of the goddess, often dancing until late at night; many observe fasts during the nine days.
  • West Bengal, Assam and Odisha: elaborate public pandals house clay idols of Durga for Durga Puja during the final five days; the festival ends with the immersion of the idols (visarjan) on Vijayadashami.
  • Karnataka: Mysuru's Dasara festivities, patronised by the Wodeyar royal family for over four centuries, feature a procession with a caparisoned elephant carrying the goddess Chamundeshwari.
  • Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala: households arrange Golu (Kolu) displays of dolls on stepped platforms and exchange visits, music and gifts.
  • Nepal: the festival is observed as Dashain, the country's longest and most important Hindu festival.

Practices

Common observances include fasting, chanting of the Devi Mahatmyam, sowing barley or wheat seeds in a pot (ghata sthapana) on the first day, wearing colours associated with each day, and honouring young girls as embodiments of the goddess (kanya puja) on the eighth or ninth day.
