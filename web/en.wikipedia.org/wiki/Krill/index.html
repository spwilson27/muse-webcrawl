Krill are small crustaceans of the order Euphausiacea, found in all the world's oceans. The name "krill" comes from the Norwegian krill, meaning "small fry of fish". Krill are an important zooplankton and a keystone food source: they feed on phytoplankton and (to a lesser extent) other zooplankton, and are in turn eaten by fish, squid, penguins, seals and baleen whales. The Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba) alone has an estimated biomass of several hundred million tonnes, making it one of the most abundant animal species on Earth, and it is harvested commercially for aquaculture feed, krill oil and omega-3 supplements.

Taxonomy and distribution

The order Euphausiacea contains two families, the Euphausiidae (with most of the 86 species, in genera such as Euphausia, Meganyctiphanes, Thysanoessa and Nematoscelis) and the deep-water Bentheuphausiidae. Important species include Antarctic krill, Northern krill (Meganyctiphanes norvegica) of the North Atlantic, and Pacific krill (Euphausia pacifica). Krill occur from the surface to the deep ocean, but the great swarming species are concentrated in productive cold waters and upwelling zones.

Anatomy and life cycle

Krill resemble small shrimp, with a chitinous exoskeleton divided into cephalothorax and segmented abdomen, feathery thoracic limbs that form a "feeding basket" for filtering phytoplankton, and prominent black compound eyes. Most species are bioluminescent, with light organs (photophores) whose flashes may aid in schooling or mating. They grow by moulting, and adults may even shrink at moulting in winter. Female Antarctic krill lay thousands of eggs that sink to depth, hatch as nauplii, and rise through successive larval stages; adults may live five to six years (longer estimates exist for Antarctic krill), forming dense surface swarms that can extend over many square kilometres and colour the sea reddish.

Ecology

Krill are central to the food webs of the Southern Ocean and other cold seas: blue whales may eat several tonnes of krill a day, and crabeater seals, penguins and many fish depend on them. By grazing phytoplankton and producing fast-sinking faecal pellets and moults, krill also shuttle carbon to the deep sea, contributing to the biological carbon pump. Changes in sea ice — under which juvenile Antarctic krill graze algae in winter — link krill populations to climate change, and krill declines have been implicated in population changes of their predators.

Fishery

Krill have been harvested since the 1960s–70s, when the Soviet Union and Japan pioneered Antarctic fisheries; the fishery is now regulated by the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), with precautionary catch limits far above actual catches (on the order of a few hundred thousand tonnes a year, mainly by Norway, China and Korea). Most of the catch goes to aquaculture feed and krill oil supplements. Krill are also eaten directly in Japan (okiami) and elsewhere.
