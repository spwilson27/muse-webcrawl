Woad (Isatis tinctoria), also called glastum in Latin and dyer's woad, is a flowering plant in the family Brassicaceae. It is the source of a blue dye obtained from its leaves — chemically the same compound, indigotin, as that in true indigo — and was the principal blue colorant of Europe from antiquity until it was displaced by imported indigo and, eventually, synthetic dyes.

History of use

Woad was cultivated across Europe and Asia from Neolithic times. Ancient writers described Britons staining their bodies blue with woad — Caesar's account of the blue-painted Britons helped fix the plant in popular memory, though scholars debate whether the substance was woad dye or something else. In the medieval period woad was a hugely valuable cash crop, enriching merchant towns such as Toulouse in France (the source of the pastel trade), Erfurt in Germany, and regions of England such as Somerset and Lincolnshire.

Dye production

The dye precursor is concentrated in the first-year leaves. Harvested leaves were crushed to a paste, formed into balls, dried, and later fermented ("couched") to produce a dyestuff that was added to a reduction vat, where cloth dipped in the yellowish liquor oxidized to blue on exposure to air. Because the dyeing process required fermentation, woad was a foul-smelling industrial product, and dyeworks were often banished outside town walls.

Decline

From the 16th century, indigo imported from India and the Americas, which yielded a stronger dye per unit weight, eroded woad's market despite protectionist edicts in several European states that stigmatized indigo as a "devil's dye". Commercial woad production in England lingered into the early 20th century, and interest has revived among craft dyers and in research on natural pigments.
