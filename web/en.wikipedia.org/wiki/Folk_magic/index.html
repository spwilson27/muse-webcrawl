Folk magic

Folk magic is the body of magical practices traditionally performed by ordinary people within a culture, in contrast to the learned, ceremonial magic of educated elites. Also called "low magic", it is practical and everyday in aim — healing illness, finding lost objects, protecting homes and livestock, ensuring love or fertility, and counteracting curses — and is transmitted through custom, oral teaching and popular books of spells rather than formal institutions.

Traditions

European folk magic was long practised by specialists such as cunning folk in Britain, the benandanti of Friuli, and village healers across the continent, using charms, herbs, amulets, holy water, prayers and Psalms. In the Americas it produced distinctive traditions: Pennsylvania Dutch pow-wow (Braucherei), Appalachian folk magic, hoodoo in the African-American South, and Mexican curanderismo. Printed manuals — the German Long Lost Friend (1820) of John George Hohman, the Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses, and Albertus Magnus collections — spread spells far beyond their regions of origin. Comparable folk-magical complexes exist worldwide, from Thai amulets to Japanese ofuda charms.

Characteristics

Folk magic typically operates within, rather than against, the dominant religion: Christian folk magicians invoked God, Christ, the Virgin and saints, and many charms are essentially prayers. Its toolkit includes knot magic, candle burning, eggs used diagnostically (as in the Malocchio ritual), witch bottles, horseshoes and rowan crosses against evil, and divination by mirrors, sieves, dreams and omens. Historians and anthropologists study folk magic as a window on pre-modern worldviews; in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries its elements fed into modern pagan witchcraft, Wicca and the folk-horror aesthetic in popular culture.
