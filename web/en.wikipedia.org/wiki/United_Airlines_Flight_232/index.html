United Airlines Flight 232

United Airlines Flight 232 was a regularly scheduled United Airlines flight from Denver to Chicago, continuing to Philadelphia, that crash-landed at Sioux Gateway Airport in Sioux City, Iowa, on 19 July 1989, after suffering a catastrophic failure of its tail-mounted engine, which led to the loss of all hydraulic flight controls. Of the 296 passengers and crew aboard, 112 died; 184 survived. Despite the deaths, the landing is considered a remarkable example of airmanship and crew resource management because of the large number of survivors.

Accident

About one hour into the flight, at cruising altitude over Iowa, the fan disk of the tail-mounted (No. 2) General Electric CF6 engine disintegrated. Debris severed lines of all three independent hydraulic systems, leaving the aircraft without functioning flight controls. The four pilots — Captain Al Haynes, First Officer William Records, Second Officer Dudley Dvorak, and training check airman Denny Fitch, who was travelling as a passenger and came to the cockpit to help — steered the DC-10 for about 44 minutes using only differential thrust from the two remaining wing engines.

Crash landing

Diverted to Sioux City, the aircraft touched down at high speed with a high sink rate; the right wing struck the ground, the aircraft broke apart and cartwheeled, coming to rest inverted in a cornfield adjacent to the runway. Emergency services were waiting, and survivors were rescued from the wreckage.

Cause

The National Transportation Safety Board determined that the probable cause was the inadequate consideration given by the engine manufacturer and regulators to human factors in inspection, together with limitations in inspection methods, which allowed a fatigue crack in the fan disk — originating from a metallurgical defect (a "hard alpha" inclusion) introduced when the titanium was manufactured — to go undetected until it failed.

Legacy

The accident prompted changes in engine inspection procedures and metallurgical quality control, and research into backup flight-control methods. It is widely studied in aviation training as an exemplar of crew resource management, and it has been dramatized in the television film A Thousand Heroes and in the play and book Flight 232.
