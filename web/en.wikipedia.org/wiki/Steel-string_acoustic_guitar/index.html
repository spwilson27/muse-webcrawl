Steel-string acoustic guitar

The steel-string acoustic guitar is a modern form of guitar that descends from the gut-strung Romantic guitar, but is strung with steel strings for a brighter, louder sound. It is often referred to simply as an acoustic guitar, but this term also includes nylon-string classical guitars. Steel-string instruments became the standard form of guitar in North American folk, country, blues, bluegrass and much popular music during the 20th century.

Construction

Because steel strings exert far more tension than gut or nylon, steel-string guitars are built more robustly. The top (soundboard), usually spruce, is reinforced internally with bracing — most famously the X-bracing developed by Christian Frederick Martin in the 19th century — and the neck contains an adjustable steel truss rod to resist the string tension. The body is typically made of tonewoods such as mahogany or rosewood for the back and sides. Common body shapes include the dreadnought (introduced by C. F. Martin & Company in 1916), the jumbo, the orchestra model (OM), and smaller parlor and concert sizes.

Types

Steel-string guitars are usually categorized by body size and shape. Larger bodies produce more volume and bass response and are favoured by strummers, while smaller bodies are often preferred by fingerstyle players for their balance. Twelve-string guitars, with six doubled courses of strings, are a prominent variant, as are resonator guitars, whose metal cone resonators were developed in the 1920s to increase volume before amplification.

Playing

The instrument may be played with the fingers, with fingerpicks, or with a flatpick. It is the principal guitar of American folk music and was central to the folk revival of the 1950s and 1960s; it is also standard in country music, bluegrass, blues and singer-songwriter idioms.
