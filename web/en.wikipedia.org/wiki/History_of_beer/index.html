History of beer

Beer is one of the oldest and most widely consumed alcoholic drinks in the world, and the history of beer stretches back thousands of years to the earliest agricultural civilisations. Chemical evidence from pottery jars suggests fermented grain drinks were produced in Neolithic settlements, and beer became a staple in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, where it was consumed daily by adults and children alike as a source of nutrition and safe hydration.

Ancient world

The Sumerians recorded hymns to Ninkasi, the goddess of beer, that double as brewing recipes. The Code of Hammurabi included regulations on tavern keepers and beer prices. In Egypt, workers who built the pyramids were partly paid in beer rations. Beer spread through Europe with grain agriculture; Celtic and Germanic peoples brewed ales flavoured with herbs and mixtures known as gruit before hops became standard.

Middle Ages and early modern period

Monasteries became centres of brewing expertise, refining techniques and preserving knowledge. The use of hops, valued for their preservative and flavouring qualities, spread from the early Middle Ages and largely displaced gruit by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. In Bavaria, the Reinheitsgebot of 1516 restricted beer ingredients to barley, hops, and water (yeast being not yet understood), and is often cited as one of the oldest food-quality laws. The distinction between ale and lager emerged with cold-fermenting yeasts in Bavaria.

Industrialisation

The Industrial Revolution transformed brewing through steam power, refrigeration, and rail transport. Scientific advances — Louis Pasteur's studies of fermentation in the 1860s and Emil Christian Hansen's isolation of pure yeast cultures in 1883 — made brewing consistent and large-scale. Pale lager, developed in Pilsen in 1842, became the dominant global style. Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) closed most American breweries and reshaped the industry worldwide.

Modern era

The late twentieth century saw the rise of global brewing conglomerates alongside a craft beer revival, beginning in the United Kingdom and United States in the 1970s, that restored interest in traditional styles, small-scale production, and flavour diversity. Craft brewing has since spread worldwide, while beer remains among the most consumed beverages on Earth.
