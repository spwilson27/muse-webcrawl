Mauna Kea (Hawaiian: [ˈmɐwnə ˈkɛə]; abbreviation for Mauna o Wākea, "Mountain of Wākea") is a dormant shield volcano on the island of Hawaiʻi. Its peak is 4,207.3 m (13,803 ft) above sea level, making it the highest point in the state of Hawaii and the second-highest peak of an island on Earth. Most of the volcano is underwater; measured from its base on the ocean floor, Mauna Kea rises over 10,210 m (33,500 ft), taller than Mount Everest measured from sea level.

Geology

Mauna Kea is one of five volcanoes forming the island of Hawaii, created by the Hawaii hotspot. It is in the post-shield stage of volcanic evolution and last erupted roughly 4,500 years ago. Its summit hosts alpine tundra, glacial deposits from the Pleistocene, and Lake Waiau, one of the highest lakes in the United States. Snow occasionally falls on the summit in winter.

Astronomy

The summit's dry, stable atmosphere and distance from artificial light make it one of the world's premier sites for ground-based astronomy. The Mauna Kea Observatories host thirteen telescope facilities operated by institutions from eleven countries. Telescope construction on the mountain has been controversial because of the summit's sacred status in Native Hawaiian culture; planned projects such as the Thirty Meter Telescope have drawn sustained protests.

Cultural significance

In Native Hawaiian tradition, the peaks of the island are sacred, and Mauna Kea is the most sacred of them, the realm of the snow goddess Poliʻahu and the home of the sky father Wākea. Historically only high-ranking aliʻi were permitted to visit the summit. The mountain's adze quarry was a major source of dense basalt for tools.
