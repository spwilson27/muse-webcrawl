Gaussian noise, named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, is a type of signal noise that has a probability density function (pdf) equal to that of the normal distribution, which is also known as the Gaussian distribution. In other words, the values that the noise can take are Gaussian-distributed.

Definition

A random variable is said to have Gaussian noise if its probability density is the bell-shaped Gaussian function, characterized by a mean (often zero) and a variance (or standard deviation). A special case is white Gaussian noise, in which the values at any pair of times are identically distributed and statistically independent; in signal processing, "Gaussian noise" commonly refers to additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN), the standard channel model in communications theory.

Causes

Gaussian noise arises naturally from the sum of many independent random contributions, as guaranteed by the central limit theorem. Principal sources include thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise generated by the random motion of charge carriers in conductors, shot noise, and the electronic noise of amplifiers and image sensors. In digital images, Gaussian noise results primarily from poor illumination and high sensor temperature or transmission errors.

Applications and analysis

The AWGN channel is the basic model used to analyze modulation and coding schemes in telecommunications, with performance measured by bit error rate against signal-to-noise ratio. In image processing, Gaussian noise is a standard degradation model for testing denoising algorithms such as Gaussian smoothing and the Wiener filter. Gaussian noise processes also underpin Brownian motion and the Black–Scholes model in finance.
