Rock formation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rock formation is an isolated, scenic, or spectacular surface rock outcrop. Rock formations are usually the result of weathering and erosion sculpting the existing rock. The term rock formation can also refer to specific sedimentary strata or other rock unit in stratigraphic and petrologic studies.

Formation

Rock formations are created by the processes of weathering and erosion acting over geological timescales. Differential erosion, in which softer rock layers erode faster than harder ones, produces many characteristic shapes such as hoodoos, arches, stacks, and balanced rocks. Wind, water, ice, and temperature changes all contribute to sculpting rock into distinctive forms.

Notable examples

Famous rock formations include Uluru in Australia, the hoodoos of Bryce Canyon in Utah, the Old Man of the Mountain in New Hampshire (collapsed in 2003), the Wave in Arizona, and the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland, which consists of interlocking basalt columns formed by an ancient volcanic fissure eruption. Natural arches such as Delicate Arch in Utah and sea stacks such as the Old Harry Rocks in England are also celebrated examples.

Stratigraphic usage

In geology, a formation is a body of rock having a consistent set of physical characteristics (lithology) that distinguishes it from adjacent bodies of rock, and which occupies a particular position in the layers of rock exposed in a geographical region. Formations are the fundamental unit of lithostratigraphy and are given formal names, such as the Morrison Formation.
