Self-censorship

Self-censorship is the act of censoring or classifying one's own discourse, speech, writing, art, or other expression out of fear of, or deference to, the sensibilities or preferences of others, and often without overt pressure from any party or institution of authority. It may be voluntary in form yet induced by anticipated punishment, professional harm, social sanction, or violence.

Causes and settings

Self-censorship is commonly reported among journalists, publishers, academics, artists, and media organizations. News outlets may avoid reporting that would offend advertisers, owners, governments, or powerful groups; publishers and film studios alter or withdraw works to preserve access to important markets or to avoid controversy; and individuals on social media moderate what they say in anticipation of harassment, "cancel" campaigns, or employer discipline. Authoritarian states are frequently said to govern as much through induced self-censorship — unclear red lines that encourage over-compliance — as through formal censorship.

Debates

Not all restraint is regarded as harmful: tact, professional standards, and consideration for audiences are sometimes defended as legitimate editorial judgement rather than self-censorship. The concept is contested where the boundary lies between prudence and suppression. Survey research by press-freedom organizations regularly asks journalists whether they avoid certain topics, and links high levels of self-censorship to weak legal protections, concentrated media ownership, violence against reporters, and polarization. Related concepts include the chilling effect in free-speech law and Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann's spiral of silence, in which fear of isolation suppresses minority views.
