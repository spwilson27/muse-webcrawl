Legislature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A legislature is an assembly with the authority to make laws for a political entity such as a country or city. Legislatures form an important part of most governments; in the Separation of powers model, they are contrasted with the executive and judicial branches of government. Laws enacted by legislatures are generally known as primary legislation or statutes.

Structure

Legislatures may be unicameral (a single chamber) or bicameral (two chambers, typically an upper house and a lower house). Upper houses — often called senates — frequently represent regions or states (as in the United States Senate and the German Bundesrat), are appointed or indirectly elected (as formerly in the British House of Lords), and usually have revising or delaying powers rather than control of finance. Lower houses are normally elected directly and hold the confidence that sustains governments in parliamentary systems.

Functions

Beyond law-making, legislatures approve budgets and taxation, scrutinise and question the executive, ratify treaties in some systems, confirm appointments, and in some constitutions conduct impeachments and declare war. Much detailed work is done in committees, which take evidence and amend bills. The legislative process typically involves several readings of a bill, committee scrutiny, votes in each chamber, and formal assent by the head of state.

Size and membership

Legislatures vary enormously in size: China's National People's Congress has around 3,000 members, the German Bundestag several hundred, and small states may have assemblies of a few dozen. Members are chosen under the country's Electoral system and are variously understood as delegates of their electors, trustees exercising independent judgement, or representatives of their political party.
