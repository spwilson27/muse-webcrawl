Sanding (locomotive)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sanding in railway operations refers to the application of sand to the rails in front of a locomotive's driving wheels to improve adhesion (traction) between wheel and rail. Locomotives carry sand in a sandbox (or sandboxes) and deliver it through pipes by gravity, steam or compressed air when the driver operates the sanding gear.

Purpose

Steel wheels on steel rails have low friction, which is efficient for rolling but limits traction and braking. Adhesion falls sharply when rails are wet, greasy, icy or contaminated with leaf mulch in autumn. Sanding increases the coefficient of friction at the contact patch, helping a locomotive start a heavy train, climb gradients without slipping, and brake more reliably. It is also used in emergencies to shorten stopping distances.

Equipment

On steam locomotives, sand was stored in a dome-shaped sandbox on the boiler or in boxes on the running plate, and delivered by gravity or steam ejector to pipes ahead of (and sometimes behind) the driving wheels. Diesel and electric locomotives use air-operated sanders fed from boxes on the bogies or underframe. Modern multiple units and trams have automatic sanding linked to wheel-slip and emergency-brake systems. Sanding is regulated on some railways because excessive sand can insulate wheels from rails and interfere with track circuits that detect trains; conversely, too little sanding in leaf-fall season contributes to delays, a notorious issue on British railways.

Sand

Railway sand must be dry, free-flowing and of controlled grain size; wet sand clogs the pipes. Depots keep sand in drying plants and refill locomotive sandboxes as part of routine servicing. Portable trackside "sanding" by hand was historically used on steep inclines and at starting points.
