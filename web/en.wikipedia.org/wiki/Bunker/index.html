Bunker

A coastal defence bunker of the Atlantic Wall

A bunker is a defensive military fortification designed to protect people or valued materials from falling bombs, artillery, or other attacks. Bunkers are usually underground or built of thick reinforced concrete, distinguishing them from pillboxes (small, often surface-level gun emplacements) and from simple trenches.

Types

Bunkers range from small personnel shelters and command posts to vast complexes. Famous examples include the Führerbunker in Berlin, the submarine pens built by Germany along the French Atlantic coast, and the Cold War continuity-of-government facilities such as the Cheyenne Mountain Complex in the United States. Modern bunkers may be hardened against nuclear blast and equipped with independent air, water and power systems, leading to their adoption as survival shelters by governments, militaries and private individuals.

History

The word derives from German usage popularised during the First World War, when concrete shelters proliferated on the Western Front. Bunkers reached their greatest scale in the Second World War: the German Atlantic Wall and Siegfried Line, Japanese island defences honeycombed with tunnels and bunkers, and the extensive air-raid shelters of Britain. During the Cold War, civil-defence bunkers, fallout shelters and hardened missile silos were constructed on a large scale.

Design

Protection comes from massive roof and wall thickness, burial under earth, and "burster" layers designed to detonate shells before they reach the main structure. Blast doors, dog-leg entrances and filtered ventilation guard against overpressure and chemical or radiological contamination. Many wartime bunkers survive today as museums, memorials, storage facilities and even hotels.
