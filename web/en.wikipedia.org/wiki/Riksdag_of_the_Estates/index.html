Riksdag of the Estates

The four estates: nobility, clergy, burghers, and peasants

The Riksdag of the Estates (Swedish: Ståndsriksdagen) was the name used for the Estates of Sweden when they were assembled. Until its dissolution in 1866, the institution was the highest organ of state in Sweden, alongside the king. It was an assembly representing the four estates of the realm: the nobility, the clergy, the burghers, and the peasants (freeholding farmers).

History

The origins of the Riksdag are usually traced to the assembly at Arboga in 1435, when the nobleman Engelbrekt Engelbrektsson was elected leader of the realm. The four-estate structure was consolidated during the reign of Gustav Vasa in the sixteenth century. A Riksdag Ordinance of 1617 gave the assembly a more formal organization. During the Age of Liberty (1719–1772), the Riksdag was the dominant power in Swedish government, meeting regularly and controlling royal appointments and policy.

Composition

The four estates deliberated and voted separately, and most decisions required the agreement of at least three estates. The House of Nobility was chaired by the Lord Marshal, the clergy by the Archbishop of Uppsala, and the burghers and peasants each elected their own speakers. The peasant estate was distinctive in European comparison: Swedish freeholding farmers formed a politically recognized estate of the realm, unlike the peasantry in most of Europe.

Abolition

After long debate, the estate representation voted its own abolition in 1865–1866. It was replaced by a bicameral Riksdag under the Representation Reform of 1866, with a First Chamber elected indirectly and a Second Chamber elected directly by property-owning men.
