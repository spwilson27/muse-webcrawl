Ganglioside


Structure of ganglioside GM1, a common brain ganglioside.

A ganglioside is a molecule composed of a glycosphingolipid (ceramide and oligosaccharide) with one or more sialic acids (e.g. N-acetylneuraminic acid, NANA) linked on the sugar chain. The 60+ known gangliosides mainly differ in the position and number of NANA residues. Gangliosides are present and concentrated on cell surfaces, especially in nerve cells of the central nervous system, where they make up about 6% of all phospholipids in the brain and a large fraction of grey-matter lipids.

Function

Gangliosides modulate cell signal transduction, cell adhesion, cell recognition and membrane protein activity. They act as receptors for certain toxins and viruses: cholera toxin binds GM1, and botulinum and tetanus toxins bind gangliosides on nerve terminals.

Nomenclature

Gangliosides are named with a capital G, a letter indicating the number of sialic acids (M = mono, D = di, T = tri, Q = quad), and a number based on their migration order in chromatography, reflecting the length of the neutral sugar chain.

Clinical significance

Defects in ganglioside degradation cause lysosomal storage diseases: Tay–Sachs disease results from hexosaminidase A deficiency and GM2 accumulation, and Gaucher and Niemann–Pick diseases involve related sphingolipid pathways. Autoantibodies against gangliosides are associated with Guillain–Barré syndrome and other peripheral neuropathies. GM1 ganglioside has been investigated as a neuroprotective agent in Parkinson's disease.
