Sir Robert Alexander Watson-Watt (13 April 1892 – 5 December 1973) was a Scottish pioneer of radio direction finding and radar technology. He led the development of the Chain Home radar system, which was vital to the defence of Britain during the Battle of Britain in 1940.

Robert Watson-Watt, developer of Britain's radar defences.

Early life

Watson-Watt was born in Brechin, Angus, and educated at the University of St Andrews, graduating in electrical engineering in 1912. He worked as a meteorologist at the Royal Aircraft Factory at Farnborough, using radio methods to detect thunderstorms and warn aviators of their approach.

Development of radar

In 1935, asked to comment on reports of a German "death ray", Watson-Watt instead proposed detecting aircraft by reflected radio waves. His memorandum led to the Daventry Experiment of February 1935, in which a BBC short-wave transmitter's signal, reflected from a passing bomber, was received miles away. Funding followed, and by 1939 a chain of radar stations, Chain Home, guarded Britain's coasts. Watson-Watt was knighted in 1942.

Later life

During and after the war Watson-Watt worked in the United States and Canada on radar and air traffic control, and promoted the "Cult of the Imperfect", his philosophy that one should give decision-makers "the third-best available technology" in time rather than the best too late. He died in Inverness in 1973.
