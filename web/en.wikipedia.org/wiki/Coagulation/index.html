Coagulation

Coagulation, also known as clotting, is the process by which blood changes from a liquid to a gel, forming a blood clot. It potentially results in hemostasis, the cessation of blood loss from a damaged vessel, followed by repair. The mechanism involves activation, adhesion, and aggregation of platelets, as well as deposition and maturation of fibrin. Disorders of coagulation include conditions of excessive bleeding (such as hemophilia) and excessive clotting (thrombophilia) that can cause thrombosis.

The coagulation cascade

Coagulation begins almost instantly after injury to a vessel wall. Platelets adhere to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor and form a primary plug, while the coagulation cascade — a series of enzymatic activations of clotting factors, traditionally divided into intrinsic, extrinsic, and common pathways — culminates in thrombin converting soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands that stabilise the clot. Calcium ions and phospholipid surfaces are essential cofactors. The modern "cell-based model" emphasises initiation on tissue-factor-bearing cells, amplification, and propagation on the platelet surface.

Regulation and disorders

Anticoagulant mechanisms — antithrombin, the protein C and S system, and fibrinolysis by plasmin — confine clotting to the injury site and later dissolve the clot. Deficiencies of factor VIII and IX cause hemophilia A and B; vitamin K deficiency impairs several factors; and inherited or acquired thrombophilias raise the risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Laboratory tests such as prothrombin time (PT/INR) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) assess the pathways, and anticoagulant drugs (heparin, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants) exploit them therapeutically.
