Fetomaternal microchimerism

Fetomaternal microchimerism, also known as fetal microchimerism, is the presence of a small number of cells in one individual that originated in a genetically distinct individual — in this case, cells exchanged between a fetus and its mother during pregnancy. Traffic of cells across the placenta is bidirectional: fetal cells enter the mother's circulation and tissues (fetal microchimerism), and maternal cells enter the fetus (maternal microchimerism). These cells can persist for decades after delivery.

Discovery and detection

That fetal cells could be found in maternal blood was demonstrated in the 1960s and 1970s, and the long-term persistence of fetal cells in mothers — including male (Y-chromosome-bearing) cells in women who had borne sons — was established in the 1990s, notably by Diana Bianchi and colleagues. Detection relies on PCR for Y-chromosome sequences or on genotyping of polymorphic markers that distinguish mother and child.

Persistence and distribution

Fetal cells have been found in maternal blood, bone marrow, skin, liver, thyroid, and even the brain, decades after pregnancy. Some appear to be stem or progenitor cells capable of differentiating into multiple tissue types; fetal cells have been observed at sites of maternal injury, for example in healed caesarean-section scars, suggesting they may participate in tissue repair.

Health implications

Microchimerism has been implicated both in harm and in benefit. It was proposed as a trigger for autoimmune diseases that predominantly affect women after childbearing years, such as systemic sclerosis (which closely resembles chronic graft-versus-host disease) and autoimmune thyroid disease. Conversely, epidemiological studies have associated fetal microchimerism with a reduced risk of breast cancer and better outcomes in some conditions, and it may contribute to maternal immune surveillance and wound healing. The evidence remains correlational and the field is actively debated.
