Maltose, also known as malt sugar, is a disaccharide formed from two units of glucose joined by an α(1→4) glycosidic bond. It has the chemical formula C12H22O11 and is roughly 30–60% as sweet as sucrose.

Formation and occurrence

Maltose is produced when the enzyme beta-amylase breaks down starch, as happens when seeds germinate — the process that gives malted grain its name and its usefulness in brewing. In animals, maltose is an intermediate product of starch digestion by amylase enzymes in the gut, and is further hydrolysed to glucose by the enzyme maltase-glucoamylase at the intestinal brush border.

Properties

Maltose is a reducing sugar because one of its glucose units can open to expose a free aldehyde group. The related isomer isomaltose has an α(1→6) linkage instead. Maltose is the sugar famously detected in the Fehling's and Benedict's tests as a brick-red precipitate when heated.

Uses

Beyond brewing and baking, maltose syrup is a common sweetener in East Asian confectionery; the chewy sweet known as maltose candy is a traditional treat in China, sometimes eaten wrapped around chopsticks.
