The Jin dynasty (Chinese: 晉朝; pinyin: Jìn Cháo) was an imperial dynasty of China that existed from 266 to 420. It was founded by Sima Yan (Emperor Wu), whose family had risen to control the state of Cao Wei during the Three Kingdoms period. In 280 the Jin conquered Eastern Wu and reunified China, ending the Three Kingdoms era. The dynasty's history divides into the Western Jin (266–316), ruling a unified China from Luoyang, and the Eastern Jin (317–420), a rump regime south of the Yangtze after the north fell to non-Han ("Five Barbarian") states.

Western Jin and reunification

Sima Yan forced the last Wei emperor to abdicate in 266 and proclaimed the Jin. In 280 his armies descended the Yangtze and absorbed Wu, reunifying China for the first time since the Han collapse. The early reign was prosperous and famously extravagant — the court at Luoyang became a byword for aristocratic wealth-display, as in the anecdotes of the magnate Shi Chong. But the enfeoffment of numerous Sima princes with armies set the stage for catastrophe: after Emperor Wu's death in 290, rival princes fought the War of the Eight Princes (291–306), a civil war that devastated the north and paralysed the state.

Fall of the north

Exploiting the chaos, Xiongnu and other non-Han peoples established rival kingdoms in the north in the period known as the Sixteen Kingdoms. Xiongnu forces of the Han-Zhao state sacked Luoyang in 311 (the Disaster of Yongjia), capturing Emperor Huai, and took Chang'an in 316, capturing Emperor Min; the Western Jin ended. Masses of northern aristocrats and commoners fled south across the Yangtze — the "crossing of the gentry" — transforming the demography and culture of the south.

Eastern Jin

In 317 the prince Sima Rui re-established the dynasty at Jiankang (modern Nanjing) as Emperor Yuan, founding the Eastern Jin. The regime depended on great émigré clans such as the Wang of Langya — the saying ran that "the Wangs and the Simas rule together" — and on powerful regional strongmen. Its greatest triumph came in 383 at the Battle of Fei River, where a smaller Eastern Jin army under Xie An and Xie Xuan routed the vast forces of Fu Jian of Former Qin, preserving the south and shattering the northern empire. Internal revolts by generals such as Wang Dun, Su Jun and Huan Wen nonetheless weakened the court. In 420 the general Liu Yu, who had risen through the Northern Garrison Army, forced the last Jin emperor to abdicate and founded the Liu Song dynasty, opening the period of the Northern and Southern dynasties.

Culture

Despite political fragmentation, the Jin era was culturally brilliant: it saw the flowering of xuanxue ("dark learning") philosophy and "pure conversation", the calligraphy of Wang Xizhi, the poetry of Tao Yuanming at the dynasty's end, landscape painting's first master Gu Kaizhi, and the deepening spread of Buddhism through translators such as Faxian, who travelled to India from Eastern Jin territory.
