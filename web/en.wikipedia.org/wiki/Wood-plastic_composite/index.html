Wood-plastic composites (WPCs) are composite materials made of wood fibre or wood flour and thermoplastics such as high-density polyethylene (HDPE), low-density polyethylene (LDPE), polypropylene (PP), or polyvinyl chloride (PVC). In addition to wood fibre and plastic, WPCs can also contain other ligno-cellulosic and/or inorganic filler materials. They are a subset of a larger category of materials called natural fibre plastic composites (NFPCs), which may contain no cellulose-based fibre fillers such as pulp fibres, peanut hulls, coffee husks, bamboo, or straw.

Chemical additives seem practically "invisible" (except for mineral fillers and pigments, if added) in the composite structure. They provide for integration of the polymer and wood flour (powder) while facilitating optimal processing conditions.

History

The first wood-plastic composites were developed in Italy in the 1970s, when the company Covema of Milan began blending wood flour with polypropylene. The technology spread to North America in the early 1990s, when manufacturers such as Trex Company began marketing WPC decking boards made largely from recycled plastic film and reclaimed sawdust. By the 2000s WPC decking had become a major segment of the residential decking market in the United States, competing with preservative-treated timber and tropical hardwoods.

Production

WPCs are produced by thoroughly mixing ground wood particles and heated thermoplastic resin. The most common method is extrusion, in which the molten mixture is forced through a die of the desired cross-section; injection moulding is used for smaller items such as automotive trim and consumer goods. The ratio of wood to plastic typically ranges from 30:70 to 70:30 by weight. Because the wood flour is processed at temperatures below its degradation point (around 200 °C), the plastics used are generally those with low melting points.

Properties and uses

Compared with solid timber, WPCs are highly resistant to rot, decay, and insect attack, and they do not splinter or warp as readily. They can be moulded with or without simulated wood grain details. Their principal applications are outdoor decking, fencing, park benches, window and door frames, cladding, and moulded automotive interior parts.

Disadvantages include lower stiffness than solid wood, susceptibility to fading and staining, thermal expansion greater than that of timber, and the potential for creep under sustained load. WPCs also absorb less water than conventional wood but are not completely waterproof; prolonged immersion can cause swelling at exposed edges.

Environmental considerations

WPCs are often promoted as environmentally friendly because they can use recycled plastics and waste wood from sawmills, and because the finished product's durability reduces demand for preservative-treated lumber. However, the mixture of wood and plastic makes WPCs difficult to recycle at end of life, and most end up in landfill.
