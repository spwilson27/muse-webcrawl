Bonapartism

Bonapartism is the political ideology associated with Napoleon Bonaparte and his followers and successors. In its narrow sense it denotes the movement that sought the restoration of the House of Bonaparte to power in France; in a broader sense it describes a form of government in which a charismatic leader, often from the military, rules with authoritarian power while claiming to embody the will of the whole people, legitimised by plebiscites rather than by parliamentary institutions.

History

After Napoleon's fall in 1815, Bonapartists remained a significant faction in French politics, and in 1848 his nephew Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte was elected President of the Second Republic, seizing dictatorial power in the coup of December 1851 and reigning as Emperor Napoleon III of the Second Empire until 1870.

Marxist usage

Karl Marx analysed Louis-Napoléon's coup in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte (1852), and in later Marxist theory "Bonapartism" came to describe a regime in which the executive and the military-bureaucratic apparatus rise above a deadlocked class struggle, ruling with relative autonomy while ultimately preserving the existing property order. The term has since been applied, by analogy, to regimes far beyond France.
