Psychological fiction, also known as psychological realism, is a narrative genre of fiction that emphasises the interior characterization and mental and emotional states of its characters. Rather than concentrating on plot events, psychological fiction explores the motivations, perceptions, memories and inner conflicts that drive behaviour, often rendering consciousness itself as the principal scene of the story.

Characteristics

The genre is defined by its inward focus. Common techniques include stream of consciousness, in which a character's thoughts are presented in a continuous, associative flow; interior monologue; free indirect discourse, which blends the narrator's voice with a character's perceptions; and the unreliable narrator, whose account the reader must interpret against the grain. Time is frequently handled subjectively, with flashbacks and memories interrupting the present action.

History

Precursors of psychological fiction include the epistolary novels of the 18th century, such as Samuel Richardson's Clarissa (1748), which dwelt at length on its heroine's inner responses. In the 19th century, Fyodor Dostoevsky produced what many regard as the genre's foundational works — Crime and Punishment (1866) and Notes from Underground (1864) — anatomising guilt, obsession and self-deception. Henry James refined the method in novels such as The Portrait of a Lady (1881), restricting narration to a central consciousness.

In the early 20th century, modernist writers including Virginia Woolf, James Joyce and William Faulkner pushed psychological representation further, dissolving conventional plot in favour of the texture of moment-to-moment awareness. Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925) unfolds largely within a single day of its characters' thoughts.

Subgenres and influence

Psychological fiction overlaps with the psychological thriller, which applies the same inward techniques to suspense narratives, and with the philosophical novel. Its methods have become so widespread that most modern literary fiction incorporates some degree of psychological interiority.
