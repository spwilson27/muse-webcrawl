The curse of the pharaohs or mummy's curse is a legendary curse said to fall upon anyone who disturbs the tomb of an ancient Egyptian pharaoh, bringing bad luck, illness or death. The legend became world-famous after the opening of the tomb of Tutankhamun in 1922 by Howard Carter's expedition, when a series of deaths among people connected with the excavation was sensationalised by the popular press.

The Tutankhamun deaths

Carter's patron, the 5th Earl of Carnarvon, died in Cairo in April 1923, a few weeks after cutting his cheek while shaving and developing an infection that led to pneumonia. Newspapers seized on the story; the novelist Marie Corelli had recently popularised the idea of an ancient "poison" or curse guarding the tomb, and Sir Arthur Conan Doyle suggested elemental spirits. Other deaths later attached to the legend included visitors and team members over the following decade — although of the 58 people present at the opening of the burial chamber, only a handful died within the next ten years, and Carter himself lived until 1939, dying aged 64. A 2002 study in the British Medical Journal of Westerners present at the tomb's openings found no statistically significant difference in their survival compared with those not exposed.

Origins and reality

Ancient Egyptian tombs occasionally carried inscriptions warning robbers, and a handful of genuine "curse" formulae are known from Old Kingdom mastabas, but there is no evidence that a curse inscription was found in Tutankhamun's tomb — the famous text "Death shall come on swift wings to him who disturbs the peace of the King" appears to be a press invention. Rational explanations proposed for isolated illnesses include toxic moulds (such as Aspergillus), bacteria and dust-borne pathogens in sealed tombs, none of which accounts for the pattern as a whole better than chance and selective reporting.

In popular culture

The curse became a staple of horror fiction and cinema, feeding the mummy genre from Universal's The Mummy (1932) onward, and recurring in novels, films, comics and video games. The story remains one of the most durable media myths of the 20th century and a standard example in discussions of sensational journalism and confirmation bias.
