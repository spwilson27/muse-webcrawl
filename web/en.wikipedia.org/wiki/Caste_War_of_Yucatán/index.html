The Caste War of Yucatán (Spanish: Guerra de Castas) (1847–1901) was one of the most successful Indigenous revolts in the history of the Americas: a mass uprising of Maya peasants of the Yucatán Peninsula against the Yucatecos, the European-descended population that held political and economic power in the region. At its height the rebels drove the Yucatecan forces back to Mérida and Campeche and nearly took the peninsula; thereafter the Maya rebels of the east maintained an independent state centred on Chan Santa Cruz for over fifty years, sustained by trade in arms with British Honduras.

Origins and outbreak

Causes included heavy taxes and church fees imposed on Maya communities, loss of communal lands to expanding sugar and henequen estates, and the chaos of Yucatán's separatist conflicts with Mexico, which put modern rifles into Maya battalions. The revolt began in July 1847 after the execution of the Maya leader Manuel Antonio Ay at Valladolid. Under chiefs such as Jacinto Pat and Cecilio Chi, rebel forces swept across the peninsula in 1848; Yucatán's governor, Miguel Barbachano, even sought annexation by the United States, Spain or Britain in exchange for aid. The rebels' advance stalled at the gates of Mérida — tradition holds that the planting season called many Maya fighters home — and government forces, reinforced from Mexico, gradually reconquered the west.

Chan Santa Cruz and the Cruzob

Around 1850, at a cenote shrine called Chan Santa Cruz ("Little Holy Cross"), a prophetic cult arose around a "Speaking Cross" that issued letters and commands in the name of God, giving the eastern rebels — the Cruzob ("People of the Cross") — a theocratic state organisation. Their capital grew into a town of perhaps 10,000–30,000 with temples, schools and a military hierarchy. The Cruzob traded mahogany and other forest products for rifles from British Honduras (Belize), and inflicted repeated defeats on Yucatecan expeditions. Conflict over logging rights contributed to border tensions and incidents such as the attack on Orange Walk (1872).

End of the war

An 1893 treaty between Mexico and Britain cut off the rebels' arms supply at the source. Under President Porfirio Díaz, federal general Ignacio A. Bravo led a methodical advance into Quintana Roo, occupying Chan Santa Cruz on 5 May 1901 — the date conventionally taken as the war's end, though Maya resistance and autonomous "subdelegado" communities persisted for decades, and the last Cruzob strongholds were not fully integrated until the 1930s. The war cost Yucatán perhaps a third to half of its population through battle, disease and emigration.
