Vitamin B12, also known as cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin involved in metabolism, DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation and the function and development of the brain and nerve cells. It is the largest and most structurally complex vitamin, built around a cobalt atom held in a corrin ring, and is produced only by certain bacteria and archaea.

Absorption and sources

Humans obtain B12 from animal-derived foods. Absorption requires intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein secreted by the stomach's parietal cells, with uptake in the terminal ileum; the liver stores several years' supply. Failure of absorption — as in pernicious anaemia, an autoimmune destruction of parietal cells — causes deficiency even with adequate intake. Vegans and older adults are advised to use fortified foods or supplements.

Deficiency

Deficiency produces megaloblastic anaemia (historically "pernicious anaemia", fatal before liver therapy in the 1920s and isolation of the vitamin in 1948) and potentially irreversible neurological damage including subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. Dorothy Hodgkin determined its structure by X-ray crystallography in 1956, work contributing to her Nobel Prize.

Forms

Supplements and fortified foods usually contain cyanocobalamin; naturally occurring forms include methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin, the coenzyme forms used in methionine synthesis and methylmalonyl-CoA metabolism.
