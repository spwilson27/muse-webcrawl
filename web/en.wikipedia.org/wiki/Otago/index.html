Otago (Māori: Ōtākou) is a region of New Zealand located in the southern half of the South Island, administered by the Otago Regional Council. It is the country's second-largest local government region by area, encompassing the city of Dunedin, the inland basin of Central Otago, the lakes district around Queenstown and Wānaka, and the coastal districts of North and South Otago.

History

The name derives from the Māori settlement of Ōtākou at the mouth of Otago Harbour, long occupied by Ngāi Tahu. European settlement began with whaling stations in the 1830s, followed in 1848 by the organised Scottish Free Church settlement that founded Dunedin — the name is the Gaelic form of Edinburgh. The discovery of gold by Gabriel Read at Gabriel's Gully in 1861 triggered the Otago gold rush, drawing prospectors from Australia and California and making Dunedin, for a time, New Zealand's largest and richest city. Grand Victorian and Edwardian buildings, the University of Otago (founded 1869, the country's oldest), and the frozen-meat trade from Port Chalmers all date from this era of prosperity.

Geography

Otago's landscape ranges from the moist coastal hills around Dunedin to the semi-arid intermontane basins of Central Otago, the driest and most continental part of New Zealand, where summer temperatures can exceed 30 °C and winters bring hard frosts. The Clutha River (Mata-Au), New Zealand's largest by volume, drains Lakes Wakatipu, Wānaka, and Hāwea through the region to the Pacific. Queenstown-Lakes District is a major international tourism destination centred on Queenstown, Arrowtown, and Wānaka, known for skiing, adventure tourism, and Central Otago pinot noir wines.

Economy

Historically based on sheep farming, gold, and port trade, the modern Otago economy rests on tourism, education and health services in Dunedin, agriculture and viticulture inland, and hydroelectric generation on the Clutha at Clyde and Roxburgh dams. The Otago Polytechnic and University of Otago make Dunedin one of the country's principal university cities.
