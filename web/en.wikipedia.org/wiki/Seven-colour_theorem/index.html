Seven-colour theorem

A map on a torus may require as many as seven colours.

The seven-colour theorem states that any map drawn on a torus (a surface shaped like a doughnut) can be coloured with at most seven colours, so that no two adjacent regions receive the same colour — and that seven colours are sometimes necessary. It is the torus case of the Heawood conjecture, which gives a formula for the maximum number of colours needed for maps on surfaces of given genus.

Background

For the plane and the sphere, the corresponding result is the famous four colour theorem, proved in 1976. Percy John Heawood conjectured in 1890 that a surface of genus g needs at most ⌊(7 + √(49 − 24χ))/2⌋ colours, where χ is the Euler characteristic; for the torus (χ = 0) this yields seven. Gerhard Ringel and J. W. T. Youngs proved the conjecture for all orientable surfaces in 1968 (the Ringel–Youngs theorem), completing the map colour theorem — of which the torus case had already been established earlier, together with explicit examples of toroidal maps needing all seven colours.
