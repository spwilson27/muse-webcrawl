We Can Do It!

"We Can Do It!" is an American World War II wartime poster produced by J. Howard Miller in 1943 for Westinghouse Electric as an inspirational image to boost female worker morale. It depicts a woman in a work shirt and polka-dot headscarf flexing her arm under the slogan "We Can Do It!"

History

The poster was displayed only briefly inside Westinghouse factories in early 1943 and was not widely seen during the war. It was one of a series Miller made for the company to discourage labour unrest and absenteeism. The image was likely modelled on a photograph of a Michigan factory worker, Geraldine Hoff (later Doyle), operating a metal press.

Rediscovery and legacy

The poster was rediscovered in the 1980s and became widely reproduced as a feminist icon, retroactively associated with "Rosie the Riveter" — the cultural figure representing American women who worked in factories during the war. That association is historically loose: the famous Norman Rockwell "Rosie" painting and the song of the same name were the well-known wartime images, while Miller's poster faded from view until its revival. Naomi Parker Fraley was later identified by researchers as the most likely woman in the photograph that inspired Miller, a claim discussed in scholarship on the poster's history.

Usage

The image has been widely parodied and adapted in advertising, politics, and popular culture, and appears on countless products; it is now in the public domain and held by the National Archives.
