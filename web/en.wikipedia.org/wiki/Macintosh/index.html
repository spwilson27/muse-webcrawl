Macintosh

The Macintosh, commonly shortened to Mac, is a family of personal computers designed and marketed by Apple Inc. since January 1984. The original Macintosh was the first mass-produced personal computer to feature a graphical user interface (GUI) built around a mouse and a built-in display. Macs run Apple's macOS operating system and are known for their emphasis on design, media production and tight hardware–software integration.

History

The Macintosh project began in 1979 under Jef Raskin, who envisioned an inexpensive, easy-to-use computer for the average consumer. Steve Jobs took over the project in 1981 and redirected it toward the graphical interface concepts developed at Xerox PARC and embodied in Apple's Lisa. The Macintosh 128k was unveiled with the famous "1984" Super Bowl advertisement directed by Ridley Scott, which cast Apple as challenging a dystopian computing establishment.

Evolution

The platform passed through three processor architectures: Motorola 68000-series chips, then PowerPC processors from 1994, then Intel x86 processors from 2006, and finally Apple's own ARM-based Apple silicon (beginning with the M1 in 2020), which brought large gains in performance per watt. Notable models include the iMac (1998), credited with reviving Apple's fortunes; the MacBook lines; and compact desktops such as the Mac mini.

Software and market

Macs historically held small market share against Windows PCs but built strong positions in education, publishing, design, music and software development. The Mac App Store, UNIX underpinnings (macOS is certified UNIX), and interoperability with the iPhone have been central to the platform's modern appeal.
