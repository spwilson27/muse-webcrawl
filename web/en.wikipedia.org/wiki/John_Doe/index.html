John Doe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

John Doe (feminine: Jane Doe) is a multiple-use name used in English-speaking countries to denote a hypothetical male person, a party whose true name is unknown or must be withheld, or an unidentified human body. The name has its origins in English legal procedure and remains standard usage in law, medicine, law enforcement and everyday speech.

Legal history

The name arose in the medieval English action of ejectment, a fictional lawsuit used to try title to land. To make the action work, the claimant leased the land to a fictitious tenant, conventionally named John Doe, who was then "ejected" by another fictitious character, Richard Roe, standing in for the actual defendant. The Doe/Roe device flourished from the fourteenth century until the action of ejectment was abolished in 1852, by which time the names had become general placeholders for unknown or anonymous men.

Modern usage

Law

In court filings, unknown defendants are routinely sued as "John Doe" (often numbered — Doe 1, Doe 2, and so on) until they can be identified and named; the practice is common in American civil litigation, including suits against anonymous internet users. A "Jane Roe" was the legal pseudonym of the plaintiff in the landmark abortion case Roe v. Wade (1973). Subpoenas directed at unknown persons are sometimes called John Doe subpoenas, and DNA indictments of unidentified suspects are brought against "John Doe" identified by a genetic profile.

Medicine and law enforcement

Hospitals and morgues register unidentified patients and corpses as John Doe or Jane Doe, sometimes with local variants (in some jurisdictions, "John Smith" or alphanumeric codes). Unidentified children are occasionally called "Baby Doe."

Everyday and cultural use

"John Doe" serves as a generic everyman in forms, examples and sample documents, much like the surname "Smith." The phrase also colours popular culture — for instance the amnesiac protagonist of Frank Capra's film Meet John Doe (1941) and the alias used by the killer in the film Se7en (1995).

Equivalents in other countries

Many languages have parallels: Spanish uses Fulano de Tal; German traditionally used Max Mustermann for sample names; French uses Monsieur Tout-le-monde or Jean Dupont; and British usage adds "Joe Bloggs" for an ordinary man. Legal systems in various countries maintain their own conventional placeholder names for unknown parties.
