Cocaine dependence

Cocaine dependence
SpecialtyPsychiatry, addiction medicine
SymptomsCompulsive cocaine use, craving, tolerance, withdrawal dysphoria
Risk factorsRoute of administration (smoked/injected), genetics, environment, co-occurring mental illness
TreatmentPsychosocial therapy, contingency management, cognitive behavioural therapy

Cocaine dependence is a neurological disorder characterised by compulsive use of the stimulant drug cocaine despite harmful consequences, together with a withdrawal syndrome on cessation of prolonged use. It arises from the drug's action on the brain's reward circuitry: cocaine blocks the dopamine transporter, preventing reuptake of dopamine (and also of norepinephrine and serotonin), so dopamine accumulates in synapses of the mesolimbic pathway, reinforcing drug-seeking behaviour. With chronic use, neuroadaptations produce tolerance, sensitisation to drug cues, and diminished responsiveness to natural rewards.

Signs and symptoms

Dependence develops most rapidly with smoked (crack cocaine or freebase) and injected cocaine, which reach the brain within seconds, though intranasal and oral use can also produce it. Features include intense craving, loss of control over use, binge patterns ("runs") in which the drug is taken repeatedly until supplies or money are exhausted, and continued use despite cardiovascular, psychiatric, and social harm. Intoxication produces euphoria, increased energy, tachycardia, hypertension, and dilated pupils; high doses can cause agitation, paranoia, and psychosis.

Withdrawal

Unlike alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, cocaine withdrawal is not typically medically dangerous, but it is marked by a "crash" of dysphoria, fatigue, hypersomnia or insomnia, increased appetite, vivid unpleasant dreams, psychomotor changes, and strong craving, which may persist in waves for weeks.

Treatment

No medication has been approved specifically for cocaine dependence, so treatment is primarily psychosocial. Contingency management — providing tangible rewards for verified abstinence — has the strongest evidence base, alongside cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, and twelve-step facilitation. Investigational approaches have included disulfiram, modafinil, topiramate, and immunotherapy (a cocaine vaccine), none with conclusive results. Treatment of co-occurring disorders such as depression and ADHD is an important component of care.

Epidemiology

Cocaine is used by an estimated 20 million people worldwide each year, of whom roughly one in six who use it develop dependence. In the United States, cocaine has been a major contributor to overdose deaths, particularly in combination with opioids such as fentanyl.
