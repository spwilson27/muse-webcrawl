Nizam al-Mulk

Abu Ali Hasan ibn Ali Tusi (1018 or 1019 – 14 October 1092), better known by his honorific title Nizam al-Mulk ("Order of the Realm"), was a Persian scholar, political philosopher and vizier of the Seljuk Empire. Serving the sultans Alp Arslan and Malik-Shah I, he was effectively the empire's chief administrator for nearly thirty years and is remembered as one of the most capable statesmen of the medieval Islamic world. He founded the Nizamiyya network of colleges and wrote the Siyasatnama ("Book of Government"), a classic of the mirror-for-princes genre. He was assassinated in 1092, traditionally by an agent of the Order of Assassins.

Early life

Nizam al-Mulk was born in Radkan near Tus in Khorasan, the son of a revenue official. Educated in Shafi'i law and administration, he entered Ghaznavid service and, after the Seljuk conquest of Khorasan, served the Seljuk governors of the region before entering the service of Alp Arslan, whom he accompanied on campaigns across the empire.

Vizierate

Appointed vizier in 1064 when Alp Arslan became sultan, Nizam al-Mulk retained the office under Malik-Shah I (r. 1072–1092). He systematised the administration of the empire, developed the iqta system of military land assignments, maintained an extensive intelligence network, and advised the sultan on appointments and campaigns, including the victory over the Byzantines at Manzikert in 1071 (won under Alp Arslan).

The Nizamiyya

To train administrators and counter Ismaili (Fatimid) propaganda, Nizam al-Mulk founded a network of madrasas called the Nizamiyya in cities including Baghdad, Nishapur, Isfahan, Basra and Herat. The Baghdad Nizamiyya (founded 1065) was the most famous: one of its professors was the theologian al-Ghazali. These state-supported colleges are sometimes described as among the earliest universities of the medieval world.

The Siyasatnama

Written near the end of his life at Malik-Shah's request, the Siyasatnama offers advice on kingship: justice, the ruler's accessibility to petitioners, the management of officials, spies and armies, and warnings against heresy and disorder. Its anecdotes about earlier Persian and Islamic rulers make it a key source for medieval Iranian political thought.

Assassination

Relations between the aged vizier and Malik-Shah deteriorated in 1092 amid succession intrigues. On 14 October 1092, travelling with the court in western Iran, Nizam al-Mulk was stabbed by a man disguised as a Sufi petitioner — traditionally identified as an Assassin (fidai) of Hassan-i Sabbah, whose Ismaili movement the vizier had worked to suppress. Malik-Shah himself died a few weeks later, and the empire dissolved into succession wars.
