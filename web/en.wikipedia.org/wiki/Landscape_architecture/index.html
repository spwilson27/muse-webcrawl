Landscape architecture is the design of outdoor areas, landmarks, and structures to achieve environmental, social-behavioural, or aesthetic outcomes. It involves the systematic design and general engineering of various structures for construction and human use, investigation of existing social, ecological, and soil conditions and processes in the landscape, and the design of other interventions that will produce desired outcomes.

Scope of practice

The professional practitioner, called a landscape architect, works on projects ranging from private gardens and residential grounds to parks, campuses, plazas, waterfronts, green infrastructure, habitat restoration, and regional planning. Tasks include site analysis, grading and drainage design, planting design, and the integration of built structures with natural systems. The profession is distinct from landscape gardening by its emphasis on design, planning, and engineering at multiple scales.

History

Garden design is ancient — from Persian paradise gardens to André Le Nôtre's gardens at Versailles — but landscape architecture as a modern profession emerged in the 19th century. The term was popularised after Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux, designers of New York's Central Park (from 1858), adopted the title "landscape architect." Olmsted's firm went on to design park systems, campuses, and suburbs across North America. In the 20th century the field expanded to include modernist design (such as the work of Roberto Burle Marx), ecological planning (Ian McHarg's Design with Nature, 1969), and landscape urbanism.

Professional status

In many jurisdictions the title and practice of landscape architecture are licensed, requiring accredited education (such as degrees accredited by the Landscape Architectural Accreditation Board in the United States) and examinations. Professional bodies include the American Society of Landscape Architects (founded 1899) and the International Federation of Landscape Architects (founded 1948).
