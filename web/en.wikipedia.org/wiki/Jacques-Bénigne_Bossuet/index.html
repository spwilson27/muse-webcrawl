Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet

Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet

Born: September 27, 1627, Dijon, France

Died: April 12, 1704, Paris, France

Offices: Bishop of Condom (1669); Bishop of Meaux (1681)

Known for: Funeral orations; Discourse on Universal History; defending Gallicanism and divine-right monarchy

Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet (September 27, 1627 – April 12, 1704) was a French bishop, theologian, and court preacher, celebrated as one of the greatest masters of French prose and oratory. Known as the "Eagle of Meaux," he was the leading religious voice of the reign of Louis XIV and a forceful defender of the divine right of kings and of the Gallican liberties of the French church.

Life

Bossuet was born into a family of Burgundian lawyers in Dijon and educated by the Jesuits and at the Collège de Navarre in Paris, where he took his doctorate in theology in 1652. His preaching quickly won fame in Metz and Paris. Appointed bishop of Condom in 1669 and tutor to the Dauphin in 1670, he supervised the heir's education and composed for him the Discourse on Universal History (1681), a providential sweep of history from Creation to Charlemagne. As bishop of Meaux from 1681, he was a diligent diocesan while remaining the court's chief ecclesiastical spokesman.

Works and controversies

Bossuet's Funeral Orations — for Henrietta Maria of England, for her daughter Henrietta Anne, and for the Great Condé, among others — are classics of French literature, meditating on the vanity of earthly greatness. In Politics Drawn from the Very Words of Holy Scripture (published 1709) he gave the classic theoretical statement of divine-right absolutism. He drafted the Four Gallican Articles of 1682 asserting the French church's independence from papal temporal power; he campaigned, controversially, in support of the revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685) and against Protestantism in his History of the Variations of the Protestant Churches (1688); and in the 1690s he led the successful campaign against the Quietism of Madame Guyon and Fénelon, whose Télémaque he helped condemn.

Legacy

Bossuet's majestic periodic style made him a model of French eloquence for two centuries, and his universal history and political theology remain central documents of the grand siècle, even as his intolerance toward Protestants and Quietists has drawn lasting criticism.
