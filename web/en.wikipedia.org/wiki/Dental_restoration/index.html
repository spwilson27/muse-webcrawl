Dental restoration

Dental restoration, also known as a dental filling, is the treatment used to restore the function, integrity, and morphology of missing tooth structure resulting from caries (dental decay) or external trauma. Restorations are divided into direct restorations, placed and cured directly in the prepared cavity in the mouth, and indirect restorations — inlays, onlays, crowns, and veneers — fabricated outside the mouth (traditionally in a dental laboratory, increasingly by CAD/CAM milling) and then cemented in place.

Materials

  • Dental amalgam — a durable silver-coloured alloy of mercury with silver, tin, and copper; long standard for posterior teeth, now being phased down under the Minamata Convention on Mercury.
  • Composite resin — tooth-coloured resin filled with glass or silica particles, bonded to the tooth with adhesive systems; the most common direct material today.
  • Glass ionomer cement — bonds chemically to tooth tissue and releases fluoride.
  • Ceramics (porcelain, lithium disilicate, zirconia) — used for crowns, inlays, and veneers.
  • Gold alloys — exceptionally durable and kind to opposing teeth, used for cast restorations.

Procedure

For a direct restoration the dentist removes decayed and weakened tissue (using a handpiece and burs, sometimes guided by caries-detecting dyes), prepares the cavity, places a matrix if a contact surface is involved, and builds up the material in increments. Indirect restorations require an impression or digital scan, a provisional restoration, and a second visit for fitting. Restorations have a finite service life — commonly a decade or more — and their failure (fracture, wear, or secondary caries at the margins) is the most common reason for re-treatment in general dental practice.
