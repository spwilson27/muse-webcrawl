Fresnel equations

Reflection and refraction of light at an interface.

The Fresnel equations (or Fresnel coefficients) describe the reflection and transmission of light (and, more generally, electromagnetic radiation) when incident on an interface between different optical media. They were deduced by the French engineer and physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel in the early 19th century, and were among the first quantitative successes of the wave theory of light.

Description

When light strikes an interface between media of refractive indices n1 and n2, some is reflected and some is refracted. The fractions depend on the angle of incidence and on whether the light is polarized parallel (p) or perpendicular (s) to the plane of incidence. The amplitude reflection coefficient for s-polarized light is:

rs = (n1cos θi − n2cos θt) / (n1cos θi + n2cos θt)

and for p-polarized light:

rp = (n2cos θi − n1cos θt) / (n2cos θi + n1cos θt)

The power reflectance is the squared magnitude of the reflection coefficient, and the transmittance follows from conservation of energy. At normal incidence the reflectance simplifies to ((n1 − n2)/(n1 + n2))2 — about 4% for an air–glass interface.

Special cases

For p-polarized light the reflectance falls to zero at Brewster's angle, and when light travels from a denser to a rarer medium beyond the critical angle it undergoes total internal reflection, in which case the transmitted wave becomes evanescent. The equations predict the partial polarization of reflected light, exploited in polarized sunglasses, and are foundational in thin-film optics, fiber optics and computer-graphics rendering.
