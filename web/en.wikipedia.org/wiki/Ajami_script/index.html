Ajami script

Ajami (from Arabic عجمي, ʿajamī, "non-Arab, foreign") refers to adaptations of the Arabic script for writing African languages, particularly in West Africa, including Hausa, Wolof, Fulfulde, Yoruba, and historically Swahili and others in East Africa. The name derives from the Arabic word for a non-Arab or Persian.

History

Ajami writing spread with Islam and Qur'anic education from roughly the 14th century onward. It was used for religious poetry, chronicles, legal documents, correspondence, medicine and commerce, producing large manuscript literatures in languages such as Hausa, Wolof (in the form called Wolofal), and Fulfulde. Colonial administrations generally promoted Latin scripts, and Ajami literacy declined in formal education, though it remains in use in religious and community contexts.

Characteristics

Ajami adaptations add letters or diacritics to represent sounds absent from Arabic, such as implosives and ejectives in Hausa and Wolof. Conventions vary by language and region, and standardisation efforts continue in countries including Nigeria, Senegal and Niger.
