Coat of arms of the United States

The obverse of the Great Seal, bearing the coat of arms

Armiger: United States of America

Adopted: 1782

Shield: Paleways of thirteen pieces Argent and Gules, a chief Azure

Supporter: A bald eagle displayed

Motto: E pluribus unum ("Out of many, one")

The coat of arms of the United States is the arms borne on the obverse of the Great Seal of the United States. In heraldic terms, the United States is unusual in that the national arms are displayed by a single supporter, a bald eagle, rather than by a conventional crest and pair of supporters. The arms were adopted in 1782, when the Continental Congress approved the final design of the Great Seal, prepared by Secretary of Congress Charles Thomson drawing on proposals by Pierre Eugene du Simitiere, Francis Hopkinson and William Barton.

Blazon and symbolism

The shield consists of thirteen vertical stripes alternating white and red, with a blue horizontal band (chief) at the top. The thirteen stripes represent the original Thirteen Colonies; their unity under the chief symbolises the colonies joined under and supported by Congress. The colours are those of the national flag: white signifies purity and innocence, red hardiness and valour, and blue vigilance, perseverance and justice.

The bald eagle, a species unique to North America, holds an olive branch in its right talon and a bundle of thirteen arrows in its left, denoting the power of peace and war. Its head is turned toward the olive branch, indicating a preference for peace. In its beak the eagle carries a scroll inscribed with the motto E pluribus unum. Above its head is a "glory" of thirteen stars arranged in a circle, breaking through a cloud, representing the new constellation of states taking its place among the sovereign powers of the earth.

Use

Unlike many countries, the United States does not employ its arms broadly as a general national emblem separate from the Great Seal; the arms appear on passports, the seal and flag of the President, and various federal insignia, usually in the form of the eagle displayed bearing the shield. Strictly speaking, only the shield constitutes the coat of arms proper; the eagle is its supporter.
