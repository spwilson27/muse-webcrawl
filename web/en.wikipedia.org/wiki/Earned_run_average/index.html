Earned run average

AbbreviationERA
SportBaseball
FormulaERA = 9 × (earned runs / innings pitched)

In baseball statistics, earned run average (ERA) is the average number of earned runs a pitcher allows per nine innings pitched. It is the most widely used measure of a pitcher's effectiveness.

Calculation

ERA is calculated by dividing the total number of earned runs allowed by the number of innings pitched, and multiplying by nine:

ERA = 9 × (ER / IP)

Only runs scored without the aid of an error or a passed ball count as earned runs; runs that score because of defensive mistakes are "unearned" and excluded, on the theory that the pitcher should not be penalised for his fielders' errors. Relief pitchers are charged with earned runs for runners they allow to score, including inherited runners in some scoring conventions of other leagues; in Major League Baseball inherited runners are charged to the pitcher who allowed them to reach base.

Interpretation

Lower is better. What counts as a good ERA varies with the scoring environment of the era: in the high-offence late 1990s an ERA under 4.00 was good, while in the "dead-ball era" ERAs under 2.00 were common. The lowest single-season ERA in the live-ball era is Bob Gibson's 1.12 in 1968; the lowest career ERA is Ed Walsh's 1.82. Because ERA depends on defence, park and league, analysts have developed adjustments such as ERA+ and defence-independent statistics like FIP.

History

The statistic was devised by Henry Chadwick in the 19th century as a refinement of simple run averages and was adopted as an official statistic by the National League in 1912 and the American League in 1913.
