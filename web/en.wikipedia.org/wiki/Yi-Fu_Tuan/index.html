Yi-Fu Tuan (Chinese: 段義孚; 5 December 1930 – 10 August 2022) was a Chinese-American geographer and a founder of humanistic geography. He is best known for his work on the human experience of space and place, and for coining the term topophilia, meaning the affective bond between people and place.

Life and career

Tuan was born in Tianjin, China, the son of a diplomat, and was educated in China, Australia, the Philippines, and England. He studied at University College London and the University of Oxford, then earned his PhD in geography at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1957, with a dissertation on the geomorphology of New Mexico. He taught at several American universities, most notably the University of Minnesota and, from 1984 until his retirement, the University of Wisconsin–Madison, where he was the J.K. Wright and Vilas Professor of Geography.

Work

Tuan's writing shifted geography's focus from spatial analysis toward lived experience, perception, and meaning. His major books include Topophilia (1974), a study of environmental perception and values; Space and Place: The Perspective of Experience (1977), which contrasts "space" as openness and movement with "place" as pause and attachment; Landscapes of Fear (1979); Segmented Worlds and Self (1982); Dominance and Affection: The Making of Pets (1984); The Good Life (1986); and Escapism (1998). His later works, including Coming Home to China (2007) and Humanist Geography: An Individual's Search for Meaning (2012), took a more personal, essayistic turn.
