Fisher's exact test is a statistical significance test used in the analysis of contingency tables. Although in practice it is employed when sample sizes are small, it is valid for all sample sizes. It is named after its inventor, Ronald Fisher, and is one of a class of exact tests, so called because the significance of the deviation from the null hypothesis can be calculated exactly, rather than relying on an approximation.


A 2×2 contingency table with fixed margins

Principle

For a 2×2 table with cell counts a, b, c and d and total n, the test conditions on the row and column totals being fixed. Under the null hypothesis of no association, the count in any one cell follows a hypergeometric distribution, and the probability of the observed table is p = [(a+b)! (c+d)! (a+c)! (b+d)!] / (a! b! c! d! n!). The p-value is obtained by summing the probabilities of all tables with the same margins that are as extreme as, or more extreme than, the observed table.

Famous example

Fisher devised the test around the "lady tasting tea" experiment described in his book The Design of Experiments (1935): Muriel Bristol claimed she could tell whether milk had been added to tea before or after the water. Fisher's analysis of her perfect score on eight cups became a celebrated illustration of hypothesis testing and randomisation.

Use and alternatives

The test is standard in medical and biological research for small samples, for example comparing the proportions of patients responding to two treatments. For larger tables the Fisher–Freeman–Halton extension generalises the test to r×c tables. For large samples, the chi-squared test gives a close approximation and is cheaper to compute; because Fisher's test conditions on both margins, some statisticians prefer unconditional alternatives such as Barnard's test, which can be more powerful.
