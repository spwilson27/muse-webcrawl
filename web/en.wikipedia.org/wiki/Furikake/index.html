Furikake (振り掛け, "to sprinkle") is a dry Japanese seasoning meant to be sprinkled on top of cooked rice, vegetables, and fish. Typically a savoury-sweet mixture of dried, ground fish (usually bonito), sesame seeds, chopped seaweed, sugar, and salt, it transforms plain rice into a flavourful meal and is a standard feature of bento boxes and onigiri.

History

Furikake is generally credited to Suekichi Yoshimaru, a pharmacist in Kumamoto in the Taishō era (1912–1926), who developed a calcium-rich ground-fish powder — originally called Gohan no Tomo ("A Friend for Rice") — to combat malnutrition and calcium deficiency in the Japanese diet. Commercial versions spread nationally after the Second World War, aided by companies such as Marumiya (whose Noritama, with egg and nori, launched in 1960) and Mishima Foods, whose colourful varieties made furikake a pantry icon.

Varieties and uses

Modern furikake ranges from salmon, shiso, and wasabi flavours to sweet children's blends and regional specialities. Beyond rice, it seasons ochazuke (rice with green tea), buttered noodles, popcorn, avocado toast, and Hawaiian dishes such as furikake salmon and musubi. Like harissa in the Maghreb, it is a concentrated dry seasoning that turns a staple starch into a complete-tasting dish.
