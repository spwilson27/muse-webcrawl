Common-mode signal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A common-mode signal is the component of an analog signal that is present identically on both conductors of a two-conductor (differential) system, measured with respect to a common reference, usually ground. If the two conductor voltages are V+ and V−, the common-mode voltage is their average, Vcm = (V+ + V−)/2, while the useful differential signal is their difference.

Origins

Common-mode voltages arise from electromagnetic interference coupled equally into both wires, ground-potential differences between equipment, and imperfect balance in sources and loads. Because the interference appears identically on both lines, a receiver that responds only to the difference can reject it.

Rejection

The ability of a differential amplifier or receiver to reject common-mode signals is quantified by the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), usually expressed in decibels. Instrumentation amplifiers are designed for very high CMRR. Techniques for limiting common-mode problems include balanced lines and twisted-pair cabling, common-mode chokes (ferrite beads and baluns), isolation transformers, and optical isolation.

Applications

Differential signalling standards such as RS-485, USB, Ethernet over twisted pair, and LVDS all specify a maximum tolerable common-mode range at the receiver. In power systems, common-mode currents on protective conductors are a concern for electromagnetic compatibility and for residual-current devices.
