Robinson annulation

The Robinson annulation is a chemical reaction used in organic synthesis to create a six-membered ring containing three new carbon–carbon bonds, forming a fused ring system. It was reported in 1935 by the British chemist Sir Robert Robinson, and it combines two reactions in sequence: a Michael addition followed by an intramolecular aldol condensation.

Mechanism

In the classical procedure, a ketone reacts with methyl vinyl ketone (butenone) or a synthetic equivalent such as the Mannich base of butenone. In the first step, the enolate of the ketone adds in a Michael fashion to the α,β-unsaturated ketone, producing a 1,5-diketone. Base then promotes an intramolecular aldol reaction: an enolate of the newly installed side chain attacks the original carbonyl, and the resulting β-hydroxy ketone dehydrates on heating to give a cyclohexenone — specifically a fused enedione or hydroxycyclohexenone product — with the new ring annulated onto the original ketone's ring or chain.

Applications

The annulation is a cornerstone method for constructing the steroid and terpene skeletons, because it builds the characteristic six-membered rings of those natural products. A famous variant is the Wieland–Miescher ketone synthesis, an asymmetric (proline-catalysed, Hajos–Parrish) Robinson annulation used in the total synthesis of steroids — one of the earliest examples of organocatalysis. The annulation also features in numerous total syntheses of complex natural products.

Variations

Variants use different Michael acceptors, masked forms of methyl vinyl ketone (such as 1,3-dichloro-cis-2-butene in the Wichterle reaction), and asymmetric catalysts to set stereochemistry. Mannich-base acceptors avoid the polymerisation problems of methyl vinyl ketone itself.
