Catastrophism
FieldGeology, palaeontology
Key figuresGeorges Cuvier, William Buckland
Opposed byUniformitarianism (Hutton, Lyell)

In geology, catastrophism is the theory that the Earth has largely been shaped by sudden, short-lived, violent events, possibly worldwide in scope. This contrasts with uniformitarianism (sometimes called gradualism), according to which slow incremental changes, such as erosion, brought about all the Earth's geological features. The proponents of catastrophism argued that the geological record showed the past was punctuated by floods, volcanic upheavals and other rapid events that reshaped landscapes and extinguished whole faunas.

History

The theory was developed in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, notably by the French anatomist Georges Cuvier, who observed abrupt breaks between fossil faunas in the strata of the Paris Basin and inferred a series of revolutions or catastrophes. British geologists such as William Buckland initially linked the most recent catastrophe to the biblical Flood. From the 1830s, Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology advanced uniformitarianism so successfully that catastrophism fell from favour among professional geologists for more than a century.

Modern synthesis

Contemporary geology accepts that gradual processes dominate, but also that rare catastrophic events — asteroid impacts (such as the one that ended the Cretaceous, recognised after the 1980 Alvarez hypothesis), megafloods such as those that carved the Channeled Scablands, super-eruptions and rapid climate shifts — have played major roles at particular moments. This moderated position is sometimes called neocatastrophism or actualistic catastrophism.
