The Mandate for Palestine was a League of Nations mandate for British administration of the territories of Palestine and (until 1923) Transjordan, in effect from 1923 to 1948. It implemented Britain's wartime pledges: the mandate's preamble incorporated the 1917 Balfour Declaration's support for "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people", alongside a safeguard for the civil and religious rights of the non-Jewish communities.

Origins

After the Ottoman Empire's defeat, the San Remo conference of 1920 assigned Palestine to Britain under a Class A mandate. The mandate's terms reflected contradictory British promises: the Balfour Declaration, the McMahon–Hussein Correspondence, and the Sykes–Picot Agreement. Winston Churchill's 1922 White Paper attempted to reassure Arabs that the national home would not mean subordination of Palestine's Arab majority.

Administration and tensions

Britain governed through a High Commissioner (the first, Herbert Samuel, a Jew and Zionist sympathiser) and encouraged Zionist institution-building, including the Jewish Agency. Jewish immigration, land purchases, and the growth of the Yishuv provoked Arab fears of dispossession, sparking riots in 1920, 1921, 1929 (Hebron massacre), and the 1936–1939 Arab revolt, which Britain suppressed with force. The 1939 White Paper then restricted Jewish immigration just as European Jewry faced the Holocaust, alienating the Zionist movement.

End of the mandate

After the Second World War, Jewish insurgency against British rule (including the King David Hotel bombing of 1946) and the Anglo-American Committee's recommendations pushed Britain to refer the question to the United Nations, which in 1947 voted for partition. Britain withdrew on 14 May 1948, the day David Ben-Gurion declared the State of Israel; neighbouring Arab states invaded the next day, beginning the 1948 Arab–Israeli War.
