Vitamin deficiency

Vitamin deficiency, also termed avitaminosis (complete absence) or hypovitaminosis (insufficiency), is the condition of a long-term lack of a vitamin. When caused by inadequate intake it is a primary deficiency; when due to an underlying disorder such as malabsorption it is a secondary deficiency. Conversely, overconsumption of vitamins can cause hypervitaminosis. Deficiency diseases were historically among the great scourges of sailors, armies and poor populations, and their cure led to the discovery of vitamins themselves.

Major deficiency diseases

Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness and xerophthalmia and remains a leading preventable cause of childhood blindness in developing countries, as well as increasing mortality from infections. Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beriberi and, in alcoholism, Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. Vitamin B3 (niacin) deficiency causes pellagra — dermatitis, diarrhoea and dementia. Vitamin B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia and irreversible neurological damage; it may arise from pernicious anaemia or vegan diets without supplementation. Vitamin C deficiency causes scurvy, once devastating on long sea voyages. Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults. Vitamin K deficiency impairs blood clotting and can cause haemorrhagic disease of the newborn.

Causes and risk groups

Risk factors include poverty and restricted diets, alcoholism, bariatric surgery and gastrointestinal diseases that impair absorption (coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis), chronic kidney disease, and medications such as metformin (B12) or isoniazid (B6). Folate deficiency in early pregnancy causes neural tube defects in the fetus, which is why many countries mandate folic-acid fortification of flour and recommend supplements to women of childbearing age.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical features and laboratory measurement of blood levels (for example serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D or methylmalonic acid for B12 status). Treatment is replacement of the deficient vitamin, orally or parenterally, together with correction of the underlying cause. Public-health measures — iodised salt analogues such as fortified milk, flour and cereals, and targeted supplementation programmes — have greatly reduced deficiency diseases worldwide.
