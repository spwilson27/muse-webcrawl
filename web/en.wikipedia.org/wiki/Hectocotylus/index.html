Hectocotylus

A hectocotylus (plural: hectocotyli) is one of the arms of male cephalopods that is modified for transferring spermatophores to the female during mating. The term derives from Greek for "hundred cups," referring to the rows of suckers on the arm. In many octopuses the hectocotylus is the third right arm, ending in a spoon-like structure called the ligula used to deposit sperm packets inside the female's mantle cavity.

Discovery and misidentification

Detached hectocotyli found inside female argonauts and other octopuses were originally described by Georges Cuvier in 1829 as parasitic worms, which he named Hectocotylus octopodis. Later zoologists, including Japetus Steenstrup, recognised in the 1850s that the structures were in fact the detachable reproductive arms of the male.

Variation

In decapods such as squid and cuttlefish, one or both of the ventral arms is hectocotylised, bearing modified suckers or papillae for gripping the spermatophore. In the blanket octopus and argonauts, the male is dramatically smaller than the female and the hectocotylus breaks off (autotomises) inside the female, where it may remain alive and moving for some time. The dwarf male of the argonaut is rarely seen because it dies after this single mating.
