Flameout

A flameout is the run-down of a jet engine caused by the extinction of the flame in the combustion chamber, typically because of fuel starvation, compressor stall, or ingestion of foreign material, rain, hail or volcanic ash. It can also be caused by a local disruption of airflow, faulty engine control, or operation outside the engine's flight envelope.

Causes and recovery

Early jet engines were highly susceptible to flameout following disturbances of inlet airflow, and handling the throttle roughly could cause the compressor to stall or surge. Modern engines use digital engine controls (FADEC) and automatic relight systems that greatly reduce the risk. If a flameout occurs at sufficient altitude, the crew can usually restart the engine by windmilling or using the starter, following checklists specific to the aircraft type.

Famous flameout incidents include the 1982 case of British Airways Flight 9, in which all four engines of a Boeing 747 flamed out after flying through a cloud of volcanic ash from Mount Galunggung, and the 1988 TACA Flight 110, whose engines flamed out in heavy rain and hail; both aircraft landed safely. Dual-engine flameouts from fuel contamination or exhaustion led to the 2001 Air Transat Flight 236 glide to Azores and the 1983 "Gimli Glider" incident.
