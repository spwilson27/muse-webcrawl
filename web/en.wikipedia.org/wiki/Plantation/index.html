Plantation

A plantation is a large agricultural estate, usually in a tropical or subtropical region, dedicated to growing a single cash crop — such as sugarcane, cotton, tobacco, coffee, tea, rubber, bananas, or oil palms — on a commercial scale, often for export. Plantations are typically characterised by monoculture, large workforces, and substantial capital investment in processing facilities on site.

History

The plantation system developed with European colonisation: Portuguese sugar estates on Madeira and São Tomé in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries became the model for the Caribbean and Brazil, where plantations depended on the forced labour of enslaved Africans transported in the Atlantic slave trade. In British North America and the United States South, tobacco and later cotton plantations likewise rested on enslaved labour until abolition in 1865. After emancipation, plantations in the Caribbean and Asia turned to indentured labourers, many from India and China, and sharecropping. Colonial plantations for rubber, tea, and coffee spread across Africa and Southeast Asia, and in Ireland the word "plantation" also denoted the settlement of colonists on confiscated land, as in the Plantation of Ulster.

Modern plantations

Today plantations remain central to the production of palm oil (Malaysia and Indonesia), bananas, tea, coffee, and timber pulp, increasingly owned by corporations rather than resident planters. They are criticised for deforestation, biodiversity loss, land conflicts with local and Indigenous communities, and poor labour conditions, while defenders cite their efficiency and export earnings. Certification schemes for sustainable palm oil, timber, and coffee attempt to address these concerns.
