Ping-pong diplomacy refers to the exchange of table tennis players between the United States and the People's Republic of China in April 1971, which marked a thaw in Sino-American relations after more than two decades of hostility and led to President Richard Nixon's historic visit to China in 1972.

Background

The United States and the PRC had had almost no contact since the Chinese Communist victory in 1949. By 1971, both sides had reasons to seek rapprochement: China feared the Soviet Union after border clashes in 1969, and the Nixon administration sought leverage in the Vietnam War and Cold War politics.

The exchange

During the World Table Tennis Championships in Nagoya, Japan, American player Glenn Cowan boarded the Chinese team's bus, where champion Zhuang Zedong greeted him and presented him with a gift; photographs of the encounter were widely published. On 10 April 1971 the American team was invited to China, becoming the first American sports delegation to visit since 1949. They toured the Great Wall, played exhibition matches, and were received by Premier Zhou Enlai, who told them they had opened a new chapter in relations. Chinese players made a return visit to the United States in 1972.

Consequences

The exchange generated enormous public goodwill and signalled to both publics that official contacts were coming. Within days the United States lifted its trade embargo against China, and in July 1971 Henry Kissinger secretly visited Beijing to arrange Nixon's February 1972 trip, which culminated in the Shanghai Communiqué. The phrase "ping-pong diplomacy" has since become a general term for sports exchanges used to improve diplomatic relations.
