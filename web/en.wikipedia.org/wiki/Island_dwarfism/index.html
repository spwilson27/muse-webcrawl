Island dwarfism

Island dwarfism, or insular dwarfism, is the evolutionary process by which large mainland animals evolve smaller body size after colonising islands. It is one half of the "island rule" described by biologist J. Bristol Foster in 1964, the other half being island gigantism in small species.

Causes

Proposed drivers include limited food resources and territory, which favour smaller individuals needing less energy; release from predation, which removes selection for large size; and reduced competition or altered reproductive strategies. The relative importance of these factors is debated and probably varies between taxa.

Examples

Famous cases include the dwarf elephants and mammoths of Mediterranean islands and California's Channel Islands, dwarf hippopotamuses of Cyprus and Crete, the small hominin Homo floresiensis of Flores, dwarf dinosaurs of Hațeg Island in Romania, and the extinct dwarf buffalo and deer of various archipelagos. Living examples include the Key deer of Florida and some island foxes and sloths.

Conservation

Insular dwarfs are often highly vulnerable to introduced predators, habitat change and hunting, and many became extinct soon after human arrival on their islands.

References: Foster (1964), "Evolution of mammals on islands", Nature; Lomolino on the island rule.
