Chagatai (also spelled Chaghatay, Jagatai, or Chagatay) is an extinct Turkic language that was once widely spoken in Central Asia and remained the shared literary language of the region into the early 20th century. It is named after Chagatai Khan, the second son of Genghis Khan, whose ulus covered much of Central Asia.

History

Chagatai developed from the Karluk branch of the Turkic languages, heavily influenced by Arabic and Persian vocabulary and literary conventions. It is classified with modern Uzbek and Uyghur, which are its direct descendants through the Karluk group.

The classical period of Chagatai literature is associated with the Timurid era, especially the works of Ali-Shir Nava'i (1441–1501), who championed Turkic as a literary language equal to Persian in his Muhakamat al-Lughatayn ("Judgment of the Two Languages"). The memoirs of Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire, the Baburnama, were also written in Chagatai.

Decline

By the 19th century Chagatai was increasingly displaced in writing by localized Turkic vernaculars. In the Soviet era it was supplanted by standard literary Uzbek and Uyghur, based on local dialects. The name "Chagatai" also continues in scholarship for the broader group of late medieval Central Asian Turkic texts.
