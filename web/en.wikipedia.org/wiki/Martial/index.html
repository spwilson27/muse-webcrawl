Marcus Valerius Martialis (known in English as Martial; c. 38–41 – c. 102–104 AD) was a Roman poet born in Hispania (modern Spain) best known for his twelve books of Epigrams, published in Rome between AD 86 and 103 during the reigns of the emperors Domitian, Nerva and Trajan. In these poems he satirises city life and the scandalous activities of his acquaintances, often with a sharp, biting wit.

Martial perfected the short, pointed epigram.

Life

Born at Bilbilis in Hispania, Martial came to Rome in AD 64 to complete his education, hoping for advancement under fellow Spaniards such as Seneca — whose fall after the Pisonian conspiracy ruined those hopes. He lived modestly for decades in rented rooms, dependent on patrons, before fame brought an equestrian rank, a house on the Quirinal, and eventually retirement to his native Bilbilis, funded by his admirer Pliny's nephew, Pliny the Younger, who wrote warmly of him after his death.

Epigrams

Some 1,500 of Martial's epigrams survive. Early works include the Liber Spectaculorum on the opening of the Colosseum (AD 80), the Xenia and Apophoreta (gift tags for Saturnalia presents), and then the twelve numbered books. Ranging from obscene jokes and invective to tender epitaphs — such as that for the slave girl Erotion — the poems build to a sting in the final line, fixing the form of the epigram for all later European literature.

Influence

Martial was read throughout the Middle Ages and imitated from the Renaissance onward; his name became the byword for epigrammatic wit, and the adjective "martial" in the epigrammatic sense derives from him.
