Necessary and Proper Clause

Necessary and Proper Clause

Location: Article I, Section 8, Clause 18, U.S. Constitution

Also called: Elastic Clause

Key case: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

The Necessary and Proper Clause, also known as the Elastic Clause, is a provision in Article One of the United States Constitution: Congress shall have power "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States."

Interpretation

In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall read the clause broadly: "Let the end be legitimate... and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end... are constitutional." The clause, with the Commerce Clause, underpins much federal legislation and the doctrine of implied powers.

Debates and limits

Anti-Federalists warned the clause would swallow state authority, prompting the Tenth Amendment. Modern cases such as United States v. Comstock (2010) apply a deferential test, while NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) held the clause cannot alone justify a "great substantive and independent power" like the individual insurance mandate.
