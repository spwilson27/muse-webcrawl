Magnetic shielding

Magnetic shielding is the practice of reducing the magnetic field in a space by blocking it with barriers made of conductive or ferromagnetic material. Unlike electric fields, magnetic fields cannot be truly blocked — there are no magnetic charges ("monopoles") to terminate field lines — so shielding works by diverting field lines around the protected volume.

Mechanisms

For static and low-frequency fields, enclosures of high-permeability alloys such as mu-metal (a nickel–iron alloy) provide a low-reluctance path that shunts flux around the interior. Multiple nested layers, separated by air gaps, perform better than a single thick layer. For alternating fields, conducting shields also exploit eddy currents, which generate opposing fields; this is the principle of the Faraday cage applied to the magnetic component. Superconducting enclosures can expel magnetic flux entirely via the Meissner effect.

Applications

Magnetic shielding protects sensitive instruments and experiments: photomultiplier tubes, cathode-ray tubes, electron microscopes, SQUID magnetometers, and atomic clocks. Magnetically shielded rooms, built with several layers of mu-metal and aluminium, suppress the Earth's field and environmental noise for biomagnetic measurements such as magnetoencephalography. Shielding is also used to keep stray fields from pacemakers, magnetic storage media, and loudspeaker magnets away from nearby equipment.

Limitations

High-permeability materials saturate in strong fields, after which shielding effectiveness collapses; mu-metal also loses permeability if bent or struck and may need re-annealing in hydrogen. Holes for cables and doors are unavoidable weak points in practical enclosures.
