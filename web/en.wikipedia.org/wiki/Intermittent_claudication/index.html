Intermittent claudication

SpecialtyVascular surgery, cardiology
SymptomsCramping leg pain on exertion, relieved by rest
CausesPeripheral artery disease (atherosclerosis)
Risk factorsSmoking, diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol

Intermittent claudication is a symptom that describes muscle pain on mild exertion (ache, cramp, numbness or sense of fatigue), classically in the calf, that is caused by inadequate blood supply to the exercising muscle and is relieved by a short period of rest. It is the most common presenting symptom of peripheral artery disease (PAD), in which atherosclerotic narrowing of the arteries limits blood flow to the limbs. The word claudication comes from the Latin claudicare, "to limp".

Signs and symptoms

The pain of intermittent claudication is characteristically reproducible: it begins after walking a fairly consistent distance, forces the person to stop, and eases within a few minutes of rest. Depending on the level of arterial narrowing, pain may occur in the calf (femoropopliteal disease), thigh or buttock (aortoiliac disease). On examination, pulses in the affected leg may be diminished or absent, and the skin may show signs of chronic ischaemia.

Causes

The overwhelming cause is atherosclerosis of the arteries supplying the legs. Risk factors mirror those of other cardiovascular diseases: cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, increasing age and a family history of vascular disease. Less common causes include vasculitis, popliteal artery entrapment and cystic adventitial disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the history, palpation of pulses and measurement of the ankle-brachial pressure index (ABPI), in which a ratio below 0.9 indicates peripheral artery disease. Duplex ultrasound, CT angiography and MR angiography can localise the narrowing when intervention is contemplated. Exercise testing may be used to confirm the diagnosis where resting measurements are equivocal.

Treatment

Management has two aims: relieving symptoms and reducing cardiovascular risk. Supervised exercise therapy — regular walking to the point of pain — is a first-line treatment that significantly increases walking distance. Risk-factor modification includes smoking cessation, antiplatelet therapy, statins, and control of blood pressure and diabetes. The drug cilostazol may improve walking distance in selected patients. For disabling symptoms, endovascular angioplasty or surgical bypass may be considered. Only a minority of patients progress to critical limb ischaemia, but the condition is a marker of widespread atherosclerosis and increased risk of heart attack and stroke.
