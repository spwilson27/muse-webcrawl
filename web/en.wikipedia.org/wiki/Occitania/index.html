Occitania (Occitan: Occitània) is the historical region in Southern Europe where the Occitan language was historically the main language spoken, and where it is sometimes still used as a second language. It encompasses the southern half of France as well as the Val d'Aran in Spain, the Occitan Valleys of Italy, and Monaco. The name derives from òc, the Occitan word for "yes", contrasting the region with the northern langue d'oïl.

Geography

Occitania stretches from the Atlantic coast near Bordeaux to the Italian border, and from the Pyrenees and Mediterranean northward to roughly the Loire. It includes Provence, Languedoc, Gascony, Auvergne, Limousin, Dauphiné and parts of the French Basque and Catalan borderlands. Major cities include Marseille, Toulouse, Montpellier, Nice, Bordeaux and Clermont-Ferrand.

History

In the Middle Ages the region was a patchwork of counties and duchies, culturally distinct from the French royal domain. It produced the troubadour lyric tradition that shaped European poetry. The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) against the Cathars brought northern French domination, and the Treaty of Paris (1229) began the absorption of Languedoc into the French crown. Over subsequent centuries French progressively displaced Occitan in administration and education, a process accelerated by compulsory schooling in the Third Republic.

Language and culture

Occitan is a Romance language with several dialects, including Provençal, Gascon, Languedocien, Limousin, Auvergnat and Vivaro-Alpine. The nineteenth-century Félibrige movement, led by Frédéric Mistral — who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904 — sought to revive its literary use. Today Occitan is classified as endangered, though it enjoys co-official status in Spain's Val d'Aran and recognition in Italy. The Occitan cross and the anthem "Se Canta" are widely used regional symbols.

Modern administrative region

Since 2016 an administrative region of France has officially borne the name Occitanie, formed from the former Languedoc-Roussillon and Midi-Pyrénées regions, though it covers only part of historical Occitania. Regionalist movements continue to campaign for greater recognition of the language and culture across the whole area.
