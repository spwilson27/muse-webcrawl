Massacre

A massacre is an event of killing a number of people, usually people who are not combatants or who are defenceless, typically in a particularly brutal or indiscriminate manner. The word carries a strongly negative connotation and implies the deliberate murder of a group perceived as unable to resist. There is no universally accepted legal definition of the term, and it is not defined as a specific crime in international law, though the acts it describes may constitute war crimes, crimes against humanity, or genocide.

Etymology

The word entered English in the late 16th century from Middle French massacre, meaning slaughter or carnage, possibly derived from the Old French maçacre (a butcher's block or shambles). Its modern usage was popularised by events such as the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which thousands of French Huguenots were killed.

Definition and usage

Because "massacre" is a loaded term, its application to particular events is frequently contested: those who use it characterise the killings as atrocities, while opponents may dispute the label. Historians and journalists therefore sometimes disagree about whether specific mass killings — in battle, conquest, or civil conflict — should be termed massacres. Some scholars propose criteria such as the victims' defencelessness, the absence of military necessity, and the scale and one-sidedness of the killing.

Notable examples

Events commonly described as massacres include the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre (1572), the Boston Massacre (1770), the Wounded Knee Massacre (1890), the Nanjing Massacre (1937), the Katyn massacre (1940), the My Lai massacre (1968), and the Srebrenica massacre (1995), the last of which has been legally adjudicated as genocide. Mass shootings in civilian settings are also frequently termed massacres.
