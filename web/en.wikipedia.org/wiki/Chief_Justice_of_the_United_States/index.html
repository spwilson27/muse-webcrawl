The chief justice of the United States is the chief judge of the Supreme Court of the United States and the highest-ranking officer of the U.S. federal judiciary. Article II, Section 2, Clause 2 of the U.S. Constitution grants plenary power to the president of the United States to nominate, and, with the advice and consent of the Senate, appoint a chief justice, who serves until they resign, retire, die, or are removed from office through impeachment.

The chief justice is one of nine justices and presides over the Court's public sessions and private conferences. In addition to the duties shared with the associate justices, the chief justice assigns the writing of the majority opinion when in the majority, presides over presidential impeachment trials in the Senate, and administers the presidential oath of office. The chief justice also serves as chancellor of the Smithsonian Institution and heads the Judicial Conference of the United States.

Selection and tenure

Like all federal judges, the chief justice is nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate, and holds office during good behaviour — in practice, for life. A sitting associate justice may be elevated to chief justice, subject to a separate confirmation, as happened with William Rehnquist in 1986. Seventeen men have served as chief justice since 1789.

History

John Jay, the first chief justice, was appointed by George Washington in 1789. John Marshall, the fourth chief justice (1801–1835), greatly strengthened the Court, establishing judicial review in Marbury v. Madison (1803). Charles Evans Hughes and Harlan F. Stone both served first as associate justices; Stone was elevated by Franklin D. Roosevelt. John Roberts has served as the 17th chief justice since 2005.

Duties

The chief justice presides over oral arguments, leads the justices' conferences where votes are cast in order of seniority, and supervises the federal court system through the Judicial Conference and the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts. By tradition, the chief justice also administers the oath of office to newly inaugurated presidents, though this is not a constitutional requirement.
