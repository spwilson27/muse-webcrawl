Glastonbury Tor is a hill near Glastonbury in the English county of Somerset, topped by the roofless St Michael's Tower, a Grade I listed building. The site is managed by the National Trust and designated a scheduled monument. The Tor rises about 158 metres (518 ft) above the flat Somerset Levels and is a distinctive landmark for miles around.

Geology and history

The Tor is formed of layers of Lias rocks — clays capped by harder Midford Sands — that resisted erosion while the softer surrounding land wore away. Flint tools show Neolithic occupation, and terraces once cut into its slopes remain a subject of debate, interpreted variously as defences, farming terraces, or a ritual labyrinth. A church of St Michael stood on the summit from the medieval period; it was destroyed by an earthquake in 1275 and rebuilt in the 14th century. Abbot Richard Whiting was hanged, drawn and quartered on the Tor in 1539 during the Dissolution.

Mythology

The Tor is deeply associated with the legends of Avalon and King Arthur; in Celtic mythology it was linked with Gwyn ap Nudd, lord of the Otherworld (Annwn), and with the entrance to the fairy realm. It remains a focus of pilgrimage and neo-pagan practice, and features a "zodiac" theory proposed by Katherine Maltwood in the 1930s.
