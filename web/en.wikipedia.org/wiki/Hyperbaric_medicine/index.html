Hyperbaric medicine

Hyperbaric medicine is the medical treatment of patients in a pressurized chamber, most commonly hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), in which a patient breathes 100 percent oxygen at pressures greater than 1.4 atmospheres absolute. The treatment increases the amount of oxygen dissolved in blood plasma, which can reach tissues with compromised blood supply. Hyperbaric chambers are also used to treat divers suffering from decompression sickness.

Recognized indications

The Undersea and Hyperbaric Medical Society recognizes indications including:

  • Decompression sickness and arterial gas embolism
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning
  • Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis) and other necrotizing soft tissue infections
  • Problem wounds, especially diabetic foot ulcers
  • Delayed radiation injury (soft tissue and bony radionecrosis)
  • Compromised skin grafts and flaps
  • Central retinal artery occlusion and sudden idiopathic sensorineural hearing loss
  • severe anemia and intracranial abscess in some circumstances

Mechanism

At pressures of 2–3 atm, dissolved plasma oxygen rises roughly twentyfold, sufficient in principle to meet resting tissue oxygen demand even without hemoglobin. High oxygen tensions also enhance neutrophil killing of bacteria, promote angiogenesis and fibroblast activity in chronic wounds, reduce edema through vasoconstriction, and shrink gas bubbles in decompression illness according to Boyle's law.

Risks and contraindications

Adverse effects include middle-ear barotrauma (the most common), sinus and pulmonary barotrauma, oxygen toxicity seizures, and temporary changes in vision. An untreated pneumothorax is an absolute contraindication. Hyperbaric oxygen is also promoted for many unapproved conditions — such as autism, cerebral palsy, and general "wellness" — for which evidence is lacking, and regulators have warned against such marketing. Mild steel "soft" chambers operating at low pressures have drawn particular criticism when marketed for these purposes.
