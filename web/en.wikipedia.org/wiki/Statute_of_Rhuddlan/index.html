Statute of Rhuddlan

The Statute of Rhuddlan (Welsh: Statud Rhuddlan), also known as the Statutes of Wales (Latin: Statuta Wallie), was enacted at Rhuddlan Castle in North Wales on 3 March 1284 by King Edward I of England. It followed the conclusion of the Conquest of Wales by Edward I and provided the constitutional framework for the government of the Principality of Wales, introducing English common law and administrative structures into the annexed Welsh territories.

Background

After campaigns in 1277 and 1282–83, Edward I defeated Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales, who was killed in December 1282; his brother Dafydd was executed in 1283. The Treaty of Aberconwy (1277) had already stripped Llywelyn of most of his lands, and the final conquest left Edward in control of the principality of Gwynedd and the former Welsh-ruled lands of the north and west.

Provisions

The statute annexed the principality to the English crown (but did not incorporate it into the Kingdom of England), divided it into the counties of Anglesey, Caernarfon and Merioneth in the north-west, and Flint in the north-east, alongside the earlier counties of Carmarthen and Cardigan in the south-west. Each county was administered by a sheriff, and the government of the principality was headed by the Justiciar of North Wales and the Justiciar of West Wales.

English criminal law was applied wholesale in Wales. Civil law was a mixture: Welsh law (Cyfraith Hywel) survived in modified form for matters such as land inheritance (partible inheritance among sons) and some civil claims, though under English court procedures. The statute also regulated the courts, offices and revenues of the principality for more than 250 years, until the Laws in Wales Acts 1535 and 1542 fully incorporated Wales into England's legal system.

Legacy

The statute was accompanied by a great programme of castle-building — Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech and later Beaumaris — and the founding of fortified boroughs for English settlers. Tradition holds that Edward presented his infant son, the future Edward II, born at Caernarfon in April 1284, to the Welsh as their prince, originating the title Prince of Wales for the English heir apparent.
