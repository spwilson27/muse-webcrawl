Fort Ticonderoga

Fort Ticonderoga
Location: Ticonderoga, New York, U.S.
Built: 1755–1757 (as Fort Carillon)
Wars: French and Indian War; American Revolutionary War
Status: Museum

Fort Ticonderoga is an 18th-century star fort in northeastern New York, situated on a promontory overlooking the narrows where Lake Champlain drains toward the Hudson River valley. Its position commanded the strategic water route between New France and the British colonies, making it — in the words of contemporaries — a "key to a continent". The name Ticonderoga derives from an Iroquoian word meaning "between two waters" or "where the waters meet".

French and Indian War

The French built the fort in 1755–1757 as Fort Carillon. In July 1758, a much smaller French force under the Marquis de Montcalm repelled a frontal assault by a large British army under James Abercrombie in the Battle of Carillon, one of the bloodiest battles of the French and Indian War. The following year the British under Jeffery Amherst returned, the French destroyed the fort's magazine and withdrew, and the British rebuilt it as Fort Ticonderoga.

American Revolution

On 10 May 1775, in one of the first offensive actions of the American Revolutionary War, Ethan Allen and the Green Mountain Boys, together with Benedict Arnold, surprised the small British garrison and captured the fort "in the name of the Great Jehovah and the Continental Congress". Its cannon were hauled by Henry Knox overland to Boston in the winter of 1775–76 — the "noble train of artillery" — and emplaced on Dorchester Heights, forcing the British evacuation of Boston. The British retook Ticonderoga in July 1777 during Burgoyne's Saratoga campaign, when artillery placed on the undefended height of Mount Defiance made the position untenable; after Burgoyne's surrender the fort was abandoned.

Preservation

After decades of ruin, the fort was restored in the early 20th century by Stephen and Sarah Pell and opened to the public in 1909. It now operates as a museum with reenactments, exhibits on its French, British, and American occupations, and extensive gardens.
