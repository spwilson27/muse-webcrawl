Diabetes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic diseases characterised by high blood sugar over a prolonged period, resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Insulin, a hormone from the beta cells of the pancreas, enables cells to take up glucose; without effective insulin, glucose accumulates in the blood while cells starve. Symptoms include frequent urination, thirst and hunger; untreated, diabetes damages eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and vessels, and is a leading cause of blindness, kidney failure, amputation and cardiovascular disease.

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune destruction of beta cells, usually beginning in childhood or youth, requiring lifelong insulin. Type 2, far commoner (about 90% of cases), combines insulin resistance with inadequate secretion and is strongly associated with obesity, inactivity and genetics; it develops gradually and can often be delayed or partly reversed by lifestyle change. Gestational diabetes occurs in pregnancy. Over 500 million adults worldwide live with diabetes, a figure that has risen steeply with obesity.

History and treatment

Diabetes was named in antiquity for its sweet urine ('mellitus' = honeyed). The isolation of insulin in 1921–22 at Toronto (Banting, Best, Macleod, Collip) transformed type 1 diabetes from a fatal disease into a chronic one; the first patient, Leonard Thompson, was treated in January 1922. Type 2 treatment begins with diet, activity and metformin, escalating to other drugs and insulin; newer GLP-1 drugs have reshaped care.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on fasting glucose, the oral glucose tolerance test, or HbA1c — the glycated-haemoglobin measure reflecting average blood sugar over about three months. Long-term monitoring of HbA1c, blood pressure and lipids, plus eye, foot and kidney screening, prevents much of the burden of complications.
