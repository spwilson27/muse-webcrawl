Rebozo

A rebozo is a long flat garment used by women in Mexico and Central America, similar to a shawl or scarf. It is usually woven of cotton, wool, silk, or rayon, often with intricate ikat (jaspe) patterns and hand-knotted fringes, and is regarded as an emblem of Mexican identity.

Uses

The rebozo is remarkably versatile: it serves as a head or shoulder covering, a fashion accessory, a wrap for warmth, a carrying cloth for babies and market goods, and a support during pregnancy and labor. Midwives traditionally use rebozo techniques (the manteada) to massage and help position a baby during pregnancy and childbirth. Rebozos are also part of traditional dress for celebrations such as Día de los Muertos and Mexican Independence Day festivities.

History and production

The garment emerged in colonial Mexico from a blend of indigenous, Spanish, and Asian (via the Manila galleon) textile traditions. Famous production centers include Santa María del Río in San Luis Potosí, Tenancingo in the State of Mexico, and communities in Michoacán. Fine silk rebozos woven on backstrap or pedal looms can take months to complete. Frida Kahlo frequently wore rebozos and helped make them an iconic symbol of mexicanidad.
