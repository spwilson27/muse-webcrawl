Promissory estoppel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Promissory estoppel is a doctrine in contract law under which a promise may be enforced, wholly or in part, even though it is not supported by consideration, where the promisor should reasonably have expected the promise to induce reliance, the promisee did in fact rely on it, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

In England the doctrine was revived in Central London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947), where Denning J held that a wartime promise to accept reduced rent bound the landlord for the war years despite the absence of fresh consideration; it operates there mainly as a shield (suspending or extinguishing rights) rather than as a sword creating new causes of action (Combes v Combes, 1951). In the United States, Restatement (Second) of Contracts §90 gives the doctrine wider scope, supporting enforcement of charitable subscriptions and reliance losses in commercial negotiations. Australian law (“equitable estoppel”, Waltons Stores v Maher, 1988) is more expansive still.
