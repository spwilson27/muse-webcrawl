Happy Ending problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The "Happy Ending" problem is a result in discrete geometry concerning the existence of convex polygons among sets of points in general position in the plane. It states that any set of five points in the plane, no three collinear, contains the vertices of an empty-or-filled convex quadrilateral, and more generally led to the question of how many points are needed to guarantee a convex n-gon.

History

The problem was posed in Budapest in the 1930s by Esther Klein, who observed that any five points in general position contain a convex quadrilateral. Paul Erdős and George Szekeres generalised the question: they proved that for every n there is a finite number N(n) of points that forces a convex n-gon, and conjectured the exact value N(n) = 2n−2 + 1. Erdős named it the "Happy Ending" problem because Klein and Szekeres met through the work, married, and—according to Erdős—lived happily ever after.

Known values

The exact values known are N(3) = 3, N(4) = 5, N(5) = 9 (proved by Makai, with the proof verified by computer-assisted and later human arguments) and N(6) = 17, established by Szekeres and Peters in 2006 using extensive computer search. For larger n only bounds are known: the Erdős–Szekeres conjecture that N(n) = 2n−2 + 1 remains open, though Andrew Suk's 2016 breakthrough dramatically improved the upper bound to near-exponential tightness.

Related results

The problem is intimately connected with the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on monotone subsequences and with Ramsey theory. Variants ask for empty convex polygons (containing no other points of the set); Erdős showed that arbitrarily large point sets in general position always contain empty convex hexagons, but Harborth proved that ten points are needed to force an empty convex pentagon and Horton constructed arbitrarily large sets with no empty convex heptagon.
