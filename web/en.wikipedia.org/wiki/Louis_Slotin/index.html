Louis Slotin

Louis Alexander Slotin (1 December 1910 – 30 May 1946) was a Canadian physicist and chemist who took part in the Manhattan Project. He became the second person to die in a criticality accident, after Harry Daghlian, when a plutonium core — afterwards nicknamed the "demon core" — went critical during an experiment he was conducting at Los Alamos.

Career

Born in Winnipeg, Manitoba, Slotin earned his doctorate in physical chemistry from King's College London in 1936. He worked at the University of Chicago with Eugene Rabinowitch and later joined the Manhattan Project, working on cyclotrons and then at Los Alamos, where his skill at assembling bomb cores made him one of the laboratory's leading experts. He assembled the core of the Trinity test device, the first nuclear weapon detonated.

The criticality accident

On 21 May 1946, Slotin was demonstrating a criticality experiment to colleagues: he was lowering a beryllium hemisphere over the 6.2 kg plutonium core, keeping it propped open with a screwdriver in a procedure colleagues called "tickling the dragon's tail". The screwdriver slipped, the hemisphere closed, and the core went supercritical, releasing a burst of radiation visible as a blue flash. Slotin reacted instantly, flipping the hemisphere off with his hand and shielding the others in the room, but he had received a lethal dose of roughly 1,000 rads. He died nine days later of acute radiation syndrome, aged 35. The same core had killed Harry Daghlian in a similar accident in 1945. Los Alamos subsequently ended hands-on criticality experiments of this kind. See also Demon core.
