Bona vacantia

Bona vacantia is a legal concept from Latin meaning "vacant goods": property with no clear owner. In common-law jurisdictions it typically covers the estates of people who die intestate with no entitled relatives, and the assets of dissolved companies that were not distributed before dissolution. Such property passes to the Crown or the state, subject to any later claims by rightful owners or heirs.

England and Wales

In England and Wales, bona vacantia are administered by the Bona Vacantia Division of the Government Legal Department on behalf of the Treasury Solicitor. Assets of dissolved companies vest in the Crown automatically upon dissolution, though the Crown may disclaim onerous property such as contaminated land or burdensome leases. Estates of deceased persons who die without a will and without known kin are collected and advertised — the division's published "unclaimed estates" list is widely used by heir-hunting firms — and relatives who come forward within the statutory period (generally up to 30 years, with interest for the first 12) may claim them. In the counties palatine of Lancaster and the Duchy of Cornwall, bona vacantia go instead to the Duchies, which donate much of the proceeds to charity.

Other jurisdictions

In Scotland, ownerless property falls to the Crown as ultimus haeres and is administered by the Queen's and Lord Treasurer's Remembrancer. In the United States, the analogous doctrine is escheat: unclaimed or ownerless property reverts to the state, and modern statutes extend this to dormant bank accounts and uncashed cheques. Civil-law systems have comparable rules, often treating ownerless goods as part of the public domain.

Disclaimer and restoration

Because the Crown takes bona vacantia in the state it finds them, it may disclaim property that is a liability rather than an asset. Dissolved companies may be restored to the register, in which case their property is treated as if it had never vested in the Crown.
