Units of measurement

A unit of measurement is a definite magnitude of a quantity, defined and adopted by convention or by law, that is used as a standard for measurement of the same kind of quantity. Any other quantity of that kind can be expressed as a multiple of the unit of measurement. For example, a length is a physical quantity; the metre, a unit of length, represents a definite predetermined length, and when we say 10 metres, we actually mean 10 times the definite predetermined length called "metre".

History

Units of measurement were among the earliest tools invented by humans. Primitive societies needed rudimentary measures for many tasks: constructing dwellings of an appropriate size and shape, fashioning clothing, or bartering food or raw materials. Early systems were often based on the human body — the cubit, the foot, the hand — and therefore varied from place to place. Standardization advanced with trade; the metric system, created during the French Revolution, spread worldwide in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Systems of units

The International System of Units (SI) is the modern form of the metric system and the world's most widely used system of measurement. It defines seven base units: the metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), ampere (electric current), kelvin (temperature), mole (amount of substance), and candela (luminous intensity). Other systems include the imperial and US customary units, still used in the United States, and various natural and astronomical unit systems in science.

Conversion

Converting between units requires a conversion factor — a ratio expressing how many of one unit equal another. Errors in unit conversion have caused notable failures, such as the loss of NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999, when a subcontractor used pound-force seconds instead of newton seconds.
