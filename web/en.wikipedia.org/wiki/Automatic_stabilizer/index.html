Automatic stabilizer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Automatic stabilizers are features of the structure of modern government budgets, particularly income taxes and welfare spending, that act to dampen fluctuations in real GDP. They work without any deliberate action by policymakers: when the economy slows, tax revenues fall and transfer payments rise automatically, cushioning the decline in disposable income and aggregate demand. When the economy booms, the process reverses.

How they work

Income taxes

With a progressive income tax, tax liabilities fall more than proportionally when incomes decline, so the effective tax rate drops in a downturn. This automatically leaves households with a larger share of their (reduced) income, moderating the fall in consumption.

Transfer payments

Unemployment benefits, welfare payments and similar transfers increase automatically as more people become eligible during a recession and fall back as employment recovers. Farm price supports and corporate profit taxes have similar stabilising effects.

Effect on the budget

Automatic stabilizers cause the budget deficit to widen in recessions and shrink in expansions, independently of any discretionary fiscal policy. Economists therefore distinguish the actual budget balance from the cyclically adjusted (structural) balance when judging the stance of policy. The overall size of the government sector matters: countries with larger tax-and-transfer systems tend to have stronger automatic stabilisation.

Advantages and limitations

Unlike discretionary stimulus, automatic stabilizers involve no recognition, decision or implementation lags, and they withdraw automatically as the economy recovers. Their limitations are that they only cushion, and cannot by themselves reverse, a downturn; they may also dampen incentives at the margin, and the resulting deficits add to public debt. They are a central mechanism in Keynesian economics for smoothing the business cycle, working alongside discretionary measures and the money supply channel of monetary policy.
