Pictish stones are monumental carved stones found across the former Pictish territories of north and east Scotland, dating from roughly the 6th to the 9th centuries AD. They form the most durable legacy of the Picts and are decorated with a distinctive, still-undeciphered set of symbols, as well as Christian crosses, biblical scenes, warriors, hunts, and fantastical beasts.

Classification

The standard scheme (Allen and Anderson, 1903) divides the stones into three classes. Class I stones are rough boulders or pillars incised with symbols only. Class II stones are shaped cross-slabs carved in relief, combining a prominent cross with symbols and figure scenes — such as the great slabs at Aberlemno, one of which appears to depict the Battle of Dun Nechtain (685). Class III stones are free-standing crosses and recumbent grave markers without the pagan symbols.

The symbols

Around 40 distinct symbols recur in consistent pairings: geometric devices (crescent and V-rod, double disc and Z-rod, triple disc), everyday objects (mirror, comb, sword), and animals, most famously the enigmatic swimming "Pictish Beast", sometimes identified as a dolphin. Because pairs of symbols are repeated across many stones, scholars have proposed that they record names, lineages, marriages, or memorials of individuals — but no decipherment commands consensus, and the symbols' meaning remains one of the great puzzles of early medieval Britain.

Survival

Many stones were reused in church walls or as building material; significant collections are held at Meigle and St Vigeans museums and the National Museum of Scotland, while others still stand in their original churchyard or field settings. The stones connect with the ogham inscriptions studied under Pictish language.
