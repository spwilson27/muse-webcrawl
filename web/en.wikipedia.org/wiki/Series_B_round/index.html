Series B round

A Series B round (also known as series B financing or series B funding) is the second round of financing for a business through investment, including private equity investors and venture capitalists. Successive rounds of financing or funding are termed Series A, Series B, Series C, and so on, because they typically involve the issue of new shares designated by that letter.

Typical characteristics

By the time a company reaches a Series B round, it has generally achieved product–market fit, has a proven business model, and is generating meaningful revenue. The capital raised is usually directed toward scaling: expanding the product, growing sales and marketing, entering new markets, and hiring. Valuations at Series B are typically substantially higher than at Series A, reflecting reduced risk, and the rounds commonly involve established venture capital firms, sometimes including strategic or corporate investors.

In the United States, Series B rounds have historically raised amounts in the tens of millions of dollars, though sizes vary widely by sector and market conditions. Investors receive preferred stock, and the terms are negotiated in a term sheet covering valuation, liquidation preferences, board composition, and protective provisions.

Place in the funding sequence

Series B follows seed funding and the Series A round, and precedes Series C and later rounds, mezzanine financing, and ultimately an initial public offering (IPO) or acquisition. Companies that raise multiple later rounds may progress through Series D, E, and beyond, sometimes using lettered extensions or designations such as Series B-1.
