Union (American Civil War)

The Union was the federal government of the United States and the 23 states that remained loyal to it during the American Civil War (1861–1865). President: Abraham Lincoln. Capital: Washington, D.C.

During the American Civil War, the Union was the United States of America as the loyal federal government — also known as "the North" — led by President Abraham Lincoln. It was opposed by the secessionist Confederate States of America. The Union comprised the federal government and the states that did not secede: the free states of the North and West, plus the slaveholding border states of Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri, which remained in the Union despite internal divisions; West Virginia separated from Virginia in 1863 and joined as a new state.

Wartime government

Lincoln's administration raised an army of over two million men through volunteers and, from 1863, conscription; built a navy that blockaded Southern ports; and financed the war through bonds, income taxes, and greenback currency. Republican Congresses enacted landmark legislation including the Homestead Act, the Morrill land-grant act, and authorization of the transcontinental railroad. Under Lincoln's war powers the government suspended habeas corpus in places, and in 1863 the Emancipation Proclamation made abolition a war aim; the Thirteenth Amendment, passed by Congress in 1865, abolished slavery nationwide.

Outcome

Superior population, industry, and rail capacity, together with commanders such as Ulysses S. Grant and William Tecumseh Sherman, brought Union victory in 1865, preserving the United States as a single nation and ending slavery. The war cost roughly 360,000 Union military deaths; it was followed by the Reconstruction era in the former Confederate states.
