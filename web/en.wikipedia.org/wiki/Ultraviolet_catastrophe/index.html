Ultraviolet catastrophe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph comparing the Rayleigh–Jeans law diverging to infinity at short wavelengths with Planck's law matching data]
The classical Rayleigh–Jeans prediction (diverging) versus Planck's law (matching experiment).

The ultraviolet catastrophe, also called the Rayleigh–Jeans catastrophe, is the error at short wavelengths in the Rayleigh–Jeans law (or, equivalently, in the classical prediction of the total radiated energy) for the emission of radiation from an idealised black body. Classical statistical mechanics — via the equipartition theorem — assigns equal average energy to every mode of the radiation field; because there are infinitely many high-frequency modes, the predicted total energy diverges to infinity.

The divergence was fully recognised around 1900–1905 (the colourful name, coined by Paul Ehrenfest in 1911, came later). Observation showed emissions peaked and then fell at short wavelengths, and Max Planck's 1900 fix — quantising the energy exchanges as E = hν — both matched experiment and launched quantum theory. In Planck's law the population of high-frequency modes is exponentially suppressed because a single quantum costs more energy than the thermal average supplies.

Why it mattered

The ultraviolet catastrophe is the textbook example of a classical theory destroying itself by extrapolation: the failure was not in a detail but in the foundations — the assumed continuity of energy. Together with the Photoelectric effect and atomic spectra, it forced the discontinuity at the heart of quantum mechanics. The phrase is now used metaphorically in physics for any runaway divergence from accumulating short-distance modes.
