Abraham Wald

Abraham Wald (October 31, 1902 – December 13, 1950) was a Hungarian-American mathematician and statistician who made foundational contributions to decision theory, statistical sequential analysis, geometry and econometrics. He is widely remembered for his Second World War work on survivorship bias, in which he showed how analysts could draw correct conclusions from data on returning aircraft by accounting for the planes that never came back.

Life

Wald was educated at the University of Vienna, where he studied under Karl Menger and worked in pure geometry. Barred from academic posts in Austria as a Jew, he worked for economist Oskar Morgenstern. After the Anschluss in 1938 he emigrated to the United States, joining the Cowles Commission and then Columbia University, where he led the Statistical Research Group during the war. He and his wife died in an air crash in the Nilgiri Hills of India in December 1950 while on a lecture tour.

Contributions

Wald founded statistical decision theory, treating estimation and hypothesis testing as problems of choosing actions under uncertainty with a loss function; the Wald test and Wald's equation are named for him. He developed sequential analysis — the sequential probability ratio test — allowing experiments to end as soon as enough evidence accumulated, which saved resources in wartime quality control. His 1943 memorandum for the Statistical Research Group analysed damage patterns on returning bombers and concluded that armour belonged where the returning planes showed the least damage, because planes hit in those areas had been lost — the classic illustration of survivorship bias.
