Huáscar (Quechua: Waskar; birth name Topa Cusi Huallpa; c. 1491 – 1532/1533) was the Sapa Inca of the Inca Empire from 1527 to 1532. Succeeding his father Huayna Capac in Cusco, he fought a devastating civil war against his half-brother Atahualpa, who held the empire's northern army and capital-in-waiting at Quito. Huáscar's defeat and capture in 1532, weeks before Francisco Pizarro's landing, left the empire divided and leaderless at the moment of the Spanish invasion.

Accession

Huayna Capac died around 1527, probably of an epidemic — often identified as smallpox — that was already sweeping the Andes ahead of direct European contact; his designated heir Ninan Cuyochi died of the same disease. From Cusco, the high priesthood and nobility crowned Huáscar, a son of Huayna Capac by his sister-wife Chuqui Huipa. His name recalled the great golden chain (huasca) forged for his birth celebrations. His older half-brother Atahualpa, a favorite of his father, remained in the north with the empire's veteran field army and its generals — Quizquiz, Chalcuchimac, and Rumiñahui — a dual arrangement Huáscar came to read as rebellion in waiting.

Civil war

Relations collapsed as Huáscar arrested and humiliated his father's retainers, attempted to subordinate the great ancestral estates (panacas) and their royal mummies — the political-economic base of the Cusco nobility — and demanded Atahualpa come to Cusco and submit. After clashes at Tomebamba and the frontier, open war began about 1529. Atahualpa was captured once, at Tomebamba, and escaped; thereafter his generals' professional armies drove steadily south, winning at Mullihambato, Ambato, and Quipaipán. Huáscar's own offensives, led at first by his brother Atoc and later by himself, met repeated disaster; contemporary chronicles portray him as capricious, drunken, and contemptuous of the Inca religious establishment, alienating the very elites whose support he needed.

Capture and death

In April 1532 Huáscar was defeated and captured at the Battle of Quipaipán, near Cusco. Quizquiz occupied the capital and massacred Huáscar's family and kin. The captive emperor was carried north in humiliation. In November 1532 Pizarro captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca, and soon word came that Huáscar had offered the Spaniards an even greater ransom than Atahualpa's famous room of gold if they would free and restore him. Fearing exactly that, Atahualpa — himself a prisoner — ordered his brother killed. Huáscar was executed, by most accounts drowned in the Andamarca River (some say burned, an annihilation of the body that horrified Andeans, for it destroyed his chance of mummified ancestorhood) in late 1532 or early 1533, while the Spaniards formally blamed Atahualpa for the murder at his trial.

Legacy

The civil war, the epidemic, and the purge of the Cusco elite crippled the empire on the eve of conquest, and Huascán loyalists later collaborated with the Spaniards against Atahualpa's generals — a fissure Pizarro exploited throughout the conquest. Huáscar's name survives, among other places, in the great snow peak Huascarán, Peru's highest mountain, and in the Peruvian Navy's monitor Huáscar, captured by Chile in the War of the Pacific and preserved as a museum ship — an ironic double fame for the empire's unluckiest emperor.
