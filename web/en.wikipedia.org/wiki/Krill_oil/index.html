Krill oil

Krill oil
SourceAntarctic krill (Euphausia superba)

Key componentsEPA, DHA, phospholipids, astaxanthin

Krill oil is an extract prepared from the species of Antarctic krill Euphausia superba. Two of the most important components in krill oil are omega-3 fatty acids similar to those in fish oil, and phospholipid-derived fatty acids (PLFA), mainly phosphatidylcholine (alternatively referred to as marine lecithin). Krill oil also contains the carotenoid antioxidant astaxanthin, which gives the oil and its capsules their red colour.

Composition

Unlike fish oil, in which omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) are carried mainly on triglycerides, a large proportion of the omega-3s in krill oil are bound to phospholipids. It is claimed by manufacturers that this makes the fatty acids more bioavailable, and some studies have found higher incorporation of EPA and DHA into plasma after krill oil than after fish oil at comparable doses, though findings are mixed and doses in commercial krill products are typically lower. Trans-fatty acids are virtually absent from krill oil.

Uses and evidence

Krill oil is sold as a dietary supplement marketed for heart health, joint health, and relief of premenstrual symptoms. Research has examined its effects on blood lipids; a 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis found that krill oil supplementation significantly lowered LDL cholesterol and triglycerides while raising HDL cholesterol, though the reviewers noted the small number and size of available trials. The oil is commonly consumed as softgel capsules, which are smaller than typical fish oil capsules and are promoted as producing less fishy aftertaste or reflux.

Harvesting and sustainability

Krill are harvested in the Southern Ocean under the management of the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR), which sets precautionary catch limits. Because krill are a keystone species supporting whales, seals, penguins, and fish, the fishery has drawn scrutiny from conservation groups; some producers have obtained certification from the Marine Stewardship Council. Studies of oil from krill and from the copepod Calanus finmarchicus have also raised interest in alternative sources of marine omega-3s.
