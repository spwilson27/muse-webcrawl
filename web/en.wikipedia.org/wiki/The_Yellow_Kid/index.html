The Yellow Kid is an American comic strip character created by Richard F. Outcault in the strip Hogan's Alley, which appeared in Joseph Pulitzer's New York World from 1895 and later in William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal. The Kid — Mickey Dugan, a bald, jug-eared street urchin in an oversized yellow nightshirt — is generally credited as the first commercially successful comic-strip character and a landmark in the development of the medium.

The character and strip

Hogan's Alley depicted life in a New York tenement slum with a large cast of ragamuffin children. The Yellow Kid stood out: his nightshirt carried his dialogue — written in phonetic slum slang ("Hully gee!") — rather than speech balloons. When the feature began appearing in colour, the Kid's shirt was printed in a striking yellow that became his defining feature. The strip combined humour with occasional sharp social commentary on slum life, politics and consumer culture.

The newspaper war and "yellow journalism"

In 1896 Hearst hired Outcault away from Pulitzer; Pulitzer responded by hiring George Luks to continue Hogan's Alley, so for a time two Yellow Kids appeared in rival papers, with lawsuits over the character. The fierce circulation battle between the two publishers — in which sensational comics were a key weapon — gave rise to the term "yellow journalism", coined from the Kid's shirt, and the rival strips are often cited as one of the factors that whipped up public support for the Spanish–American War.

Innovations and legacy

The Yellow Kid was a merchandising phenomenon — appearing on buttons, cigarette packs, posters and stage productions — an early demonstration of character licensing. Outcault went on to create Buster Brown (1902). Although the Kid's own strip ended by 1898, historians regard Hogan's Alley as the template for the newspaper comic strip: recurring characters, sequential panels, integration of words and pictures, and mass popularity — the foundation on which later strips and, eventually, comic books were built.
