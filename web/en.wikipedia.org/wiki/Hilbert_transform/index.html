Hilbert transform

In mathematics and signal processing, the Hilbert transform is a specific singular integral that takes a function u(t) of a real variable and produces another function of the same variable, H(u)(t). It is given by convolution with the function 1/(πt) and is thus defined as a Cauchy principal value integral. It is named after David Hilbert, who first introduced the operator in work on Riemann–Hilbert problems.

Properties

The Hilbert transform acts as a 90-degree phase shifter: positive-frequency components are shifted by −90°, negative-frequency components by +90°, while amplitudes are unchanged. In the frequency domain it corresponds to multiplying the Fourier transform by −i·sgn(f). Applying the transform twice returns the negation of the original function.

Analytic signal

Combining a real signal with its Hilbert transform as the imaginary part yields the analytic signal, from which instantaneous amplitude (envelope) and instantaneous phase and frequency can be computed. This is fundamental in communications theory, for example in generating single-sideband modulation, and in signal demodulation.

Related topics

The discrete Hilbert transform is implemented approximately using FFT methods. The Hilbert transform of the logarithm of a magnitude spectrum underlies homomorphic signal processing, and the Kramers–Kronig relations in physics are a Hilbert-transform pair connecting the real and imaginary parts of causal response functions.
