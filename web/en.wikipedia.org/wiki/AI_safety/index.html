AI safety

AI safety studies how to build systems that reliably do what their designers intend.

AI safety is an interdisciplinary field concerned with preventing accidents, misuse and other harmful consequences that could arise from artificial intelligence systems. It encompasses technical research — on aligning systems with human intentions, robustness, interpretability and monitoring — and governance work on standards, evaluations and policy. The field ranges from near-term problems, such as bias and reliability in deployed models, to long-term concerns about highly capable future systems, sometimes discussed under existential risk from artificial intelligence.

Technical research areas

Alignment research studies how to specify goals so that a system pursues what its designers actually intend rather than a literal proxy — the problem of specification gaming or "reward hacking". Techniques include reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), constitutional methods, and debate and amplification schemes. Robustness work addresses distribution shift and adversarial inputs, aiming for systems that behave acceptably outside their training conditions. Interpretability (including mechanistic interpretability) seeks to understand the internal computations of neural networks well enough to detect deception or hidden objectives. Evaluation and auditing develop benchmarks and red-teaming methods to measure dangerous capabilities before deployment, while control research studies how to monitor and constrain models that may be more capable than their overseers.

History

Concerns about machine intelligence behaving contrary to human interests appear in Norbert Wiener's work of 1960 and in I. J. Good's 1965 speculation about an "intelligence explosion". The field took organised form in the 2000s and 2010s with the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (founded 2000), Nick Bostrom's Superintelligence (2014), Stuart Russell's Human Compatible (2019), and safety teams inside major laboratories. Public attention rose sharply after 2022 with large language models; events such as the 2023 Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit and the creation of government AI Safety Institutes in the UK and US institutionalised evaluation and standards work.

Debates

Researchers disagree about the probability and timescale of catastrophic risks from advanced AI, the tractability of alignment, and the balance between safety regulation and innovation. Critics also warn that long-term risk discourse can distract from present harms, such as discrimination and labour displacement, which others in the field treat as part of the same agenda under AI ethics. Surveys of machine learning researchers show broad support for prioritising safety research alongside capabilities.
