Pluralistic ignorance


Illustration placeholder: group members each assuming the others endorse a norm none of them privately accepts

Pluralistic ignorance is a situation in social psychology in which each member of a group privately rejects a norm, belief, or behaviour, but incorrectly assumes that most other members accept it, and therefore goes along with it publicly. It has been described as "a situation where a minority position on a given topic is wrongly perceived to be the majority position, or where the majority position is wrongly perceived to be a minority position."

Pluralistic ignorance can help explain why bystanders fail to intervene in emergencies (the bystander effect), why unpopular norms persist, and why members of a group may each believe they are alone in their doubts.

Concept and history

The term was introduced by Floyd Allport in 1924 and developed by Daniel Katz and Richard Schanck in the 1930s. A classic demonstration came from studies of racial attitudes on American campuses, where students privately held more liberal views than they attributed to their peers and conformed to the perceived, more conservative norm.

Research examples

Drinking on campus

Studies at Princeton University by Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller found that students privately felt less comfortable with campus alcohol use than they believed their peers did. Because each student assumed the others endorsed heavy drinking, the norm persisted and individuals drank more than they personally wished to.

The Emperor's New Clothes

The phenomenon is often illustrated by Hans Christian Andersen's tale "The Emperor's New Clothes", in which onlookers pretend the emperor is dressed because each believes everyone else can see the clothes.

Bystander inaction

In an emergency, each witness looks to the others for cues; seeing no one else react, each concludes the situation is not serious, and the collective calm is misread as evidence that nothing is wrong.

Consequences and remedies

Pluralistic ignorance can sustain segregation, unhealthy drinking cultures, and organisational silence in the face of misconduct. It can be dispelled when even a few individuals voice their private views, revealing the shared doubt — a dynamic also relevant to the spiral of silence. Survey feedback that publishes actual majority views (social norms marketing) has been used to correct misperceptions, for example on college campuses.
