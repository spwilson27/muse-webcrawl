The Théâtre-Français, better known as the Comédie-Française, is the French national theatre company, founded in 1680 by decree of Louis XIV through the merger of the troupe of the Hôtel Guénégaud (heir to Molière's company) and that of the Hôtel de Bourgogne. It is the oldest active theatre company in the world and the only French state theatre with its own permanent troupe of actors, the sociétaires.

History

The company was created seven years after the death of Molière and inherited his troupe's repertoire and prestige; the Comédie-Française is still nicknamed "la Maison de Molière" (the House of Molière), although Molière never worked under its name. Granted a monopoly over spoken drama in French in Paris, the company weathered the Revolution — during which its actors were briefly imprisoned — and in 1799 took up residence in the Salle Richelieu in the Palais-Royal complex, its home ever since.

Organisation

The troupe is organised under statutes tracing back to the 1680s, as codified in the Napoleonic Moscow Decree of 1812: actors progress from pensionnaire to sociétaire, sharing in the company's governance and profits under an administrateur général appointed by the government. The repertoire centres on the French classical canon — Molière, Racine, Corneille, Marivaux, Musset — alongside modern and foreign works.

Venues

Besides the Salle Richelieu, the company performs at the Théâtre du Vieux-Colombier (acquired 1993) and the Studio-Théâtre in the Carrousel du Louvre. Its archives and register of performances, kept since the 17th century, are a major resource for theatre history.
