Thoracic vertebrae

Thoracic vertebrae
Latin: vertebrae thoracicae
Identifiers: TA98 A02.2.03.001; FMA 9139
Part of: vertebral column
Articulates with: ribs, adjacent vertebrae

In vertebrates, thoracic vertebrae compose the middle segment of the vertebral column, between the cervical vertebrae and the lumbar vertebrae. In humans, there are twelve thoracic vertebrae, numbered T1 through T12 from top to bottom, and they increase in size from T1 to T12 as they bear progressively more body weight. The thoracic vertebrae articulate with the ribs and so, together with the ribs and sternum, form the thoracic cage that protects the heart and lungs.

A typical thoracic vertebra, viewed from above and from the side.

General characteristics

Thoracic vertebrae are distinguished from those of other regions by the presence of costal facets, where the heads and tubercles of the ribs articulate. A typical thoracic vertebra has a medium-sized, heart-shaped vertebral body; a long, downward-sloping spinous process; and transverse processes that bear facets for the tubercles of the ribs. The vertebral foramen is roughly circular and smaller than in the cervical region.

Individual vertebrae

The first thoracic vertebra (T1) resembles a cervical vertebra in some respects and articulates with the first rib. The ninth through twelfth vertebrae have progressively more lumbar-like features; T11 and T12 lack facets on their transverse processes, and T12 in particular resembles a lumbar vertebra. The atypical vertebrae (T1, T9–T12) have complete costal facets for their own ribs only, whereas typical thoracic vertebrae have demifacets shared between adjacent levels.

Clinical significance

Excessive curvature of the thoracic spine produces kyphosis. Thoracic vertebral fractures may result from trauma or from osteoporosis-related compression, and because the spinal canal is relatively narrow in this region, fractures here carry a significant risk of spinal cord injury.

Other animals

The number of thoracic vertebrae varies among mammals: most have between 12 and 15, while some species, such as sloths, depart widely from this range. In birds and other vertebrates, thoracic vertebrae may be fused to provide rigidity for locomotion or flight.
