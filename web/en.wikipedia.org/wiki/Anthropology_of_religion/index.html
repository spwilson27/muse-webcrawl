Anthropology of religion

The anthropology of religion involves the study of religious institutions in relation to other social institutions, and the comparison of religious beliefs and practices across cultures. It is one of the foundational subfields of anthropology, central to the work of early figures such as E. B. Tylor and James George Frazer.

History

Early anthropologists debated religion's origins: Tylor proposed animism as the earliest form; Frazer's The Golden Bough traced a progression from magic through religion to science. Émile Durkheim argued in The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912) that religion expresses society worshipping itself, distinguishing the sacred from the profane.

Key concepts

Core topics include myth, ritual, rites of passage (van Gennep; Victor Turner's concept of liminality and communitas), sacrifice, shamanism, witchcraft and sorcery, syncretism, millenarian movements, and the definition of religion itself — Clifford Geertz famously defined religion as a cultural system of symbols establishing powerful moods and motivations.

Contemporary directions

Recent work studies world religions anthropologically, religion and politics, secularism, pilgrimage, and the cognitive science of religion, which asks how features of human cognition shape recurring religious ideas.
