Palaestra

A palaestra (Greek: παλαίστρα) was a wrestling school in ancient Greece, functioning as part of or alongside the gymnasium. It consisted of a rectangular courtyard surrounded by colonnades and rooms, where athletes trained in wrestling, boxing and the pankration — the "ground" or combat events, as distinct from running and throwing, which took place in the open gymnasium and stadium. By extension, the word came to denote the whole exercise ground, and in Roman times large palaestrae were built within bath complexes.

Architecture

The Roman architect Vitruvius describes the palaestra in De architectura: a square or oblong peristyle courtyard, with porticoes on all sides and exedrae and rooms for lectures, oiling, dusting with sand, washing and storing equipment. The courtyard surface was of sand, softening falls in wrestling. The palaestra at Olympia, built in the 3rd century BCE, measured about 66 metres square and is one of the best-preserved examples.

Social role

More than a sports facility, the palaestra was a social and intellectual centre for male citizens and ephebes: philosophers taught in its exedrae, and it features in Plato's dialogues — the Charmides and Lysis are set in palaestrae. Training was supervised by a paidotribes (trainer), and boys exercised naked, anointed with olive oil that was scraped off afterwards with a strigil.
