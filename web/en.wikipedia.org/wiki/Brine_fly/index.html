Brine fly

Scientific name: Ephydra hians (and relatives)

Family: Ephydridae (shore flies)

Habitat: Alkaline and saline lakes

Brine flies are small flies of the family Ephydridae that live in and around highly saline and alkaline waters, most famously the alkali fly Ephydra hians of Mono Lake in California and the brine flies of the Great Salt Lake in Utah. Their larvae graze on algae in water far too salty for most animals, and adult flies can walk underwater enclosed in a bubble of air.

Underwater behaviour

Adult alkali flies crawl down rocks and vegetation into the water to feed and lay eggs. Their bodies are covered with dense, water-repellent hairs and a waxy coating that traps a silvery film of air around them, forming a bubble that functions as a physical gill and prevents wetting. Mark Twain described the Mono Lake flies in Roughing It (1872), noting that "you can hold them under water as long as you please — they do not mind it — they are only proud of it", and that they emerge dry when released. Research published in 2017 showed that the flies' extra-hairy, wax-coated bodies, together with the lake's high carbonate content, make water "wetter" to other insects but keep the alkali flies dry.

Ecology

Brine flies are enormously abundant: at the Great Salt Lake and Mono Lake, billions of flies and their pupae provide a critical food source for migrating birds, including eared grebes, phalaropes, and California gulls. Native peoples, including the Kutzadika'a of Mono Lake, traditionally harvested the pupae (called kutsavi) as a food rich in protein and fat. The flies coexist with brine shrimp in hypersaline food webs based on algae.
