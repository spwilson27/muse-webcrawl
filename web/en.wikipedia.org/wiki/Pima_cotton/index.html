Pima cotton is a type of extra-long staple cotton grown chiefly in the southwestern United States, derived from the species Gossypium barbadense. It is prized for fibres that are longer, stronger, and finer than ordinary upland cotton, producing soft, durable, and lustrous fabrics.

History

The cotton takes its name from the Pima Indians (Akimel O'odham) of Arizona, who helped cultivate experimental crops at the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Sacaton station in the early 1900s. Breeders crossed Egyptian and Sea Island cottons to produce a variety suited to the irrigated deserts of Arizona, California, New Mexico, and Texas. The best-known trademarked form is Supima ("superior Pima"), a brand maintained by American growers since 1954.

Properties

Pima fibres typically measure 35 mm or longer. The resulting yarns resist pilling, fraying, and fading better than short-staple cotton and are used in premium shirting, towels, sheets, and knitwear.

Cultivation

Pima requires a long, hot growing season with irrigation and is hand- or machine-picked with greater care than upland cotton. Peru also grows a celebrated G. barbadense cotton marketed under the same name.
