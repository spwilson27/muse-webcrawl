2I/Borisov, originally designated C/2019 Q4 (Borisov), is the first observed interstellar comet and the second observed interstellar object after ʻOumuamua. It was discovered on 30 August 2019 by the Crimean amateur astronomer Gennady Borisov with a 0.65-metre telescope he had built himself, and was confirmed to be on a hyperbolic trajectory with an eccentricity of about 3.36, proving it originated outside the Solar System.

Characteristics

Unlike the inert, enigmatic ʻOumuamua, Borisov behaved like a familiar Solar System comet: as it approached the Sun it developed a coma and a short dust tail, and spectroscopic observations showed the usual cometary gases, including cyanogen and diatomic carbon. Hubble Space Telescope observations constrained its nucleus to roughly 400–500 metres across. Ultraviolet and submillimetre observations revealed it was unusually rich in carbon monoxide relative to water — more CO-rich than nearly all Solar System comets — suggesting it formed in the cold outer reaches of its home system, possibly around a red dwarf star.

Trajectory and fate

Borisov reached perihelion at just over 2 AU on 8 December 2019 and passed closest to Earth later that month at about 1.9 AU. In March 2020 observers reported the comet ejecting material and apparently splitting, with a fragment separating from the nucleus, though it survived as a single main body. It is now receding into interstellar space in the direction of the constellation Telescopium and will never return.

Significance

Because Borisov was discovered inbound and could be studied for months, it provided the first detailed look at the chemistry of a body formed around another star, demonstrating that the raw materials of comet formation elsewhere closely resemble our own — with carbon monoxide abundance the notable exception that hints at its birth environment.
