The half-value layer (HVL) of a material is the thickness of that material required to reduce the intensity of a beam of radiation — typically X-rays or gamma rays — to one half of its initial value. It is a standard measure of beam quality in medical physics and of shielding effectiveness in radiation protection.

Definition

For a monoenergetic, narrow beam undergoing exponential attenuation, intensity follows I = I0·e−μx, where μ is the linear attenuation coefficient. The half-value layer is then given by HVL = ln 2 / μ ≈ 0.693 / μ. For polyenergetic X-ray beams, a first HVL (the thickness reducing air kerma to half) is used to characterise beam quality and specify filtration.

Typical values

HVL depends strongly on photon energy and material density. For example, the HVL of lead is about 0.4 mm for caesium-137 gamma rays (662 keV) and roughly 1 cm at higher megavoltage therapy energies, while the HVL of concrete for the same caesium source is several centimetres. In diagnostic radiology, HVL is measured in millimetres of aluminium, with typical values of 2–5 mm Al.

Applications

Shielding designers commonly use HVLs (and the related tenth-value layer, TVL) to estimate barrier thicknesses quickly: each additional HVL halves the transmitted intensity. The concept is analogous to the half-life in radioactive decay and to the mass attenuation coefficient formalism.
