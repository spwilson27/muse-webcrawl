Ikat

Ikat (from Malay and Indonesian mengikat, "to tie") is a dyeing technique originating from Indonesia used to pattern textiles, employing resist dyeing on the yarns prior to weaving. The threads are tightly wrapped in bundles according to the desired pattern, dyed (sometimes repeatedly in several colours), and then woven; the pattern emerges as the pre-dyed threads are aligned on the loom, giving ikat its characteristic slightly blurred or "feathered" edges.

Types

In warp ikat, only the warp threads are resist-dyed; in weft ikat the weft carrying the pattern must be carefully aligned during weaving, making it slower and more difficult. Double ikat, in which both warp and weft are dyed so the pattern interlocks precisely, is famously produced in Tenganan, Bali, and in the patola cloths of Patan, India, historically traded to Indonesia.

Distribution

Ikat is made across Indonesia, and related techniques developed in India, Japan (where it is called kasuri), Central Asia (the abr silks of Uzbekistan), West Africa, and pre-Columbian and modern Central and South America.
