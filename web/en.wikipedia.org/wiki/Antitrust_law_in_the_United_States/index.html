United States antitrust law

Antitrust law in the United States
Key statutesSherman Act (1890); Clayton Act (1914); Federal Trade Commission Act (1914); Hart–Scott–Rodino Act (1976)
EnforcersDOJ Antitrust Division; Federal Trade Commission; state attorneys general; private plaintiffs

Antitrust law in the United States is a collection of federal and state government laws that regulate the conduct and organization of businesses in order to promote competition and prevent unjustified monopolies. The three core federal statutes are the Sherman Act of 1890, the Clayton Act of 1914, and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914. Enforcement is shared between the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission, and private parties may also sue, with successful plaintiffs entitled to treble damages.

History

The Sherman Act, named for Senator John Sherman, was a response to the growth of giant industrial "trusts" such as Standard Oil. Section 1 prohibits contracts, combinations, and conspiracies in restraint of trade, while Section 2 prohibits monopolization and attempts to monopolize. Early enforcement was weak, but the Act was famously used to break up Standard Oil and American Tobacco in 1911. The rule of reason, articulated in Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States (1911), holds that only unreasonable restraints of trade violate Section 1, though certain conduct such as price-fixing is treated as illegal per se.

Clayton Act and later legislation

The Clayton Act addressed specific practices the Sherman Act had not clearly reached, including price discrimination (later amended by the Robinson–Patman Act), exclusive dealing, and mergers whose effect "may be substantially to lessen competition" (strengthened by the Celler–Kefauver Act of 1950). The Hart–Scott–Rodino Act of 1976 requires parties to large mergers to notify the agencies and observe a waiting period before closing. Later landmarks include the 1982 breakup of AT&T and the government's case against Microsoft around 2000.

Modern debates

From the 1970s, the "consumer welfare standard" associated with the Chicago School narrowed enforcement by focusing on effects on prices and output. In the 2010s and 2020s a "New Brandeis" movement argued for a broader approach attentive to market structure and the power of large technology platforms, prompting renewed litigation and debate over reform.
