Fair trade is a trade arrangement designed to help producers in developing countries achieve sustainable and equitable trade relationships. The fair trade movement combines the payment of higher prices to exporters with improved social and environmental standards. The movement focuses in particular on commodities, or products that are typically exported from developing countries to developed countries, but is also used in domestic markets, most notably in handicrafts, coffee, cocoa, wine, sugar, fruit, flowers and gold.

History

The movement emerged after the Second World War with religious and solidarity organisations such as Ten Thousand Villages (founded 1946) selling handicrafts from poor communities. In the Netherlands, Fair Trade Original began importing cane sugar and crafts in the 1950s–60s. The first formal fair trade certification label, Max Havelaar, was launched in 1988 in the Netherlands, initially for coffee from Mexican cooperatives. National labelling initiatives merged into Fairtrade Labelling Organizations International (FLO, now Fairtrade International) in 1997.

Principles

  • A minimum price covering the average costs of sustainably producing a crop, acting as a safety net when market prices fall.
  • A Fairtrade Premium — an additional sum paid to producer organisations for community development projects.
  • Improved working conditions and prohibition of forced and child labour.
  • Democratic organisation of smallholder cooperatives.
  • Environmental standards restricting harmful agrochemicals.

Products and scale

Coffee was the first and remains one of the largest fair trade products; others include cocoa, bananas (about one-third of bananas sold in some European markets are Fairtrade-certified), tea, cotton and flowers. By the 2020s, more than 1.8 million farmers and workers participated in Fairtrade-certified producer organisations worldwide.

Criticism

Critics, including some economists, argue that certification benefits only a small share of production (producers typically sell only part of their crop on fair trade terms), that fees burden poor producers, and that the minimum price can encourage oversupply. Other studies and organisations dispute these findings and defend the movement's community and income effects.
