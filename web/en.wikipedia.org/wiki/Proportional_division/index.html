Proportional division

A proportional division is a kind of fair division in which a resource is divided among n partners such that each partner receives a part worth at least 1/n of the whole resource by their own subjective valuation. Proportionality is one of the most basic fairness criteria in the theory of fair division, studied since the 1940s in the context of problems such as cake cutting and the division of estates.

Definitions

A division is proportional if every participant values their own share at no less than their proportional entitlement—an equal share when all entitlements are equal, or a weighted share when participants have differing claims, as in bankruptcy problems. A stronger requirement, envy-freeness, demands that no participant prefers another's share to their own; every envy-free division is proportional, but not vice versa.

Protocols

For two people, the classic divide-and-choose procedure guarantees proportionality. For more participants, procedures such as the last-diminisher protocol and the Dubins–Spanier moving-knife procedure achieve proportional division of a divisible resource. Proportional division of indivisible goods is generally impossible exactly, and the theory studies relaxations and randomized mechanisms. See also fair division and fair cake-cutting.
