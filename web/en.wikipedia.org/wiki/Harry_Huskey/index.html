Harry Douglas Huskey (January 19, 1916 – April 9, 2017) was an American computer scientist and mathematician, one of the earliest pioneers of electronic digital computing. He worked on the ENIAC and EDVAC projects, designed the SWAC computer at the National Bureau of Standards, led the design of the commercially successful Bendix G-15, and spent much of his later career teaching at the University of California.

Early life and education

Huskey was born in the Smoky Mountains of North Carolina and grew up in Idaho. He studied mathematics at the University of Idaho and Ohio State University, receiving his PhD in 1943 with research on quasi-groups. He taught mathematics at the University of Pennsylvania, where the ENIAC project was underway.

Computing career

At Penn, Huskey joined the ENIAC team and travelled to England in 1946–47, where he worked with Alan Turing at the National Physical Laboratory on the ACE design. Back in the United States he joined the National Bureau of Standards' Institute for Numerical Analysis at UCLA, where he directed the construction of the SWAC (Standards Western Automatic Computer), completed in 1950 and for a time the fastest computer in the world. He later consulted for the Bendix Corporation, designing the G-15 (1956), often described as one of the first "personal" computers because it could be run by a single operator. He also designed the G-20.

Academic career

Huskey joined the University of California, Berkeley in 1954, teaching mathematics and helping to found its computer centre and computer science teaching; he later moved to the University of California, Santa Cruz, where he helped establish the computer and information sciences programme and was instrumental in building computer science education in several countries abroad. He was president of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) in the early 1980s and received the IEEE Computer Pioneer Award. Huskey died in 2017 at the age of 101, among the last surviving members of the ENIAC generation.
