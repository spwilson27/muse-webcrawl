Ems Telegram

The Ems Telegram (also known as the Ems Dispatch) was a telegram sent on 13 July 1870 by Heinrich Abeken, a senior official of the Prussian Foreign Office, from the spa town of Bad Ems to Chancellor Otto von Bismarck in Berlin. Its edited publication by Bismarck provoked France into declaring war on Prussia, setting off the Franco-Prussian War.

Background

After the Spanish throne was offered to Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, a relative of the Prussian king, France demanded guarantees that the candidacy would never be renewed. Leopold's candidacy was withdrawn, but the French ambassador, Count Vincent Benedetti, pressed King Wilhelm I at Bad Ems for a formal assurance. The King politely declined and reported the encounter by telegram.

Bismarck's edit

Bismarck, dining with generals von Moltke and von Roon, condensed Abeken's report so that the encounter read as a mutual insult: the King appeared to have brusquely dismissed the ambassador, and the ambassador appeared to have demanded the impossible. Released to the press on Bastille Day, the edited text inflamed French opinion — Bismarck later said it acted "like a red rag on the Gallic bull". Mistranslation of the German word Adjutant into French worsened the impression of a snub.

Consequences

France declared war on 19 July 1870. The Franco-Prussian War ended in French defeat, the capture of Napoleon III at Sedan, the proclamation of the German Empire at Versailles in January 1871, and French revanchism that shaped European politics for decades.
