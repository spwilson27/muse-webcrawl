Yamato period

The Yamato period (大和時代, Yamato-jidai) is the period of Japanese history when the imperial court ruled from what is now Nara Prefecture, then known as Yamato Province. Conventionally dated from about 250 to 710 AD, it is divided into the Kofun period (c. 250–538) and the Asuka period (538–710), and marks the emergence of a unified Japanese state under the Yamato court.

Kofun period

The period takes its first name from the enormous keyhole-shaped burial mounds (kofun) built for the Yamato kings and great clan chiefs, the largest being the Daisen Kofun attributed to Emperor Nintoku. Power rested with the Yamato court and allied uji clans, administered through hereditary occupational groups (be). Through the immigrant toraijin from the Korean peninsula and China, Japan received iron-working, horse trappings, Sue ware pottery, sericulture, and writing. Yamato maintained close ties with the Korean kingdoms, particularly Baekje, and at times a Japanese presence (the "Wa") is recorded on the peninsula.

Asuka period

The introduction of Buddhism — traditionally dated to 538 or 552, when the king of Baekje sent a Buddha image and sutras — opens the Asuka period, named for the Asuka region south of Nara where the court was often based. Buddhism was championed by the Soga clan and by Prince Shōtoku, regent from 593, whose Seventeen-article Constitution (604) and cap-ranks system began centralising government on Chinese models. After the Soga were overthrown in 645, the Taika Reforms sought to establish a Chinese-style bureaucratic empire; the process culminated in the Taihō Code (701) and the transfer of the capital to Nara in 710.

Society and culture

The Yamato state laid the foundations of the Japanese imperial institution, which claims descent from the Yamato kings and regards them as descendants of the sun goddess Amaterasu. Shinto shrine worship developed alongside the imported Buddhist faith, and Chinese characters were adapted to write Japanese, producing the earliest Japanese literature by the following Nara period.
