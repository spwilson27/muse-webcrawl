Charles Scott Sherrington

Sir Charles Scott Sherrington (27 November 1857 – 4 March 1952) was an English neurophysiologist, histologist, bacteriologist and pathologist. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1932 with Edgar Adrian for their work on the functions of neurons. Sherrington coined the terms synapse and proprioception and founded the modern study of the integrative action of the nervous system.

Life and career

Sherrington studied medicine at St Thomas' Hospital and Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge. He held chairs at the University of Liverpool and King's College London before becoming Waynflete Professor of Physiology at Oxford in 1913. He served as president of the Royal Society from 1920 to 1925.

Work

His classic The Integrative Action of the Nervous System (1906) laid out how reflexes are coordinated by the spinal cord and brain, introducing concepts such as reciprocal inhibition and the final common pathway of motor neurons. Working with the anatomist Michael Foster's circle and corresponding with Santiago Ramón y Cajal, Sherrington gave the junction between neurons the name "synapse" (from the Greek for "to clasp"). He also mapped dermatomes, developed the concept of proprioception — the sense of the body's position — and described decerebrate rigidity.
