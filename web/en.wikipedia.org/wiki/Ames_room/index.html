An Ames room is a distorted room used to create an optical illusion of relative sizes. Invented in 1934 by American ophthalmologist Adelbert Ames Jr., and constructed the following year by carpenter William H. Ittelson, the room appears to be an ordinary rectangular room when viewed monocularly through a peephole, but its true shape is trapezoidal: the walls are slanted and the ceiling and floor are at angles, so that one back corner is much farther from the viewer than the other.

Effect

Because the viewer assumes the room is rectangular, a person standing in the far corner appears dramatically smaller than a person in the near corner, and someone walking across the back of the room appears to grow or shrink as they move. The illusion demonstrates the visual system's reliance on assumptions about the environment — in this case, that rooms are rectangular and that objects at the same apparent position are at the same distance.

Uses

Ames rooms have been used in psychology demonstrations and in film-making to create size effects, a famous example being the forced-perspective techniques in The Lord of the Rings films, which employed related principles. Related illusions include the Ames window and the Ames chair.
