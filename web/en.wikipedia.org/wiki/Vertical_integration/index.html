Vertical integration

A vertically integrated firm controls multiple stages of its supply chain.

In microeconomics, management, and international political economy, vertical integration is an arrangement in which the supply chain of a company is integrated and owned by that company. Usually each member of the supply chain produces a different product or (market-specific) service, and the products combine to satisfy a common need. It contrasts with horizontal integration, wherein a company produces several items that are related to one another.

Types

Backward integration occurs when a company expands into activities concerned with the inputs it uses — for example, a car manufacturer acquiring a steel mill. Forward integration occurs when a company expands toward its customers — for example, a film studio acquiring a cinema chain, or a manufacturer opening its own retail stores. Balanced integration is a combination of both.

Advantages and disadvantages

Vertical integration can lower transaction costs, secure supplies, improve coordination, and capture upstream and downstream profit margins. It can also reduce flexibility, raise capital requirements, and create barriers to entry that attract antitrust scrutiny. The decision to "make or buy" is central to transaction cost economics, associated with Ronald Coase and Oliver Williamson.

Examples

Historic examples include Andrew Carnegie's steel empire, which controlled iron ore mines, railroads, and mills, and the Ford Motor Company's River Rouge Complex, which took in raw materials and shipped out finished cars. Modern examples include Apple designing its own silicon, Netflix producing original content, and SpaceX manufacturing most components in-house.
