A brain–computer interface (BCI), sometimes called a brain–machine interface, is a direct communication pathway between an enhanced or wired brain and an external device. BCIs are often used to assist, augment, or repair human cognitive or sensory-motor functions, bypassing the body's normal neuromuscular output channels.

Approaches

BCIs are classified by invasiveness. Non-invasive systems, most commonly electroencephalography (EEG) headsets, record brain activity from the scalp with limited bandwidth but no surgical risk. Partially invasive systems such as electrocorticography (ECoG) place electrodes on the brain's surface. Invasive systems implant microelectrode arrays in the cortex, recording spikes from individual neurons with high fidelity but requiring neurosurgery and facing long-term signal stability challenges.

Applications

Clinical research has enabled people with paralysis to control computer cursors, robotic arms, and their own re-animated limbs, and to type and generate speech via decoding of attempted movement or imagined handwriting. In 2004 Matthew Nagle became the first human to receive a chronic intracortical implant (the BrainGate system) and control a prosthetic arm. Companies such as Neuralink and Synchron have since begun human trials of implanted and endovascular devices. Animal research dates to the 1960s–70s, including work by Eberhard Fetz and by Jacques Vidal, who coined the term "brain–computer interface" in 1973.

Ethics

The technology raises questions of mental privacy, agency, identity, and equitable access, which ethicists group under the emerging field of neurorights; Chile in 2021 became the first country to legislate protections for mental data and neurotechnology.
