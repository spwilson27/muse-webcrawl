The Kingdom of Kent (Old English: Cantwara rīce) was an early medieval kingdom in what is now South East England, one of the seven traditional kingdoms of the Anglo-Saxon Heptarchy. Founded by Jutish settlers in the fifth century — traditionally by the brothers Hengist and Horsa — it was the earliest of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms to be documented in detail, the first to convert to Christianity, and the first to issue a written law code. It was gradually subordinated to stronger neighbours from the late seventh century and finally absorbed into Wessex in the ninth century.

History

Origins

Roman Cantium, the territory of the Cantiaci, was among the first parts of post-Roman Britain to be settled by Germanic peoples, identified by Bede as Jutes. Tradition, recorded by Bede and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, tells how the British king Vortigern invited the brothers Hengist and Horsa to settle on the Isle of Thanet and how they turned on their hosts, founding the Kentish kingdom around 455; Horsa is said to have been killed at the Battle of Aylesford. Whatever the literal truth, archaeology shows rich Jutish cemeteries and strong Frankish connections across the Channel.

Æthelberht and Christianisation

Kent reached its greatest power under Æthelberht (r. c. 589–616), third of the Bretwaldas, who married the Frankish Christian princess Bertha and in 597 received the mission of St Augustine sent by Pope Gregory the Great. Æthelberht's conversion made Canterbury the cradle of the English Church — Augustine became its first archbishop — and his law code (c. 602), the earliest surviving document in Old English, fixed tariffs of compensation in 90 clauses. Kentish primacy passed after his death; his son Eadbald initially relapsed into paganism before converting.

Decline and absorption

From the later seventh century Kent came under pressure from Mercia and Wessex; King Wihtred (r. 690–725) restored order and issued his own laws, but after his death the kingdom fragmented among rival kings. Mercian supremacy under Offa (who briefly abolished the Kentish kingship) was followed by the Kentish revolt of Eadberht Præn (796–798). In 825, after the Battle of Ellandun, Kent passed to Wessex, being governed by West Saxon sub-kings; Viking attacks, including the Great Heathen Army's wintering on Thanet and Sheppey, ravaged the county, and in 871 the last vestiges of a separate Kentish kingdom disappeared into the unified West Saxon realm.

Legacy

Kent's dual division into eastern and western lathes, its distinctive customs such as gavelkind land tenure, and the archbishopric of Canterbury all long preserved the imprint of the early kingdom, and the modern ceremonial county of Kent closely matches its extent.
