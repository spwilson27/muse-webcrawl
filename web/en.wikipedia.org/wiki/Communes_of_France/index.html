The communes of France are the lowest level of administrative division in the French Republic. A commune is a town or municipality governed by a municipal council and a mayor. France has roughly 35,000 communes — far more than any other European country — ranging from Paris, with over two million inhabitants, to tiny villages with a handful of residents, and even a few destroyed in the First World War that retain a mayor and council despite having no population.

History

The communes were created during the French Revolution, by decrees of December 1789, largely following the boundaries of existing parishes and towns; France began with some 41,000 of them. Unlike in many countries, subsequent reforms rarely forced mergers, so the map of communes still reflects medieval settlement patterns. Voluntary mergers accelerated after 2010 under the commune nouvelle framework, slightly reducing the total.

Governance and powers

Communes are responsible for local services including civil registration, primary schools' premises, local roads, water supply, planning permission, and municipal police in some areas. The mayor is both the executive of the commune and an agent of the state, officiating at marriages and maintaining public order. Councillors are elected for six-year terms; the number of seats scales with population.

Intercommunality

Because many communes are too small to deliver services efficiently, most belong to intercommunal bodies (intercommunalités) — communities of communes, agglomeration communities, or metropolises — which pool functions such as transport, waste, and economic development while leaving the communes themselves intact.
