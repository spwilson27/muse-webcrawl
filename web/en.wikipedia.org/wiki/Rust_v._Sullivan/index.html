Rust v. Sullivan

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Full nameIrving Rust et al. v. Louis W. Sullivan, Secretary of Health and Human Services, et al.

Rust v. Sullivan, 500 U.S. 173 (1991), was a decision of the Supreme Court of the United States that upheld regulations of the Department of Health and Human Services prohibiting employees in federally funded family-planning projects from engaging in abortion counseling or referrals, a restriction commonly known as the "gag rule."

Background

Title X of the Public Health Service Act, enacted in 1970, provides federal funding for family planning services, but Section 1008 of the Act states that none of the funds "shall be used in programs where abortion is a method of family planning." In 1988, the Secretary of Health and Human Services issued new regulations interpreting Section 1008 to bar Title X projects from counseling about, referring for, or advocating abortion, and requiring physical and financial separation between Title X projects and abortion-related activities.

Dr. Irving Rust and other Title X grantees, together with physicians, challenged the regulations on statutory and constitutional grounds, arguing that they exceeded the Secretary's authority and violated the First and Fifth Amendments.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Chief Justice William Rehnquist, the Court upheld the regulations. The Court held that the regulations were a permissible construction of Title X under the deference framework of Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., because Congress had not spoken directly to the issue and the Secretary's interpretation was reasonable. On the First Amendment claim, the Court reasoned that the government may selectively fund a program to encourage certain activities it believes to be in the public interest while declining to fund others, and that the regulations did not suppress dangerous ideas but merely ensured that federal funds were spent for the purposes for which they were authorized. The Court also rejected the Fifth Amendment argument, holding that the regulations did not deny women a right to obtain an abortion but only limited the scope of the federally funded project.

Aftermath

The regulations were rescinded by President Bill Clinton in 1993, shortly after he took office. The case remains a frequently cited precedent on government funding of speech and on the unconstitutional conditions doctrine, and later decisions, including Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez (2001), distinguished its reasoning.
