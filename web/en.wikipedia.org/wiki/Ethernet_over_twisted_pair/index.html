Ethernet over twisted pair

Ethernet over twisted-pair technologies use twisted-pair cables for the physical layer of an Ethernet computer network. They are a subset of all Ethernet physical layers. Twisted-pair cabling, terminated with 8P8C modular connectors (often called RJ45), became the dominant medium for local area networks from the 1990s onward, displacing coaxial cable.

History

Early Ethernet ran over coaxial cable, but workstation wiring was far easier with the unshielded twisted-pair cable already used for telephones. StarLAN (1 Mbit/s) demonstrated the concept in the mid-1980s, and in 1990 the IEEE standardised 10BASE-T (IEEE 802.3i), which ran 10 Mbit/s Ethernet over two pairs of Category 3 cable up to 100 metres. Its star topology, centred on hubs and later switches, made structured cabling the norm in offices.

Standards

Successive generations increased speed while retaining the 100-metre channel limit: 100BASE-TX (Fast Ethernet, 1995) uses two pairs of Category 5 cable; 1000BASE-T (Gigabit Ethernet, 1999) uses all four pairs of Category 5e with echo cancellation and PAM-5 signalling; 10GBASE-T (2006) requires Category 6a for the full 100 m. Shorter-reach variants include 2.5GBASE-T and 5GBASE-T over Cat 5e/6, and single-pair Ethernet (10BASE-T1) for automotive and industrial use. Pin assignments follow the T568A or T568B conventions; modern equipment automatically handles crossover through Auto MDI-X.

Cabling

Twisting the pairs reduces crosstalk and electromagnetic interference. Cable categories (Cat 5e, Cat 6, Cat 6a, Cat 8) specify bandwidth and crosstalk performance. The same cabling can carry power via Power over Ethernet (PoE), supplying IP phones, cameras and wireless access points.
