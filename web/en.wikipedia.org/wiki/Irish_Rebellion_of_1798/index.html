Irish Rebellion of 1798

Date24 May – 12 October 1798
LocationIreland
ResultRebellion suppressed; Acts of Union 1800

The Irish Rebellion of 1798 (Irish: Éirí Amach 1798) was a major uprising against British rule in Ireland, organised principally by the Society of United Irishmen, a republican movement inspired by the American and French revolutions and led by figures including Theobald Wolfe Tone and Lord Edward FitzGerald.

Background

The United Irishmen, founded in Belfast in 1791, sought parliamentary reform and Catholic emancipation, and increasingly an independent Irish republic. The movement united Catholics, Protestants and Dissenters, but government repression, martial law and the disarming of Ulster drove it underground and towards armed revolt with promised French assistance. A French expedition under General Hoche was scattered by storms at Bantry Bay in December 1796.

The rising

The rebellion broke out in May 1798 in Leinster, with fighting in counties Kildare, Meath and Wexford, where rebels held Enniscorthy and Wexford town before being defeated at the Battle of Vinegar Hill on 21 June. In Ulster, risings led by Henry Joy McCracken in Antrim and Henry Munro in Down were quickly crushed. A small French force under General Humbert landed at Killala in August, won at Castlebar, but surrendered at Ballinamuck in September. Wolfe Tone, captured aboard a French ship at Lough Swilly, died in prison in November.

Aftermath

Estimates of the dead range from 10,000 to 30,000 or more. The rebellion was followed by the Acts of Union 1800, which abolished the Irish Parliament and created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. The rising of 1798 — "the Year of the French" — remains a foundational episode in Irish republican tradition, commemorated in songs such as "The Wind That Shakes the Barley" and "Kelly the Boy from Killanne".
