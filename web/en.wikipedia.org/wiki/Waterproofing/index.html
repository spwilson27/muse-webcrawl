Waterproofing

Applying a sheet waterproofing membrane to a flat roof.

Waterproofing is the process of making an object or structure waterproof or water-resistant so that it remains relatively unaffected by water or resisting the ingress of water under specified conditions. Such items may be used in wet environments or underwater to specified depths. The term is applied in construction to protect buildings, bridges, tunnels, basements and roofs from water ingress, and also to clothing, electronics and consumer goods.

Methods in construction

Building waterproofing systems take several forms. Membrane systems — sheets of modified bitumen, PVC, TPO or EPDM rubber — are applied to roofs and below-grade walls, with seams heat-welded or glued. Liquid-applied membranes (polyurethane, acrylic or cementitious coatings) are sprayed or rolled on to form seamless layers. Crystalline admixtures and coatings react with concrete to block pores. Waterstops are embedded in construction joints to seal them, and drainage composites and dimpled sheets relieve hydrostatic pressure against basement walls. Interior systems combine sealants, sump pumps and interior drainage channels.

Waterproofing versus damp proofing

Waterproofing resists water under hydrostatic pressure (for example, a below-grade wall below the water table), whereas damp proofing merely resists dampness and capillary moisture without hydrostatic load. Selection depends on the water table level, soil conditions and the structure's use. Failure modes include poor detailing at joints and penetrations, mechanical damage to membranes, and ultraviolet degradation of exposed materials. Waterproofing is tested by flood tests, electronic leak detection and, for clothing, hydrostatic head tests.
