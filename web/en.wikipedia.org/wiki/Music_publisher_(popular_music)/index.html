Music publisher (popular music)

A music publisher in popular music is an individual or company responsible for exploiting, administering and protecting musical works (songs) on behalf of their composers and songwriters. Where a record label deals with sound recordings, the music publisher deals with the underlying composition — the melody and lyrics — and earns income whenever that composition is recorded, performed, broadcast, streamed or licensed.

A music publisher administers songs and collects royalties for songwriters.

Functions

The publisher's core functions include registering works with performing rights organizations such as ASCAP, BMI and PRS; issuing mechanical licences; collecting performance, mechanical and synchronization royalties; placing songs with recording artists; and pitching works for use in film, television, advertising and video games ("sync licensing"). Major publishing groups include Sony Music Publishing, Universal Music Publishing Group and Warner Chappell Music.

Publishing deals

Songwriters typically assign some or all of their copyright to a publisher in exchange for an advance and administrative services. Common arrangements include full publishing deals, co-publishing deals (in which the writer retains a share of the publishing income) and administration deals, in which the writer keeps ownership and the publisher merely collects royalties for a fee.

History

Music publishing grew out of the sheet-music trade of the nineteenth century, epitomised by New York's Tin Pan Alley. As radio, records and later streaming transformed the industry, publishers became central to the economics of popular songwriting, and publishing catalogues became valuable investment assets.
