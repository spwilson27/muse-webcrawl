Hard bop is a subgenre of jazz that developed in the mid-1950s, incorporating influences from rhythm and blues, gospel music, and the blues, especially in the saxophone and piano playing. It is generally considered a response by African American musicians to the cool jazz and West Coast styles that had become commercially dominant, re-emphasising the blues roots and church inflections of the music.

Characteristics

Hard bop retained bebop's fast tempos, complex chord changes and improvisational virtuosity, but melodies became simpler and more soulful, often built on gospel-like piano voicings and blues forms. Drumming grew more assertive and interactive, pianists used percussive block chords, and the standard ensemble became the quintet of trumpet, saxophone, piano, bass and drums. The term "hard bop" was coined by critic John S. Wilson in 1954, and the style's recording home was above all Alfred Lion's Blue Note Records.

History

The style coalesced around 1954–1955 in the groups of drummer Art Blakey and pianist Horace Silver — the Jazz Messengers — and in the quintet of trumpeter Clifford Brown and drummer Max Roach. Miles Davis's recordings with his first great quintet (featuring John Coltrane and Red Garland), Sonny Rollins's albums of 1956–57 (Saxophone Colossus, Tenor Madness), and Charles Mingus's workshop bands extended the idiom. Cannonball Adderley and the soul jazz offshoot brought hard bop inflections to a wide audience with hits such as "Mercy, Mercy, Mercy" (1966). By the late 1960s hard bop had evolved into post-bop, but Blakey's Jazz Messengers remained a hard bop institution and finishing school for young musicians until 1990.
