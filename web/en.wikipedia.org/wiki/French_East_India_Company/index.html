French East India Company

A French East India Company vessel.

The French East India Company (French: Compagnie française pour le commerce des Indes orientales) was a commercial enterprise founded in 1664 by Jean-Baptiste Colbert, finance minister to Louis XIV, to compete with the English and Dutch East India companies for trade in the Eastern Hemisphere.

History

The company's home port was Lorient in Brittany — a town whose name derives from l'Orient. It established trading posts (comptoirs) in India at Surat, Masulipatnam, Pondicherry (founded 1674), Chandernagore, Mahé, and Karikal, as well as on Mauritius (Île de France) and Réunion. Under governors such as Joseph François Dupleix in the 1740s, the company intervened in Indian politics and built a territorial empire in the Carnatic, clashing with the British East India Company in the Carnatic Wars. Defeat in the Seven Years' War and the Treaty of Paris (1763) reduced French India to a few unfortified trading posts.

Dissolution

Financially weakened and criticised as a burdensome monopoly, the company lost its privileges in 1769 and was dissolved; a successor company created in 1785 was in turn abolished during the French Revolution in 1794. The French comptoirs in India remained French possessions, in shrinking form, until their de facto transfer to India in 1954.
