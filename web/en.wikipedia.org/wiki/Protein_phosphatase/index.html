Protein phosphatase

A protein phosphatase catalyses dephosphorylation.

A protein phosphatase is a phosphatase enzyme that removes a phosphate group from the phosphorylated amino acid residue of its substrate protein. Protein phosphorylation is one of the most common forms of reversible post-translational modification, and protein phosphatases perform the opposite reaction to protein kinases, together controlling much of cell signalling.

Mechanism

Phosphatases catalyse the hydrolysis of a phosphomonoester, removing a phosphate moiety from the substrate. Water is split during the reaction, with the hydroxyl group attaching to the phosphate ion and the proton to the leaving group. The net effect is the destruction of a phosphomonoester bond and the creation of a phosphate ion and a molecule bearing a free hydroxyl group.

Classification

The human genome encodes roughly 200 protein phosphatases. The largest families are the serine/threonine phosphatases — including PP1, PP2A and calcineurin (PP2B), which are part of the PPP family, and the Mg2+/Mn2+-dependent PPM family — and the protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs), which use a cysteine-based mechanism and include dual-specificity phosphatases that also act on lipids and nucleic acids.

Physiological roles

PP1 and PP2A together account for most serine/threonine dephosphorylation in cells, regulating glycogen metabolism, muscle contraction, cell division, and synaptic plasticity. Calcineurin is the target of the immunosuppressants ciclosporin and tacrolimus. Defects in phosphatases cause disease; loss of the tumour suppressor PTEN, a lipid phosphatase, is common in cancers. The marine toxin okadaic acid, a PP1/PP2A inhibitor, causes diarrhetic shellfish poisoning.
