Galactose (from the Greek γάλακτος, galaktos, "milk") is a monosaccharide sugar that is about as sweet as glucose and about 65% as sweet as sucrose. It is a C-4 epimer of glucose: the two differ only in the orientation of the hydroxyl group on carbon 4. Galactose exists as D- and L-enantiomers, of which the D-form occurs in nature.

Sources and metabolism

Galactose is best known as a component of the disaccharide lactose, the sugar of milk, which consists of galactose joined to glucose. During digestion, the enzyme lactase splits lactose; the resulting galactose is converted in the liver to glucose-1-phosphate through the Leloir pathway and enters glycolysis or glycogen synthesis. Galactose is also a building block of galactolipids in plant membranes, of agar and carrageenan in seaweeds, and of blood-group and cell-surface glycans.

Galactosemia

Defects in the enzymes of the Leloir pathway cause galactosemia, an inherited metabolic disorder. In classic galactosemia, deficiency of galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase leads to accumulation of toxic metabolites, causing liver damage, cataracts and intellectual disability in untreated infants. Newborn screening and a galactose-free diet are the standard management.
