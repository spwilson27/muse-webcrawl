Egg rolling

Egg rolling is a traditional Easter game played with decorated hard-boiled eggs. Children roll eggs down a slope or across a lawn, competing to see whose egg travels furthest, reaches a goal or survives uncracked. It symbolises, in Christian tradition, the rolling away of the stone from the tomb of Christ.

Traditions by country

In the United Kingdom, egg rolling (known in northern England and Scotland as pace-egging, from "Pasch" for Easter) takes place on hills such as Avenham Park in Preston, where large crowds roll decorated eggs down the slope on Easter Monday. In Germany (Eiertrudeln) and other parts of Europe similar customs occur.

In the United States, the most famous egg roll is the White House Easter Egg Roll, held on the South Lawn on Easter Monday since 1878 (with interruptions), in which children roll eggs with long-handled spoons. A predecessor event took place at the US Capitol until Congress banned it from the grounds in 1876 because of damage to the lawn; President Rutherford B. Hayes then opened the White House grounds.

In Egypt, the spring festival of Sham Ennessim includes egg decorating and related customs, and egg games appear in many cultures around Easter and spring festivals.

Related customs

Egg rolling is related to egg tapping (knocking eggs together to test their strength), egg hunts and pace egg plays.
