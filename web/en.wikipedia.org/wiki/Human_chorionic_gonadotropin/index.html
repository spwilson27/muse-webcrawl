Human chorionic gonadotropin

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is a hormone produced by trophoblast cells of the placenta during pregnancy. It is a glycoprotein composed of an alpha subunit shared with other pituitary hormones and a distinctive beta subunit. Detection of hCG in urine or blood is the basis of most pregnancy tests.

Function

hCG maintains the corpus luteum during early pregnancy, ensuring continued production of progesterone until the placenta takes over steroid synthesis. Concentrations rise rapidly in the first weeks, roughly doubling every two days, peak around weeks 8–11, and then decline.

Clinical uses

Beyond pregnancy testing, serum hCG measurement is used in screening for fetal chromosomal abnormalities and as a tumour marker in gestational trophoblastic disease and some germ cell tumours. Pharmaceutically prepared hCG is used in fertility treatment to trigger ovulation and, in men, to stimulate testosterone production. Its misuse as a doping agent and in fad diets has drawn regulatory warnings.
