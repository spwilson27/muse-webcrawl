Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a group of strains of the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus that have acquired resistance to many antibiotics, including all standard beta-lactams such as methicillin, penicillins, and most cephalosporins. MRSA is a major cause of healthcare-associated infection and a prominent example of antimicrobial resistance; it is colloquially called a "superbug".

Mechanism and history

Resistance is conferred mainly by the mecA gene, carried on a mobile chromosomal cassette (SCCmec), which encodes an alternative penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a, with low affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics. MRSA was first identified in the United Kingdom in 1961, only about a year after methicillin entered clinical use, and spread through hospitals worldwide in subsequent decades. From the 1990s, genetically distinct community-associated strains — often producing the Panton–Valentine leukocidin toxin — began causing infections in healthy people without healthcare exposure.

Clinical features

MRSA colonises the nose and skin of many healthy carriers. Infections range from boils, abscesses, and cellulitis to pneumonia, bloodstream infection (bacteraemia), endocarditis, and surgical-site infection. Treatment relies on antibiotics outside the beta-lactam class, such as vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, or trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, guided by susceptibility testing; abscesses often require surgical drainage.

Prevention

Control measures in hospitals include hand hygiene, screening and isolation of carriers, environmental cleaning, and antimicrobial stewardship. Rates of healthcare-associated MRSA bacteraemia fell markedly in many countries during the 2000s and 2010s following such programmes.
