Late Roman Empire

The Roman Empire around AD 395, at the death of Theodosius I

The Late Roman Empire is the period of Roman history conventionally dated from the accession of Diocletian in AD 284, which ended the Crisis of the Third Century, to the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476, or, in the East, to the reign of Justinian I in the sixth century. It was characterised by profound administrative, military, social, and religious transformation, including the division of the empire into eastern and western halves and the Christianisation of Roman society.

Reforms of Diocletian and Constantine

Diocletian stabilised the empire after decades of civil war and inflation. He divided imperial authority through the Tetrarchy, a system of two senior emperors (Augusti) and two junior colleagues (Caesares); reorganised the provinces into smaller units grouped under dioceses and vicars; reformed taxation and the currency; and expanded the army and bureaucracy. Constantine I, who reunified the empire after civil wars, founded Constantinople as a new imperial capital in 330 and favoured Christianity, convening the First Council of Nicaea in 325.

Division and decline in the West

After the death of Theodosius I in 395, the empire was permanently divided between his sons. The fifth century saw the Western Empire weakened by usurpations, the loss of revenue-rich provinces such as Africa to the Vandals, and the settlement of Germanic peoples within its borders. Rome was sacked by the Visigoths in 410 and by the Vandals in 455. In 476 the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus.

Survival in the East

The Eastern Roman Empire, wealthier and more defensible, survived the fifth century and, under Justinian I (r. 527–565), temporarily reconquered parts of Italy, Africa, and southern Spain. The East endured as the Byzantine Empire until 1453.

Society and religion

Late antiquity saw the triumph of Christianity, which became the state religion under Theodosius I, alongside vibrant pagan, Jewish, and heretical movements. The period also produced influential figures such as Augustine of Hippo, Jerome, and Ambrose of Milan, and saw the beginnings of monasticism.
