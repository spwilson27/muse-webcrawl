Kaddare script

Script typeAlphabet
CreatorSheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur
Created1952
LanguagesSomali
DirectionLeft-to-right

The Kaddare script is a writing script created in 1952 by Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur for transcribing the Somali language. It is one of several indigenous scripts devised for Somali in the 20th century, alongside the Osmanya script and the Borama (Gadabuursi) script.

Invention

Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur was a religious teacher (a qadi) in the Borama area of northwestern Somalia, a region with a strong tradition of learning and of experiments with writing Somali. His father, Sheikh Nuur, was himself a noted scholar, and the family was connected with the circle that produced the Borama script of Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Ahmed. In 1952 Sheikh Abdurahman Sheikh Nuur devised the Kaddare alphabet, naming it after a local place (Kaddare). Like its predecessors, the script was created in response to the long-standing debate about how Somali — historically written in Arabic script by scholars — should be officially written.

Design

Kaddare is a true alphabet with separate letters for consonants and vowels, written from left to right. Some of its letterforms were adapted from Arabic and Ethiopian characters, while others were original inventions. It represents the long and short vowels of Somali and the language's characteristic consonants, including the pharyngeal and glottal sounds, without recourse to the digraphs needed in Latin orthography.

Fate

Although the Kaddare script was documented by linguists studying the Somali "script question" — it is discussed in the work of B. W. Andrzejewski and other Somali specialists — it was never adopted by the Somali state. When the Somali government finally settled the national orthography question in 1972, it chose the Latin-based Somali alphabet devised by Shire Jama Ahmed. Kaddare survives today mainly in linguistic literature and among the several historical Somali scripts studied by script historians and occasionally proposed for revival.
