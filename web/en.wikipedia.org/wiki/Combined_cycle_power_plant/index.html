Combined cycle power plant

A combined cycle power plant is an assembly of heat engines that work in tandem from the same source of heat, converting it into mechanical energy. On land, when used to make electricity the most common type is called a combined cycle gas turbine (CCGT) plant, which burns natural gas in a gas turbine whose hot exhaust then drives a steam turbine.

Principle

In a CCGT plant, a gas turbine generates electricity and its waste heat is captured by a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG) to produce steam for a steam turbine, generating additional electricity from the same fuel. Where a simple-cycle gas turbine converts roughly 35–40% of fuel energy to electricity, modern combined cycle plants achieve efficiencies of over 60%; the most advanced H- and J-class machines have set records above 63%.

Advantages and use

  • Highest efficiency of any fossil-fuel generation technology
  • Lower carbon dioxide emissions per unit of electricity than coal plants
  • Fast construction and relatively low capital cost per megawatt
  • Flexible operation — modern plants can ramp quickly to complement intermittent wind and solar generation

Combined cycle plants became the dominant form of new gas-fired generation from the 1990s onward, especially in North America, Europe, and East Asia. Variants include integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC), which gasifies coal or other feedstocks before combustion.

Drawbacks

Plants depend on natural gas supply and prices, produce carbon emissions that must fall for deep decarbonization, and lose efficiency when cycled frequently. Some plants are being designed for future conversion to hydrogen fuel.
