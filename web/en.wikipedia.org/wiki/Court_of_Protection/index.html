The Court of Protection is a superior court of record in England and Wales, created by the Mental Capacity Act 2005. It has jurisdiction over the property, financial affairs and personal welfare of people who lack the mental capacity to make decisions for themselves — for example because of dementia, learning disability, brain injury or mental illness.

Functions

The court can make declarations about a person's capacity, make one-off decisions on their behalf (such as consenting to medical treatment or deciding where they should live), appoint deputies to make ongoing decisions, and rule on the validity and operation of lasting powers of attorney and advance decisions to refuse treatment. It can also authorise deprivations of liberty in care settings. In deciding cases it applies the principles of the 2005 Act: a person must be assumed to have capacity unless proved otherwise, and any decision made for someone who lacks capacity must be in their best interests and the least restrictive option.

Organisation and controversy

The court is headed by a President — Sir Andrew McFarlane became the first combined President of the Family Division and Court of Protection era leadership, and Victoria Sharp subsequently served as Vice-President; district and circuit judges also sit as Court of Protection judges. Hearings were historically held in private, leading to criticism of "secret courts"; since 2016 most hearings have been held in public, subject to reporting restrictions protecting the person concerned. High-profile cases concerning withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, sterilisation and deprivation of liberty attract regular media attention.

History

The court's functions descend from the Crown's ancient parens patriae role over "lunatics and idiots", administered through the Lord Chancellor and, from 1845, the Masters in Lunacy and the earlier Court of Protection established in 1947 under mental health legislation. The 2005 Act replaced that system when it came into force on 1 October 2007.
