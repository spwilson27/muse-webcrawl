Samori Ture

Bornc. 1830, Manyambaladugu, present-day Guinea
Died2 June 1900, Ndjolé, Gabon
Known forFounder of the Wassoulou Empire; resistance to French colonisation
Reignc. 1878–1898

Samori Ture (c. 1830 – 2 June 1900), also known as Samori Touré or Almamy Samore Lafiya Toure, was a Muslim cleric, military leader and founder of the Wassoulou Empire, an Islamic state in present-day Guinea, Mali, Sierra Leone and Ivory Coast. He led one of the longest and most determined resistances to French colonial expansion in West Africa.

Early life and rise

Born to a Dyula merchant family, Samori was captured in a raid in his youth and spent years in servitude before gaining freedom and building a following as a soldier. By the 1860s and 1870s he had united the Wassoulou region, converted to and promoted Islam, and taken the title almamy (religious and political leader). His state was organised for war, with a cavalry corps and infantry armed, in part, with modern repeating rifles obtained through trade with Freetown.

Resistance to France

French forces advancing from Senegal clashed with Samori from 1882. After early treaties, including agreements at Kéniéba Koura and Bissandougou, failed to hold, war resumed. Samori adopted scorched-earth tactics and shifted his empire eastward into present-day Ivory Coast, prolonging resistance through the 1890s. His mobile warfare and diplomatic manoeuvring frustrated successive French columns, but French firepower, blockade of his arms supplies and encirclement steadily closed in.

Capture and death

Samori was captured on 29 September 1898 by a French column under Captain Henri Gouraud at Guélémou. He was exiled to Ndjolé in Gabon, where he died of pneumonia in 1900.

Legacy

Samori is remembered in Guinea as a national hero and a symbol of African resistance to colonialism; his great-grandson Ahmed Sékou Touré became Guinea's first president. Historians note both his state-building and military skill and the coercion and slave-raiding that underpinned his empire.
