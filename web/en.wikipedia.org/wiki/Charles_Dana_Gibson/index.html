Charles Dana Gibson (September 14, 1867 – December 23, 1944) was an American graphic artist, best known for his creation of the Gibson Girl, an iconic representation of the beautiful and independent Euro-American woman at the turn of the 20th century. His pen-and-ink drawings, published chiefly in Life magazine, helped define American ideals of beauty, fashion and manners for a generation.

Charles Dana Gibson, creator of the Gibson Girl

Early life and career

Gibson was born in Roxbury, Massachusetts, a descendant of Senators James DeWolf and William Bradford. He studied at the Art Students League of New York and sold his first drawing to Life magazine in 1886. His work soon appeared in all the major American magazines, including Harper's Weekly, Scribner's and Collier's, and he became one of the highest-paid illustrators in the country.

The Gibson Girl

Gibson's most famous creation, the Gibson Girl, embodied the fashionable, confident "New Woman" of the 1890s and 1900s: tall, slender, with hair piled high, elegantly dressed and serenely self-assured. The image was widely imitated and reproduced on countless products. Gibson's wife, Irene Langhorne, and her sisters — including Nancy Astor — were among the models who inspired the type.

Later career

In 1904 Gibson signed a celebrated contract with Collier's Weekly reportedly worth $100,000. After the First World War the Gibson Girl faded from fashion, and Gibson turned increasingly to oil painting. In 1932 he became editor, and later owner, of Life magazine. He retired to Maine and died in New York City in 1944.
