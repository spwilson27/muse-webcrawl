Double jeopardy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Double jeopardy is a procedural defence (and constitutional right in many systems) that prevents an accused person from being tried again on the same (or similar) charges after a legitimate acquittal or conviction. Known in civilian systems as ne bis in idem ("not twice for the same"), it appears in the US Fifth Amendment ("nor shall any person be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb"), the ICCPR (Article 14(7)), the EU Charter (Article 50) and the common law since at least the medieval pleas of autrefois acquit/convict.

Scope and exceptions

The protection attaches when jeopardy begins (jury sworn, or first witness in bench trials), bars retrial after acquittal absolutely in US law, and permits retrial after most mistrials and after convictions reversed on appeal (except where reversal was for insufficient evidence). Exceptions and qualifications drive litigation: the US "separate sovereigns" doctrine allows federal and state prosecutions for the same act; the UK, since the Criminal Justice Act 2003, permits retrial after acquittal for serious offences where new and compelling evidence emerges (first used in the Julie Hogg murder case's retrial) — a reform balancing finality against public confidence. Civil suits and administrative sanctions over the same conduct are generally outside the doctrine, though their punitive character is increasingly scrutinised.
