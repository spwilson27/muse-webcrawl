Burgernomics

Burgernomics is an informal economic concept introduced by The Economist magazine in 1986 through the Big Mac index. The index uses the price of a McDonald's Big Mac in different countries to test the theory of purchasing power parity (PPP): in the long run, exchange rates should move so that an identical basket of goods costs the same everywhere. If a Big Mac is cheaper in one country than another, its currency is judged "undervalued" against the benchmark (usually the U.S. dollar).

Rationale

The Big Mac was chosen because it is produced locally in more than 100 countries with a nearly identical recipe, combining traded inputs (beef, flour) with non-traded ones (rent, labor), making it a miniature basket of goods and services.

Criticisms and refinements

Economists note that burger prices reflect local wages, rents, taxes, and demand, so deviations from PPP are expected — poorer countries tend to have cheaper Big Macs. The Economist later published a GDP-adjusted index to account for this. A "Tall Latte index" using Starbucks coffee prices applies the same idea. Despite its light-hearted origin, the Big Mac index is widely cited in textbooks and financial journalism as an accessible illustration of exchange-rate economics.
