Cross product

In mathematics, the cross product or vector product is a binary operation on two vectors in three-dimensional space (ℝ³), denoted by the symbol ×. Given two linearly independent vectors a and b, the cross product a × b is a vector that is perpendicular to both of them, and thus normal to the plane containing them. It has many applications in mathematics, physics, engineering and computer programming.

Definition

The cross product is defined by the formula:

a × b = ‖a‖ ‖b‖ sin(θ) n

where θ is the angle between a and b in the plane containing them, ‖·‖ denotes vector length, and n is a unit vector perpendicular to that plane, directed according to the right-hand rule. The magnitude of the cross product equals the area of the parallelogram with sides a and b.

In coordinates, if a = (a₁, a₂, a₃) and b = (b₁, b₂, b₃), then:

a × b = (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, a₃b₁ − a₁b₃, a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)

Properties

The cross product is anticommutative (a × b = −b × a) and distributive over addition, but it is not associative; instead it satisfies the Jacobi identity. Unlike the dot product, it is defined (as an ordinary vector) only in three dimensions — and, exceptionally, in seven dimensions via the octonions. In physics it appears in the definitions of torque, angular momentum, and the Lorentz force on a charged particle.

History

The operation emerged in the 19th century from the work of Hermann Grassmann and William Rowan Hamilton's quaternions; Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside independently distilled the modern vector notation, separating the cross product from the dot product, in the 1880s.
