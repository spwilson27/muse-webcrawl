Kyoto University (京都大学, Kyōto daigaku), abbreviated to Kyodai (京大), is a national research university located in Kyoto, Japan. Founded in 1897 as Kyoto Imperial University, it is the second oldest university in Japan and one of the former Imperial Universities. It is consistently ranked among the top universities in Japan and Asia and is noted for the number of Nobel laureates and Fields medalists among its alumni and faculty.

History

The university was established in 1897, some three decades after the University of Tokyo, and began with colleges of law, medicine, and science and engineering. Its early development was shaped by an ethos of academic freedom — sometimes summarised by its motto of "freedom of academic culture" — that contrasted with the more state-directed culture of Tokyo. The institution was renamed Kyoto University after the Second World War under the new national education system.

Academics and research

Kyoto University comprises ten faculties, numerous graduate schools, and research institutes including the Yukawa Institute for Theoretical Physics and the Institute for Chemical Research. The university has produced a distinguished line of scientists: Nobel laureates in physics include Hideki Yukawa, the first Japanese Nobel laureate, and Shin'ichirō Tomonaga, along with later laureates in physics, chemistry and medicine such as Susumu Tonegawa, Ryōji Noyori, Shinya Yamanaka and Akira Yoshino. Mathematician Heisuke Hironaka and Kiyoshi Itō are also associated with the university. Its primate research and its work in areas from particle physics to induced pluripotent stem cells have given it an international profile.

Campus

The main Yoshida Campus in Sakyo Ward is recognisable by its clock tower, a symbol of the university, built in 1925. Additional campuses include Uji and Katsura, which hosts engineering departments in a modern "Techno-Science Hill" setting.
