Biscuits and gravy is a popular breakfast dish in the United States, especially in the South. It consists of soft dough biscuits covered with gravy made from the drippings of cooked pork sausage, white flour, milk, and often lard, seasoned with black pepper; the result is commonly called sausage gravy or sawmill gravy.

History

The dish emerged as a distinct regional breakfast in the southern United States after the American Revolutionary War era, when foodstuffs were in short supply. Inexpensive and filling, it became a staple for workers: sawmill workers in the southern Appalachians were given the gravy early in the day, giving "sawmill gravy" its name. Biscuits, leavened with baking soda or baking powder rather than yeast, could be made quickly each morning from flour and lard.

Preparation and variations

The biscuits are typically soft, flaky buttermilk biscuits. The gravy is made by browning pork sausage in a skillet, stirring flour into the rendered fat to form a roux, and adding milk until thickened. Variations include tomato gravy, chocolate gravy (served in parts of the Ozarks and Appalachia), and red-eye gravy made with ham drippings and coffee. A common restaurant serving is a "half order" of two biscuit halves under gravy, frequently topped with eggs or accompanied by home fries.

Cultural significance

Biscuits and gravy is regarded as an emblem of Southern cuisine and appears on diner and fast-food breakfast menus across the country.
