The Copper Scroll (3Q15) is one of the Dead Sea Scrolls, unique among them in being engraved on metal rather than written on parchment or papyrus. Found in Cave 3 at Qumran in 1952, it lists some sixty-four locations where large quantities of gold, silver and other treasures were supposedly hidden.

Discovery and opening

The scroll was found in two pieces, rolled up and heavily oxidised. Because it could not be unrolled without disintegrating, it was taken to Manchester, England, where in 1955–56 it was sawn into strips by H. Wright Baker so the text could be read. It is now displayed at the Jordan Museum in Amman.

Contents

Written in a form of Mishnaic Hebrew, the text gives terse directions to caches of treasure — in many cases enormous quantities of talents of gold and silver, along with vessels and tithes — hidden in cisterns, tombs, aqueducts and ruins around Judaea, including Jerusalem and the wilderness near Qumran. Some entries end with groups of Greek letters whose meaning is debated.

Interpretation

Scholars disagree fundamentally about the document. Some regard it as a genuine inventory — possibly of Temple treasure hidden before the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, or of the Qumran community's assets — while others consider the quantities so implausibly large (amounting to tonnes of precious metal) that they treat the scroll as folklore or a work of fiction. No cache matching the descriptions has ever been found. The scroll's language and metal medium have also led some to connect it with legends of the Temple treasury recorded in later sources such as the Treatise of the Vessels.
