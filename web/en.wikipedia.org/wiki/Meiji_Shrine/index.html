Meiji Shrine (明治神宮, Meiji Jingū) is a Shinto shrine in Shibuya, Tokyo, dedicated to the deified spirits of Emperor Meiji and his wife, Empress Shōken. Located in a 70-hectare man-made forest adjacent to Yoyogi Park and Harajuku Station, it is one of Japan's most popular shrines, receiving millions of visitors each year, especially at hatsumōde, the first shrine visit of the New Year.

History

After the emperor's death in 1912 and the empress's in 1914, the Japanese Diet passed a resolution to commemorate their virtues; an iris garden in Yoyogi that the couple had visited was chosen as the site. Construction began in 1915, and some 110,000 volunteers planted roughly 100,000 trees donated from all over Japan, creating an artificial forest intended to be self-sustaining and eternal. The shrine was formally dedicated on 1 November 1920.

The original buildings were destroyed in the Tokyo air raids of 1945. The present shrine, rebuilt largely through public fundraising, was completed in October 1958.

Grounds

Broad gravel paths pass beneath two great torii gates — the largest wooden myōjin-style torii in Japan — through dense forest to the main shrine buildings, built mainly of Japanese cypress with copper roofs. Near the entrance stand walls of sake barrels offered by brewers and of wine barrels from Burgundy, reflecting Emperor Meiji's role in Japan's opening to the West. The precinct also includes the Meiji Jingu Gaien, treasure museum, and the Inner Garden (Gyoen), famous for its irises, where the empress's teahouse stood.

Festivals and customs

The Autumn Grand Festival around 3 November (the emperor's birthday, Culture Day) features offerings of traditional performing arts, including Noh, kyōgen, yabusame (horseback archery) and martial arts demonstrations. Meiji Shrine is a favoured venue for traditional Shinto weddings, and visitors write wishes on wooden ema plaques hung near the sacred camphor tree.
