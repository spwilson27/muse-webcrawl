Passenger pigeon

The passenger pigeon (Ectopistes migratorius) is an extinct species of pigeon that was endemic to North America. Once arguably the most abundant bird in the world, with a population estimated at 3–5 billion in the early 19th century, it was driven to extinction by hunting and habitat loss within decades.

Passenger pigeon

Scientific name: Ectopistes migratorius
Status: Extinct (1914)
Last individual: Martha, Cincinnati Zoo

Description and ecology

Males had blue-grey upperparts, wine-red underparts and iridescent neck feathers. Immense migratory flocks — sometimes over a mile wide and hundreds of miles long — darkened skies for days, feeding mainly on mast (acorns, beechnuts, chestnuts) in eastern deciduous forests. The species nested colonially in huge colonies.

Extinction

Commercial hunting with nets, guns and even dynamite, combined with deforestation and a possible Allee effect in this highly social species, caused a catastrophic collapse after about 1870. The last wild birds died around 1900–1901; the last captive, Martha, died on 1 September 1914 at the Cincinnati Zoo. The passenger pigeon became an iconic symbol of human-caused extinction and inspired early conservation laws.
