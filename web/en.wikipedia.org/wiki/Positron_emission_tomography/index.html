Positron emission tomography (PET) is a functional imaging technique used to observe metabolic processes in the body as an aid to the diagnosis of disease. The system detects pairs of gamma rays emitted indirectly by a positron-emitting radiotracer, which is introduced into the body on a biologically active molecule called a radioactive tracer. Three-dimensional images of tracer concentration within the body are then constructed by computer analysis.

Unlike techniques such as CT and MRI, which chiefly image anatomy, PET images biological function — for example blood flow, glucose metabolism or receptor binding — and can therefore detect disease before anatomical changes appear.

Principle

A tracer is labelled with a positron-emitting radionuclide such as fluorine-18, carbon-11, oxygen-15 or gallium-68, and injected into the patient. As the isotope decays it emits a positron, which travels a short distance in tissue before annihilating with an electron; the annihilation produces two 511 keV photons emitted in nearly opposite directions. Rings of detectors around the patient record these photon pairs in coincidence, and tomographic reconstruction algorithms convert the data into images of tracer distribution.

Tracers

The most widely used tracer is fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG), an analogue of glucose: cells with high glucose uptake, including many tumours and the brain, accumulate it. Fluorine-18 has a half-life of about 110 minutes, long enough for regional distribution from a cyclotron. Other tracers include carbon-11 compounds for neuroreceptor studies, rubidium-82 and nitrogen-13 ammonia for myocardial perfusion, and PSMA-targeting agents (e.g. gallium-68 or fluorine-18 labelled) for prostate cancer.

Applications

PET is most heavily used in oncology for detecting cancers, staging, checking for recurrence and assessing treatment response. In neurology it is used in the evaluation of dementia (including amyloid and tau imaging), epilepsy localisation and movement disorders, and in cardiology for assessing myocardial viability and perfusion. PET is also an important research tool in drug development.

PET/CT and PET/MRI

Modern scanners almost always combine PET with computed tomography (PET/CT, introduced commercially in 2001) in a single gantry, so that functional images are precisely registered with anatomy; PET/MRI systems, introduced around 2010, offer the same fusion with magnetic resonance imaging, useful in neurology and paediatric imaging, with lower radiation dose.

Safety

The radiation dose from a typical FDG PET scan is on the order of several millisieverts; the tracer decays and is excreted quickly. The short half-lives require nearby cyclotron production for most tracers.

History

The technique grew out of work at the Massachusetts General Hospital in the 1950s–70s and the first human tomographs built under Michael Ter-Pogossian, Michael Phelps, Edward Hoffman and others at Washington University in St. Louis in the mid-1970s. Phelps is often called the father of PET.
