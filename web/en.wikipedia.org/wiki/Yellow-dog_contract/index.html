Yellow-dog contract

Yellow-dog contract
TypeEmployment agreement
TermEmployee agrees not to join a trade union
Outlawed byNorris–La Guardia Act (1932), United States
Key casesHitchman Coal & Coke Co. v. Mitchell (1917)

A yellow-dog contract is an agreement between an employer and an employee in which the employee agrees, as a condition of employment, not to become a member of a labor union. The term originated in the United States in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when such contracts were a principal weapon of employers resisting unionization.

History

The phrase first appeared in print in the 1920s in union publications, which derided a worker willing to sign such a pledge as no better than a "yellow dog." Employers valued the contracts because courts would enforce them: in Hitchman Coal & Coke Co. v. Mitchell (1917), the U.S. Supreme Court upheld an injunction preventing union organizers from inducing workers under yellow-dog contracts to join a union. In Adair v. United States (1908) and Coppage v. Kansas (1915), the Court struck down federal and state laws that attempted to ban the contracts, treating them as protected by freedom of contract under the due process clauses.

Abolition

Congress ended the practice with the Norris–La Guardia Act of 1932, which declared yellow-dog contracts unenforceable in federal courts and sharply limited labor injunctions. The National Labor Relations Act of 1935 went further, making it an unfair labor practice for an employer to require non-union membership as a condition of employment. Comparable prohibitions were adopted in other industrial countries, and compelled non-union agreements are now broadly regarded as inconsistent with freedom of association.
