Balkh


The Green Mosque (Masjid Sabz) at Balkh.

Balkh is a town in the Balkh Province of northern Afghanistan, located near the Balkh River, about 20 kilometres northwest of the provincial capital Mazar-i-Sharif and some 74 kilometres south of the Amu Darya and the border with Uzbekistan. One of the oldest settlements in Central Asia, Balkh was the capital of ancient Bactria and a famed centre of Zoroastrianism, Buddhism, and later Islamic learning. Arab authors called it Umm al-Bilad, the "Mother of Cities".

History

The region of Bactria, watered by the Balkh River (the ancient Bactrus), was settled by Iranian peoples in the first millennium BC. Balkh is traditionally associated with the prophet Zoroaster, who some accounts say died there, and its fire temple, the Naw Bahar, was a major pilgrimage site that later sources describe as having been converted first into a Buddhist monastery and then, after the Arab conquest in the eighth century, marked by a great mosque.

Achaemenid Persia ruled Bactria until Alexander the Great conquered it in 329 BC; he married Roxana, a Bactrian noblewoman. Under the Hellenistic Greco-Bactrian kingdom and the Kushan Empire, Balkh flourished as a Silk Road city where Buddhism thrived — the Nawbahar monastery attracted pilgrims such as the Chinese monk Xuanzang, who visited in the seventh century.

Arab armies took Balkh in the eighth century; the influential Barmakid family of viziers traced its origins to the city's hereditary temple administrators. Under the Samanids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks, Balkh was a celebrated centre of learning — the theologian and jurist Abu Hanifa's school flourished in the region, and the poet Rumi (Jalal al-Din Muhammad Balkhi) was born there in 1207 before his family migrated west.

Decline

The Mongol invasion of 1220 devastated Balkh: Genghis Khan's armies destroyed the city and massacred much of its population. Although partially rebuilt under the Timurids and later contested by Uzbeks, Persians, and Afghans, Balkh never regained its former importance; irrigation canals silted and malaria spread, and in the nineteenth century regional primacy passed to nearby Mazar-i-Sharif.

Today

Modern Balkh is a small town set amid the massive ancient mud-brick ramparts of the old city (the Bala Hissar). Notable remains include the ninth-century Haji Piyada mosque (also called the Masjid-e Noh Gonbad, the "Mosque of Nine Domes"), among the earliest Islamic monuments in Afghanistan; the Timurid shrine and Green Mosque built in the fifteenth century; and the Madrasa of Sayed Subhan Quli Khan. The ruins remain archaeologically rich but have suffered from looting and conflict.
