The Holocaust in Bohemia and Moravia

The Holocaust in Bohemia and Moravia was the persecution, deportation and murder of the Jews of the Czech lands following the German occupation of Czechoslovakia in 1938–1939 and the creation of the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia in March 1939. Of roughly 118,000 people classified as Jews in the Protectorate by Nazi racial definitions, the great majority were deported; around 78,000 were murdered.

Background

After the Munich Agreement of September 1938, the Sudetenland was annexed to Germany and its Jewish population fled inland or was expelled. When German troops occupied the remainder of Bohemia and Moravia on 15 March 1939, the Protectorate was placed under a Reich Protector — initially Konstantin von Neurath, and from September 1941 the notorious Reinhard Heydrich.

Persecution and deportation

Anti-Jewish measures escalated rapidly: exclusion from professions, expropriation ("Aryanisation") of property, forced registration, and from 1941 the wearing of the yellow star. In October 1941 the first transports left Prague for the Łódź Ghetto. From November 1941 the fortress town of Terezín (Theresienstadt) was converted into a ghetto-camp through which some 140,000 Jews passed; about 33,000 died there of starvation and disease, and some 88,000 were deported onward, chiefly to Auschwitz-Birkenau, where most were murdered.

Aftermath

Only about 10,000–14,000 Jews of the Protectorate survived. Postwar Czechoslovakia saw restitution disputes, emigration, and decades in which public memory of the Holocaust was marginalised under communist rule; memorialisation expanded after 1989, notably at Terezín and at Prague's Pinkas Synagogue, whose walls bear the names of Bohemian and Moravian victims.
