Lithium, principally as lithium carbonate and lithium citrate, is a medication used primarily in the treatment of bipolar disorder, where it stabilizes mood and reduces the risk of mania, depression, and suicide. It is also used as an augmentation agent in major depressive disorder. Lithium is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Lithium is a first-line treatment for bipolar disorder, effective in preventing manic and depressive episodes and in acute mania. Meta-analyses have found that lithium reduces the risk of suicide in people with mood disorders, an effect not clearly shared by other mood stabilizers. It is also used to augment antidepressants in treatment-resistant depression and, off-label, in conditions such as cluster headache and certain movement disorders.

Mechanism of action

The precise mechanism by which lithium stabilizes mood is not fully understood. Proposed mechanisms include modulation of neurotransmitter systems (serotonin, dopamine, glutamate), inhibition of inositol monophosphatase affecting second-messenger signalling, inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK-3), and neuroprotective effects involving proteins such as Bcl-2 and brain-derived neurotrophic factor.

Adverse effects and monitoring

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index, so blood levels are monitored, typically targeting 0.6–1.0 mmol/L for maintenance. Common side effects include tremor, increased thirst and urination, weight gain, and gastrointestinal upset. Long-term use can affect the kidneys (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, chronic kidney disease) and thyroid (hypothyroidism), and hyperparathyroidism may occur. Toxicity, which may arise from dehydration, drug interactions (notably with diuretics and NSAIDs), or overdose, can cause confusion, ataxia, and seizures and can be fatal.

History

Lithium salts were used in the nineteenth century to treat gout and mania. Their modern psychiatric use dates to 1949, when the Australian psychiatrist John Cade reported the calming effect of lithium in manic patients. Widespread adoption followed the work of Mogens Schou and others in Denmark in the 1950s and 1960s; lithium carbonate was approved in the United States in 1970.
