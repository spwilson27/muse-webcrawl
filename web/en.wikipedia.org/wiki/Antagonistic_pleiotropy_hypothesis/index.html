Antagonistic pleiotropy hypothesis

The antagonistic pleiotropy hypothesis is an evolutionary explanation for senescence (ageing) proposed by George C. Williams in 1957. It holds that natural selection can favour genes that have beneficial effects early in life, when selection is strong, even if the same genes have harmful effects later in life, when selection is weak — a phenomenon known as antagonistic pleiotropy because one gene influences multiple traits with opposing fitness effects.

Background

The hypothesis builds on Peter Medawar's mutation accumulation theory and complements the disposable soma theory of ageing. Because the force of natural selection declines with age — older individuals have already reproduced — mutations with late-acting deleterious effects can persist, and genes offering early reproductive advantages at a later cost will actively be selected for.

Examples

Proposed examples include genes promoting high early-life fertility or growth at the cost of late-life disease, testosterone-related trade-offs in males, and cellular mechanisms such as telomere shortening, which limits cancer in youth but contributes to tissue decline in old age. The tumour suppressor protein p53 is often cited: strong cancer surveillance early in life may accelerate stem-cell exhaustion later. Huntington's disease has also been discussed as a possible case, with some studies reporting higher early fertility in carriers.
