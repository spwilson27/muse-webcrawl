Justinian I (c. 482 – 14 November 565), also known as Justinian the Great, was Byzantine emperor from 527 to 565. His reign is known for the ambitious (and ultimately over-stretching) reconquest of the western Mediterranean — renovatio imperii — the codification of Roman law in the Corpus Juris Civilis, and the building of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople.

Working with his wife and co-ruler Theodora, his general Belisarius, and jurist Tribonian, Justinian recovered North Africa from the Vandals and Italy from the Ostrogoths, though the long Gothic War devastated Italy. His reign also saw the Nika riots (532), which nearly toppled him, the outbreak of the devastating Plague of Justinian (541), and religious policies aimed at uniting Chalcedonian and Miaphysite Christians.

Law and building

The Corpus Juris Civilis (Code, Digest, Institutes, Novels) preserved and systematised Roman law and became foundational for civil-law traditions in Europe. After the Nika riots burned much of the city, Justinian rebuilt Constantinople on a grand scale, crowned by Hagia Sophia (537).
