Abandonment

Abandonment is the act of relinquishing, deserting, or giving up control over something — property, a person in one's care, a legal claim, or a ship at sea — usually with no intention of resuming it. The term carries specific, differing meanings across law, psychology and maritime practice; on Wikipedia it also functions as a disambiguation topic pointing to several specialised articles.

In law

In property law, abandoned property is tangible or intangible property voluntarily relinquished by its owner with the intent never to reclaim it; it may then be acquired by a finder or escheat to the state, and is distinguished from lost or mislaid property. Abandonment is also an element or offence in several areas: child abandonment (desertion of a child by a parent or guardian, criminalised in most jurisdictions, with "safe haven" laws allowing anonymous surrender of newborns in many U.S. states), spousal desertion (historically a ground for divorce), abandonment of duty in military law, and abandonment of an application or trademark in intellectual property, where rights lapse through non-use with intent not to resume.

In psychology

In psychology and popular usage, abandonment refers to the subjective experience of being deserted by an attachment figure, and to the fears and patterns it may leave: "abandonment issues" are associated with attachment disorders, borderline personality disorder, and post-traumatic responses to childhood neglect or loss. Abandonment anxiety in infants is studied under separation anxiety and attachment theory.

In maritime law

In marine insurance, abandonment is the surrender of a ship or cargo to underwriters after a constructive total loss, entitling the insured to claim as for an actual total loss; the doctrine dates to early modern insurance practice and the French délaissement. A crew's abandonment of a vessel ("abandon ship") is distinct from the legal act.
