Jersey barrier

Type: Modular traffic barrier
Material: Concrete or plastic
Typical height: 810 mm (32 in) for standard profile
Named for: New Jersey, United States

A Jersey barrier, Jersey wall, or Jersey bump is a modular concrete or plastic barrier used to separate lanes of traffic and to protect pedestrians and workers from vehicles. It is named after the U.S. state of New Jersey, where its characteristic profile was developed in the 1950s. Portable sections are widely used in road construction, crowd control, and perimeter security.

Design

The barrier's cross-section has a distinctive shape: a wide base, a lower face inclined at about 55 degrees, and an upper face only slightly inclined. When a vehicle strikes the barrier at a shallow angle, the lower slope engages the tire and lifts the vehicle, redirecting it along the barrier while minimizing damage and the risk of the vehicle vaulting or rolling over into oncoming traffic. A related design, the taller F-shape barrier, refines the profile, and the constant-slope barrier simplifies construction. Standard concrete sections are commonly 810 mm (32 in) tall and are joined end-to-end with steel connectors.

History

Concrete median barriers were first installed in California in the 1940s. New Jersey's highway department refined the profile in the 1950s, and crash testing in that decade established the standard 32-inch shape. The design spread across North America and worldwide as divided highways expanded, and precast sections became ubiquitous in temporary traffic management.

Other uses

Beyond highways, Jersey barriers serve as blast and vehicle barriers at military bases, embassies, and public events — where, especially in deployed settings, related variants such as the taller "Texas" and "Alaska" barriers are used — and as security perimeters around pedestrian zones.
