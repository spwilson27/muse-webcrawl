Armenian (հայերեն, hayeren) is an Indo-European language that forms its own independent branch of the family. It is the official language of Armenia and is spoken by the worldwide Armenian diaspora.

History

The Armenian alphabet was created around AD 405 by the monk Mesrop Mashtots, traditionally to make the Bible accessible in Armenian. Grabar, or Classical Armenian, became the literary language for over a millennium. In the 19th century two modern literary standards emerged: Eastern Armenian, based on the dialect of Yerevan and used in Armenia and among Iranian Armenians, and Western Armenian, based on the dialect of Istanbul, used widely in the diaspora. The two differ in phonology and some grammar, though they remain substantially mutually intelligible in writing.

Classification and features

Armenian was once thought to be closely related to Greek and Iranian, having absorbed a large Iranian loanword vocabulary during centuries of Parthian and Persian influence. Its sound history includes a consonant shift reminiscent of Grimm's law. The language is agglutinative in parts of its morphology, has seven noun cases in the classical language, no grammatical gender, and a postposed definite article.

Speakers

About three million people speak Armenian in Armenia itself, with large communities in Russia, the United States, France, Lebanon, Iran and elsewhere. Western Armenian is classified by UNESCO as an endangered language in its diaspora settings.
