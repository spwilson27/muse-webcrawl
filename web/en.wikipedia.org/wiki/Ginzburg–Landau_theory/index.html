Ginzburg–Landau theory

Ginzburg–Landau theory is a phenomenological theory of superconductivity proposed by Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau in 1950. It describes a superconductor by a complex order parameter ψ whose squared magnitude gives the density of superconducting electrons, and expands the free energy in powers of ψ and its gradients.

Key results

The theory predicts a coherence length ξ and a penetration depth λ; their ratio κ distinguishes type-I from type-II superconductors. Alexei Abrikosov used the theory to predict the vortex lattice in type-II materials, work that (with Ginzburg and Anthony Leggett) earned the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Applications

Generalised Ginzburg–Landau models are used far beyond superconductors, including superfluids, phase transitions, and pattern formation, and the framework underlies the Landau theory of second-order phase transitions.
