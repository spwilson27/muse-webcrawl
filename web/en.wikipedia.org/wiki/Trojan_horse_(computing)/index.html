Trojan horse (computing)

In computing, a Trojan horse (or simply Trojan) is any malware that misleads users of its true intent by disguising itself as a legitimate program. The term is derived from the ancient Greek story of the deceptive Trojan Horse that led to the fall of the city of Troy.

Behaviour

Unlike computer viruses and worms, Trojans generally do not attempt to inject themselves into other files or propagate themselves autonomously; instead, they rely on social engineering to persuade a user to download and execute them. Once active, a Trojan may grant an attacker remote access to the infected machine (a remote access Trojan, or RAT), log keystrokes, steal credentials and banking data, install additional malware such as ransomware, enrol the machine in a botnet for spam or distributed denial-of-service attacks, or mine cryptocurrency.

Notable examples

Early examples include the AIDS Trojan of 1989, distributed on floppy disks and often cited as the first ransomware. Later, widespread banking Trojans such as Zeus (Zbot) and its successor variants stole enormous sums through credential theft and man-in-the-browser attacks. The Emotet malware, first identified in 2014, evolved into a major distribution platform for other malicious payloads before a multinational law-enforcement takedown in 2021.

Defences

Protection relies on keeping software and operating systems patched, downloading software only from trusted sources, being wary of unsolicited email attachments, and using up-to-date anti-malware tools and application allow-listing. User education against social engineering remains one of the most important countermeasures.
