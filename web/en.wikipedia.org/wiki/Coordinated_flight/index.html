Coordinated flight

In aviation, coordinated flight of an aircraft is a flight condition in which the aircraft's yaw and roll are balanced so that the airflow is aligned with the fuselage and there is no sideways acceleration, or "slip". In a coordinated turn, the lift force is tilted by the bank angle and the rudder is used to counteract adverse yaw, so the aircraft turns smoothly without skidding or slipping.

Slips and skids

Uncoordinated flight takes two forms. In a slip, the aircraft's nose points outward from the turn and the aircraft slides toward the inside of the turn; too little rudder (or too much bank) causes a slip. In a skid, excess rudder yaws the nose into the turn and the aircraft slides outward. Skids are particularly hazardous at low altitude and speed because they can precipitate a spin.

Instrumentation

Pilots maintain coordination using the turn and slip indicator — the "ball" of the inclinometer — applying rudder pressure to "step on the ball" and keep it centred between the reference marks. A simple yaw string taped to the canopy or windscreen serves the same purpose on gliders and light aircraft. In straight flight the ball should also be centred, which may require compensating for propeller effects such as P-factor and slipstream.

Technique

Because aileron deflection initially produces adverse yaw — the descending wing's aileron creating more drag — the pilot applies rudder in the same direction as the roll when entering a turn, then maintains just enough rudder to keep the flight coordinated throughout the turn and the rollout.
