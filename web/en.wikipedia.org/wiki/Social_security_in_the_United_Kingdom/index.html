Social security in the United Kingdom is the system of state-provided financial support for people who are retired, unemployed, ill, disabled, bereaved, or on low incomes, together with payments for children and housing costs. It is commonly called the "welfare" or "benefits" system. Spending on social security is the largest single component of UK public expenditure, and more than half of it goes to pensioners, principally through the State Pension.

History

The system's roots lie in the Poor Laws, which from 1601 made parishes responsible for their poor, and the punitive workhouse regime of the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. The modern system began with the Liberal reforms of 1906–1914: old-age pensions (1908) and National Insurance against sickness and unemployment (1911). The decisive expansion followed William Beveridge's 1942 report Social Insurance and Allied Services, which proposed tackling the "five giants" of Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. The post-war Labour government implemented much of the plan through the National Insurance Act 1946 and National Assistance Act 1948, alongside the National Health Service.

Main components

The State Pension is a contributory benefit based on National Insurance records; a flat-rate "new State Pension" applies to those reaching pension age since April 2016, uprated by the "triple lock". Working-age support has been progressively consolidated into Universal Credit, introduced from 2013, which merges six means-tested benefits and tax credits into a single monthly payment. Disability support includes Personal Independence Payment and Disability Living Allowance; families receive Child Benefit (near-universal, though clawed back from higher earners); and Housing Benefit and the housing element of Universal Credit help with rent.

Devolution and debate

Social security is partly devolved: Scotland has gained powers over disability and some other benefits, delivered by Social Security Scotland, while Northern Ireland operates its own system broadly mirroring Great Britain's. The system is a perennial focus of political debate — over benefit levels and adequacy, conditionality and sanctions, the two-child limit on some payments, assessment processes for disability benefits, and the cost and design of the state pension.
