Alamut is a region in the western Alborz mountains of northern Iran, and the name of the mountain fortress that served as the headquarters of the Nizari Ismaili state from 1090 until its destruction by the Mongols in 1256. The castle stood on a narrow rock spur some 2,000 metres above sea level, commanding the Alamut valley near modern Qazvin.

History

In 1090 the missionary Hassan-i Sabbah seized Alamut — according to legend by purchasing it from its lord for 3,000 gold dinars — and built it into the centre of a network of fortresses across Persia and Syria. From Alamut the Nizari leadership directed their resistance to the Seljuk Empire and later foes, becoming famous (and feared) for targeted killings carried out by their fedayeen, the so-called "Assassins". Alamut was also famed for gardens and for a great library.

In 1256 the Mongol prince Hülegü, on campaign orders from Möngke Khan, forced the surrender of the last lord of Alamut, Rukn al-Din Khurshah; the fortress was demolished and its library burned, though the Persian historian Ata-Malik Juvayni, who accompanied the campaign, salvaged selected volumes.

Legacy

The ruins of Alamut remain a major historical site and tourist destination in Iran. The fortress and Hassan-i Sabbah figure in medieval European chronicles, in Marco Polo's account of the "Old Man of the Mountain", and in modern literature and games.
