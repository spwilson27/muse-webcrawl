Scottish Americans

Total populationSeveral million reporting Scottish ancestry (with ~8–9 million more reporting Scots-Irish ancestry)
RegionsThroughout the United States; historically the South, Appalachia, New England
LanguagesEnglish, Scottish Gaelic (heritage), Scots
ReligionPredominantly Protestant (Presbyterian historically), Catholic and others

Scottish Americans (Scottish Gaelic: Ameireaganaich Albannach) are Americans who have full or partial Scottish ancestry. Scots have emigrated to North America since the 17th century, as merchants, soldiers, indentured servants, transported Jacobites and Covenanters, and later as industrial workers and professionals. Millions of Americans report Scottish ancestry in census surveys, and a still larger number descend from the Ulster Scots ("Scots-Irish") who settled the Appalachian backcountry in the 18th century.

History

Early Scottish settlers arrived in the colonial era, including prisoners transported after the Jacobite risings of 1715 and 1745 and after the Battle of Dunbar (1650) and Worcester. Highland Scots settled the Cape Fear valley of North Carolina, while Lowland Scots were prominent in trade and the professions. In the 19th century, heavy emigration from Scotland brought Scots to industrial cities and mining regions across the United States. Scots and their descendants played prominent roles in American business and industry — figures such as steel magnate Andrew Carnegie, born in Dunfermline — as well as in politics: many signers of the Declaration of Independence and numerous US presidents had Scottish or Scots-Irish ancestry.

Culture

Scottish-American cultural life is expressed through Highland games, pipe bands, Burns suppers celebrating poet Robert Burns, tartan and clan societies, and St Andrew's societies, the oldest of which date to the colonial period. The US Senate has recognised 6 April as National Tartan Day, commemorating the Declaration of Arbroath and Scottish contributions to American independence.
