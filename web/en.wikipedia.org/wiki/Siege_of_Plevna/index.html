Siege of Plevna

The siege of Plevna, or siege of Pleven, was a major land battle of the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878, fought from July to December 1877 at the fortified Bulgarian town of Pleven. An Ottoman army under Osman Nuri Pasha dug in at this road junction and repelled three major Russian and Romanian assaults, delaying the Russian advance toward Constantinople for five months before being starved into surrender on 10 December 1877. Plevna is a landmark in military history: entrenched infantry armed with modern breech-loading rifles repeatedly destroyed attacking formations, foreshadowing the trench warfare of 1914–1918.

Background

After crossing the Danube in June 1877, Russian forces advanced rapidly into Bulgaria, but Osman Pasha marched his army from Vidin and occupied Plevna, athwart the Russian line of advance, on 19 July, and began throwing up a ring of earthwork redoubts.

The assaults

The First Battle of Plevna (20 July) and the Second (30 July) — hasty, poorly coordinated Russian attacks — were thrown back with heavy losses by aimed rifle fire from the redoubts. The Third Battle (11–12 September), a deliberate assault by more than 80,000 Russians and Romanians after days of bombardment, captured a few redoubts, including the Grivitsa works taken by the Romanians, but failed overall at a cost of some 18,000 Allied casualties. The brilliant Russian general Mikhail Skobelev distinguished himself in the southern attacks. After the third failure, the Allies adopted the advice of the engineer general Eduard Totleben, hero of Sevastopol, and settled into a complete investment, cutting all roads and starving the garrison.

Surrender

With food exhausted and ammunition low, Osman Pasha attempted a breakout on the night of 9–10 December 1877; wounded in the attempt, he surrendered his sword and army — some 43,000 men — the following day. The fall of Plevna freed the Russian armies to cross the Balkan Mountains in mid-winter and advance on Adrianople and Constantinople, bringing the war to its conclusion in the Treaty of San Stefano. Osman Pasha's defence was admired even by his enemies, and he returned to Istanbul a hero, honoured with the title "Ghazi" (the Victorious).
