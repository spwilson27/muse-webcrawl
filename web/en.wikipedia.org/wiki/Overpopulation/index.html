Overpopulation

Overpopulation is the state in which a population exceeds the sustainable carrying capacity of its environment, or is perceived to do so. For human populations, the concept concerns whether the number of people exceeds the Earth's ability to provide resources and absorb waste at acceptable living standards. Human population surpassed 8 billion in November 2022, and United Nations medium projections suggest a peak of around 10.4 billion in the 2080s.

History of the concept

The idea is most associated with Thomas Robert Malthus, whose 1798 Essay on the Principle of Population argued that population grows geometrically while subsistence grows arithmetically, producing inevitable checks of famine and disease. Malthusian thinking influenced Darwin and later writers such as Paul R. Ehrlich, whose 1968 bestseller The Population Bomb predicted mass famine in the 1970s and 1980s — predictions widely criticized after the Green Revolution raised food production.

Contemporary views

Mainstream demography emphasizes that environmental impact depends not only on population size but on consumption and technology, summarized by the I = PAT identity (Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology). Per-capita resource use in wealthy countries far exceeds that in populous poorer countries. Fertility rates have fallen below replacement level in more than half of all countries, shifting policy concern in many places toward population ageing and decline. Critics of "overpopulation" framing argue it misdirects attention from consumption patterns and has historically been used to justify coercive policies; proponents respond that population growth remains a multiplier of environmental pressures such as biodiversity loss and climate change.
