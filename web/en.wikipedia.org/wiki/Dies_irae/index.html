Dies irae

"Dies irae" (Latin for "Day of Wrath") is a Latin sequence attributed to either Thomas of Celano of the Franciscans (1200–1265) or Latino Malabranca Orsini (d. 1294). It describes the Last Judgment, the trumpet summoning souls before God, where the saved will be delivered and the unsaved cast into eternal flames.

The sequence was used in the Roman Rite Requiem Mass until 1970, and its dramatic text and chant melody have been quoted by countless composers.

Text

The poem of 57 lines in trochaic metre opens: Dies irae, dies illa / Solvet saeclum in favilla / Teste David cum Sibylla ("Day of wrath, that day / shall dissolve the world in ashes / as David and the Sibyl testify"). Its closing sections, including the Lacrimosa, plead for mercy.

Musical settings

The plainsong melody is quoted in settings of the Requiem by Mozart, Verdi, Berlioz and many others, and appears as a symbol of death and doom in works by Liszt (Totentanz), Saint-Saëns (Danse macabre), Rachmaninoff, and in modern film scores.

Liturgical use

After the Second Vatican Council the sequence was removed from the ordinary Requiem liturgy, though it remains in the Liturgy of the Hours and may be used optionally in Masses for the Dead.
