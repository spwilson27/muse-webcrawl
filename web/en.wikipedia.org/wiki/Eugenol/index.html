Eugenol is an allyl-substituted guaiacol, a member of the phenylpropanoid family of organic compounds. It is a pale-yellow oily liquid extracted from certain essential oils, above all oil of cloves, of which it constitutes 70–90 percent; it also occurs in nutmeg, cinnamon, bay leaf and sweet basil. Eugenol is responsible for the characteristic aroma of cloves and has a long history of use in dentistry, perfumery and flavouring, as well as being the defining flavour compound of clove cigarettes (kretek).

Properties and production

Eugenol (C10H12O2) is slightly soluble in water and freely soluble in organic solvents; it darkens and thickens on exposure to air. Industrially it is obtained by steam distillation of clove buds, leaves or stems and subsequent extraction. Its isomer isoeugenol, produced by base-catalysed isomerisation of the allyl side chain, is a key intermediate in the synthesis of vanillin.

Uses

In dentistry, eugenol mixed with zinc oxide forms a soothing temporary filling and impression material; clove oil has been used for centuries as an analgesic for toothache, and eugenol shows local anaesthetic and antiseptic activity. It is widely used as a fragrance and flavour ingredient, as a fish anaesthetic in aquaculture and laboratory work, and as a starting material for fragrances and pharmaceuticals. Eugenol also attracts certain orchid bees, which collect it as a pheromone precursor.

Safety

In the quantities used in food, eugenol is generally recognised as safe, and it is approved as a flavouring. Concentrated clove oil can, however, irritate skin and mucous membranes and is toxic in overdose, particularly to children; eugenol is also a known contact allergen in some individuals. Clove cigarettes were banned in the United States as flavoured cigarettes in 2009.
