Karanis

Karanis is an ancient agricultural town in the Faiyum Oasis of Egypt, founded in the third century BCE during the Ptolemaic period and abandoned around the fifth century CE. It is one of the best-preserved Greco-Roman towns in Egypt, famous for the extraordinary quantity of papyri and everyday objects recovered from its ruins, which provide an unusually detailed picture of daily life in Roman Egypt.

History

The town was established under Ptolemy II Philadelphus (285–246 BCE) as part of a scheme to settle Greek veterans and Egyptian farmers on newly reclaimed land around Lake Moeris. It flourished under Roman rule, when its population may have reached several thousand. Karanis contained two stone temples dedicated to the crocodile gods Pnepheros and Petesouchos, mud-brick houses, granaries, bathhouses, and an extensive network of streets. The settlement declined in the third and fourth centuries CE as irrigation faltered, and was largely abandoned by the fifth century.

Excavations

Major excavations were carried out by the University of Michigan between 1924 and 1935, producing tens of thousands of artifacts and hundreds of papyrus documents, including tax rolls, letters, leases, and the archive of the soldier Claudius Terentianus. Finds from Karanis are now held at the Kelsey Museum of Archaeology in Ann Arbor and the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.
