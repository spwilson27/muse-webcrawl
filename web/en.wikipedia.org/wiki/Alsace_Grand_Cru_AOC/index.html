Alsace Grand Cru is an Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée (AOC) for wines produced in specific, delimited vineyard sites in the Alsace wine region of northeastern France. Unlike Burgundy or Bordeaux, where the appellation attaches to the site alone, Alsace Grand Cru wines are labelled with both the name of the grand cru vineyard and, following Alsatian custom, the grape variety. There are 51 grands crus, spread along the foothills of the Vosges mountains.

Regulations

Grand cru status was first granted in 1975 to Schlossberg, with twenty-four sites recognized in 1983 and the remainder added by 2007. Yields are restricted (generally to a maximum of 55–66 hectolitres per hectare depending on the decree), minimum ripeness requirements are higher than for the basic Alsace AOC, and grapes must be hand-harvested. With a handful of exceptions permitting blends (notably Altenberg de Bergheim and Kaefferkopf), wines must be made entirely from one of the four "noble" varieties: Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot gris, and the Muscats. Late-harvest (Vendange Tardive) and botrytised (Sélection de Grains Nobles) wines may also carry the designation.

Geology and sites

The grands crus occupy a mosaic of soils on the Vosges foothills — granite, gneiss, schist, sandstone, limestone, marl, and volcanic rock — whose diversity is a central theme of Alsatian terroir. Famous sites include Rangen (volcanic, the southernmost grand cru, at Thann), Hengst and Brand (at Wintzenheim and Turckheim), Schoenenbourg (at Riquewihr), and Zotzenberg, unique for permitting the Sylvaner variety.
