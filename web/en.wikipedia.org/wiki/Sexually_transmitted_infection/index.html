Sexually transmitted infection

A sexually transmitted infection (STI), also referred to as a sexually transmitted disease (STD) or venereal disease (VD), is an infection transmitted by sexual contact, including vaginal, anal, and oral sex. Many STIs cause no symptoms at first, which increases the risk of unknowing transmission. The WHO estimates that more than one million curable STIs are acquired every day worldwide among people aged 15–49.

The four curable STIs tracked globally by WHO — syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis — account for an estimated 374 million new cases annually. Viral STIs such as HIV and herpes are incurable but manageable with treatment.

Symptoms and complications

Symptoms, when present, may include genital discharge, sores or warts, pelvic pain, and burning on urination. Untreated infections can cause pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, cervical and other cancers (from HPV), congenital infections, and increased susceptibility to HIV.

Prevention and control

Consistent condom use greatly reduces transmission. Vaccines protect against HPV and hepatitis B; HIV risk can be reduced with pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and by antiretroviral treatment that suppresses viral load ("undetectable = untransmittable"). Public-health measures include screening of at-risk groups, partner notification, and prompt treatment; antibiotic resistance, especially in gonorrhoea, is a growing concern.

History

Venereal diseases have been described since antiquity; syphilis spread dramatically in Europe after the 1490s. The term "venereal" derives from Venus, Roman goddess of love. Schaudinn and Hoffmann identified the syphilis spirochaete in 1905, and penicillin transformed treatment from the 1940s. The emergence of HIV/AIDS in the 1980s reshaped STI policy and safe-sex education worldwide.
