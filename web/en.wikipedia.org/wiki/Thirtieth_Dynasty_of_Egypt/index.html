The Thirtieth Dynasty of ancient Egypt (380 – 343 BC) was the last dynasty of native Egyptian rulers. Founded by Nectanebo I of Sebennytos, who overthrew the Twenty-ninth Dynasty, it ruled a resurgent, independent Egypt that repeatedly repelled Achaemenid Persian attempts at reconquest — until Artaxerxes III finally subdued the country in 343 BC, opening the Second Persian Period and ending three thousand years of native pharaonic rule.

Nectanebo I and Teos

Nectanebo I (reigned 380–362 BC) secured his throne with Athenian and Spartan assistance, defeated a Persian invasion in 373 BC when the invading army was caught by the Nile flood in the Delta, and launched a great programme of temple building across Egypt, of which the mammisi at Dendera and works at Philae and Karnak survive. His son Teos (362–360 BC) taxed the temples heavily to fund a campaign into Phoenicia and Syria; the exactions provoked revolt at home, and Teos was overthrown while abroad.

Nectanebo II and the fall

Nectanebo II (360–343 BC), the last native pharaoh, mounted the throne amid the revolt and proved an energetic builder — his obelisks, now in Rome, and temples from Behbeit el-Hagar to Alexandria attest a final flowering of pharaonic art. With Greek mercenary support he defeated a first Persian assault in 351 BC, but in 343 BC Artaxerxes III returned in force, breached the Delta defences at Pelusium, and drove Nectanebo II into exile, traditionally in Nubia. In later legend "Nectanebus" became a magician-king and, in the Alexander Romance, the secret father of Alexander the Great.
