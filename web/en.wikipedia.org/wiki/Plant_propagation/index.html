Plant propagation

Plant propagation is the process of creating new plants from existing ones, by gardeners, nurseries, and plant breeders. It is divided into sexual propagation, using seeds — the products of pollination and fertilisation — and asexual or vegetative propagation, which produces genetically identical clones of the parent plant.

Sexual propagation

Sowing seed is the cheapest and most natural method and is standard for most vegetables, annual flowers, and forestry. Because seedlings segregate genetically, seed propagation preserves variation but does not reproduce the exact qualities of a named cultivar; many fruit trees grown from seed differ markedly from their parent. Seeds may require treatments such as stratification (cold), scarification, or soaking to break dormancy.

Vegetative propagation

Vegetative methods include taking cuttings of stems, leaves, or roots; layering, in which a stem roots while still attached; division of clumps, bulbs, corms, and tubers; and grafting and budding, uniting a scion of a desired cultivar onto the rootstock of another plant. Many plants propagate themselves naturally by runners (strawberries), rhizomes, suckers, or bulbils. Micropropagation — tissue culture of meristem cells on sterile nutrient medium — allows the rapid mass production of identical, disease-free plants and is used commercially for orchids, bananas, and many ornamentals. Vegetative propagation preserves desirable traits exactly and can reproduce sterile plants, but clonal populations share susceptibility to disease, and viruses can accumulate through generations unless clean stock is maintained.
