An autoregressive–moving-average (ARMA) model is a stochastic model used in time series analysis to describe and forecast a (weakly) stationary time series. It combines an autoregressive (AR) part, in which the current value is regressed on its own past values, with a moving-average (MA) part, in which the current value depends on past forecast errors (shocks). The model is usually written ARMA(p, q), where p is the order of the AR part and q the order of the MA part.

Definition

The AR part of order p expresses the value Xt as a constant plus a linear combination of the p previous values plus white noise εt. The MA part of order q expresses Xt as a mean plus the current shock and a linear combination of the q previous shocks. Combining them gives the ARMA(p, q) equation, often written compactly with the backshift operator B. Pure AR(p) and MA(q) models are the special cases q = 0 and p = 0.

Fitting and use

The Box–Jenkins methodology popularised ARMA modelling in the 1970s: identify candidate orders from the autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) functions, estimate parameters by maximum likelihood, and check that residuals resemble white noise. A parsimonious ARMA model can capture the behaviour of processes that would need many terms of a pure AR or pure MA representation.

Limitations and extensions

ARMA models assume stationarity; series with trends or unit roots are first differenced, yielding the ARIMA (integrated) family, with SARIMA adding seasonal terms. Exogenous inputs give ARMAX, vector generalisations give VARMA, and coupling ARMA mean equations with GARCH variance equations extends the framework to volatility modelling in finance.
