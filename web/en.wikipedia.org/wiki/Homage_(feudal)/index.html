Homage (feudal)

A vassal doing homage before his lord

Homage (from Medieval Latin hominaticum, "pertaining to a man") in the Middle Ages was the ceremony in which a feudal tenant or vassal pledged reverence and submission to his feudal lord, receiving in exchange the symbolic title to what would be called a fief—territory owned by the lord that would be held by the tenant as long as he served his lord faithfully. The ceremony and the relationship it created formed one of the pillars of the feudal system in medieval Europe.

The ceremony

In the classic form of the ceremony, the vassal knelt before his lord, placed his joined hands between the lord's hands (the immixtio manuum), and declared himself "the man" (homo) of the lord for the fief he was to hold. The lord then raised him and kissed him, and the vassal swore an oath of fealty (fidelity), usually on a Bible or sacred relic. The lord then performed investiture, handing over a symbolic object such as a clod of earth, a branch, or a banner representing the fief.

Liege homage

As feudal relationships multiplied, a vassal might owe homage to several lords. To resolve conflicting obligations, the concept of liege homage developed: the liege lord was the one to whom the vassal's primary duty belonged. Kings worked to ensure that homage owed to them took precedence, and in some cases monarchs themselves performed homage for lands they held from another king — most famously, the kings of England did homage to the kings of France for their continental possessions such as the Duchy of Aquitaine.

Decline

With the growth of money economies, professional armies, and centralised states from the late Middle Ages onward, personal homage gradually lost practical importance, surviving in some places as a formal ceremony into the early modern period.
