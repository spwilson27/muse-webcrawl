Eoin MacNeill (Irish: Eoin Mac Néill; 15 May 1867 – 15 October 1945) was an Irish scholar, historian, and nationalist politician. He was a co-founder of the Gaelic League, the movement that drove the revival of the Irish language, and chief of staff of the Irish Volunteers. He is notable for his attempt to countermand the Easter Rising of 1916, and later served as Minister for Education in the Irish Free State.

Early life and the language revival

MacNeill was born John MacNeill in Glenarm, County Antrim, into a Catholic family. Educated in Belfast, he worked as a civil service clerk in Dublin while studying law and, increasingly, early Irish history and language. In 1893, together with Douglas Hyde, he founded the Gaelic League (Conradh na Gaeilge), and he edited its newspaper An Claidheamh Soluis. His scholarship transformed the study of early Irish law and history, and in 1908 he became the first professor of early and medieval Irish history at University College Dublin.

The Irish Volunteers and the Easter Rising

In November 1913, MacNeill's article "The North Began" argued that nationalists should imitate the unionist Ulster Volunteers by forming their own volunteer force. The Irish Volunteers were founded later that month with MacNeill as chief of staff, though the secret Irish Republican Brotherhood infiltrated the leadership and planned an insurrection without his knowledge. When MacNeill learned, days before Easter 1916, that a rising had been scheduled under cover of Volunteer "manoeuvres" — and that the expected German arms shipment had been scuttled — he issued a countermanding order, published in the Sunday Independent, cancelling the mobilization. The Military Council proceeded anyway on Easter Monday with a much-reduced force. After the Rising, MacNeill was arrested and sentenced to life imprisonment, but was released in the general amnesty of 1917.

Political career

MacNeill was elected to the First Dáil and served as Minister for Finance and later for Industries in the revolutionary government. He supported the Anglo-Irish Treaty and in the Irish Free State became the first Minister for Education (1922–1925), overseeing the establishment of a national school curriculum with a strong Irish-language component. His position on the Boundary Commission of 1925 — whose report's suppression forced his resignation — ended his ministerial career. He returned to scholarship, publishing works including Celtic Ireland (1921) and Early Irish Laws and Institutions (1935). He died in Dublin in 1945.
