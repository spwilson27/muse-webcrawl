Drang nach Osten

Drang nach Osten (German for "drive to the East", "push eastward" or "desire to push East") is a term coined in the 19th century to describe the German expansion into Eastern Europe. The concept underpins the German settlement of Eastern and Central Europe from the Middle Ages onward, as well as various political and military ambitions of German states.

Historical background

The phrase is associated with the medieval Ostsiedlung ("east settling"), in which ethnic Germans migrated into territories east of the Elbe and Saale rivers, areas then inhabited largely by Slavs and Balts. German-speaking settlers founded towns, monasteries and agricultural communities across what are now Poland, the Czech lands, the Baltic states and beyond.

19th and 20th centuries

In the 19th century the term was taken up by nationalist writers, who framed eastward expansion as a historic German destiny. The idea was later radicalized by the Nazi regime, whose policies of Lebensraum ("living space") sought the conquest, depopulation and Germanization of Eastern Europe, culminating in the invasion of Poland and the Soviet Union during the Second World War and the atrocities of Generalplan Ost.

Aftermath

After 1945, Germany's eastern territories were transferred to Poland and the Soviet Union, and millions of ethnic Germans were expelled westward. The term remains in use in historiography as a shorthand for the long history of German eastward expansion and its often violent consequences.
