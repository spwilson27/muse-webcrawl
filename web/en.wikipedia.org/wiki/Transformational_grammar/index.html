Transformational grammar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transformational grammar is a theory of generative grammar, developed principally by Noam Chomsky from the 1950s, in which the sentences of a language are generated from abstract underlying structures (deep structures) by formal operations called transformations that yield the pronounced forms (surface structures). The theory aims to model the tacit knowledge that lets native speakers produce and understand an unlimited number of sentences, including sentences they have never heard before.

Origins

Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957), based on work by his teacher Zellig Harris, argued that phrase-structure grammars alone could not account for systematic relations between sentence types — for example, between an active sentence ("The cat chased the mouse") and its passive counterpart ("The mouse was chased by the cat"), or between a declarative and the corresponding question. Transformational rules were proposed to derive one structure from the other, capturing these relations directly in the grammar rather than generating each sentence type independently.

Deep and surface structure

In the "Standard Theory" of Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965), a base component (phrase-structure rules plus a lexicon) generates deep structures, which receive a semantic interpretation; transformational rules then map deep structures onto surface structures, which are given a phonetic interpretation. The distinction explained how two sentences could share one meaning with different forms (active/passive) and how one surface form could be ambiguous between two deep structures ("Flying planes can be dangerous").

Later developments

The framework evolved through the Extended Standard Theory, Government and Binding theory (1981), and the Minimalist Program (from 1993), with transformations progressively restricted and generalised — most notably into the single operation "Move α" and later the feature-driven operation of movement under Merge. Phenomena motivating transformations include wh-movement in questions, raising, and passivisation. Alternative generative frameworks, such as Lexical Functional Grammar and Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar, developed partly in reaction to transformational analyses, encoding the same relations without derivations between structures.

Criticism and influence

Transformational grammar was central to the "cognitive revolution" and to the founding of modern theoretical linguistics. Critics — including the generative semanticists of the late 1960s and later usage-based and construction-grammar theorists — have disputed the psychological reality of deep structure, the universality claims attached to the theory, and the poverty-of-the-stimulus arguments used to support innate grammatical knowledge. Nevertheless, the concepts of derivation, movement and underlying structure remain foundational in syntax.
