The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station is a United States scientific research station at the geographic South Pole in Antarctica, named in honour of Roald Amundsen and Robert F. Scott, whose expeditions reached the Pole in 1911 and 1912. Continuously occupied since 1956, it is administered by the National Science Foundation through the United States Antarctic Program.

History and buildings

The first station was built by the U.S. Navy for the International Geophysical Year in 1956–57 and is now buried beneath accumulated snow. It was replaced in 1975 by a geodesic dome (the "Dome"), and in turn by the current Elevated Station, a modular building on stilts completed in 2008 that can be jacked up as snow accumulates. A ceremonial pole surrounded by Antarctic Treaty flags and a marker at the surveyed geographic pole — which moves about ten metres per year with the ice — are maintained nearby.

Research

The station supports research in astronomy and astrophysics (exploiting the clear, dry polar air), glaciology, geophysics and atmospheric science. Major instruments include the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, buried in the ice sheet, and the South Pole Telescope, which studies the cosmic microwave background. The station is supplied by ski-equipped LC-130 aircraft and, since 2008, by overland tractor traverse from McMurdo Station; it is isolated with no flights for roughly eight and a half months of winter.

Climate and life

Temperatures at the Pole range between about −13 °C and −82 °C, with an annual mean near −49 °C. The sun rises and sets only once a year. The winter crew of around fifty maintains the station and its science through the months of darkness.
