Wake turbulence

Wake turbulence is a disturbance in the atmosphere that forms behind an aircraft as it passes through the air. It includes vortices that remain in the atmosphere for some time after an aircraft passes. Wake turbulence is especially hazardous to aircraft flying through the wake of another, larger aircraft, and is a factor considered in minimum separation requirements between aircraft during take-off and landing.

Formation

Lift-induced wingtip vortices are produced because of the pressure differential between the upper and lower surfaces of a wing. Air flows from below the wing up and around the wingtip to the region above the wing, creating a rotating pair of counter-rotating vortices that trail behind the aircraft. The strength of the vortices depends primarily on the weight, speed, and configuration of the generating aircraft.

Effects and avoidance

An aircraft encountering another's wake may experience a rolling moment strong enough to exceed its control authority. Pilots are trained to avoid wakes by staying above the flight path of preceding aircraft and using separation minima based on weight categories. The phenomenon has been implicated in several accidents, including the crash of American Airlines Flight 587, where wake encounter contributed to the circumstances of the accident sequence.
