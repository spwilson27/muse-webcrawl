Waring's problem

Waring's problem

In number theory, Waring's problem asks whether each natural number k has an associated positive integer g(k) such that every natural number is the sum of at most g(k) natural-number k-th powers. The problem was named after the English mathematician Edward Waring, who stated it in 1770 in his Meditationes Algebraicae — without proof — having observed that every number is a sum of at most 4 squares, 9 cubes, or 19 fourth powers.

History

The case of squares had been settled by Lagrange's four-square theorem (1770), which says every natural number is a sum of at most four squares, so g(2) = 4. David Hilbert proved in 1909 that g(k) exists for every k, using a remarkable identity; his proof was non-constructive and gave no practical bound. In the 1920s Hardy and Littlewood developed their circle method to attack the problem, introducing the function G(k), the smallest number of k-th powers needed to represent all sufficiently large integers.

The function g(k)

A lower bound comes from considering the number 2k⌊(3/2)k⌋ − 1, which forces g(k) ≥ 2k + ⌊(3/2)k⌋ − 2. It is conjectured — and verified for many k — that equality holds except in rare cases. Dickson, Pillai, Rubugunday, and Niven completed the determination in the 20th century for most values: g(3) = 9 (only 23 and 239 need nine cubes), g(4) = 19, g(5) = 37, g(6) = 73. The general formula is confirmed for all k up to large bounds, with remaining uncertainty only when (3/2)k has an unusually large fractional part.

The function G(k)

For sufficiently large numbers far fewer powers usually suffice: G(2) = 4, G(3) is known to lie between 4 and 7 (large numbers that are sums of at most four cubes abound in theory, though none is explicitly known to require checking), and G(4) = 16, proved by Davenport in 1939. Bounds for G(k) in general — roughly k log k — remain an active area of analytic number theory, advanced by Vinogradov's methods and modern refinements of the circle method.
