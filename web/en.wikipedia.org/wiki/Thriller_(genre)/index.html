Thriller (genre)

Thriller is a genre of fiction with numerous, often overlapping, subgenres, including crime, horror and detective fiction. Thrillers are characterised by the moods they elicit, giving their audiences heightened feelings of suspense, excitement, surprise, anticipation and anxiety. Successful examples of thrillers are the films of Alfred Hitchcock, often called the "Master of Suspense".

Characteristics

A thriller typically centres on a protagonist in danger, often with restricted means of escape, pursued by an antagonist or caught in a threatening situation. Plot devices include cliffhangers, red herrings, plot twists, ticking clocks, and high stakes — frequently life and death. Unlike horror, the thriller generally aims at tension and excitement rather than fear and disgust; unlike mystery, it commonly reveals the antagonist early and builds suspense from what the audience knows rather than what it does not.

History

Antecedents include ancient epics and tales such as The Count of Monte Cristo, gothic fiction, and the spy novels of John Buchan (The Thirty-Nine Steps, 1915) and later John le Carré. In film, Hitchcock's work from The Lodger (1927) through Psycho (1960) defined the genre's visual language. Post-war thrillers diversified into psychological, legal, medical and techno-thriller subgenres, with authors such as Patricia Highsmith, Frederick Forsyth, Michael Crichton, John Grisham and Gillian Flynn becoming bestsellers.

Subgenres

  • Psychological thriller — emphasis on mental states, unreliable narration and perception.
  • Spy thriller — espionage and intelligence agencies.
  • Crime thriller — criminals and law enforcement in a suspense framework.
  • Techno-thriller — detailed technology and military hardware.
  • Legal and medical thrillers — professional settings as frameworks for suspense.
