Scottish Hammer Throw

The Scottish hammer throw is a traditional Scottish athletic event, one of the heavy events contested at Highland games. It differs from the Olympic hammer throw in the implement used and the technique required: the Scottish hammer consists of a round metal head attached to a long, rigid shaft of cane, rattan or wood, and the athlete must throw with their feet in a fixed position, without the spinning turns used in the Olympic event.

The implement

The men's hammer head weighs 16 lb (7.26 kg) for the "light" hammer or 22 lb (9.98 kg) for the "heavy" hammer, with an overall length of about 50 inches (1.28 m); the women's implements weigh 12 lb (5.44 kg) and 16 lb (7.26 kg) respectively. The rigid shaft, unlike the wire of the Olympic hammer, cannot flex, which places a premium on smooth swings and a strong finish.

Technique

The thrower stands facing away from the throwing direction, feet planted — competitors traditionally wear boots fitted with protruding spikes at the toes, dug into the turf, to anchor themselves. The hammer is swung around the head in an increasing rhythm, typically two or three full winds, winding the body against the weight before releasing the hammer over the shoulder to fly behind-to-front over the field. Distance is measured from the trig (a wooden marker at the front of the throwing area) to where the head first lands. Because the feet may not move, the throw is often said to be the purest test of brute strength among the Highland events.

History

Hammer throwing in Scotland is documented from at least the 16th century — legend attributes a contest to King Henry VIII's visit — and the event was codified with the other heavy events in the 19th century as Highland games were formalised in Scotland and the diaspora. The Scottish event is considered an ancestor of the Olympic hammer throw, which developed from British and Irish practice in the late 19th century and entered the Olympics in 1900.
