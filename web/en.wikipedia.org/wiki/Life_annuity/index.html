Life annuity

A life annuity is a financial contract, usually issued by an insurance company, that pays a regular income to a person (the annuitant) for as long as they live, in exchange for a lump sum or a series of payments. Life annuities are used mainly in retirement planning to insure against longevity risk — the risk of outliving one's savings.

Types

  • Immediate annuity — payments begin shortly after purchase
  • Deferred annuity — payments begin at a future date, with an accumulation phase before
  • Fixed annuity — payments are fixed in amount
  • Variable annuity — payments vary with the performance of underlying investments
  • Joint and survivor annuity — payments continue until both of two people die
  • Annuity certain — payments continue for a guaranteed minimum period even if the annuitant dies

Valuation

The price of a life annuity depends on the payments promised, interest rates, and the annuitant's expected mortality, described by a life table. Actuaries compute the actuarial present value of the annuity as the sum of each payment discounted for interest and for the probability of survival to the payment date. Because annuitants pool mortality risk — those who die early subsidise those who live long — a life annuity yields "mortality credits" unavailable from bonds alone.

History

Life annuities were sold by Roman and medieval institutions, and governments in 17th- and 18th-century Europe raised finance by selling life annuities, famously in the schemes analysed by Johan de Witt in 1671, one of the founding works of actuarial science. Related arrangements include the tontine, in which payments accrue to survivors.

Markets

Some countries require pension savers to annuitise part of their retirement savings; others treat annuities as a voluntary product. Economists have long puzzled over the "annuity puzzle": despite the theoretical advantages of insuring longevity, voluntary demand for life annuities is lower than standard models predict.
