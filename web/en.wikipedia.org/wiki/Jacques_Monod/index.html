Jacques Lucien Monod (9 February 1910 – 31 May 1976) was a French biochemist who won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1965, shared with François Jacob and André Lwoff, "for their discoveries concerning genetic control of enzyme and virus synthesis."

Work

Working at the Pasteur Institute in Paris, Monod studied how bacteria regulate enzyme production. With Jacob he proposed the operon model — demonstrated on the lac operon of E. coli — in which structural genes are switched on or off by repressor proteins acting on an operator site, founding the field of gene regulation. He also helped develop the theory of allosteric proteins.

Chance and Necessity

Monod's philosophical bestseller Chance and Necessity (1970) argued that life arises from random mutation sifted by necessity-like natural selection, and that human beings are the product of chance in a universe indifferent to them — a stark statement of the modern scientific worldview that sparked wide debate.

Life

During World War II Monod served in the French Resistance, rising to a senior role in the Forces Françaises de l'Intérieur. He later became director of the Pasteur Institute (1971–1976). He died of leukemia in 1976 and is buried in Cannes.
