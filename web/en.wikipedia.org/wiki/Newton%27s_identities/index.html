In mathematics, Newton's identities, also known as the Newton–Girard formulae, are relations between two kinds of symmetric polynomials: the power-sum symmetric polynomials and the elementary symmetric polynomials. Evaluated at the roots of a monic polynomial, they give identities relating the coefficients of the polynomial to sums of powers of its roots, and they allow each family to be computed recursively from the other.

Statement

For variables x₁, ..., xₚ, let pₖ denote the k-th power sum x₁ₖ + x₂ₖ + ... + xₚₖ and eₖ the k-th elementary symmetric polynomial. The identities express eₖ in terms of the preceding elementary polynomials and power sums, and conversely each power sum in terms of the elementary polynomials, with alternating signs.

Albert Girard published the relations in 1629, before Newton; Newton rediscovered and generalised them around 1666 without, apparently, knowing Girard's work.

Applications

The identities are used in the theory of symmetric functions, in Galois theory when relating symmetric functions of roots, in algebraic combinatorics, and in areas such as coding theory and the computation of traces in linear algebra. A closely related set of formulas connects power sums to complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials.

In combinatorics they also appear in the study of partition theory and in proofs about the ring of symmetric functions, where the two families form alternative sets of generators.
