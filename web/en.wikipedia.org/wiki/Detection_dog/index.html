Detection dog

A detection dog at work with its handler.

A detection dog or sniffer dog is a dog that is trained to use its senses to detect substances such as explosives, illegal drugs, wildlife scat, currency, blood, and contraband electronics such as illicit mobile phones. The sense most used by detection dogs is smell. Hunting dogs that search for game, and search dogs that work to find missing humans are generally not considered detection dogs, but are instead assigned their own categories. There is also a large body of scientific evidence to support the application of medical detection dogs, capable of detecting diseases such as cancer and monitoring conditions such as diabetes.

History

The use of dogs to detect substances dates to the Second World War, when dogs were used to locate mines and explosives. The modern detection dog programme grew rapidly in the 1970s as law enforcement agencies expanded drug interdiction efforts. Dogs have since been trained to detect a wide variety of targets, including agricultural pests, invasive species, and diseases of plants and animals.

Training

Detection dogs are typically trained using positive reinforcement, in which the dog learns to associate the target odour with a reward such as a toy or food. Handlers train dogs to indicate a find either actively, by barking, scratching, or pawing, or passively, by sitting or staring at the source of the odour. Passive indication is preferred around explosives, where disturbance could be dangerous.

Functions

Detection dogs work in airports, border crossings, prisons, schools, and military operations. Specialised dogs detect bed bugs, termites, mould, accelerants in arson investigations, and even electronic storage devices. Conservation detection dogs locate endangered species and their scat for ecological research.

Accuracy

A dog's sense of smell is estimated to be tens of thousands of times more sensitive than a human's, with up to 300 million olfactory receptors. Accuracy varies with training, the target substance, and environmental conditions, and researchers continue to study the reliability of dog alerts in legal settings.
