Euphemism

A euphemism is an innocuous word or expression used in place of one that is deemed offensive or suggests something unpleasant. Some euphemisms are intended to amuse, while others use bland, inoffensive terms for concepts the user wishes to downplay or disguise. Euphemisms may be used to soften topics such as death, sex, bodily functions, and profanity, or to make political and institutional language appear more favourable than the underlying reality.

Etymology

The word euphemism comes from the Greek word εὐφημία (euphemia), meaning "the use of words of good omen", itself derived from εὖ (eu), "good", and φήμη (pheme), "speech" or "saying". In ancient Greek religion, euphemia also referred to the ritual silence kept during sacrifices to avoid words of ill omen.

Types

Euphemisms take many forms: abbreviations of taboo words, foreign borrowings, circumlocutions, understatements, and technical or bureaucratic jargon. Common examples include "passed away" for died, "restroom" for toilet, "let go" for fired, and "collateral damage" for civilian casualties in warfare.

The euphemism treadmill

The linguist Steven Pinker popularised the term euphemism treadmill for the process by which a euphemism, once it becomes closely associated with the concept it was meant to soften, itself acquires negative connotations and is replaced by a fresh term. Successive terms for the same condition or group may cycle through this process repeatedly.

Opposites

The opposite of a euphemism is a dysphemism, an expression chosen to be deliberately offensive or disparaging. Between the two lies the neutral or orthophemism, a direct but unmarked term.
