Kalua pig, or kalua pork, is a traditional Hawaiian dish and cooking method. The word kālua ("to cook in an underground oven") refers to the traditional preparation in which a whole pig is cooked in an imu, an underground earth oven. Kalua pig is a staple of the luau feast and of the Hawaiian plate lunch, where it is often served with cabbage.

Traditional preparation

To build an imu, a pit is dug and lined with stones heated by a fire of hardwoods such as kiawe (mesquite). The pig is salted with Hawaiian sea salt, sometimes stuffed with hot stones, then placed on the heated rocks over a layer of banana stalks and covered with banana and ti leaves, wet burlap and earth to trap steam and smoke. After many hours of slow cooking, the meat becomes tender, moist and smoky, and is shredded by hand into long strands.

Modern preparation

Because digging an imu is impractical for most cooks and restaurants, modern kalua pig is most often made from pork butt (shoulder) seasoned only with Hawaiian sea salt and a small amount of liquid smoke, wrapped in banana or ti leaves (or foil), and slow-roasted in an oven or in a slow cooker or pressure cooker until it shreds easily. The minimalist seasoning is considered part of the dish's identity. Kalua pig is frequently cooked together with cabbage in the final stage, and leftovers are used in dishes such as kalua pig and cabbage plate lunches.
