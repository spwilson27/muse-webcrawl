Stamp Act Congress

The Stamp Act Congress was a meeting of delegates from nine of the Thirteen Colonies held in New York City from October 7 to 25, 1765, to coordinate colonial opposition to the Stamp Act of 1765, Parliament's first direct tax on the American colonies. It was the first gathering of representatives from several colonies convened to devise a common response to British policy, and an important precursor to the Continental Congresses of the American Revolution.

Background

The Stamp Act, passed in March 1765, required colonists to buy stamped paper for newspapers, legal documents, pamphlets, and playing cards. Colonists objected that only their own elected assemblies could tax them — "no taxation without representation." The Massachusetts Assembly, acting on a proposal by James Otis, issued a circular letter inviting the colonies to send delegates to a congress in New York. Nine colonies sent delegations: Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, and South Carolina. The assemblies of Virginia, North Carolina, and Georgia were prevented from participating by their royal governors, and New Hampshire declined but later approved the Congress's work.

Proceedings

Twenty-seven delegates met at City Hall (later Federal Hall) in New York, electing Timothy Ruggles of Massachusetts as chairman. Figures included John Dickinson of Pennsylvania, who drafted much of the Congress's output, as well as James Otis, Robert R. Livingston, Philip Livingston, Caesar Rodney, and Christopher Gadsden. Ruggles, a loyalist, refused to sign the petitions and was later censured by the Massachusetts Assembly.

Declaration of Rights and Grievances

On October 19 the Congress adopted the Declaration of Rights and Grievances, a set of fourteen resolutions. It acknowledged "all due subordination" to Parliament but asserted that colonists possessed the rights of Englishmen, that only colonial assemblies could impose taxes on them, that trial by jury was a fundamental right threatened by the expanded vice-admiralty courts, and that the Stamp Act and other duties had a "manifest tendency to subvert the rights and liberties of the colonists." The Congress also sent petitions to the king, the House of Lords, and the House of Commons.

Outcome

Combined with colonial boycotts of British goods and crowd actions against stamp distributors, the protests persuaded Parliament to repeal the Stamp Act in March 1766 — though Parliament simultaneously passed the Declaratory Act asserting its authority to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever," setting up future conflicts.
