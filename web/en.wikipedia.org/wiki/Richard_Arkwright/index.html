Sir Richard Arkwright (23 December 1732 – 3 August 1792) was an English inventor and entrepreneur who became a leading figure of the Industrial Revolution. Although the patents for his spinning frame and carding machine were eventually overturned amid disputes over originality, he is credited with creating the modern factory system by combining powered machinery, semi-skilled labour, capital, and raw materials in one integrated enterprise.

Early life

Arkwright was born in Preston, Lancashire, the youngest of seven children in a poor family. He was taught to read and write by a cousin, apprenticed as a barber and wig-maker, and later travelled collecting human hair for wigs — a trade that gave him both capital and business connections.

The water frame and Cromford

Working with clockmaker John Kay, Arkwright developed a roller-spinning machine that produced a much stronger thread than the spinning jenny, suitable for warp. He patented the spinning frame in 1769. In 1771 he and partners built a water-powered mill at Cromford in Derbyshire — the water frame — considered the first successful water-powered cotton spinning mill and the prototype of the modern factory. A second, larger mill followed, along with mills in Lancashire and Scotland built under licence.

Later life and legacy

A court case in 1785 revoked Arkwright's patents, ruling them insufficiently novel and vaguely specified, yet he remained wealthy and respected: he served as High Sheriff of Derbyshire and was knighted in 1786. He died at Willersley Castle, Cromford, in 1792, leaving a fortune estimated at over £500,000. His Derwent Valley Mills are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
