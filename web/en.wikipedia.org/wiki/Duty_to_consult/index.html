Duty to consult

Duty to consult
Jurisdiction: Canada
Source: Section 35, Constitution Act, 1982; honour of the Crown

The duty to consult is a statutory, contractual, and common law obligation in Canadian Aboriginal law requiring the Crown to consult with Indigenous peoples — and where appropriate accommodate their interests — when it contemplates conduct that might adversely affect asserted or established Aboriginal or treaty rights protected by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.

Origins

The modern duty was articulated by the Supreme Court of Canada in Haida Nation v. British Columbia (Minister of Forests), 2004 SCC 73. The Court grounded the duty in the honour of the Crown and the goal of reconciliation: the Crown must consult in good faith, with the intention of substantially addressing Indigenous concerns, even before rights are proven in court. The depth of consultation lies on a spectrum proportionate to the strength of the claim and the seriousness of the potential impact.

Development

Taku River Tlingit (2004) and Mikisew Cree (2005) extended the duty to treaty contexts; Rio Tinto Alcan v. Carrier Sekani (2010) clarified its triggers; Tsilhqot'in Nation (2014) addressed consent after title is established; and Clyde River and Chippewas of the Thames (2017) considered regulatory processes. The duty cannot be delegated away, though procedural aspects may be carried out by tribunals or project proponents.
