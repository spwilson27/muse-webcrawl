An ecosystem engineer is any organism that creates, significantly modifies, maintains or destroys a habitat. The concept was formalised by Clive G. Jones, John H. Lawton and Moshe Shachak in 1994. By changing the availability of resources to other species and modifying environmental conditions, ecosystem engineers can have effects on communities far out of proportion to their abundance.

Types

Jones and colleagues distinguished two broad types:

  • Autogenic engineers change the environment through their own physical structures — their living or dead tissues. Trees, corals and kelp are examples: a forest or reef is itself the habitat.
  • Allogenic engineers change the environment by transforming materials from one state to another. Beavers felling trees to build dams, earthworms turning soil, and burrowing mammals are examples.

Examples

Beavers are the textbook case: their dams convert streams into ponds and wetlands, altering hydrology, sediment flow and nutrient cycling, and creating habitat for fish, amphibians, waterfowl and insects. Other well-studied engineers include prairie dogs, whose burrow systems shelter many other species; elephants, which topple trees and maintain savanna; termites, whose mounds structure tropical soils; oysters and mussels, which build reefs that filter water and shelter juveniles; and gopher tortoises, whose burrows are used by hundreds of commensal species.

Significance

Ecosystem engineering overlaps with the ideas of the keystone species and niche construction. Because engineers modulate resource availability, their loss — or their introduction as invasive species — can cascade through ecosystems. Recognising engineering effects has practical value in conservation and restoration: reintroducing beavers, for example, is used in some regions to restore wetlands, raise water tables and buffer drought and flooding.

Criticism

Some ecologists argue the concept is so broad — arguably all organisms modify their environment to some degree — that it risks losing precision, and have debated where engineering ends and ordinary trophic or competitive effects begin.
