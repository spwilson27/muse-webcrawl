Peterhof Palace

Peterhof (Russian: Петергоф, Petergof, from German Peterhof, "Peter's Court") is a series of palaces and gardens located in Petrodvorets, Saint Petersburg, Russia, commissioned by Peter the Great as a direct response to the Palace of Versailles built by Louis XIV of France. Sometimes called the "Russian Versailles", the palace-ensemble, with its celebrated fountains fed entirely by gravity, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Peter the Great first built a modest wooden lodge on the southern shore of the Gulf of Finland in 1705 and, after his victories in the Great Northern War, expanded the estate into a grand summer residence. The Grand Palace was built from 1714 to 1725 and enlarged by Empress Elizabeth to designs by Bartolomeo Francesco Rastrelli in the 1740s–1750s. The palace served as the principal summer residence of the tsars until 1917.[1]

The Grand Cascade and fountains

Peterhof is famous for its system of about 150 fountains, including the Grand Cascade below the palace with 64 fountains and 255 gilded bronze statues and reliefs, centred on the Samson Fountain, whose 20-metre jet is powered solely by the natural pressure from reservoirs in the Ropsha Heights — a feat of 18th-century hydraulic engineering with no pumps. Trick fountains in the Lower Gardens were designed to soak unsuspecting guests.

War and restoration

German troops occupied Peterhof from 1941 to 1944 and destroyed much of the palace; the Samson statue was looted and later recreated from photographs in 1947. Extensive restoration after the Second World War returned the palaces and gardens to their former appearance, and the fountains were relit in 1945–1947.
