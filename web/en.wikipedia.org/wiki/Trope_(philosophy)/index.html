Trope (philosophy)

In metaphysics, a trope is an abstract particular — an individual property instance. Where realism about universals holds that two red apples share one and the same property, redness, trope theory holds that each apple has its own distinct redness-trope: the redness of this apple is numerically different from the redness of that one, however exactly similar they may be. Tropes are sometimes called "abstract particulars" (a term from D. C. Williams) or "modes" in older scholastic terminology.

Trope theory

Trope theorists, including D. C. Williams, Keith Campbell, and later Anna-Sofia Maurin and others, aim to give an account of properties, objects, and resemblance without positing universals. On the simplest version, an ordinary concrete object is nothing but a bundle of compresent tropes — a particular shape-trope, colour-trope, mass-trope and so on, held together by a relation of compresence. This is a form of bundle theory that avoids some problems of substratum theories, since tropes themselves are particular and need no bare particular to inhere in.

Resemblance between tropes is treated as primitive or as grounded in exact similarity classes of tropes: the set of all exactly resembling redness-tropes plays the role that the universal redness plays for the realist. Relations are also handled as tropes — a particular instance of "being taller than" between two people is a relational trope.

History

Ideas resembling tropes appear in medieval discussions of individual accidents and formal distinctions, and in the work of philosophers such as John Duns Scotus. The modern theory was introduced by D. C. Williams in his 1953 paper "On the Elements of Being" and developed extensively by Keith Campbell in Abstract Particulars (1990). Trope theory has been influential in the metaphysics of science, where it is used in accounts of laws of nature, causation, and dispositions.

Criticisms

Critics object that trope theory merely shifts the problems it was meant to solve: the relation of compresence, if itself a trope, threatens a regress (a problem related to Bradley's regress); and treating resemblance between tropes as primitive may be no more explanatory than the universals it replaces. Defenders reply that every theory must take something as primitive, and that tropes offer a leaner ontology of a single category of entity.
