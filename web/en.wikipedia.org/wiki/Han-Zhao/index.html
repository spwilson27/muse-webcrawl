Han-Zhao

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Han-Zhao (simplified Chinese: 汉赵; traditional Chinese: 漢趙; pinyin: Hàn Zhào; 304–329 AD), also known as Former Zhao (前趙), was a dynastic state of the Sixteen Kingdoms period in Chinese history. It was founded by Liu Yuan, a chieftain of the Southern Xiongnu, and was ruled by the Liu family and later by the usurper Shi Le's rivals before its destruction by Later Zhao.

History

In 304, amid the chaos of the War of the Eight Princes that tore apart the Western Jin dynasty, Liu Yuan declared himself King of Han, claiming descent from the Han dynasty through a marriage alliance between the Han imperial house and the Xiongnu chanyu. In 308 he proclaimed himself emperor, and in 311 his forces captured the Jin capital Luoyang in the Disaster of Yongjia, capturing Emperor Huai. In 316 his son Liu Cong's armies took Chang'an and captured Emperor Min, effectively ending Western Jin control of the north.

Liu Cong renamed the state from Han to Zhao in 319, shortly before his death; the dynasty is therefore known to historians as Han-Zhao. After a succession struggle, power passed to Liu Yao, who moved the capital to Chang'an. In the west, meanwhile, the Jie general Shi Le had founded his own rival Zhao (Later Zhao). In 329 Shi Le's forces captured and executed Liu Yao, extinguishing Han-Zhao.

Significance

Han-Zhao was the first of the Sixteen Kingdoms and the first regime founded by a non-Han people to rule a large part of northern China. Its destruction of Western Jin power in the north opened a period of division lasting nearly three centuries.
