Square of opposition

The traditional square of opposition.

In term logic, the square of opposition is a diagram representing the relations between the four basic propositions of Aristotelian logic:

A: All S are PE: No S are P
I: Some S are PO: Some S are not P

The diagram originates in the analysis of syllogistic inference in Aristotle's On Interpretation and the Prior Analytics, and was elaborated by Apuleius and Boethius. For two millennia it stood at the centre of logical education.

Relations

Contradictories (A–O and E–I) cannot both be true and cannot both be false. Contraries (A–E) cannot both be true but can both be false. Subcontraries (I–O) can both be true but cannot both be false. Subalternation holds between A and I, and between E and O: the truth of the universal implies the truth of the particular.

Modern criticism

The traditional square presupposes that universal propositions have existential import — that the subject term refers to at least one existing thing. Modern predicate logic, following Frege and Russell, treats "All S are P" as a conditional that can be vacuously true when nothing is S. On that reading, most of the square's relations collapse, leaving only the contradictories. Defenders of term logic reply that natural-language universals do carry existential presuppositions, and modal and other extensions of the square (such as the modal square and Blanché's hexagon) continue to be studied.
