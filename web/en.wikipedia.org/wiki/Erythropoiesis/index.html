Erythropoiesis

Erythropoiesis (from Greek erythro-, "red", and -poiesis, "making") is the process which produces red blood cells (erythrocytes), the development from erythropoietic stem cell to mature red blood cell. It is stimulated by decreased oxygen in circulation, detected by the kidneys, which then secrete the hormone erythropoietin (EPO). This hormone stimulates proliferation and differentiation of red cell precursors, promoting the release of reticulocytes into the blood.

Process

In adults, erythropoiesis occurs mainly in the red bone marrow. Committed progenitors (BFU-E and CFU-E) give rise to a proerythroblast, which passes through basophilic, polychromatic and orthochromatic erythroblast stages while synthesising haemoglobin and condensing its nucleus. The nucleus is expelled to form a reticulocyte, which leaves the marrow and matures into an erythrocyte over one to two days in the circulation. The whole sequence takes about a week, and a healthy adult produces roughly two million red cells per second to match their destruction.

Regulation and clinical relevance

Besides EPO, effective erythropoiesis requires iron, vitamin B12 and folate; deficiencies of these, kidney failure (with low EPO), or marrow disorders cause anaemia. Excessive production occurs in polycythaemia. Recombinant erythropoietin is used therapeutically in renal anaemia and has been misused as a doping agent in endurance sport. In the fetus, red cells are produced first in the yolk sac, then mainly in the liver and spleen, before the marrow takes over.
