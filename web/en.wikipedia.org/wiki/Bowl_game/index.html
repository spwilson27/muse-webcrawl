The Rose Bowl in Pasadena, California, "The Granddaddy of Them All," is the oldest bowl game, first played in 1902 and annually since 1916.

In North America, a bowl game is one of a number of postseason college football games that are primarily played by teams in the NCAA Division I Football Bowl Subdivision (FBS). For most of its history, the Division I bowl system served as the postseason for college football, in contrast to the playoffs used in other sports and in lower NCAA divisions. The first bowl game was the 1902 Rose Bowl, played between Michigan and Stanford; the term "bowl" derives from the shape of the Rose Bowl stadium in Pasadena, California, and was adopted by other postseason venues.

Bowl games originally featured the best teams in the country, but by the 21st century the number of bowls had grown to more than forty, and teams generally need only a non-losing record (typically six wins) to be "bowl eligible." The most prestigious bowls — the Rose, Sugar, Orange, Cotton, Peach, and Fiesta, collectively the "New Year's Six" — host the College Football Playoff semifinals in rotation.

History

The 1902 game, part of Pasadena's Tournament of Roses festival, was so one-sided (Michigan won 49–0) that organisers replaced football with other events until 1916, when the game resumed permanently. Other cities in warm-weather regions copied the formula to attract winter tourists: the Orange Bowl (Miami, from 1935), Sugar Bowl (New Orleans, 1935), Sun Bowl (El Paso, 1935), and Cotton Bowl (Dallas, 1937) joined the Rose as the major bowls. For decades the bowls were exhibitions matched by conference tie-ins and did not determine a national champion, which was instead decided by the final AP and Coaches polls — sometimes crowned before the bowls were played.

Various alliance systems (the Bowl Coalition, Bowl Alliance, and from 1998 the Bowl Championship Series) tried to guarantee a No. 1 vs. No. 2 match-up, and in 2014 the College Football Playoff introduced a seeded playoff whose semifinals rotate among the New Year's Six bowls. The growth in the number of bowls — from 11 in the early 1970s to over 40 — was driven by television rights, especially after ESPN began owning and operating many of the newer games.

Eligibility and selection

An FBS team must finish with at least as many wins as losses and ordinarily at least six wins (one of which may be against an FCS opponent) to be bowl eligible. Conferences have contractual tie-ins with particular bowls, which select among eligible teams in an order set by the conference. If there are not enough eligible teams to fill all slots, teams with 5–7 records may be chosen based on Academic Progress Rate scores. Players traditionally receive gifts from bowl sponsors (within NCAA value limits), and participating schools receive payouts that are shared with their conferences, though many teams lose money on travel expenses.

Major bowls

  • Rose Bowl (Pasadena, 1902/1916– ) — traditionally Big Ten vs. Pac-12
  • Sugar Bowl (New Orleans, 1935– ) — traditionally SEC vs. Big 12
  • Orange Bowl (Miami, 1935– ) — traditionally ACC vs. an at-large team
  • Cotton Bowl Classic (Arlington, 1937– ) — at-large New Year's Six bowl
  • Peach Bowl (Atlanta, 1968– ) — New Year's Six bowl
  • Fiesta Bowl (Glendale, 1971– ) — New Year's Six bowl; site of several classic championship games

Economics and criticism

Bowl games are generally operated by local nonprofit organisations and generate significant tourism and television revenue, but investigative reporting and the book Death to the BCS criticised executive compensation, tax-exempt status, and the financial terms imposed on schools (such as mandatory ticket purchases). Supporters respond that bowls reward players with a postseason experience, give fans destination events, and fund local charities. The arrival of the playoff and its expansion to twelve teams (from the 2024 season) has refocused attention on the top of the sport, while the large slate of minor bowls continues as television programming and a reward for mid-tier teams.

Other uses

The term "bowl" is also used for a number of college all-star games (such as the Senior Bowl and the former East–West Shrine Game, now the East–West Shrine Bowl), for championship games in some Canadian and arena football contexts, and metaphorically in other sports. In Canadian football, the Vanier Cup and the Grey Cup use "cup" terminology, but the Mitchell Bowl and Uteck Bowl are national semifinal games in U Sports.
