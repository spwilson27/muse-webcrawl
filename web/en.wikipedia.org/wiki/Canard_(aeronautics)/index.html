Canard (aeronautics)

In aeronautics, a canard is a wing configuration in which a small forewing or foreplane is placed forward of the main wing of a fixed-wing aircraft, or a small horizontal surface mounted at the nose of the aircraft. The term originates from the French for "duck", from the appearance of early aircraft such as the Santos-Dumont 14-bis, whose forward surfaces suggested a duck's outstretched neck.

Design types

A canard may serve purely as a control surface (control-canard), may contribute substantially to lift (lifting-canard), or may be designed to stall before the main wing so that the nose drops and the aircraft recovers, making a full stall of the main wing nearly impossible. Closely coupled canards on delta-wing fighters interact with the main wing's vortex flow to improve manoeuvrability at high angles of attack.

History

The Wright Flyer of 1903 used a forward elevator for pitch control. Canards fell out of favour for decades but returned with supersonic research aircraft, Burt Rutan's homebuilt designs such as the VariEze and Long-EZ, and European fighters including the Saab Viggen and Gripen, the Dassault Rafale, and the Eurofighter Typhoon.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include stall resistance, the lift of both surfaces acting upward (unlike a conventional tail's downforce), and improved agility. Disadvantages include the need for careful centre-of-gravity placement, possible interference of the canard's wake with the main wing, and increased radar cross-section on stealth aircraft.
