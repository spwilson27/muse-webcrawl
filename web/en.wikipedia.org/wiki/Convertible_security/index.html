Convertible security

A convertible security is a financial instrument — most commonly a bond or preferred stock — that can be converted by the holder into another security of the same issuer, usually common stock. Convertible securities combine features of debt and equity: they pay periodic income like bonds while offering potential participation in the appreciation of the underlying shares.

TypesConvertible bonds, convertible preferred stock
Converts intoUsually common stock of the issuer
Key termsConversion price, conversion ratio, conversion premium

Features

The conversion ratio specifies how many shares the holder receives for each security converted; dividing the par value by the ratio gives the conversion price. Convertible bonds typically pay lower interest rates than comparable straight debt because investors value the conversion option. Issuers may call the bonds, which can force conversion when the stock price rises sufficiently.

Advantages and risks

For issuers, convertibles offer cheaper financing and a way to issue equity at a premium to the current share price. For investors, they provide downside protection through the bond floor together with equity upside. Risks include dilution of existing shareholders, subordination in bankruptcy, and sensitivity to both interest rates and the underlying stock price.

Variants

Related instruments include exchangeable bonds (convertible into shares of a different company), contingent convertibles (CoCos) used by banks, and mandatory convertibles that convert automatically at maturity.
