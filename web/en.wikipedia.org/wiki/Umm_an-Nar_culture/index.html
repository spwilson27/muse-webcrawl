Umm an-Nar culture

The Umm an-Nar culture was a Bronze Age culture of the Oman Peninsula, in present-day United Arab Emirates and northern Oman, flourishing from roughly 2600 to 2000 BCE. It is named after the island of Umm an-Nar near Abu Dhabi, where Danish archaeologists first excavated its distinctive tombs in the 1950s.

Characteristics

The culture is noted for large circular collective tombs built of dressed stone, some divided into multiple chambers and decorated with carved reliefs of animals and humans. Settlements such as those at Bat, Hili and Tell Abraq include round towers, houses and irrigation features. The people herded livestock, farmed dates and wheat, fished, and smelted local copper.

Trade

Umm an-Nar communities participated in the maritime trade of the Gulf, exchanging copper for goods from Mesopotamia, Iran and the Indus Valley; the region is identified with the land called Magan in Mesopotamian texts. The culture was succeeded by the Wadi Suq culture around 2000 BCE.
