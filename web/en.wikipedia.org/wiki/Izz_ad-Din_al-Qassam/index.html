Izz ad-Din al-Qassam

Izz ad-Din al-Qassam (1882–1935).

Izz ad-Din ibn Abd al-Qadar ibn Mustafa ibn Yusuf ibn Muhammad al-Qassam (1882 – 20 November 1935) was a Syrian-born Muslim cleric and preacher who became a leader of Arab resistance against British and French colonial rule and Zionist settlement in the Levant. In Mandatory Palestine he organised armed cells among peasants and the urban poor of Haifa, preaching that struggle against the British Mandate and the Zionist movement was a religious duty.

Life

Born in Jableh, in Ottoman Syria, al-Qassam studied at al-Azhar in Cairo, where he came under the influence of Islamic reformist thought. He fought against the Italian invasion of Libya in 1911 and against the French occupation of Syria after the First World War, for which a French court sentenced him to death in absentia. Fleeing to Haifa in 1922, he became a popular preacher at the Istiqlal Mosque and a registrar of marriages, building a following among dispossessed peasants driven off the land and migrant labourers in the port city.

Armed organising and death

From the late 1920s al-Qassam secretly recruited and trained small guerrilla bands, sometimes linked to the "Black Hand" attacks on Jewish settlements and British targets. In November 1935, after his group killed a British police sergeant, the authorities hunted him down; he was killed in a shoot-out near Ya'bad on 20 November 1935. His funeral in Haifa drew thousands and became a landmark in Palestinian national mobilisation, helping to spark the 1936–1939 Arab revolt.

Legacy

Al-Qassam is remembered as a foundational figure of Palestinian armed resistance. The military wing of Hamas is named the Izz ad-Din al-Qassam Brigades in his honour, and the Qassam rockets fired from Gaza also bear his name.
