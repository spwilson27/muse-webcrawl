Segedunum

Segedunum was a Roman fort at modern Wallsend, North Tyneside, in Tyne and Wear, England. Built around AD 127, it stood at the eastern end of Hadrian's Wall, guarding the eastern extremity of the frontier where the wall met the River Tyne. Its name is believed to derive from British Celtic roots meaning "strong fort" or "victory fort."

History

The fort covered about 4.1 acres (1.7 hectares) and straddled the line of the Wall, with barracks, headquarters, granaries, a hospital, and a bath house outside the walls. It was garrisoned by auxiliary infantry and cavalry units, including Thracian and Lingonian cohorts in the 2nd–4th centuries. After the Roman period the site was gradually buried beneath the shipbuilding town of Wallsend, and much of the fort was covered by terraced housing and industry.

Excavation and museum

Excavations from the 1970s onward, after demolition of housing on the site, uncovered much of the fort plan, including the foundations of the headquarters building and a section of Hadrian's Wall running down to the Tyne. The site opened as a museum in 2000, with a full-scale reconstruction of a Roman bath house and a 35-metre viewing tower overlooking the fort outline. A preserved stretch of wall at Wallsend and the fort's eastern position make it the starting point of the Hadrian's Wall Path national trail.
