Olympus Mons

Olympus Mons is a shield volcano on Mars, the tallest volcano and the tallest known mountain in the Solar System. It stands about 21.9 km (13.6 mi) above the Martian datum — roughly two and a half times the height of Mount Everest above sea level — and spans about 600 km in diameter, roughly the size of France. Its summit caldera is some 80 km wide.

Geology

Olympus Mons is the youngest of the large volcanoes of the Tharsis Montes region. Its enormous size is attributed to Mars's comparatively still crust — lacking plate tectonics, lava flows accumulated over a single hotspot for billions of years — together with low surface gravity and low erosion rates. The volcano's flanks have an average slope of only about 5 degrees, and the summit experiences atmospheric pressure around 12 percent of the Martian average, effectively reaching into near-space. A towering cliff, or basal scarp, up to 8 km high surrounds parts of the edifice. Spacecraft observations show a few small, geologically young lava flows, suggesting the volcano may not be fully extinct.

Observation history

Nineteenth-century astronomers saw Olympus Mons as a bright feature called Nix Olympica ("Snows of Olympus"), long suspected to be a mountain. The Mariner 9 orbiter confirmed in 1971–72 that it was a giant volcanic mountain.
