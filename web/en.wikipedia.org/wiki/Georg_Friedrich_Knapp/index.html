Georg Friedrich Knapp (7 March 1842 – 20 February 1926) was a German economist and founder of the chartalist school of monetary theory. His book Staatliche Theorie des Geldes (The State Theory of Money, 1905; English translation 1924) argued that money is a creation of law rather than a commodity, and it became a foundational text for later theories including Modern Monetary Theory.

Life

Knapp was born in Giessen, the son of the chemist Friedrich Ludwig Knapp. He studied in Munich, Berlin and Göttingen, initially working in statistics before turning to economics. He taught at the University of Leipzig and then, for most of his career, at the University of Strasbourg, where he also worked on agrarian history, publishing studies of the emancipation of peasants in Prussia. After the First World War, when Strasbourg returned to France, he retired to Darmstadt.

The State Theory of Money

Knapp argued that money's essence lies not in the material of coins or notes but in the legal ordinances governing their use: the state defines the unit of account, issues tokens, and — crucially — accepts those tokens in payment of taxes, which is what makes them generally acceptable. He coined the terms "chartal" (from Latin charta, a token or ticket) and "autometallic" money. John Maynard Keynes cited Knapp approvingly in his Treatise on Money (1930), and twentieth-century chartalists and post-Keynesians developed his argument.
