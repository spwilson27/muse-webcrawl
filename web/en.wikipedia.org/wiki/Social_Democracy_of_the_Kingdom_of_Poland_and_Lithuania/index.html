Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania

The Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania (Polish: Socjaldemokracja Królestwa Polskiego i Litwy, SDKPiL) was a Marxist political party founded in 1893 in the Polish lands then under Russian rule. It was one of the most radical and internationalist of the early Polish socialist movements, distinguished by its opposition to Polish national independence as a political goal and by its close association with Rosa Luxemburg and Leo Jogiches.

History

The party was founded in 1893 as the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (SDKP), drawing together Polish Marxist groups in the industrial cities of Congress Poland, above all Warsaw and Łódź. In 1899 it merged with Lithuanian social democrats and adopted the name SDKPiL. Rosa Luxemburg, one of the founders, had set out the party's theoretical position in her 1898 doctoral thesis on the industrial development of Poland, arguing that the Polish economy was organically integrated into the Russian Empire and that Polish independence would set back the prospects of socialist revolution.

The SDKPiL participated actively in the Revolution of 1905 in the Kingdom of Poland, organising strikes and armed clashes. In 1906 it joined the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party as an autonomous territorial organisation, maintaining close relations with both the Bolshevik and Menshevik factions but gravitating toward Lenin's side. The party was weakened by splits in 1911–1912, when a Warsaw committee broke away, but it reunified during the First World War.

In December 1918, following the collapse of the Central Powers and the outbreak of revolution in Germany, the SDKPiL merged with the PPS-Left to form the Communist Workers' Party of Poland, later the Communist Party of Poland.

Ideology

The SDKPiL held that the immediate task of Polish workers was socialist revolution in alliance with the Russian proletariat, not the restoration of an independent Polish state, which it regarded as a diversion promoted by the nationalist Polish Socialist Party. This "Luxemburgist" position on the national question later became a point of contention between Luxemburg and Lenin, most famously in Luxemburg's writings on the national question and her 1918 critique of the Russian Revolution.
