Laryngeal cancer

Laryngeal cancer

Specialty: Oncology, otorhinolaryngology
Symptoms: Hoarseness, voice change, sore throat, difficulty swallowing, neck lump
Risk factors: Smoking, alcohol, HPV, occupational exposures
Types: Glottic, supraglottic, subglottic

Laryngeal cancer is a malignant tumour of the larynx (voice box). Most laryngeal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas arising from the lining of the larynx. They are classified by their site of origin relative to the vocal cords: glottic (on the cords themselves, the most common), supraglottic (above the cords), and subglottic (below the cords, the rarest). Worldwide, on the order of 180,000 new cases and 100,000 deaths occur each year, with men affected several times more often than women.

Signs and symptoms

Persistent hoarseness or a change in the voice lasting more than a few weeks is the classic early symptom of glottic cancer, because even a small tumour interferes with vocal cord vibration. Later symptoms may include a chronic sore throat, pain or difficulty when swallowing, referred ear pain, a lump in the neck from spread to lymph nodes, coughing up blood, and breathing difficulty or noisy breathing (stridor) as the airway narrows.

Causes and risk factors

Tobacco smoking is the dominant risk factor, and heavy alcohol use acts together with smoking to multiply the risk. Human papillomavirus infection, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and occupational exposures such as asbestos and certain industrial dusts and fumes also contribute. The disease is most common in people in their 60s and 70s.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by examination of the larynx with a flexible endoscope (laryngoscopy) and biopsy, with CT, MRI, or PET imaging to stage the tumour and check for spread. Treatment depends on stage and site and aims, where possible, to preserve the voice: early cancers may be treated with radiotherapy or laser microsurgery alone, while advanced cancers often require combinations of surgery (partial or total laryngectomy), radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Total laryngectomy leaves the patient breathing through a neck stoma and speaking with the aid of voice rehabilitation or an electrolarynx. Quitting smoking and alcohol markedly improves outcomes; five-year survival ranges from over 80 percent for early glottic cancers to around 40 percent or less for advanced disease with nodal spread.
