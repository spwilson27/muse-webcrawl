Statics

Statics analyses systems in which the sum of forces and the sum of moments both equal zero.

Statics is the branch of mechanics concerned with the analysis of force and torque (moment) acting on physical systems that do not experience acceleration — that is, systems in static equilibrium with their environment. It is a subfield of classical mechanics and is fundamental to structural, civil, and mechanical engineering.

Principles

From Newton's second law, a body in equilibrium must satisfy two conditions: the vector sum of all forces acting on it is zero (ΣF = 0), which prevents translational acceleration, and the sum of all moments about any point is zero (ΣM = 0), which prevents rotational acceleration. Solving these equations for unknown reaction forces at supports, cables, and joints is the core task of statics. Free-body diagrams, which isolate a body and show every external force on it, are the standard tool for setting up such problems.

Applications

Statics is used to design and analyse bridges, buildings, dams, trusses, cranes, and machine components. Its methods cover concentrated and distributed loads, friction, centroids and centres of mass, moments of inertia, and the internal forces in beams and frames. Related branches include hydrostatics (fluids at rest) and the statics of deformable bodies treated in strength of materials.

History

The subject originates in antiquity: Archimedes formulated the law of the lever in the third century BC. Medieval scholars developed the theory of moments, and the modern vector-based treatment was consolidated in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries within analytical mechanics.
