Bichirs (family Polypteridae) are a group of primitive freshwater ray-finned fishes found in Africa. The family comprises the genus Polypterus, whose species are known as bichirs, and the single species of Erpetoichthys, the reedfish or ropefish. Bichirs are elongate, heavily scaled fishes with a distinctive row of separate small dorsal finlets, lobed pectoral fins, and a pair of lungs that allows them to breathe air.

Characteristics

Bichirs are among the most basal living ray-finned fishes, retaining features seen in fossil forms from the Devonian and Carboniferous: thick ganoid scales, a heterocercal-like tail, fleshy fin bases, and ventral paired lungs connected to the gut. Adults range from about 30 cm to nearly 1 m in the largest species. They are nocturnal bottom-dwelling predators that feed on invertebrates, small fishes and amphibians, locating prey largely by smell. Because they can gulp air at the surface, they tolerate oxygen-poor swamp waters, and juveniles have external gills reminiscent of larval salamanders.

Species and aquarium keeping

Recognised species of Polypterus include the Nile bichir (P. bichir), Senegal bichir (P. senegalus), ornate bichir (P. ornatipinnis), saddled bichir (P. endlicheri) and the marbled bichir (P. palmas). Several species are popular in the aquarium trade, where they are valued for their hardiness and prehistoric appearance, though they require large tanks and tight-fitting lids, as they are strong jumpers and can survive short periods out of water.
