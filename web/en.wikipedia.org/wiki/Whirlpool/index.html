Whirlpool

A whirlpool is a body of swirling water produced by the meeting of opposing currents or by water flowing around an obstacle. Whirlpools form in rivers, straits and tidal channels, and in the sea where tides race through narrow passages.

Formation

When a fast current meets a slower one, or flows past a rock or headland, the shear between the flows can curl into a rotating vortex. In tidal straits the effect is strongest at the turn of the tide: water forced through a narrow gap accelerates and, meeting eddies on the far side, spins into a standing vortex. Most whirlpools are small and harmless, but the largest — such as Saltstraumen in Norway, the Moskstraumen off the Lofoten islands, and the Naruto whirlpools in Japan — can reach tens of metres across and are dangerous to small boats.

In culture

Whirlpools have long featured in myth and literature as symbols of inescapable danger, from Charybdis in Homer's Odyssey to the maelstrom in Edgar Allan Poe's "A Descent into the Maelström". In reality, even the largest whirlpools do not suck ships to the bottom; the danger is the turbulence and the powerful currents around them.
