Felony

A felony is traditionally a crime regarded as more serious than a misdemeanor, usually punishable by imprisonment for more than one year or by death. The term originated in English common law, where felonies were originally crimes involving the confiscation of a convicted person's land and goods, to which additional punishments including capital punishment could be added; other crimes were called misdemeanors.

History

Under early common law, felony originally denoted crimes for which the penalty was forfeiture of property and, commonly, death. Over time, the category expanded and the distinctive feature became the severity of punishment rather than forfeiture. Most common law countries have now abolished the felony/misdemeanor distinction in favor of other classifications, such as indictable offences and summary offences, though the distinction remains central in the law of the United States.

United States

In the United States, a felony is a crime punishable by death or imprisonment exceeding one year. Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 3559) classifies felonies into classes A through E by maximum authorized prison term. Conviction of a felony carries collateral consequences beyond the sentence, including loss of voting rights in some states, ineligibility for certain licenses and employment, and deportation for non-citizens. The principle that a felony may be charged for serious conduct was historically linked to the three-strikes laws adopted in several states.

Examples

Offences commonly classified as felonies include murder, rape, burglary, robbery, arson, kidnapping, and aggravated assault.
