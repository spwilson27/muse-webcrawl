Kermack–McKendrick theory

Kermack–McKendrick theory is a mathematical hypothesis developed by W. O. Kermack and A. G. McKendrick in 1927 to model the numbers of people infected with a contagious disease over time. It is the basis of the SIR model and its many compartmental-model descendants in epidemiology.

The SIR model

The model divides a population into three compartments: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered or removed (R). Differential equations describe how individuals move between compartments: infection spreads at a rate proportional to contacts between susceptible and infected individuals, while infected individuals recover at a constant rate.

Threshold theorem

A celebrated result of the theory is the epidemic threshold: an epidemic can only take off if the initial density of susceptible individuals exceeds a critical value related to the ratio of the recovery rate to the infection rate. This underlies the concept of the basic reproduction number R₀ and of herd immunity.
