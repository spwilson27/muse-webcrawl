Survivor guilt

Survivor guilt (or survivor's guilt) is a mental condition in which a person believes they have done something wrong by surviving a traumatic or deadly event when others did not. Survivors may feel irrationally responsible — believing they could have done more to save others, or that they survived undeservedly — and the experience is closely associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression.

History of the concept

The phenomenon was documented systematically after the Second World War among Holocaust survivors; clinicians such as William G. Niederland described a "survivor syndrome" among concentration-camp inmates who lived while family members perished. Similar responses have been recorded among combat veterans, survivors of disasters, accidents, epidemics, and terrorist attacks, as well as among the bereaved of suicide, in communities hit by HIV/AIDS, and, in milder form, among employees who keep their jobs during layoffs.

Symptoms and features

Common features include intrusive thoughts and rumination ("Why me? Why not them?"), a sense of obligation to the dead, self-blame, difficulty experiencing pleasure or success, social withdrawal, sleep disturbance, and in severe cases suicidal ideation. Survivor guilt was listed as a symptom of PTSD in DSM-III; in DSM-5 it falls under distorted cognitions about the cause or consequences of the traumatic event leading to blame of self or others.

Cultural and individual variation

Responses vary with culture and belief: frameworks that attribute outcomes to fate, luck, or divine will can buffer or intensify guilt. Researchers distinguish trait guilt (a general disposition) from state guilt about a specific event. Philosophers and theologians also use "survivor guilt" more loosely for the moral discomfort of benefiting from others' misfortune.

Treatment

Survivor guilt typically responds to trauma-focused psychological therapies, including cognitive behavioural approaches that identify and restructure irrational responsibility beliefs, grief therapy, and peer support groups of fellow survivors. Memorial activities, altruistic work, and telling one's story are often reported as helpful.
