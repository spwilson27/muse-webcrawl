Rotifer

Kingdom: Animalia

Phylum: Rotifera

Species: approx. 2,200 described

Size: typically 0.1–0.5 mm

The rotifers (phylum Rotifera) are microscopic and near-microscopic aquatic animals, mostly between 0.1 and 0.5 mm long. Their name — Latin for "wheel-bearer" — refers to the corona, a crown of cilia at the head whose beating appears to rotate like a wheel and which drives both swimming and filter feeding. About 2,200 species have been described, living in fresh waters worldwide, in moist soils and mosses, and in the sea.

Anatomy

Rotifers have a head, trunk, and usually a foot with adhesive glands for temporary attachment. Their jaws, called trophi, are hard, articulated elements that grind captured algae, bacteria, and detritus. The body is covered by a cuticle that in many forms is stiffened into a shell-like lorica. Rotifers are eutelic — adults have a fixed number of cells (about 1,000) — and possess a simple brain, eyespots, a complete gut, and protonephridia with flame cells for osmoregulation.

Reproduction

Most rotifers reproduce by parthenogenesis: females produce diploid eggs that hatch as females, with sexual reproduction triggered only periodically by environmental cues, producing thick-walled resting eggs that survive drying and freezing. The class Bdelloidea is a biological puzzle — it consists entirely of females and appears to have reproduced without males for tens of millions of years, yet remains diverse, apparently borrowing genes from other organisms through horizontal gene transfer. Bdelloids are also famous for surviving complete desiccation (anhydrobiosis) for years; a bdelloid was revived from Siberian permafrost in 2021 after about 24,000 years.

Ecology and uses

Rotifers are key members of freshwater plankton, grazing on microbes and serving as food for fish larvae. In aquaculture, species of Brachionus are mass-cultured as the first feed for marine fish fry. They are also used as indicators of water quality and in ecotoxicology testing.
