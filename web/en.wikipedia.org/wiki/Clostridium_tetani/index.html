Clostridium tetani

Clostridium tetani is a rod-shaped, Gram-positive, anaerobic bacterium that causes tetanus. Its hardy endospores are found worldwide in soil and in the intestines of animals; when spores enter a wound with low oxygen tension, they germinate and release the potent neurotoxin tetanospasmin.

Characteristics

The vegetative cells are slender rods about 0.5 by 2.5 micrometres, motile by peritrichous flagella. On sporulation the spore forms at the cell's end and swells it, giving the classic "drumstick" or "tennis racket" appearance. Spores resist heat, desiccation, and many disinfectants and can persist in soil for years.

Pathogenesis

C. tetani itself remains localised in necrotic wound tissue, but tetanospasmin travels by retrograde axonal transport to the spinal cord and brainstem, where it cleaves synaptobrevin (VAMP) in inhibitory interneurons. Blocking release of glycine and GABA removes inhibition of motor neurons, producing the rigid paralysis, trismus ("lockjaw"), and spasms of tetanus. The toxin is among the most potent known, with a lethal dose measured in nanograms per kilogram.

History and prevention

The organism was isolated in pure culture by Kitasato Shibasaburō in 1889, working in Robert Koch's laboratory. Tetanus is prevented by the tetanus toxoid vaccine, part of standard childhood immunisation schedules worldwide; neonatal tetanus has been nearly eliminated in most countries through maternal immunisation and hygienic delivery.
