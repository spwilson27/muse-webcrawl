Year zero

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A year zero is a year in a calendar system in which the numbering begins with 0 rather than 1. There is no year zero in the traditional Anno Domini (AD/BC) system: the year 1 BC is followed immediately by AD 1. This is because the system was devised in 525 by the monk Dionysius Exiguus, before the concept of zero was used in European mathematics. A year zero does, however, exist in astronomical year numbering and in the ISO 8601 standard, where year 0 corresponds to 1 BC.

Historical background

Dionysius Exiguus computed the years since the birth of Christ using Roman numerals, which had no zero. The Venerable Bede, who popularised the system, likewise passed directly from 1 BC to AD 1. As a consequence, the turn of a century in this system falls in years ending in 01 (for example, the 21st century began on 1 January 2001, not 2000).

Astronomical year numbering

Astronomers, needing a continuous numbering for calculations, use a system in which year 0 = 1 BC, year −1 = 2 BC, and so on. This convention was introduced by Jacques Cassini in 1740 and is used in ISO 8601.

Other calendars

Some calendars do include a year zero or equivalent: the Buddhist calendar counts from the year of the Buddha's parinirvana treated as year 0 in some reckonings, and the Hindu Kali Yuga era begins at a year sometimes rendered 0. The term "Year Zero" was also used rhetorically by the Khmer Rouge, whose 1975 takeover of Cambodia was proclaimed as the start of a new era — a usage popularised by François Ponchaud's book Cambodia: Year Zero.
