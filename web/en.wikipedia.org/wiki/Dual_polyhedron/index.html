Dual polyhedron

In geometry, polyhedra are associated into pairs called duals, where the vertices of one correspond to the faces of the other and the edges correspond to edges. The dual of a polyhedron P can be constructed by placing a vertex at the centre of each face of P and connecting the vertices of adjacent faces; the dual of the dual is combinatorially the original polyhedron.

Examples

  • The cube and the octahedron are duals of each other.
  • The dodecahedron and the icosahedron are duals.
  • The tetrahedron is self-dual.
  • Each Platonic solid pairs with another (or itself) in this way; Archimedean solids dualise to the Catalan solids, and Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra form dual pairs.

Construction

For convex polyhedra, a concrete construction is polar reciprocation with respect to a sphere: each vertex maps to a plane (the polar plane) and each face to its pole, producing a dual polyhedron whose faces correspond to the original vertices. The canonical choice uses the midsphere, the sphere tangent to all edges, so that the two polyhedra share the same edge arrangement — a fact exploited by the circle packing theorem (Koebe–Andreev–Thurston theorem).

Duality preserves symmetry: a polyhedron and its dual have the same symmetry group. Euler's formula is self-dual, since duality exchanges V and F while leaving E unchanged.
