Community college

A community college is a type of educational institution. The term can have different meanings in different countries: many community colleges have an "open enrollment" policy for students who have graduated from high school, whereas in some places the term denotes a vocational or technical school. In the United States, community colleges are primarily two-year public institutions of tertiary education awarding certificates, diplomas, and associate degrees; many also offer transfer pathways to four-year universities, and a growing number offer bachelor's degrees.

United States

The first community college in the United States is generally considered to be Joliet Junior College in Illinois, founded in 1901. Community colleges expanded greatly after the Second World War, aided by the GI Bill and the 1947 Truman Commission report, which envisioned a nationwide network of affordable public colleges. Today there are roughly a thousand community colleges in the United States serving millions of students. They typically have open admissions, lower tuition than four-year institutions, and serve many part-time, working, and first-generation students.

Functions

Community colleges typically combine several missions: transfer education (the first two years of a bachelor's degree), career and technical education, workforce development, remedial or developmental education, and continuing and community education.
