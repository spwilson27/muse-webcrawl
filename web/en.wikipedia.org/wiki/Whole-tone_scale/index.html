Whole-tone scale

In music, a whole-tone scale is a scale built entirely of whole tones (whole steps). Because every interval is identical, the scale has only two distinct transpositions — the pitches C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯ and D♭–E♭–F–G–A–B together exhaust all twelve chromatic notes — and it offers no leading tone or perfect fifth to establish a tonic, giving music written in it a floating, unresolved quality.

History and use

Early examples appear in Glinka's opera Ruslan and Lyudmila (1842), where the scale accompanies the dwarf Chernomor, and in works by Liszt and Rimsky-Korsakov. It became a hallmark of musical impressionism: Claude Debussy used it extensively, most famously in "Voiles" from the first book of Préludes, to evoke veiled, dreamlike stillness. Alban Berg, Béla Bartók, and Olivier Messiaen (who catalogued it as his first "mode of limited transposition") also exploited it, and it appears in jazz, in Stevie Wonder's "You Are the Sunshine of My Life" introduction, and in cartoon music to suggest dreaming or drunkenness.

Properties

The whole-tone scale is hexatonic (six notes per octave) and symmetrical. Chords built from it are augmented triads and French augmented-sixth sonorities; dominant seventh chords with sharp fifths fit it well, which is why jazz improvisers use the scale over altered dominant chords.
