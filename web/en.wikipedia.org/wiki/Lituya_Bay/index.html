Lituya Bay

Lituya Bay (Tlingit: Ltu.aa) is a fjord on the coast of the southeastern part of the U.S. state of Alaska, about 14.5 km (9 mi) long and up to 3.2 km (2 mi) wide, located at 58°41′N 137°06′W in the Alaska Panhandle, within Glacier Bay National Park. The bay is noted for its exceptionally high tides and for the megatsunami of 1958, the tallest wave ever documented.

Geography

The bay is a T-shaped inlet of the Gulf of Alaska, carved by glaciers and fed at its head by the Lituya and Crillon glaciers. A shallow sill at its entrance — once the terminal moraine of a glacier — restricts tidal exchange and makes navigation hazardous; tidal currents through the entrance can be fierce. The Fairweather Fault, a major strike-slip fault, runs along the foot of the Fairweather Range at the head of the bay.

History

The bay was charted in 1786 by the French explorer Jean-François de La Pérouse, who named it Port des Français and lost 21 men in the tidal currents at its entrance; Cenotaph Island in the bay commemorates them. For the Tlingit people the bay was a seasonal settlement and trading place, and Tlingit oral traditions tell of destructive great waves in the bay long before written records.

The 1958 megatsunami

On 9 July 1958, an earthquake of magnitude about 7.8 on the Fairweather Fault triggered a massive rockslide at the head of the bay: roughly 30 million cubic metres of rock fell into Gilbert Inlet. The impact generated a megatsunami whose run-up reached 524 metres (1,719 ft) on the opposite slope — the highest wave run-up ever recorded — stripping trees and soil down to bedrock. The wave swept through the bay, sinking two fishing boats and killing two people; a third boat rode the wave and survived, its crew providing eyewitness accounts. Geologist Don Miller's investigation of trimlines around the bay showed that similar giant waves had occurred in 1853/54, 1899, and 1936, making Lituya Bay the type locality for landslide-generated megatsunamis.
