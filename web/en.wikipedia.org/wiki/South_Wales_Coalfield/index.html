South Wales Coalfield

The South Wales Coalfield is a large region of coal deposits extending across South Wales from Pembrokeshire in the west to Monmouthshire in the east, underlying the valleys of the Rhondda, Cynon, Taff, and other rivers. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries it was one of the world's most important coal-producing regions, famous for high-quality steam coal exported worldwide through ports such as Cardiff, Swansea, and Newport.

History

Coal mining expanded explosively from the mid-19th century; by 1913 output peaked at about 57 million tons and the industry employed over 230,000 men. The coalfield's valleys developed dense terraced mining communities with a strong tradition of trade unionism, chapels, and brass bands. The industry also suffered catastrophic disasters, including Senghenydd (1913, 439 deaths) and Aberfan (1966, 144 deaths, mostly children, when a colliery spoil tip slid onto a school).

Decline

Output declined through the 20th century as markets shrank and seams were exhausted, culminating in the bitter 1984–85 miners' strike and a rapid programme of pit closures. Deep mining ended with the closure of Tower Colliery in 2008, though the valleys' landscape, culture, and politics remain shaped by coal.
