Pure mathematics


The Mandelbrot set, studied for its intrinsic mathematical interest.

Pure mathematics is the study of mathematical concepts independently of any application outside mathematics. These concepts may originate in real-world concerns, and the results obtained may later turn out to be useful for practical applications, but pure mathematicians are not primarily motivated by such applications. Instead, the appeal is attributed to the intellectual challenge and aesthetic beauty of working out the logical consequences of basic principles.

History

The distinction between pure and applied mathematics emerged in the 19th century, as university chairs in "pure" mathematics were established, notably at Cambridge. Ancient Greek mathematicians already distinguished arithmetic (theory of numbers) from logistike (calculation). In the 20th century, the Bourbaki group championed a structural, axiomatic approach, and G. H. Hardy's A Mathematician's Apology (1940) became the classic defence of mathematics pursued for its own sake; Hardy famously boasted that number theory was useless—although it later became essential to cryptography.

Areas

Major areas of pure mathematics include:

  • Algebra – groups, rings, fields, and linear algebra
  • Analysis – real and complex analysis, measure theory, functional analysis
  • Geometry and topology – differential geometry, algebraic geometry, algebraic topology
  • Number theory – properties of integers and related structures
  • Logic and foundations – set theory, model theory, proof theory
  • Combinatorics and discrete mathematics

Relation to applied mathematics

The boundary between pure and applied mathematics is fluid: results developed abstractly—such as the Radon transform, non-Euclidean geometry, and number theory—later found applications in medical imaging, general relativity, and public-key cryptography. Mathematicians often distinguish instead between the motivation of the researcher and the logical methods employed, rather than a strict subject division.
