Dressing (medical)


Application of a sterile wound dressing.

A dressing is a sterile pad or compress applied directly to a wound to promote healing and protect the injured tissue from further harm. A bandage, by contrast, is used to hold a dressing in place, to apply pressure, or to support an injured limb.

Purposes

The main purposes of a wound dressing are to stem bleeding, absorb exudate, ease pain, provide a barrier against infection and contamination, debride dead tissue, and maintain the moist environment now known to accelerate re-epithelialisation. Dressings are also used as vehicles for topical medication.

Types

Traditional dressings include gauze swabs, lint and Gamgee tissue. Modern dressings, developed from the mid-twentieth century onward, form a large family of specialised products:

  • Film dressings — thin, transparent, adhesive polyurethane sheets that permit inspection and gas exchange.
  • Hydrocolloids — wafers forming a gel on contact with exudate, used for lightly to moderately exuding wounds.
  • Hydrogels — water-rich gels that donate moisture to dry, sloughy wounds.
  • Foam dressings — absorbent pads for heavy exudate.
  • Alginates — seaweed-derived fibres that gel in the wound bed and aid haemostasis.
  • Antimicrobial dressings — incorporating silver, iodine or honey.
  • Negative-pressure dressings — sealed systems applying controlled suction in vacuum-assisted closure therapy.

History

Wound coverings of linen, lint scraped from cloth, and plasters spread with oils, honey or wine are described from ancient Egypt and Greece onward. Joseph Lister's antiseptic carbolic dressings of the 1860s and the mass-produced sterile gauze of the First World War transformed practice. George Winter's 1962 research on moist wound healing overturned the older doctrine that wounds should be kept dry and led directly to the modern dressing industry.
