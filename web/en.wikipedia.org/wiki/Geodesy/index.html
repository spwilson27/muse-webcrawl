Overview

Geodesy is the science of accurately measuring and understanding Earth's geometric shape, its orientation in space, and its gravity field, and how all three change with time. It is the foundation beneath every map, coordinate system and navigation fix: before a position can be quoted in latitude and longitude, geodesists must say what those angles are measured on.

That surface is an ellipsoid chosen to fit the planet, refined against the geoid — the surface the oceans would take under gravity alone, which mean sea level approximates. The difference between the two, geoid height, varies by about a hundred metres around the globe.

From triangulation to satellites

Classical geodesy was done from hilltops: chains of triangles measured with theodolites, anchored by baselines and astronomical latitude observations, built the great national surveys of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The French geodesic missions of the 1730s, to Lapland and to Peru, settled the question of Earth's figure in Newton's favour: the planet is flattened at the poles.

Modern geodesy is done from space — satellite laser ranging, VLBI, GNSS and gravity missions measure the figure, the rotation and even the creeping motion of the continents, and the same observations track sea-level change and the shrinking of ice sheets.
