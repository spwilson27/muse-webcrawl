The Nazi-Soviet population transfers were a series of forced and organised movements of ethnic populations in Eastern Europe carried out in 1939–1941 under agreements between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, following the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact. The transfers formed part of Adolf Hitler's Heim ins Reich ("Home into the Reich") programme, under which ethnic Germans (Volksdeutsche) living in territories assigned to the Soviet sphere were "repatriated" to German-controlled territory, while ethnic Ukrainians, Belarusians and Russians in German-occupied areas could be transferred eastward.

Background

The secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of 23 August 1939 divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence. The German–Soviet Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Demarcation of 28 September 1939, and subsequent resettlement agreements, provided the legal framework for mutual population exchanges. Hitler announced the resettlement policy in his Reichstag speech of 6 October 1939, proclaiming a "new order of ethnographic conditions" in Europe.

The transfers

Roughly 855,000 ethnic Germans were resettled between 1939 and 1944, the majority under the Nazi-Soviet agreements of 1939–1941. The largest groups came from the Baltic states (about 68,000 Baltic Germans from Latvia and Estonia in 1939, with a further smaller wave in 1941), from Soviet-occupied eastern Poland (Volhynia, Galicia and the Narew region, about 128,000 people in late 1939–early 1940), and from Bessarabia, Bukovina and Dobruja after the Soviet annexations of 1940 (over 200,000 people). Resettlers were processed by the Volksdeutsche Mittelstelle (VoMi) and racially screened by the SS; many were settled in the annexed Polish territories of the Warthegau and Danzig-West Prussia, on farms and in homes from which Poles and Jews had been expelled.

In the opposite direction, approximately 45,000–55,000 ethnic Ukrainians, Belarusians and Russians were transferred from German-occupied Poland to the Soviet Union, though many sought to avoid transfer. Meanwhile, the Soviet authorities deported hundreds of thousands of Polish citizens and others from the annexed territories to Siberia and Kazakhstan.

End and legacy

The transfers ended with Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941. The Nazi-Soviet population exchanges are regarded as a prelude to the far larger forced migrations of the later war years and the postwar expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe, and as an early example of the ethnic re-ordering of Europe pursued by both totalitarian regimes.
