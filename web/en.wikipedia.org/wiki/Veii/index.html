Veii (also Veius; Italian: Veio) was an important ancient Etruscan city situated on the southern limits of Etruria, about 16 kilometres (9.9 mi) north-west of Rome. It was one of the wealthiest and most powerful of the twelve cities of the Etruscan League, and for centuries Rome's closest and most persistent rival, until its defeat and destruction by the Roman general Marcus Furius Camillus in 396 BC after a legendary ten-year siege.

History

The plateau of Veii was occupied from the Iron Age, developing from Villanovan villages into a major urban centre controlling a large territory between the Tiber and the coast. Veii's salt works at the river's mouth and its control of Tiber crossings brought it into repeated conflict with Rome from the regal period onwards. Roman tradition recounts a long series of wars, including the disaster of the Cremera in 477 BC, when nearly the entire Fabian clan was wiped out.

The final war began around 406 BC. According to Livy, the Romans besieged Veii for ten years, finally taking the city when soldiers tunnelled into it from a mine dug beneath its walls while Camillus drew the Veientines' attention with a diversionary attack. The population was sold into slavery and the city largely abandoned, its territory annexed as Roman ager Veientanus.

Art and archaeology

Veii is famous for its terracotta sculpture, above all the life-size Apollo of Veii (c. 510–490 BC), attributed to the workshop of Vulca, the only Etruscan artist known by name from literary sources. The sanctuary at Portonaccio, just outside the city walls, has yielded many of these masterpieces. Excavations have also revealed temples, cuniculi (drainage tunnels), necropoleis with painted and chamber tombs, and parts of the city's circuit walls. Today much of the plateau lies within the Parco di Veio, a protected regional park.
