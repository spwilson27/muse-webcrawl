Molasse

Molasse are sedimentary rocks and sediments formed in foreland basins in front of rising mountain ranges, composed chiefly of sandstones, shales and conglomerates eroded from the adjacent orogen. The term comes from French molasse, a soft sandstone used as building stone in Switzerland, ultimately from Latin mollis, "soft". Molasse is contrasted with flysch, deposited earlier in deep water during the main phase of mountain building.

Formation

As a mountain belt rises by thrusting, its weight flexes the neighbouring crust down into a foreland basin. Rivers draining the new mountains dump enormous volumes of sediment into this basin. Early, deep-marine turbidites (flysch) give way, as the basin shallows and fills, to continental molasse: alluvial fans, river plains and lake deposits, often including coal seams and fossil-rich beds. Because deposition continues while thrusting proceeds, molasse sequences are frequently folded into the front of the range.

Examples

The classic example is the Molasse Basin north of the Alps, stretching from France through Switzerland and Bavaria into Austria, divided locally into Lower Marine, Lower Freshwater, Upper Marine and Upper Freshwater Molasse of Oligocene–Miocene age. Equivalent deposits ring the Himalayas (the Siwaliks), the Andes, the Zagros and most other young mountain belts. The Swiss molasse conglomerates, called Nagelfluh, form the characteristic hills of the Alpine foothills.
