The Mexican–American War (1846–1848) was an armed conflict between the United States and Mexico, fought following the US annexation of Texas in 1845 and a dispute over whether the Texas boundary lay at the Nueces River or the Rio Grande. It was the first US war fought largely on foreign soil and staffed mainly by regulars.

After the Thornton Affair in April 1846, in which Mexican troops attacked a US patrol in the disputed territory, President James K. Polk declared that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil," and Congress declared war on May 13, 1846.

Course of the war

In the north, General Zachary Taylor won battles at Palo Alto, Resaca de la Palma, Monterrey, and Buena Vista. In the west, Stephen W. Kearny occupied New Mexico and moved into California, where US forces under John C. Frémont and Commodore Robert Stockton completed the conquest of California. In 1847 General Winfield Scott landed at Veracruz and marched inland, defeating Santa Anna at Cerro Gordo and winning the battles of Contreras, Churubusco, Molino del Rey, and Chapultepec before occupying Mexico City in September 1847.

Treaty and consequences

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed February 2, 1848, established the Rio Grande as the Texas boundary and ceded to the United States the territories of the Mexican Cession — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming — in exchange for $15 million and the assumption of claims. Mexico lost about 55% of its pre-war territory.

The war intensified the national debate over the extension of slavery into the new territories, contributing to the sectional crisis that led to the American Civil War. In Mexico the conflict is remembered as the Intervención estadounidense en México and remains a defining national trauma.
