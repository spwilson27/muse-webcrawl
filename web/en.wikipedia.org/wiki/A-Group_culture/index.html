A-Group culture

The A-Group culture was an ancient Nubian culture that flourished in Lower Nubia, between the First and Second Cataracts of the Nile, from approximately 3800 to 3100 BCE, contemporary with the late Predynastic period and the First Dynasty of Egypt.

Characteristics

The A-Group was first defined by archaeologist George Reisner during excavations in the early twentieth century. Its people were semi-nomadic pastoralists and early agriculturalists who lived in small settlements along the Nile, kept cattle, sheep and goats, and traded with Egypt for goods such as copper tools, stone vessels, and linen.

Burials

A-Group cemeteries, such as those at Qustul and Sayala, contain some of the richest early Nubian burials. The famous Qustul incense burner, decorated with imagery of a pharaoh-like figure wearing the White Crown, prompted debate about whether kingship may have originated in Nubia before Egypt — a claim most scholars now reject, interpreting the object as an Egyptian import or imitation.

Decline

The culture declined as Egypt's First Dynasty consolidated power and campaigned into Lower Nubia; by about 3100 BCE the region was largely depopulated, a state that continued until the emergence of the B-Group and C-Group cultures centuries later. Many A-Group sites were flooded by Lake Nasser following the construction of the Aswan High Dam.
