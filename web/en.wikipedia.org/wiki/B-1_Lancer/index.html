The Rockwell B-1 Lancer is a supersonic variable-sweep wing, heavy bomber used by the United States Air Force. It is commonly called the "Bone" (from "B-One"). It is one of three strategic bombers serving in the U.S. Air Force fleet, along with the B-2 Spirit and the B-52 Stratofortress, as of 2024.

Development

The B-1 was first envisioned in the 1960s as a platform that would combine the Mach 2 speed of the B-58 Hustler with the range and payload of the B-52. The original B-1A prototypes achieved speeds of Mach 2.2 at high altitude but the program was cancelled in 1977 before production. The redesigned B-1B was revived in 1981 under President Ronald Reagan, emphasizing low-level penetration at high subsonic speed, with a reduced radar cross-section and a maximum speed of about Mach 1.25.

Design

The aircraft features variable-geometry wings that sweep from 15° for takeoff and low-speed flight to 67.5° for high-speed dash. Four General Electric F101 turbofan engines power the B-1B. It has the largest internal payload of any U.S. bomber, carrying up to 75,000 pounds (34,000 kg) of ordnance in three internal bomb bays, plus external hardpoints.

Operational history

The B-1B entered service in 1986 as a nuclear bomber but was converted to a conventional-only role by the mid-1990s under arms-control agreements. It saw its first combat in Operation Desert Fox in 1998 and went on to fly extensive missions over Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, and Syria, frequently providing close air support with precision-guided munitions. The Air Force plans to retire the type as the B-21 Raider enters service.

Specifications (B-1B)

Crew: 4. Length: 146 ft (44.5 m). Wingspan: 137 ft (41.8 m) unswept, 79 ft (24.1 m) swept. Maximum speed: Mach 1.25. Range: about 5,900 nmi (10,900 km). Service ceiling: 60,000 ft (18,000 m).
