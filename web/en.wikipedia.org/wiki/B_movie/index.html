A B movie is a low-budget commercial motion picture. In its original usage during the Golden Age of Hollywood, the term more precisely identified films intended for distribution as the less-publicised bottom half of a double feature, analogous to B-sides in recorded music. Although the US production of films intended as second features largely ceased by the end of the 1950s, the term has survived with broader meanings, often connoting sensational genre subjects, modest production values, and — for affectionate fans — a campy or unpretentious charm.

History

The double feature became standard American exhibition practice during the Great Depression, and the major studios maintained "B units" while independent "Poverty Row" studios such as Monogram Pictures, Republic Pictures and Producers Releasing Corporation specialised in cheap westerns, serials, crime pictures and horror films, often shot in a week or less. The 1948 Paramount Decrees and the rise of television ended the classic B system, but producers such as Roger Corman at American International Pictures reinvented the B movie for the drive-in and youth market in the 1950s–1970s, launching the careers of directors including Francis Ford Coppola, Martin Scorsese and James Cameron. Some B pictures transcended their origins: Val Lewton's RKO horror films of the 1940s and Corman's Edgar Allan Poe cycle are now critically esteemed.

Modern usage

Today "B movie" is applied loosely to low-budget genre films, direct-to-video releases and deliberately schlocky productions (for example, the mockbusters of The Asylum and Syfy channel monster movies), as well as, by extension, to "Z movies" of even lower quality. Film scholars distinguish the historical B movie — a product of a specific industrial system — from its colloquial use as a synonym for a "bad" or cult film.
