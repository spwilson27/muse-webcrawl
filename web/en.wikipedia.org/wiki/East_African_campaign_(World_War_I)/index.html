East African campaign (World War I)

The East African campaign in World War I was a series of battles and guerrilla actions, which started in German East Africa (GEA) and spread to portions of Mozambique, Rhodesia, British East Africa, the Uganda Protectorate, and the Belgian Congo. The campaign all but ended in German East Africa in November 1917 when the Germans entered Mozambique and continued the campaign living off Portuguese supplies.


German Schutztruppe askari during the campaign

The German commander, Lieutenant Colonel (later Major General) Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck, aimed to tie down as many British resources as possible: his force, never larger than about 14,000 men (mostly African askari), successfully diverted troops, materiel and shipping that might otherwise have gone to the Western Front. The British pursued the Germans under several commanders, including Jan Smuts in 1916, but never surrounded or destroyed the mobile German column.

Course of the campaign

Early engagements included the Battle of Tanga (November 1914), a humiliating British defeat sometimes called the "Battle of the Bees", and the Battle of Jassin (1915). On Lake Tanganyika, the British won control in 1916 using the motor boats Mimi and Toutou against the German Graf von Götzen. Smuts's 1916 offensive captured the main towns and railways of GEA, yet Lettow-Vorbeck withdrew south, fighting at Mahiwa (October 1917) — one of the costliest battles of the campaign for both sides — before crossing into Portuguese Mozambique, defeating a Portuguese force at Ngomano in November 1917.

End and cost

Lettow-Vorbeck re-entered German East Africa in 1918, marched into Northern Rhodesia, and only surrendered at Abercorn on 25 November 1918, two weeks after the Armistice in Europe, having received confirmation of Germany's defeat. He returned to Germany a hero. The campaign's cost fell heaviest on African civilians and porters: hundreds of thousands of carriers served, and mortality among porters and the wider population — from disease, requisitioning, and famine — was enormous.
