The primitive equations are a set of nonlinear partial differential equations that describe the motion of the atmosphere (and, in adapted form, the ocean) and form the dynamical core of most numerical weather prediction and climate models. They consist of the momentum equations (Newton's second law applied to air parcels), the continuity equation (conservation of mass), the thermodynamic energy equation, the equation of state (ideal gas law) and, commonly, a moisture equation.

Assumptions

The equations are called "primitive" because they retain the fundamental conservation laws rather than filtered approximations, though they usually incorporate the hydrostatic approximation — vertical acceleration is neglected and pressure balances gravity — and the traditional approximation for the Coriolis force. Hydrostatic models cannot resolve non-hydrostatic phenomena such as individual thunderstorms; high-resolution models increasingly solve the fully non-hydrostatic equations instead.

History

Vilhelm Bjerknes proposed weather prediction as an initial-value problem of physics in 1904, and Lewis Fry Richardson attempted the first numerical forecast by hand calculation in 1922, using the primitive equations; his result failed largely because unfiltered gravity waves produced spurious pressure tendencies. The first successful computer forecasts in 1950 used simplified (barotropic) equations, but by the 1960s operational centres had returned to primitive-equation models, following the work of Norman Phillips, whose 1956 general-circulation experiment founded modern climate modelling.

Use

Because the equations admit fast gravity waves, numerical schemes must use semi-implicit time stepping or short time steps. Together with parameterisations of radiation, convection, clouds and boundary-layer processes, the primitive equations remain the basis of models such as those run at the ECMWF and other weather centres, and of coupled ocean–atmosphere climate models.
