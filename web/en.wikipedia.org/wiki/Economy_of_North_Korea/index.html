Economy of North Korea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pyongyang, the capital and largest city of North Korea

The economy of North Korea is a centrally planned economy in which the state directs production, prices and distribution, guided by the ruling philosophy of Juche (self-reliance) and, since the 1990s, a songun ("military-first") allocation of resources. North Korea is one of the world's poorest countries, with per-capita income estimated by the Bank of Korea at roughly one twenty-eighth that of South Korea. Official statistics are sparse, so most figures are external estimates.

History

After partition, North Korea industrialised rapidly with Soviet and Chinese assistance, initially outpacing the South. Growth stalled in the 1970s–80s as debt, inefficiency and defence burdens mounted. The collapse of the USSR ended subsidised trade: energy and food shortages cascaded into the famine of the mid-1990s (the "Arduous March"), in which hundreds of thousands died and the public distribution system largely broke down. Spontaneous markets (jangmadang) filled the gap and now supply much of the population's food and consumer goods, tolerated but periodically restricted by the state.

Sectors and sanctions

Industry concentrates on mining (coal, iron ore, magnesite), metallurgy, chemicals, textiles and military production; agriculture, constrained by terrain and inputs, chronically under-produces food. Foreign trade is dominated by China. Successive UN Security Council sanctions over North Korea's nuclear and missile programmes — tightened sharply in 2016–17 to cap oil imports and ban coal, textile and seafood exports — have constricted formal trade, while illicit revenue is reported from overseas labour, arms sales and state-directed cybercrime. Special economic zones such as Rason and the formerly joint Kaesong Industrial Complex (closed 2016) have had limited success.
