Ladybird

Ladybirds (family Coccinellidae) are small beetles, commonly known in North America as ladybugs and in Britain and Ireland as ladybirds. They range in size from about 0.8 to 18 mm. The family includes more than 6,000 described species worldwide. Many species have conspicuous aposematic coloration — typically red, orange, or yellow with black spots, or the reverse — warning predators of their unpleasant taste. Most coccinellids are beneficial predators of aphids and other plant-eating insect pests, and they are widely welcomed by gardeners.

Description

Ladybirds have round to oval, dome-shaped bodies with short legs and antennae. The elytra (hardened forewings) protect the flight wings folded beneath. Coloration varies considerably even within a species; the harlequin ladybird (Harmonia axyridis) occurs in dozens of colour forms. When threatened, ladybirds can exude alkaloid-containing haemolymph from their leg joints — a defensive "reflex bleeding" that tastes foul to predators.

Biology and diet

Most species are predatory both as larvae and adults, feeding chiefly on aphids, scale insects, thrips, and other hemipterans. A single ladybird may eat thousands of aphids in its lifetime. Some subfamilies are exceptions: the Epilachninae are herbivores and can be agricultural pests, while others feed on mildew fungi. Ladybirds undergo complete metamorphosis; the spiny, elongate larvae look quite unlike the adults. Many species overwinter as adults, sometimes aggregating in large numbers in sheltered places, including houses.

Distribution and humans

Coccinellids occur on every continent except Antarctica. Several species have been deliberately introduced to new regions as biological control agents; the vedalia beetle's control of cottony cushion scale in California citrus groves in 1888–89 is the classic founding case of biological control. The introduced harlequin ladybird has become invasive in Europe and North America, where it is implicated in declines of native ladybirds. In folklore, ladybirds are symbols of good luck, and the English name derives from "Our Lady" — the Virgin Mary, who was depicted in a red cloak in early paintings.
