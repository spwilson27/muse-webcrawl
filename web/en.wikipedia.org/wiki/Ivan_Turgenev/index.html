Ivan Turgenev

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Ivan Turgenev in the 1870s: the tall, white-bearded novelist in old age]

Ivan Sergeyevich Turgenev (9 November 1818 – 3 September 1883) was a Russian novelist, short-story writer, playwright and poet, the first Russian writer to win a major reputation in the West. His Fathers and Sons (1862) gave the language the figure of the nihilist Bazarov and its enduring theme of generational conflict; his A Sportsman's Sketches (1852) are credited with helping turn Russian opinion against serfdom.

Turgenev spent much of his life in France and Germany, attached to the singer Pauline Viardot, and became the conduit between Russian and European letters — friend to Flaubert, George Sand, Henry James and the Goncourts, and an early champion abroad of Tolstoy and Dostoevsky, with both of whom he quarrelled memorably.

Works

After the Sketches came the novels of the superfluous gentry and the new people: Rudin (1856), Home of the Gentry (1859), On the Eve (1860), Fathers and Sons (1862), Smoke (1867) and Virgin Soil (1877), besides novellas like First Love (1860) and the late, mystical Poems in Prose. His prose — limpid, musical, Flaubertian in its care — made him the stylist's novelist.

Position in Russian letters

Turgenev's westernising liberalism put him between Slavophiles and radicals, both of whom attacked Fathers and Sons on publication — Bazarov was claimed and disowned by every camp. He died at Bougival near Paris in 1883; his brain, famously weighed at autopsy, was among the largest recorded, a fact Russians cite with straight-faced pride.
