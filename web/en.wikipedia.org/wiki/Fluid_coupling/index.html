Fluid coupling

A fluid coupling or hydraulic coupling is a hydrodynamic device used to transmit rotating mechanical power. It was developed by the German engineer Hermann Föttinger in the early 20th century, originally for marine propulsion. It consists of two bladed rotors — a pump (impeller) driven by the input shaft and a turbine driven by the output shaft — enclosed in a casing filled with oil, with no direct mechanical contact between them.

Principle of operation

As the pump rotates, it flings oil outward and across the gap into the turbine, transferring angular momentum. The output shaft therefore always turns slightly slower than the input ("slip"), and torque is transmitted smoothly without shock loads. When the load stalls, the coupling slips instead of transmitting destructive torque, protecting the driving machine.

Applications

Fluid couplings are used in industrial drives for conveyors, crushers, fans, and pumps, and in diesel locomotives and some automotive applications. The related torque converter adds a stator between pump and turbine, multiplying torque at low speeds, and is the basis of most automatic transmissions. Variable-speed ("scoop tube") couplings regulate the oil level to control output speed.

Advantages and drawbacks

Advantages include smooth starting, overload protection, torsional-vibration damping, and the absence of wearing contact parts. The main drawback is energy loss as heat due to slip, typically a few percent at rated load.
