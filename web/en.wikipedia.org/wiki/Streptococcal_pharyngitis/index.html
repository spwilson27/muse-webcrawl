Streptococcal pharyngitis, commonly known as strep throat, is an infection of the back of the throat including the tonsils caused by group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes). Common symptoms include fever, sore throat, red tonsils, and enlarged lymph nodes in the front of the neck. A headache and nausea or vomiting may also occur. Some develop a sandpaper-like rash known as scarlet fever. Symptoms typically begin one to three days after exposure and last seven to ten days.

Cause and transmission

Strep throat is spread by respiratory droplets from an infected person, by touching something with droplets on it and then the mouth or nose, or through shared food and drink. It is most common in children 5 to 15 years old, and accounts for roughly 15–30% of sore throats in children and 5–20% in adults; most sore throats are viral instead.

Diagnosis

Clinical scoring systems such as the Centor criteria (fever, tonsillar exudates, tender anterior cervical nodes, absence of cough) estimate the likelihood of strep. Diagnosis is confirmed by a rapid antigen detection test or throat culture from a throat swab; culture is the reference standard, especially in children when the rapid test is negative.

Treatment

Antibiotics — penicillin or amoxicillin first-line, with alternatives such as cephalexin or macrolides for penicillin-allergic patients — shorten symptoms modestly, reduce contagiousness, and importantly prevent complications. Treatment within nine days of symptom onset prevents acute rheumatic fever. Pain and fever are managed with acetaminophen or ibuprofen.

Complications

Suppurative complications include peritonsillar abscess, otitis media, and sinusitis. Non-suppurative immune complications include acute rheumatic fever (which can damage heart valves, leading to rheumatic heart disease) and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. Rheumatic fever remains a major cause of acquired heart disease in low-income countries.
