The term big cat is used informally to refer to the largest wild members of the cat family (Felidae), especially the five species of the genus Panthera: the tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard, and snow leopard. The four classic "roaring cats" (tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard) possess a partially ossified hyoid bone permitting them to roar but not to purr continuously; the snow leopard, added to Panthera on genetic evidence, cannot roar. The term is also frequently extended to the cheetah, cougar (puma), and clouded leopards, and occasionally to the Eurasian lynx.

Characteristics

Big cats are apex predators in their ecosystems, with muscular bodies, powerful jaws, retractable claws (except the cheetah), and keen senses adapted for stalking and ambush. Sizes range from the snow leopard at roughly 22–55 kg to the Siberian tiger, the largest living felid, whose males can exceed 300 kg. Most are solitary (the lion, uniquely, lives in social prides) and most are primarily nocturnal or crepuscular hunters.

Conservation

All big cats face threats from habitat loss, poaching for skins and body parts, depletion of prey, and conflict with humans over livestock. The tiger, lion, leopard, jaguar, snow leopard, and cheetah are all listed as Vulnerable or Endangered on the IUCN Red List; several subspecies, such as the Javan and Bali tigers, became extinct in the twentieth century. Trade is restricted under CITES, and numerous reserves and anti-poaching programmes aim to protect remaining populations. Big cats have enormous cultural significance, appearing in heraldry, mythology, national symbolism, and popular media.
