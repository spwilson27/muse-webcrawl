Fatigue (material)

In materials science, fatigue is the initiation and propagation of cracks in a material due to cyclic loading. Once a fatigue crack has initiated, it grows a small amount with each loading cycle, typically producing striations on some parts of the fracture surface. The crack will continue to grow until it reaches a critical size, when the remaining uncracked cross-section can no longer support the load and sudden fracture occurs.

Stages of fatigue

Fatigue failure progresses through three stages: crack initiation, where a small crack forms at a stress concentration such as a notch, inclusion, or surface defect; crack propagation, in which the crack advances incrementally each cycle (described for many alloys by Paris's law); and final fracture, when the remaining material fails suddenly.

S–N curves and endurance limit

Fatigue life is commonly characterised with an S–N curve (Wöhler curve), plotting cyclic stress amplitude against the number of cycles to failure. Some materials, notably many steels, exhibit an endurance limit below which fatigue failure does not occur; most non-ferrous alloys do not and eventually fail at any non-zero stress amplitude.

Historical significance

Fatigue caused several famous engineering disasters, including the Versailles rail accident of 1842 and the de Havilland Comet airliner crashes of 1954, which drove modern fatigue design practice.
