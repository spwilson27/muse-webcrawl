The London County Council (LCC) was the principal local government body for the County of London throughout its existence from 1889 until 1965, when it was replaced by the Greater London Council. Created by the Local Government Act 1888, it took over many functions of the Metropolitan Board of Works and was the first directly elected authority covering the whole of the London conurbation's inner area.

History

The Progressive Party, allied with the Liberals, dominated the council's first decades, pursuing municipal enterprise in housing, education, parks and transport. The LCC built pioneering cottage estates and large blocks of flats, ran London's schools from 1904 (after the abolition of the London School Board), operated the fire brigade, ambulances and (from 1933, through London Transport) handed over its tramways. After the First World War it developed vast "out-county" estates such as Becontree in Essex and Downham in Kent. Control alternated between the Municipal Reform Party (Conservative-backed) and Labour, who governed from 1934 under Herbert Morrison and built extensive housing and the Waterloo Bridge replacement.

County Hall

From 1922 the council's headquarters was County Hall on the South Bank of the Thames, designed by Ralph Knott, directly opposite the Palace of Westminster.

Abolition

The London Government Act 1963 abolished the LCC and the County of London on 1 April 1965, replacing them with the Greater London Council and 32 London boroughs, in recognition that the built-up area had long outgrown the old county boundary.

Leaders

Notable leaders of the council included Lord Rosebery (first chairman), Herbert Morrison and Isaac Hayward.
