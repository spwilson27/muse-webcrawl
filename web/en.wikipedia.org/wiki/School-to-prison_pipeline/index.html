School-to-prison pipeline

Critics argue harsh school discipline funnels students toward the justice system

The school-to-prison pipeline is a concept describing the disproportionate tendency of minors and young adults from disadvantaged backgrounds to become incarcerated because of increasingly harsh school and municipal policies. Advocacy groups such as the ACLU and the Advancement Project popularised the term, arguing that practices that push students out of school — suspensions, expulsions, arrests, and referrals to law enforcement — raise their likelihood of later contact with the juvenile and criminal justice systems.

Contributing practices

  • Zero-tolerance policies — mandatory punishments for specified offences, expanded in the 1990s from weapons and drugs to a wide range of behaviours
  • School resource officers — police stationed in schools, whose presence correlates in some studies with increased arrests for minor misconduct
  • Out-of-school suspensions and expulsions, which remove instructional time and supervision
  • Underfunded schools with few counsellors, special education shortfalls, and high teacher turnover

Disparities

Data from the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection consistently show that Black students, students with disabilities, and (in many datasets) Native American and Latino students are suspended and expelled at rates well above their share of enrolment, and are more likely to be referred to law enforcement or arrested at school.

Responses and reform

Reform proposals include restorative justice practices, Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS), limits on suspensions for minor offences, increased counselling staff, and "memoranda of understanding" restricting police involvement in routine discipline. Several large districts, including Los Angeles and New York City, have banned suspensions for "willful defiance" and reported subsequent declines in suspension rates. Critics of the pipeline framing counter that suspensions protect learning environments and that causation between school discipline and later incarceration is complex; the debate continues in education policy research.
