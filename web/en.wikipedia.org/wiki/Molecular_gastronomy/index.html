Molecular gastronomy is a subdiscipline of food science that seeks to investigate the physical and chemical transformations of ingredients that occur in cooking. Its program includes three areas, as cooking was recognized to have three components: social, artistic and technical. Molecular cooking is one of the applications of a science-based approach to cooking, and the movement has become associated with avant-garde chefs who explored the scientific possibilities of traditional cooking.

History

The term "molecular gastronomy" was coined in 1988 by Hungarian physicist Nicholas Kurti and French physical chemist Hervé This. Kurti, who famously remarked that "we can measure the temperature in the atmosphere of Venus, but we do not know what is going on inside our soufflés," had organized workshops on the physics and chemistry of cooking from 1969 onward. In 1992 the pair organized the International Workshop on Molecular and Physical Gastronomy in Erice, Sicily, which brought scientists and professional chefs together.

The approach gained widespread public attention in the 2000s through chefs such as Ferran Adrià of elBulli in Spain, Heston Blumenthal of The Fat Duck in England, and Grant Achatz of Alinea in Chicago, whose restaurants became famous for dishes such as spherified olive juice, hot-and-cold tea, and edible balloons.

Objectives

Hervé This has set out several objectives for molecular gastronomy: to collect and test old cooking sayings and proverbs, to model and explain the chemical and physical processes behind recipes, to introduce new tools, ingredients and methods into the kitchen, and to use food preparation to explore how the senses interact with flavor perception.

Techniques

Techniques associated with molecular gastronomy include spherification (forming liquid-filled spheres using sodium alginate and calcium salts), the production of foams and airs with lecithin, gelification with agar-agar and gellan gum, flash-freezing with liquid nitrogen, low-temperature long-time cooking (sous-vide), and the use of transglutaminase ("meat glue") and maltodextrin to turn fats into powders.

Criticism and terminology

Many chefs associated with the movement, including Adrià and Blumenthal, have rejected the label "molecular gastronomy" as misleading, preferring terms such as "modernist cuisine." Hervé This himself distinguishes molecular gastronomy, the scientific discipline, from "molecular cooking," its culinary application. Others have criticized the style as gimmicky or pretentious, though its techniques have since entered mainstream professional cooking.
