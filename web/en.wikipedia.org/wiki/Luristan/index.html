Luristan or Lorestan (Persian: لرستان, "land of the Lurs") is a historical region of western Iran, in the Zagros Mountains between Iraq and the Iranian plateau, inhabited predominantly by the Lur people. The modern Iranian province of Lorestan covers the northern part of the historical region; the broader cultural-geographic Luristan also includes the Bakhtiari country and adjoining areas. The region is internationally known among archaeologists for the so-called Luristan bronzes, a distinctive group of early Iron Age bronze artefacts.

The Luristan bronzes

The term "Luristan bronzes" covers thousands of cast-bronze objects — standards and finials (including the famous "Master of Animals" standards), horse bits and cheekpieces, harness fittings, weapons, vessels, pins and idols — dating mainly from about 1200 to 650 BC. They appeared on the antiquities market from the late 1920s after large-scale unrecorded digging in the region, and most museum examples lack archaeological context; subsequent scientific excavations, notably by the Belgian archaeologist Louis Vanden Berghe and at sites such as Baba Jan, have clarified their burial contexts. The bronzes are attributed to the region's nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples, possibly including ancestors of the Kassites and later the Lurs.

History

In antiquity the region lay between Elam to the south, Media to the east and Assyria and Babylonia to the west, and was associated with peoples such as the Kassites and the Ellipi. After the Islamic conquest, medieval Luristan was divided into Greater Luristan (Lur-i Buzurg, the Bakhtiari country) and Lesser Luristan (Lur-i Kuchak, roughly modern Lorestan), ruled by local dynasties: the Hazaraspid atabaks in Greater Luristan from the 12th to the 15th century, and the Khorshidi dynasty in Lesser Luristan until its last ruler was executed by Shah Abbas I in 1597. Semi-autonomous Lur valis and tribal khans retained real power in the region into the early 20th century, when Reza Shah's campaigns forcibly settled and disarmed the tribes.

Geography and culture

Luristan's landscape of parallel oak-forested mountain ridges and deep river valleys supported a traditional economy of transhumant pastoralism, with tribes migrating seasonally between summer highlands (sardsir) and winter lowlands (garmsir). The region retains distinctive music (the sorna and dohol, and the kamancheh), mourning ceremonies, traditional dress, and a reputation for producing much of Iran's oak honey and for its handicrafts.
