Cytosine

Cytosine (symbol C) is one of the four nucleobases found in DNA and RNA, along with adenine, guanine, and thymine (or uracil in RNA). It is a pyrimidine derivative, with a heterocyclic aromatic ring and both an amine group and a keto group attached. In nucleic acids, cytosine pairs with guanine through three hydrogen bonds.

History

Cytosine was discovered and named in 1894 by Albrecht Kossel and Albert Neumann, who hydrolysed it from calf thymus tissue; its structure was established in the following decade, and it was synthesised in the laboratory in 1903.

Chemical properties

Cytosine is chemically unstable relative to the other bases: it can spontaneously deaminate to form uracil. This is one reason DNA uses thymine instead of uracil — repair enzymes can recognise uracil in DNA as damaged cytosine and remove it. Unchecked, such deamination creates C-to-T transition mutations, the most common type of point mutation in many genomes.

Methylation and epigenetics

In animals and plants, a fraction of cytosines in DNA — chiefly those in CpG dinucleotides — are methylated to 5-methylcytosine by DNA methyltransferases. This modification is a central mechanism of epigenetic gene regulation, influencing development, genomic imprinting, and X-chromosome inactivation; abnormal methylation patterns are associated with cancer. Further oxidised derivatives such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine also occur.

Other roles

Beyond nucleic acids, cytosine derivatives serve as energy-carrying and signalling molecules: cytidine triphosphate (CTP) is a cofactor in lipid synthesis, and the drug cytarabine — a cytosine arabinoside — is used in chemotherapy for leukaemia.
