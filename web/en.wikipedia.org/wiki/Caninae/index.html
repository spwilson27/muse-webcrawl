Caninae is the only living subfamily of the dog family Canidae. It contains all extant canids — the wolves, dogs, coyotes, jackals, foxes and their relatives — divided principally between the dog-like tribe Canini and the fox-like tribe Vulpini. The other canid subfamilies, the borophagines ("bone-crushing dogs") and hesperocyonines, are extinct.

Characteristics

Canines are generally long-legged, digitigrade carnivorans adapted for running, with non-retractile claws, long muzzles and a well-developed sense of smell. Most are social to some degree; members of Canini in particular form packs and include cooperative hunters, while vulpines tend to be smaller and more solitary, feeding heavily on small prey.

Evolution

Caninae originated in North America, where the earliest members, such as Leptocyon, appear in the Oligocene, more than 30 million years ago. The subfamily remained confined to North America for much of its history before spreading to other continents in the late Miocene and Pliocene. The domestic dog, descended from Pleistocene wolves, is a member of the tribe Canini.

Classification

Living genera of Canini include Canis (wolves, dogs, coyotes and jackals), Cuon (dhole), Lycaon (African wild dog), Chrysocyon (maned wolf), Speothos (bush dog) and the South American foxes. Vulpini includes Vulpes (true foxes), Nyctereutes (raccoon dogs) and Otocyon (bat-eared fox); the grey fox (Urocyon) is often placed in its own basal lineage.
