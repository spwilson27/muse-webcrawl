Fair Isle knitting
OriginFair Isle, Shetland, Scotland
TechniqueStranded colourwork
Typical motifsOXO bands, stars, crosses, rams' horns

Fair Isle knitting is a traditional knitting technique used to create patterns with multiple colours. It is named after Fair Isle, one of the Shetland Islands in northern Scotland. Fair Isle knitting gained considerable popularity after the Prince of Wales (later Edward VIII) wore a Fair Isle sweater in public in 1921.

Technique

The technique is a form of stranded colourwork in which only two yarns are used in any given round or row. The yarn not in use is carried loosely across the back of the work as a "float". Traditional garments are knitted in the round on circular or double-pointed needles, producing seamless tubes; sweaters may be cut open (steeked) to create armholes and cardigan fronts. Only two colours appear per row, though a garment may use many colours in total, changing them between pattern bands.

Patterns

Traditional Fair Isle designs consist of horizontal bands of geometric motifs — crosses, stars, diamonds and the characteristic "OXO" patterns — separated by narrow "peerie" patterns of one to three rows. Colours traditionally came from natural dyes, giving muted tones of brown, grey, rust, indigo and madder red.

History and industry

Knitting has been practised on Fair Isle since at least the 19th century, providing crucial income for the island's crofting families. The style became fashionable in the 1920s and remains strongly associated with Shetland knitwear, though the name "Fair Isle" is now often applied generically to similar stranded colourwork, much to the concern of Shetland producers who defend the genuine tradition.
