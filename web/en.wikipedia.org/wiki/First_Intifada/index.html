First Intifada

The First Intifada (Arabic: الانتفاضة الأولى), also known as the First Palestinian Intifada, was a sustained series of protests and violent riots carried out by Palestinians in the Israeli-occupied Palestinian territories and Israel. It was motivated by collective Palestinian frustration over Israel's occupation of the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, as it approached a twenty-year mark, having begun in the wake of the 1967 Six-Day War. The uprising lasted from December 1987 until the signing of the Oslo Accords in 1993 (with the 1991 Madrid Conference often taken as an earlier endpoint for its most intense phase).

Background

By 1987 Palestinians had lived under Israeli military occupation for twenty years, with expanding settlements, land confiscations, economic dependence on day labour in Israel, and no political horizon. Palestinian society had, however, developed dense networks of universities, unions, women's committees, and charitable organisations that provided the infrastructure for mass mobilisation.

Outbreak and course

The spark came on 8 December 1987, when an Israeli army truck collided with civilian cars in the Jabalya refugee camp in Gaza, killing four Palestinians. Rumours that the crash was deliberate triggered riots that spread within days across Gaza and the West Bank. The uprising combined mass demonstrations, general strikes, boycotts of Israeli goods and institutions, tax refusal (notably at Beit Sahour), stone-throwing at soldiers, and — as it wore on — Molotov cocktails and internal violence against alleged collaborators.

A clandestine Unified National Leadership of the Uprising (UNLU) coordinated actions through leaflets, while the PLO leadership in Tunis sought to direct the movement from exile. The uprising also saw the founding of Hamas in Gaza in late 1987–88 as an Islamist alternative. Israeli responses included large-scale arrests, curfews, deportations, school closures, live fire against demonstrators, and Defence Minister Yitzhak Rabin's controversial "force, might and beatings" policy of early 1988. Iconic imagery of youths with stones facing soldiers made the intifada a landmark in international coverage of the conflict.

Consequences

Estimates of deaths over the uprising commonly cite roughly 1,000–1,400 Palestinians killed by Israeli forces and civilians, and around 160–200 Israelis killed by Palestinians, in addition to hundreds of Palestinians killed by other Palestinians as suspected collaborators. Politically, the intifada pushed the conflict toward negotiation: Jordan disengaged from the West Bank in 1988, the PLO proclaimed a Palestinian state and accepted UN Resolution 242, and the Madrid Conference (1991) and the Oslo Accords (1993) followed, creating the Palestinian Authority. The far more violent armed uprising of 2000–2005 is known as the Second Intifada.
