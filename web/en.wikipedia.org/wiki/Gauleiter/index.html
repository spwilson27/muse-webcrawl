Gauleiter

A Gauleiter (German pronunciation: [ˈɡaʊlaɪtɐ]) was a regional leader of the Nazi Party (NSDAP) who served as the head of a Gau, one of the party's regional districts. The title was in use from 1925 until the fall of Nazi Germany in 1945, and ranked among the most powerful positions in the Third Reich, second within the party only to the Reichsleiter rank and the Führer himself.

Role

Gauleiter were appointed by Adolf Hitler, answered directly to him, and exercised wide authority over party affairs in their districts. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 their party districts were aligned with administrative regions, and most Gauleiter also took state office as Reichsstatthalter (Reich Governors) or Oberpräsidenten, fusing party and state power in their persons. They controlled patronage, propaganda and personnel at the regional level and were prominent enforcers of Nazi policies, including persecution of Jews and, during the Second World War, civil defence and the Volkssturm militia as Reich Defence Commissioners.

Notable Gauleiter

Well-known holders included Joseph Goebbels (Berlin), Julius Streicher (Franconia), Martin Bormann (Thuringia, before his rise at party headquarters), Karl Kaufmann (Hamburg), Erich Koch (East Prussia), Arthur Greiser (Warthegau) and Fritz Sauckel (Thuringia). After 1945 several Gauleiter were tried for war crimes and crimes against humanity; others, such as Streicher, were executed following the Nuremberg trials, while many died by suicide at the regime's collapse.

Legacy

In modern German usage, Gauleiter is sometimes used pejoratively for an authoritarian regional official or party boss. The office is a frequently cited example of the Third Reich's polycratic structure of overlapping authorities bound together by personal loyalty to Hitler.
