Mystery plays and miracle plays (distinguished as two different forms, though often used interchangeably) are among the earliest formally developed plays in medieval Europe. Medieval mystery plays focused on the representation of Bible stories in churches as tableaux with accompanying antiphonal song. They developed from the 10th to the 16th century, reaching their height of popularity in the 15th century, before being rendered obsolete by professional theatre.

Origins

The mystery play developed from liturgical drama — short dramatised episodes, such as the Quem quaeritis Easter trope, performed within church services. As these dramas grew more elaborate, they moved out of the church into the marketplace and began to be performed in the vernacular rather than Latin. The term "mystery" is commonly linked to the French mystère, and to the "mysteries" or crafts of the trade guilds that produced the plays; miracle plays, strictly defined, dramatised the lives and miracles of saints.

Development and guild production

By the 14th century, many towns staged complete cycles of plays covering sacred history from the Creation and Fall of Adam through the life, death, and resurrection of Christ to Doomsday. Performances were associated with the feast of Corpus Christi (established 1264, observed widely from the early 14th century). Each episode was mounted by a trade guild — often with an apt assignment, such as the shipwrights staging Noah's Ark or the bakers the Last Supper — on pageant wagons that processed through the town, or at fixed stations.

Major English cycles

Four substantial English cycles survive in manuscript: the York Cycle (48 plays), the Chester Cycle (24 plays), the Wakefield Cycle (the Towneley plays, 32 plays, including the famous Second Shepherds' Play), and the N-Town Cycle (42 plays). The plays blend reverent instruction with broad comedy, contemporary local references, and vivid characterisation — Herod raging, devils cavorting, and shepherds complaining about the weather before the angels appear. Analogous traditions existed across Europe, including the French mystères and German Passion plays.

Suppression and legacy

During the English Reformation, religious drama fell under suspicion; the cycles were progressively suppressed in the 1560s–1570s under Elizabeth I, the last performances taking place around 1575–1579. The mystery plays nonetheless mark a crucial bridge between liturgical ritual and the secular professional stage of the Renaissance, contributing vernacular dramaturgy, staging conventions, and popular audiences that shaped later English theatre.

Modern revivals

The York plays were famously revived at the Festival of Britain in 1951 and are now performed regularly, often on wagons through the city streets; Chester's cycle is likewise revived at intervals. The Oberammergau Passion Play in Germany, performed since 1634, is the most famous surviving relative of the tradition.
