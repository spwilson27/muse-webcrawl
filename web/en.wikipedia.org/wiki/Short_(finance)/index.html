Short (finance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, being short in an asset means the investor benefits from a decline in its price — holding a position that has negative exposure to the asset. The complementary position is being long, which benefits from a price increase. The most common way of shorting is the short sale: the investor borrows securities (typically from a broker-dealer's inventory or another client), sells them on the open market, and later buys them back ("covering") to return to the lender. If the price has fallen, the investor profits by the difference; if it has risen, the investor loses — with theoretically unlimited losses, since a price can rise without bound while it can only fall to zero.

Mechanics

A short sale requires a margin account. The broker locates shares to borrow (taking a fee and any dividends owed to the lender), the sale proceeds are held as collateral, and the position is marked to market daily. The lender may recall the shares ("buy-in"), forcing the short to cover. Short selling is regulated in most markets — for example by rules on naked shorting (selling shares not yet borrowed), circuit-breaker restrictions after sharp price falls, and disclosure requirements for large positions. Short interest (the fraction of a company's free float sold short) is a widely watched indicator.

Risks and controversies

Because losses can exceed the original stake, and because rising prices can force many shorts to cover simultaneously, short positions are vulnerable to a short squeeze, in which forced buying accelerates the price rise — as occurred famously with Volkswagen shares in 2008 and GameStop in 2021. Critics accuse short sellers of profiting from failure, spreading bearish rumours and destabilising companies; defenders, including many economists and regulators, argue that short selling improves price discovery, exposes fraud and overvaluation (short sellers were among the first to flag Enron and Wirecard), and adds market liquidity. Several countries have imposed temporary short-sale bans in crises, notably in 2008 and 2020, with mixed evidence as to their benefit.

Other ways to be short

  • Buying put options, or selling call options.
  • Selling futures contracts or entering contracts for difference (CFDs).
  • Inverse and leveraged-inverse exchange-traded funds.
  • Credit default swaps as a short position on a borrower's credit.

History

Short selling is nearly as old as joint-stock markets: Isaac Le Maire's campaign against Dutch East India Company shares led Amsterdam to ban short sales in 1610, generally considered the first such prohibition. Modern practice developed on the New York exchanges in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
