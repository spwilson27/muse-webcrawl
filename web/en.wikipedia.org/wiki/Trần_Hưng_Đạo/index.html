Trần Hưng Đạo (1228–1300), born Trần Quốc Tuấn, was a prince of the Trần dynasty and the supreme commander (Quốc công Tiết chế) of the armies of Đại Việt (Vietnam) during the second and third Mongol invasions. His victories over the forces of Kublai Khan's Yuan dynasty—culminating in the annihilation of the Mongol fleet at the Battle of Bạch Đằng in 1288—make him one of Vietnam's most revered national heroes and one of the most accomplished military commanders of the pre-modern world.

Background

Trần Quốc Tuấn was the son of Prince Trần Liễu, elder brother of the emperor Trần Thái Tông; a grievance over the throne divided the royal family, yet Hưng Đạo famously set dynastic resentment aside in the national interest—an example cited in Vietnam ever since. Learned in strategy, he authored military works, including the Hịch tướng sĩ ("Proclamation to the Officers"), a stirring exhortation that remains a classic of Vietnamese literature, and treatises on tactics.

The Mongol invasions

After helping repel the first invasion of 1258 as a young officer, Hưng Đạo commanded in the second (1285) and third (1287–88) invasions, when huge Yuan armies under Tolui's descendants and Prince Toghon invaded by land and sea. His strategy was classic: evacuate the capital Thăng Long, refuse decisive battle, harass supply lines, and strike when the invaders were exhausted and hungry. In 1288 he prepared the Bạch Đằng River with submerged iron-tipped stakes—echoing Ngô Quyền's victory over the Chinese in 938—and lured the Yuan supply fleet onto them at ebb tide, destroying it. The Yuan withdrew, and the Trần court thereafter sent tribute to avert further invasions while preserving independence.

Legacy

Trần Hưng Đạo is worshipped as a saint (Đức Thánh Trần) in Vietnamese folk religion, with temples—including the famous Kiếp Bạc Temple—hosting major festivals. Streets and squares across Vietnam bear his name, and his statue overlooking the Saigon River in Ho Chi Minh City is a landmark. His maxim that an army's strength lies in the people's support anticipates later Vietnamese military doctrine.
