Lateral ventricles

The ventricular system; the paired lateral ventricles are the largest cavities

The lateral ventricles are the two largest cavities of the ventricular system of the human brain. Each cerebral hemisphere contains one lateral ventricle: the left is designated the first ventricle and the right the second. They are C-shaped structures filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which is produced by the choroid plexus within them. Each lateral ventricle communicates with the third ventricle through the interventricular foramen (foramen of Monro).

Structure

Each lateral ventricle consists of a central part (body and atrium) and three projections called horns: the anterior (frontal) horn extending into the frontal lobe, the posterior (occipital) horn into the occipital lobe, and the inferior (temporal) horn into the temporal lobe. Structures forming their boundaries include the corpus callosum, caudate nucleus, thalamus, septum pellucidum, and hippocampus.

Development

The lateral ventricles develop from the cavity of the telencephalic vesicles of the embryonic forebrain, acquiring their curved shape as the cerebral hemispheres expand and fold during development.

Clinical significance

Enlargement of the lateral ventricles occurs in hydrocephalus, when CSF accumulates under pressure, and ventriculomegaly can also be observed in several neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions. Asymmetric or enlarged ventricles are assessed clinically by CT and MRI imaging. The volume of the lateral ventricles tends to increase with age and in conditions involving loss of brain tissue, such as Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia.
