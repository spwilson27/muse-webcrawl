Vietnam War POW/MIA issue

The Vietnam War POW/MIA issue concerns the fate of United States service personnel listed as prisoners of war (POW) or missing in action (MIA) during the Vietnam War. Following the Paris Peace Accords of 1973, 591 American prisoners were returned by North Vietnam during Operation Homecoming, but roughly 2,500 Americans remained unaccounted for across Southeast Asia, and the belief that some might still be alive in captivity became a powerful political and cultural issue in the United States.

The POW/MIA flag, created for the National League of Families, depicting a prisoner silhouetted before a guard tower.

Background

Family organisations, above all the National League of POW/MIA Families, pressed successive administrations for a full accounting. The POW/MIA flag they created, with the motto "You Are Not Forgotten", is flown over the White House, the Capitol, and countless public buildings — the only flag besides the U.S. flag to fly over the White House.

Investigations

Repeated official investigations, including the Senate Select Committee on POW/MIA Affairs (1991–1993), chaired by John Kerry with John McCain prominent, concluded that there was no compelling evidence that any American remained alive in captivity, while acknowledging that full accounting was impossible for many losses. Joint U.S.–Vietnamese recovery teams have since excavated crash and burial sites, and the Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency continues to identify remains; the number of unaccounted-for Americans from the Vietnam War has fallen to around 1,550.

Cultural impact

The conviction that prisoners had been abandoned fed a stream of films in the 1980s, such as Rambo: First Blood Part II and the Missing in Action series, and remains a resonant theme in American politics and veterans' advocacy.
