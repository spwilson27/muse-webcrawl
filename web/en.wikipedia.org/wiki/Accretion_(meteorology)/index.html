Accretion (meteorology)

In meteorology, accretion is the growth of a precipitation particle — a raindrop, snowflake or ice crystal — as it falls through a cloud and collects smaller cloud droplets or droplets of supercooled water in its path. It is one of the principal mechanisms by which precipitation particles grow large enough to fall to the ground.

Process

A falling hydrometeor sweeps out a volume of air as it descends. Cloud droplets within that volume collide with it and are captured (coalescence for liquid drops; for ice particles, the capture and freezing of supercooled droplets is called riming). Because larger drops fall faster than smaller ones, accretion preferentially grows the largest drops, broadening the droplet spectrum until rain forms.

Relation to other growth mechanisms

Accretion complements condensation (which alone produces drops too small to fall as rain), the Bergeron process (growth of ice crystals at the expense of supercooled droplets), and aggregation (the clumping of ice crystals into snowflakes). Riming by accretion of supercooled droplets produces graupel and contributes to hail growth and to aircraft icing.
