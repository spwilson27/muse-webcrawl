Wilhelm Steinitz (born Wolf Steinitz; 14 May 1836 – 12 August 1900) was an Austrian-American chess player who became the first official World Chess Champion, holding the title from 1886 to 1894. He is widely regarded as the founder of modern chess strategy, transforming the game from the swashbuckling attacking style of the Romantic era into a science of positional accumulation.

Early life and career

Steinitz was born in Prague, then part of the Austrian Empire, the youngest of thirteen children of a tailor. He learned chess at about the age of twelve and later studied mathematics at the Vienna Polytechnic. He rose quickly through the Viennese chess scene, winning the city championship in 1861, and represented Austria at the London 1862 international tournament, where he finished sixth.

In 1866 Steinitz defeated Adolf Anderssen, then considered the world's strongest player, in a match in London by eight wins to six. This victory established his claim to be the leading player of the day, though no formal world championship existed yet.

World championship

In 1886 Steinitz faced Johannes Zukertort in the first officially recognised World Chess Championship match, played across New York, St. Louis and New Orleans. Steinitz won by ten wins to five with five draws. He defended the title against Mikhail Chigorin (1889 and 1892) and Isidor Gunsberg (1890–91), but in 1894 he lost to the young German Emanuel Lasker, who would hold the crown for 27 years. A return match in 1896–97 also went to Lasker.

Chess theory

Steinitz's enduring contribution was theoretical. In his writings, including The Modern Chess Instructor (1889), he argued that a sound attack could only succeed against weaknesses in the opponent's position, and that the correct plan was to accumulate small advantages — superior pawn structure, control of key squares, the bishop pair — before striking. His principles underpin the positional school developed further by Siegbert Tarrasch, Aron Nimzowitsch and others.

Later life and death

Steinitz spent his final years in the United States, becoming an American citizen in 1888. Plagued by poverty and declining mental health, he died destitute in a New York asylum on 12 August 1900. He is buried in Evergreen Cemetery, Brooklyn.
