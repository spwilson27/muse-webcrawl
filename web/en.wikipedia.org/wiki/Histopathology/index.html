Histopathology

Histopathology (from the Greek histos "tissue", pathos "suffering" and -logia "study of") refers to the microscopic examination of tissue in order to study the manifestations of disease. Specifically, in clinical medicine, histopathology refers to the examination of a biopsy or surgical specimen by a pathologist, after the specimen has been processed and histological sections have been placed onto glass slides. In contrast, cytopathology examines free cells or tissue micro-fragments (as "cell blocks").

Collection of tissues

Histopathological examination begins with surgery, biopsy or autopsy. Tissue is preserved by fixation, usually in 10% neutral buffered formalin, to prevent decay and maintain cellular structure. The specimen is examined grossly (macroscopically), with representative portions selected for processing — a step called "cut up" or grossing.

Preparation of histological sections

The tissue is dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared, and embedded in paraffin wax. The resulting block is cut into sections typically 3–5 micrometres thick using a microtome, and the sections are floated onto glass slides. For urgent intra-operative diagnosis, frozen sections are prepared rapidly with a cryostat instead.

Staining

Sections are stained before microscopic examination. The standard stain is haematoxylin and eosin (H&E): haematoxylin colours nuclei blue-purple, while eosin stains cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink. Special stains (such as PAS, Gram, Ziehl–Neelsen and Congo red) highlight specific organisms, substances or structures, and immunohistochemistry uses labelled antibodies to detect specific antigens — essential, for example, in tumour classification. In-situ hybridisation and molecular tests increasingly complement morphology.

Interpretation

The histopathologist integrates the microscopic appearance with clinical and imaging findings to produce a diagnosis, which guides prognosis and treatment — nowhere more critically than in oncology, where tumour type, grade and stage are determined histologically. Histopathology is widely considered the "gold standard" for the diagnosis of many diseases, especially cancers.
