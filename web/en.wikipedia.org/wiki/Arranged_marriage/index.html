Arranged marriage

An arranged marriage is a type of marital union where the bride and groom are primarily selected by individuals other than the couple themselves, particularly by their parents or guardians. In some cultures, a professional matchmaker may assist in finding a spouse. Arranged marriage differs from forced marriage in that both parties consent, at least in principle, though the degree of choice varies widely: in some arrangements the couple may veto candidates or meet only briefly before the wedding, while in others — sometimes called "assisted" or "semi-arranged" marriages — parents introduce candidates whom the couple is free to accept or reject after courtship.

Arranged marriages remain common in South Asia, parts of Africa, and the Middle East.

Distribution and practice

Arranged marriage has historically been widespread across the world, including in Europe (especially among royalty and aristocracy, where marriages served dynastic alliances) and remains the predominant form in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and much of the Middle East and Africa. In India, matches are traditionally negotiated within caste and religious communities, often using horoscopes, biodata, matrimonial advertisements, and now matrimonial websites. In Japan, the omiai system of formal introductions was common into the later twentieth century.

Factors

Commonly cited considerations in arranging marriages include economic stability, religion, caste or social class, family reputation, education, and astrological compatibility. Supporters argue that parental experience and the involvement of families contribute to stable unions, and some studies report low divorce rates in arranged marriages, though such statistics reflect cultural attitudes to divorce as much as marital satisfaction. Critics emphasise risks of coercion, the thin line between arrangement and force in some settings, and restrictions on individual autonomy — issues addressed by laws against forced marriage and child marriage.
