Von Neumann paradox

The von Neumann paradox is a result in mathematics, formulated by John von Neumann in 1929, showing a paradoxical decomposition of the unit square: the square can be partitioned into finitely many pieces which can then be reassembled, using only area-preserving affine transformations (translations, reflections, and shears of determinant ±1), into two copies of the original square.

Background

The result extends the spirit of the Banach–Tarski paradox (1924), which duplicates a ball using arbitrary rotations and translations plus the axiom of choice. Von Neumann's insight was that the phenomenon is not caused by the exotic geometry of three-dimensional rotations but by algebraic properties of the transformation group: the group of area-preserving affine transformations of the plane contains a free group on two generators, and free groups admit paradoxical decompositions. This insight led von Neumann to define the notion of an amenable group: groups that are not paradoxical in this way. Abelian and solvable groups are amenable; groups containing free subgroups are not.

Statement and proof sketch

Von Neumann showed that the free group F₂ can be partitioned into sets that can be translated to reconstruct two copies of itself, and transferred this decomposition to subsets of the plane under the action of a freely acting group of affine transformations, handling fixed points by a careful argument. Unlike Banach–Tarski, the pieces in the square version can be rearranged using only area-preserving maps, which initially seems to contradict the conservation of area; the resolution is that the pieces are non-measurable sets, which have no well-defined area.

Significance

The paradox marks the origin of the modern theory of amenability, developed by von Neumann in the same 1929 paper, which became fundamental in group theory, ergodic theory, and functional analysis, and later in the study of von Neumann algebras.
