Field electron emission

Field electron emission, also known as field emission (FE) and electron field emission, is emission of electrons induced by an electrostatic field. The most common context is field emission from a solid surface into a vacuum, from a solid into another solid, or from the surface of a liquid. Field emission from a solid into vacuum requires a very high surface electric field — typically of the order of volts per nanometre — so that the potential barrier at the surface becomes thin enough for electrons near the Fermi level to pass through it by quantum mechanical tunnelling. It contrasts with thermionic emission, in which electrons are boiled off a heated cathode by thermal energy.

Theory

The theory was developed by Ralph H. Fowler and Lothar Nordheim in 1928. The Fowler–Nordheim equation relates the emitted current density to the local field and the material's work function; because tunnelling probability depends exponentially on the barrier, the current rises extremely steeply with field. A plot of log(I/V²) against 1/V (a Fowler–Nordheim plot) is approximately linear for field emission and is used to test whether an observed current is truly field emission. Real emitters are sharp tips or nanostructures that concentrate the field by a geometric enhancement factor, so macroscopic applied fields of only a few volts per micrometre can produce the required local fields at the apex.

Emitters

Classic field emitters are electrochemically etched metal needles of tungsten or molybdenum. Modern cold cathodes use arrays of microfabricated tips (Spindt cathodes), carbon nanotubes, silicon nanowires, graphene edges, and other nanomaterials with high aspect ratios and low work functions. In the field emission microscope (invented by Erwin Wilhelm Müller in 1936), the emission pattern from a sharp tip magnifies the atomic-scale structure of the tip surface onto a screen; its successor, the field ion microscope, first imaged individual atoms.

Applications

  • Cold-cathode electron sources for electron microscopes and X-ray tubes, valued for high brightness and instant switching.
  • Field emission displays (a flat-panel technology largely superseded by LCD and OLED).
  • Vacuum nanoelectronics, microwave amplifiers, and electric-propulsion neutralizers for spacecraft (field-emission electric propulsion, FEEP).
  • Unwanted field emission limits the performance of high-gradient particle accelerator cavities, high-voltage vacuum devices, and fusion equipment, where it can trigger vacuum breakdown.

Related phenomena

Field emission also occurs in liquids and at semiconductor surfaces, and in the avalanche breakdown of gases and insulators. In strong fields at a liquid surface it can produce electrospray ionisation, a technique central to mass spectrometry.
