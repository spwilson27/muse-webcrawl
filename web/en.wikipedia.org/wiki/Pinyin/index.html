Pinyin, officially Hanyu Pinyin (simplified Chinese: 汉语拼音; traditional Chinese: 漢語拼音; pinyin: Hànyǔ Pīnyīn), is the standard system for the romanization of Standard Chinese in mainland China. It uses the Latin alphabet to write the sounds of Mandarin and indicates the language's four tones with diacritical marks.

History

Pinyin was developed in the 1950s by a committee including the linguist Zhou Youguang and adopted by the government of the People's Republic of China in 1958, primarily to promote literacy and the standard language. It superseded earlier systems such as Wade–Giles and is now the standard romanization used by the United Nations, the Library of Congress, and most Western media. Taiwan officially adopted Hanyu Pinyin in 2009, though the older Tongyong Pinyin and Wade–Giles-derived spellings remain common there.

Structure

Each Chinese syllable is analysed into an initial (consonant), a final (vowel combination), and a tone. Some letters have values different from English: for example, q represents an aspirated "ch" sound, x a palatal "sh", c an aspirated "ts", and zh a retroflex "j". Tones are marked with macrons, acute, caron, and grave accents (mā má mǎ mà), or omitted in most everyday uses such as place names and personal names.

Uses

Beyond romanization, pinyin is the main method of inputting Chinese characters on computers and phones, a teaching tool for pronunciation in Chinese schools and foreign-language classes, and the basis of the standard ordering of many dictionaries.
