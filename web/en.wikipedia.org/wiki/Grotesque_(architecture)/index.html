Grotesque (architecture)

In architecture, a grotesque is a carved stone figure, usually a fantastic or mythical creature, used for decorative purposes on buildings. Grotesques are frequently found on Gothic churches and cathedrals, where they serve as ornament on cornices, parapets and pinnacles.

Distinction from gargoyles

A grotesque is often confused with a gargoyle. The difference is functional: a gargoyle is a grotesque carving that also serves as a waterspout, projecting rainwater away from the walls of a building, whereas a purely decorative carving is a grotesque (sometimes loosely called a chimera). When a grotesque does not function as a spout, it may be carved in relief or as a freestanding figure.

History

Carved grotesques became prominent in Gothic architecture from the thirteenth century onward, populating the exteriors of cathedrals such as Notre-Dame de Paris with demons, hybrid animals and caricatured human figures. They enjoyed a revival during the Gothic Revival of the nineteenth century, when architects such as Eugène Viollet-le-Duc restored and added new carvings to medieval buildings.

Meaning

The purpose of grotesques has been debated. Interpretations include apotropaic functions (warding off evil), moral instruction for an illiterate congregation, and simple displays of the stonemason's craft and humour.
