Spanish Morocco (Spanish: Protectorado español de Marruecos) was the Spanish protectorate in Morocco established in 1912 under the Treaty of Fez, which divided Morocco between France and Spain. The Spanish zone comprised a northern strip along the Mediterranean and Atlantic coasts — including the Rif mountains — and a southern enclave around Cape Juby; the city of Tangier was administered separately as an international zone.

History

Spanish control was contested from the outset. In the Rif War (1921–1926), the Berber leader Abd el-Krim proclaimed the Republic of the Rif and inflicted a catastrophic defeat on Spain at Annual in 1921. Spain recovered only after massive reinforcement, the Alhucemas landing of 1925, and joint operations with France. The army raised in the protectorate, including the Spanish Legion and the Regulares, became the military base from which Francisco Franco launched the coup of July 1936 that began the Spanish Civil War; tens of thousands of Moroccan troops fought for the Nationalists.

End of the protectorate

Following Moroccan independence from France in 1956, Spain retroceded the northern zone to Morocco on 7 April 1956; the southern Cape Juby strip followed in 1958 after the Ifni War. Spain retained the plazas de soberanía (Ceuta, Melilla, and several small territories) on the North African coast, which remain Spanish today, as well as, for a time, Ifni (retroceded 1969) and Spanish Sahara (relinquished 1975–1976).
