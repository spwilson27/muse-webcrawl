Sensorineural hearing loss

Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) is a type of hearing loss in which the underlying cause lies in the inner ear — usually the hair cells of the cochlea — or in the sensory neurons and auditory nerve pathways to the brain. It accounts for about 90% of reported hearing loss and is contrasted with conductive hearing loss, which involves the outer or middle ear.

Causes

The most common causes are ageing (presbycusis), prolonged exposure to loud noise, and genetic factors. Other causes include ototoxic drugs (such as aminoglycoside antibiotics and cisplatin), infections (including congenital cytomegalovirus and meningitis), Ménière's disease, acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma), head trauma, and sudden sensorineural hearing loss of unknown cause. Damage to the delicate hair cells of the organ of Corti is usually irreversible in humans.

Diagnosis

SNHL is identified with pure-tone audiometry and tuning-fork tests: in the Weber test sound lateralises to the better ear, and in the Rinne test air conduction remains greater than bone conduction in the affected ear. Imaging and auditory brainstem response testing may be used to exclude retrocochlear pathology.

Treatment

There is no cure for most chronic SNHL, but hearing aids amplify sound for mild-to-severe loss, and cochlear implants can restore useful hearing in severe-to-profound cases by electrically stimulating the auditory nerve. Sudden SNHL is treated as a medical emergency, commonly with corticosteroids.
