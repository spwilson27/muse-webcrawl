Golden Age of Mexican cinema

The Golden Age of Mexican cinema (Spanish: Época de oro del cine mexicano) is a period in the history of Mexican cinema spanning roughly from 1936 to 1956, when the Mexican film industry reached high levels of production, quality, and economic success, becoming the centre of commercial films in Latin America and the Spanish-speaking world.

Origins

The era is generally dated from the success of Allá en el Rancho Grande (1936), directed by Fernando de Fuentes, which established the popular comedia ranchera genre. During the Second World War, Mexico's film industry expanded rapidly: the United States and Europe curtailed production of entertainment films, while Mexico, a wartime ally, received raw film stock and equipment, allowing it to dominate Spanish-language markets.

Major figures

The period produced enduring stars including Pedro Infante, the idol of the working class; the singing charro Jorge Negrete; María Félix, known as "La Doña"; Dolores del Río; and the comic actor Cantinflas (Mario Moreno). Leading directors included Emilio "El Indio" Fernández, whose films with cinematographer Gabriel Figueroa won international prizes, and the Spanish exile Luis Buñuel, who made Los Olvidados (1950) in Mexico.

Genres and themes

Dominant genres included the ranchera musical, melodrama, cabaretera (cabaret) films, urban comedies, and historical epics. Films frequently celebrated Mexican national identity, rural values, and the legacy of the Mexican Revolution.

Decline

By the mid-1950s the deaths of major stars, rising costs, competition from television, and formulaic production led to a decline, and the Golden Age is generally considered to have ended around 1956–1959.
