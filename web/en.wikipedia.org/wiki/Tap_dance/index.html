Tap dance

Tap dance is a form of dance in which the dancer wears shoes fitted with metal "taps" on the heel and toe and strikes the floor with the feet to create percussive sounds, combining movement and rhythm. It developed in the United States during the 19th century from a fusion of African-American dance traditions, such as juba and ring shouts, with Irish jig and English clog dancing, particularly in the Five Points district of New York City.

History

Tap emerged in minstrel shows, where Master Juba (William Henry Lane) became one of the first famous Black performers. In vaudeville and early film, dancers such as Bill "Bojangles" Robinson — celebrated for his stair dance — brought tap to wide audiences. Two broad styles developed: the upright, balletic "Broadway" style of Fred Astaire and Gene Kelly in Hollywood musicals, and the earthy, improvisational "rhythm tap" (or "hoofing") rooted in jazz, practised by John Bubbles, the Nicholas Brothers and later Gregory Hines.

Technique

Basic steps include the shuffle, flap, cramp roll, buffalo, time step and pullback, combined into phrases timed to musical beats and syncopation. Dancers function as musicians, producing rhythms with brush, strike and slide sounds from the taps. Tap declined in popularity after the 1950s but enjoyed a revival from the 1970s, and Savion Glover's Bring in 'da Noise, Bring in 'da Funk (1996) re-established it as a major art form.
