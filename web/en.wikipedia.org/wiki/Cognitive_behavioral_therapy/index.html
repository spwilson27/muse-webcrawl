Cognitive behavioral therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a form of psychotherapy that aims to treat mental disorders by identifying and changing unhelpful patterns of thinking and behaviour. It is based on the theory that psychological problems are partly caused by faulty or unhelpful ways of thinking and learned patterns of behaviour, and that people can learn better ways of coping. CBT is one of the most extensively researched forms of therapy and is recommended in many clinical guidelines.

History

CBT grew out of behaviour therapy, developed in the 1950s and 1960s from learning theory, and the cognitive therapy created by American psychiatrist Aaron T. Beck in the 1960s. Beck noticed that depressed patients experienced streams of negative automatic thoughts, and he devised methods for identifying, evaluating, and restructuring them. Albert Ellis's rational emotive behavior therapy developed in parallel. The two traditions merged under the umbrella term CBT in the late 20th century.

Methods

Therapy typically involves cognitive restructuring (examining and testing unhelpful thoughts), behavioural experiments and exposure (gradually facing feared situations), activity scheduling, problem-solving training, and homework assignments between sessions. The therapist and patient work collaboratively, setting specific goals and reviewing progress. Digital and internet-delivered CBT extends access, and "third-wave" variants—such as acceptance and commitment therapy and dialectical behavior therapy—emphasize mindfulness and acceptance.

Effectiveness

Numerous meta-analyses find CBT effective for anxiety disorders, depression, insomnia, and several other conditions, with effect sizes comparable to or better than medication for some disorders and lower relapse rates in depression. Guidelines such as those of the British NICE recommend CBT as a first-line treatment for many presentations. Critics note publication bias and argue that benefits relative to other active therapies are often modest.
