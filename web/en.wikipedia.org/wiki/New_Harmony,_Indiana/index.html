New Harmony is a historic town in Posey County, Indiana, on the Wabash River, renowned as the site of two of the most famous utopian community experiments in American history. It was founded in 1814 as Harmony by the Harmony Society, a pietist religious group led by George Rapp, whose members — the Harmonists or Rappites — built a prosperous, celibate, communal town of sturdy brick buildings, mills, and vineyards before deciding to return to Pennsylvania in 1824.

The Owenite experiment

In 1825 Rapp sold the town and 20,000 acres to the Welsh industrialist and social reformer Robert Owen, who renamed it New Harmony and invited "any and all" to join his model community of equality and cooperative living. Owen attracted a remarkable cargo of scientists, educators, and reformers — the geologist William Maclure, the naturalist Thomas Say, the educator Joseph Neef, and the feminist Frances Wright — who arrived on the celebrated "Boatload of Knowledge" in 1826. The community fractured over organisation, property, and the selection of members, and by 1827 the experiment had effectively collapsed, though Owen's sons remained and became influential citizens; David Dale Owen conducted geological surveys from the town, and Robert Dale Owen became a congressman and advocate of women's rights.

Legacy

Despite the failure of communal living, New Harmony left lasting legacies: one of the first public libraries, a pioneering system of free coeducational schooling and early kindergarten ideas, and important early work in American geology and entomology. Today the town is a National Historic Landmark district, home to Paul Tillich Park, Philip Johnson's Roofless Church (1960), and a small, arts-oriented community that preserves both the Harmonist and Owenite heritage.
