Hydrophile

A hydrophilic surface causes water to spread with a low contact angle.

A hydrophile is a molecule or other molecular entity which is attracted to water molecules and tends to be dissolved by water. In contrast, hydrophobes are not attracted to water and may seem to be repelled by it. Hydrophilic molecules are those that interact favourably with water through hydrogen bonding; they are typically polar or charged and are capable of forming hydrogen bonds with water.

Properties

Hydrophilic substances dissolve readily in water and other polar solvents. Common hydrophilic groups include hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), amino (–NH2) and phosphate groups. Sugars, salts and many proteins are hydrophilic. A surface is described as hydrophilic when a water droplet spreads across it with a contact angle less than 90 degrees; superhydrophilic surfaces approach a contact angle of zero.

Amphiphiles

Molecules that possess both hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts are called amphiphiles. Soaps and detergents are amphiphiles: their hydrophilic heads face the water while their hydrophobic tails cluster away from it, forming structures such as micelles. The phospholipids of cell membranes are also amphiphilic, arranging themselves into the lipid bilayer that encloses every living cell.

Applications

Hydrophilic coatings are used to make self-cleaning glass, anti-fogging lenses, wound dressings and medical implants that resist fouling. In chromatography, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) separates polar compounds that are poorly retained by conventional reversed-phase columns.
