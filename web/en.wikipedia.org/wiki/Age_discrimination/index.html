Age discrimination

Age discrimination is discrimination against individuals or groups on the basis of their age. It is related to the concept of ageism, coined by gerontologist Robert Neil Butler in 1969 to describe stereotyping and prejudice against older people. Age discrimination most commonly affects older workers, but it can also affect younger people. It occurs in employment, healthcare, housing, finance, and social services.

Employment

In the workplace, age discrimination may appear as refusal to hire, forced or pressured retirement, demotion, denial of training, or layoff targeting older employees, sometimes motivated by higher salary, pension, or healthcare costs or by stereotypes about adaptability and technology skills. Studies using matched résumé audits have found that older applicants receive fewer callbacks, with the effect often stronger for older women.

Younger workers may face discrimination too, such as being denied responsibility or fair pay because of perceived inexperience, though legal protections typically focus on older workers.

Legislation

In the United States, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) prohibits employment discrimination against persons aged 40 and over by employers with 20 or more employees, and largely ended mandatory retirement for most occupations. The Age Discrimination Act of 1975 bars age discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance. In the European Union, the Employment Equality Framework Directive (2000/78/EC) prohibits age discrimination in employment for all ages. The United Kingdom's Equality Act 2010 likewise covers age as a protected characteristic.

Healthcare

Age discrimination in healthcare includes rationing treatments by age, dismissing symptoms as "just old age," and excluding older patients from clinical trials. During the COVID-19 pandemic, triage policies in some jurisdictions sparked debate about age-based allocation of intensive care.

Effects

Research associates perceived age discrimination with poorer physical and mental health, reduced income and retirement savings, and social isolation. The World Health Organization's 2021 report on ageism estimated that one in two people worldwide hold moderately or highly ageist attitudes.
