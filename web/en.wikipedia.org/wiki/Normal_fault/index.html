Normal fault

Schematic of a normal fault: the hanging wall (right) has slipped down

A normal fault is a type of dip-slip fault in geology in which the block of rock above the fault plane — the hanging wall — moves downward relative to the block below, the footwall. Normal faults occur in response to tensional (extensional) stress, as the Earth's crust is pulled apart, so that the horizontal length across the fault increases.

Mechanics

Normal faulting is governed by Anderson's theory of faulting: when the greatest principal stress is vertical (as produced by the weight of overlying rock) and the least principal stress is horizontal, steep faults dipping at roughly 60° form, and the hanging wall slides down under gravity. The inclined fracture along which movement occurs is the fault plane; its angle to the horizontal is the dip.

Structures

Paired normal faults create characteristic extensional terrains:

  • Horst — an upstanding block left between two down-dropped blocks.
  • Graben — a down-dropped block between two normal faults, often forming a rift valley such as the East African Rift or the Upper Rhine Graben.
  • Half-graben — a basin bounded by one major fault, common in continental rifting and in the Basin and Range Province of the western United States.

Occurrence

Normal faults dominate at divergent plate boundaries such as mid-ocean ridges, in continental rift zones, and in regions of crustal extension behind mountain belts. Very large low-angle normal faults, called detachment faults, can expose deep crustal rocks in metamorphic core complexes.
