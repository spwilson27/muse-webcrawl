Tā moko

Tā moko is the permanent marking or "tattoo" as traditionally practised by Māori, the indigenous people of New Zealand. It is one of the five main Polynesian tattoo styles (the others being Samoan, Marquesan, Tahitian and Hawaiian). Tohunga-tā-moko (tattoo specialists) were considered inviolable and sacred.

Traditional practice

Unlike most tattooing, traditional tā moko was carved into the skin with uhi (chisels) made of albatross bone, leaving grooves rather than smooth punctures; pigment made from burnt wood or other materials was worked into the cuts. Men typically received moko on the face, buttocks (raparapa) and thighs (puhoro); women's moko kauae was worn on the lips and chin. The designs encode the wearer's genealogy (whakapapa), rank, and achievements, and are considered a taonga (treasure) of great cultural significance.

Decline and revival

The practice declined sharply under colonial influence and Christian missionary pressure in the 19th and 20th centuries. Since the 1970s–80s, tā moko has undergone a major revival as part of the Māori cultural renaissance, practised today with both traditional chisels and modern tattoo machines. Designs worn by non-Māori inspired by the style are sometimes distinguished as "kirituhi" (skin art) rather than moko, since moko proper carries genealogical meaning.
