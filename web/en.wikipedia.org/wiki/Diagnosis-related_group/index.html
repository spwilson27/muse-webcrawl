A diagnosis-related group (DRG) is a system to classify hospital cases into one of originally 467 groups, later expanded and revised, that are expected to use similar amounts of hospital resources. DRGs have been used in the United States since 1983 as the basis of Medicare's Inpatient Prospective Payment System, under which hospitals are paid a predetermined amount for each discharged patient according to the DRG assigned, rather than being reimbursed for their actual costs.

History

The system was developed at Yale University by Robert B. Fetter, John D. Thompson and their colleagues, initially as a tool for utilisation review and quality monitoring. It was adopted by the Health Care Financing Administration (now the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services) following the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982 and the Social Security Amendments of 1983.

Assignment

Each discharge is assigned to a DRG based on the principal diagnosis, secondary diagnoses (complications and comorbidities), surgical procedures performed, age, sex, and discharge status. The classification first divides cases into Major Diagnostic Categories (MDCs), usually organised by organ system, then into surgical and medical partitions. Each DRG carries a relative weight reflecting the average resources required to treat cases in that group; payment is the weight multiplied by a hospital-specific base rate.

Variants and use worldwide

The original DRGs have been repeatedly revised (MS-DRGs, APR-DRGs, and others), and analogous case-mix systems have been adopted in many countries, including Germany (G-DRG), France, Australia (AR-DRG), and England (Healthcare Resource Groups). Critics note incentives for "DRG creep" — coding cases into higher-paying groups — and for early discharge.
