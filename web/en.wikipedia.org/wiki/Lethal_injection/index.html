Lethal injection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lethal injection is a method of execution in which the condemned person is injected with a combination of drugs that induces unconsciousness, paralysis and death. Proposed in 1977 by Oklahoma's state medical examiner Jay Chapman and adopted by Oklahoma and Texas the same year, it was first used in Texas in December 1982 (Charles Brooks Jr.) and became the primary or sole method in most US death-penalty states and in China, Vietnam and a few other countries.

The classic protocol used three drugs (sodium thiopental, pancuronium bromide, potassium chloride). From about 2010, manufacturers — mostly European, under export controls — refused supply for executions, forcing states to alternative drugs (pentobarbital; midazolam in controversial executions in 2014), compounding pharmacies, and secrecy laws shielding suppliers. Litigation followed: Baze v. Rees (2008) upheld the three-drug protocol against an Eighth Amendment challenge; Glossip v. Gross (2015) upheld midazolam. Medical associations prohibit physician participation, leaving states to use other personnel — a standing ethical controversy.
