Standing committee

A standing committee is a permanent committee of a legislature (or other deliberative body) established under its rules to consider legislation and oversee policy in a defined subject area. Unlike select or special committees, which are temporary and appointed for a particular purpose, standing committees persist from session to session and form the workhorses of the legislative process: most bills are referred to a standing committee, which holds hearings, amends the text, and decides whether to report it to the full chamber.

In the United States Congress

Both the House of Representatives and the Senate maintain roughly twenty standing committees each, most with jurisdiction over broad policy areas such as agriculture, armed services, foreign affairs, the judiciary, and ways and means (House) or finance (Senate). Membership is allocated between the parties roughly in proportion to their strength in the chamber, and each committee is chaired by a member of the majority party. Committees are subdivided into subcommittees that handle narrower topics. Bills that a committee fails to report almost always die, giving committees and their chairs substantial gatekeeping power. In the Senate, standing committees also conduct confirmation proceedings for presidential nominees.

In other legislatures

The term is used broadly across parliamentary systems. In the United Kingdom, "standing committees" historically referred to the committees that examined bills in detail after second reading (now called public bill committees in the House of Commons), while select committees perform scrutiny. In Canada, India, Australia, and many European parliaments, standing committees are permanent bodies mirroring government departments, reviewing bills, estimates, and administration. The United Nations and many international organizations and professional bodies also use the term for permanent committees established by their governing documents.

Functions

Standing committees typically: (1) receive and consider proposed legislation within their jurisdiction; (2) hold hearings with officials, experts, and stakeholders; (3) conduct oversight of executive agencies; (4) investigate matters within their remit, sometimes with subpoena power; and (5) report recommendations to the parent body. Their small size relative to the full chamber lets members develop subject-matter expertise.
