Concerto for Orchestra (Bartók)

The Concerto for Orchestra, Sz. 116, BB 123, is a five-movement orchestral work composed by Béla Bartók in 1943. It is one of Bartók's best-known and most popular works, commissioned by the Koussevitzky Music Foundation at the instigation of conductor Serge Koussevitzky, who premiered it with the Boston Symphony Orchestra on 1 December 1944. Bartók called it a "concerto" rather than a symphony because individual instruments and groups of instruments are treated in a soloistic, virtuosic manner throughout.

Background

Bartók had emigrated to the United States in 1940 to escape the Second World War and was living in straitened circumstances, in poor health and composing little, when the commission arrived in 1943 (Fritz Reiner and Joseph Szigeti helped secure it). He wrote the concerto in about two months at Saranac Lake, New York, revising the finale in 1945. The work's success brought him welcome recognition and income in the last two years of his life; he died of leukemia in September 1945.

Movements

  1. Introduzione — a slow, mysterious introduction leads to an energetic Allegro vivace.
  2. Giuoco delle coppie ("Game of pairs") — pairs of wind instruments present a folk-like theme, each pair separated by a characteristic interval.
  3. Elegia — a sombre slow movement drawing on "night music" textures.
  4. Intermezzo interrotto — a lyrical theme is "interrupted" by a banal, quotation-like march tune, widely heard as a parody of Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony (or of a Lehár operetta melody).
  5. Finale — a whirlwind perpetuum mobile with folk-dance energy, ending in a brilliant fugato and blaze of brass.

Reception

The concerto's blend of folk material, neoclassical clarity, and orchestral brilliance made it a staple of the repertoire and the work through which many listeners first encounter Bartók. A performance lasts roughly 35–40 minutes.
