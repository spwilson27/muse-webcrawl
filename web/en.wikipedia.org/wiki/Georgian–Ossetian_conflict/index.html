Georgian–Ossetian conflict

The Georgian–Ossetian conflict is the ethno-political conflict between Georgians and Ossetians over the former South Ossetian Autonomous Oblast in Georgia, a region internationally recognised as part of Georgia but controlled since the early 1990s by the self-proclaimed Republic of South Ossetia, which is recognised and supported by Russia. It is one of the post-Soviet "frozen conflicts."

Origins

Tensions date to the Ossetian uprisings of 1918–1920 against the Democratic Republic of Georgia, which were suppressed with heavy loss of life and remain central to Ossetian collective memory. In the Soviet period, the South Ossetian Autonomous Oblast was created within the Georgian SSR in 1922.

The 1991–92 war

As the USSR collapsed, the South Ossetian leadership sought union with North Ossetia in Russia, while the Georgian government of Zviad Gamsakhurdia abolished the region's autonomy in December 1990. Fighting broke out in January 1991; by the time a Russian-brokered ceasefire (the Sochi agreement) was signed in June 1992, some 1,000–2,000 people had been killed and tens of thousands displaced. A joint Russian-Georgian-Ossetian peacekeeping force was deployed.

Escalation and the 2008 war

Relations deteriorated after Mikheil Saakashvili became president of Georgia in 2004, pledging to restore territorial integrity. Skirmishes intensified in the summer of 2008, and on the night of 7–8 August Georgia launched an assault on Tskhinvali. Russia intervened with a large-scale invasion — the Russo-Georgian War — after which Moscow recognised South Ossetia (and Abkhazia) as independent states. Georgia withdrew from the region entirely, and ethnic Georgians were expelled from South Ossetia.

Aftermath

Since 2008 the conflict has remained unresolved, with Russian troops stationed in South Ossetia, periodic "borderisation" — the erection of fences along the administrative boundary line — and negotiations conducted through the Geneva International Discussions. Georgia regards the region as Russian-occupied territory.
