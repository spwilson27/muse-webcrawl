In the visual arts and in geometry, perspective (from Latin perspicere, "to see through") is a set of techniques for representing three-dimensional objects and spaces on a two-dimensional surface so that the image approximates the view from a particular station point. Its central device is the vanishing point, the point on the horizon line where projections of parallel lines appear to converge.

History

Ancient Greek and Roman artists used foreshortening and rudimentary spatial cues, and Chinese and Japanese painting developed oblique projection traditions, but mathematical linear perspective was invented in Florence in the early 15th century. Around 1415 Filippo Brunelleschi demonstrated the principle with a painted panel of the Florence Baptistery; Leon Battista Alberti codified it in De pictura (1435), describing the picture plane as an open window. Masaccio's Holy Trinity (c. 1427) was among the first paintings to apply the system fully, and the technique spread across Europe with the Renaissance. In the 17th century, Gérard Desargues developed projective geometry, giving perspective its rigorous mathematical foundation.

Types

  • One-point perspective: one vanishing point; faces of objects are parallel to the picture plane. Used for corridors, railway tracks, streets.
  • Two-point perspective: two vanishing points on the horizon; used for corners of buildings viewed obliquely.
  • Three-point perspective: adds a vertical vanishing point; used for tall buildings seen from above or below.
  • Curvilinear (fisheye) perspective: multiple vanishing points on a curved picture plane, approximating wide fields of view.
  • Axonometric projection: parallel projection without vanishing points (isometric, dimetric, trimetric), common in technical drawing.
  • Atmospheric (aerial) perspective: the optical effect of distance — paler colours, reduced contrast — used alongside linear perspective for depth.

Modern use

Perspective projection is fundamental to 3D computer graphics, where virtual cameras render scenes through a projective transformation, and to photography, where the geometry of the lens determines perspective distortion. Artists since the 20th century have often deliberately rejected or subverted perspective — Cubism fractured it, while Surrealism played with its impossibilities, as in M. C. Escher's paradoxical constructions.
