Carib people

The Carib people, also known as the Kalinago in the Lesser Antilles and Kalina (Galibi) on the mainland, are an Indigenous people of the northeastern coast of South America and the Caribbean islands. At the time of European contact they inhabited the Lesser Antilles, parts of the Guianas and the Orinoco region. Their name gave the Caribbean Sea its name, and a distorted form of it ("caniba") is the origin of the word "cannibal".

History

Carib-speaking groups expanded from the South American mainland into the Antilles in the centuries before Columbus, partly displacing or absorbing the earlier Taíno (Island Arawak) inhabitants. Fierce resistance to European colonisation preserved Carib independence on islands such as Dominica and St. Vincent for centuries. On St. Vincent, Caribs mixed with shipwrecked and escaped Africans to form the Garifuna people, many of whom were deported by the British to Central America in 1797.

Culture

Traditional Carib society was organised in villages led by chiefs, with men and women famously reported to speak different registers — men's speech having Carib vocabulary and women's more Arawakan — reflecting the history of intermarriage. They were skilled seafarers and canoe builders, cultivating cassava, sweet potatoes and other crops, and fishing extensively.

Today

Descendant communities include the Kalinago of Dominica, who administer the Kalinago Territory, and the Kalina of French Guiana, Suriname and Venezuela. The Carib language family remains one of the largest in South America.
