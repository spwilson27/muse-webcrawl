Accountable care organization

An accountable care organization (ACO) is a group of doctors, hospitals and other health care providers in the United States who voluntarily come together to give coordinated, high-quality care to a defined population of patients — most prominently Medicare beneficiaries — and who share in savings (and sometimes losses) if they deliver that care at lower cost while meeting quality standards. The model was promoted by the Affordable Care Act of 2010 as an alternative to pure fee-for-service payment.

How it works

An ACO is assigned a population of at least 5,000 Medicare beneficiaries (in the Medicare Shared Savings Program, MSSP). A spending benchmark is set from historical costs; if the ACO's actual spending falls below the benchmark while quality scores — on measures such as preventive care, chronic disease control and patient experience — are met, the ACO receives a share of the savings. "Two-sided" or downside-risk tracks also make the ACO repay part of any losses.

History

The term was popularised by Dartmouth health policy researcher Elliott Fisher and colleagues in the mid-2000s. The Affordable Care Act authorised the MSSP beginning in 2012 and created the Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation, which ran the Pioneer ACO Model and later models such as Next Generation ACO and ACO REACH. Commercial insurers and Medicaid programmes have also adopted ACO-like contracts.

Evidence and criticism

Evaluations generally find modest net savings (typically 1–2 per cent) and small quality improvements, with stronger results from physician-led ACOs and those accepting downside risk. Critics note administrative complexity, benchmark ratcheting that penalises efficient providers over time, and the possibility that ACOs encourage consolidation among providers.
