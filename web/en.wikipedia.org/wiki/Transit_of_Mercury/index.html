Transit of Mercury

A transit of Mercury across the Sun takes place when the planet Mercury passes directly between the Sun and a superior planet, becoming visible against the solar disk. During a transit, Mercury appears as a small black dot moving slowly across the face of the Sun.

Occurrence

Transits of Mercury occur 13 or 14 times per century, always in May or November, when Mercury is near one of the nodes of its orbit at inferior conjunction. May transits recur at intervals of 13, 23, or 33 years; November transits at 7, 13, or 33 years. Recent transits occurred on 9 May 2016 and 11 November 2019; the next will be on 13 November 2032. Because Mercury's disk is only about 1/285 the Sun's apparent diameter, a telescope with a solar filter is needed to observe the event safely.

History

Johannes Kepler predicted the 1631 transit, which Pierre Gassendi observed — the first transit of any planet ever witnessed. Edmond Halley proposed using transits of Venus to measure the astronomical unit; Mercury transits were too small for precise timing but were later used to study the "black drop effect" and, in the space age, to calibrate instruments. Transits in 2003 and 2006 were observed by spacecraft including SOHO and Hinode.
