Blind rivet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A blind rivet, commonly known by the trade name pop rivet, is a tubular rivet with a mandrel through its centre that can be installed from one side of the workpiece only. This makes it ideal for applications where access to the back ("blind") side of the joint is impossible, such as joining sheet metal, panels and hollow sections.

How it works

The rivet is inserted into a drilled hole through the materials to be joined. A rivet gun grips the mandrel and pulls it: the mandrel's enlarged head deforms the tail of the rivet body on the blind side, clamping the sheets together, until the mandrel snaps off at a pre-set breaking point.

Types

Common variants include open-end rivets for general use, sealed (closed-end) rivets that are watertight, multi-grip rivets that tolerate a range of material thicknesses, and structural blind rivets with high shear and tensile strength. They are manufactured in aluminium, steel, stainless steel, copper and Monel.

History

The pop rivet was developed in the 1930s by the George Tucker Eyelet Company and popularised under the POP trademark; blind riveting became widespread in aircraft and construction during and after the Second World War. See also Rivet.
