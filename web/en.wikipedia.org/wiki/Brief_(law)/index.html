Brief (law)

[Image placeholder: bound legal briefs]

A brief (Old French bref, "short") is a written legal document used in various legal adversarial systems that is presented to a court arguing why one party to a particular case should prevail. In England and Wales (and other Commonwealth countries), the phrase refers to the instructions given to a barrister by a solicitor, setting out the facts of the case and the work to be done.

United States

In the United States, a brief is a written argument submitted to a court, typically on appeal, in which a party explains the facts of the case, the law that applies, and why the court should rule in its favour. Amicus curiae briefs are submitted by non-parties with an interest in the outcome. Major categories include:

  • Trial briefs or memoranda of law, submitted to trial courts on points of law
  • Appellate briefs, including an opening brief, a response brief, and a reply brief
  • Merits briefs before the Supreme Court of the United States, governed by strict rules on length, formatting, and even the colour of their covers (for example, amicus briefs supporting a party are bound in green)

A typical appellate brief contains a table of contents, a table of authorities, a statement of jurisdiction, the questions presented, a statement of the case and facts, a summary of argument, the argument itself, and a conclusion. Briefs are governed by rules of court such as the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure and may be subject to word limits.

Commonwealth usage

In England and Wales, "briefing" a barrister means instructing them to appear in court; the brief historically was tied with a ribbon (traditionally pink, hence "brief" and the expression to hold a brief). The document contains the solicitor's instructions, the evidence, and counsel's fee marking.
