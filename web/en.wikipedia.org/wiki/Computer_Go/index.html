Computer Go

Computer Go is the field of artificial intelligence dedicated to creating computer programs that play the board game Go. Go resisted strong computer play far longer than chess because of its enormous branching factor (around 250 legal moves per position) and the difficulty of constructing an evaluation function for Go positions.

History

Early programs from the 1960s through the 1990s played weakly, relying on hand-crafted knowledge and patterns. A breakthrough came in the 2000s with Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), used by programs such as Crazy Stone and MoGo, which reached strong amateur dan levels. In 2015–2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo, combining deep neural networks with MCTS and reinforcement learning, defeated European champion Fan Hui and then world-class professional Lee Sedol 4–1 in March 2016. Its successor AlphaGo Zero (2017) learned entirely by self-play and surpassed previous versions; open-source projects such as Leela Zero and KataGo later reproduced superhuman strength.

Techniques

Modern programs combine policy and value neural networks with tree search. Earlier approaches included pattern databases, influence functions, and expert systems. Handicaps were traditionally used to benchmark progress before programs reached professional parity.

Impact

AlphaGo's creative moves, such as move 37 in game two against Lee Sedol, influenced human professional play, and analysis tools based on strong engines are now standard in Go study.
