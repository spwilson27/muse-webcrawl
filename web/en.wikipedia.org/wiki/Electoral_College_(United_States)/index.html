United States Electoral College

The United States Electoral College is the group of presidential electors that formally elects the president and vice president. Each state receives electors equal to its congressional delegation (senators plus representatives); the District of Columbia receives three under the 23rd Amendment, for a total of 538. A majority of 270 electoral votes is required to win.

How it works

Voters in each state vote for a slate of electors pledged to a candidate; in 48 states the winner takes all electors, while Maine and Nebraska use the congressional district method. Electors meet in December in their state capitals; Congress counts the votes in January. "Faithless electors" occasionally vote contrary to their pledge, though many states penalise or replace them, upheld in Chiafalo v. Washington (2020).

Criticism and history

Five elections (1824, 1876, 1888, 2000, 2016) produced a president who lost the national popular vote. Debates pit federalism and stability arguments against democratic-majoritarian criticism; reform proposals include the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact.
