Raymond Thornton Chandler (1888–1959) was an American-British novelist and screenwriter. Raised and educated in England, he returned to the United States and, after losing his job as an oil executive during the Great Depression, turned to writing for pulp magazines. His novels about the Los Angeles private detective Philip Marlowe established him as a master of hardboiled crime fiction and one of the great prose stylists of the genre.

Life and career

Chandler was born in Chicago and schooled at Dulwich College, London. He worked briefly in the British civil service and as a journalist, served in the Canadian Army in the First World War, and afterwards rose to a well-paid vice-presidency in a Los Angeles oil syndicate — a career destroyed by the Depression and his drinking. From 1933 he published stories in Black Mask magazine, reworking ("cannibalising") them into his first novel, The Big Sleep (1939).

Works

Chandler wrote seven completed Marlowe novels: The Big Sleep, Farewell, My Lovely (1940), The High Window (1942), The Lady in the Lake (1943), The Little Sister (1949), The Long Goodbye (1953) — widely considered his masterpiece — and Playback (1958); an eighth, Poodle Springs, was left unfinished. In Hollywood he co-wrote Double Indemnity (1944) with Billy Wilder and wrote the original screenplay The Blue Dahlia (1946). His 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder" remains a manifesto of the hardboiled school.

Style and influence

Chandler's writing is distinguished by its vivid similes, laconic dialogue and moralised vision of a corrupt city through which the detective moves — in his famous phrase — as a knight: "down these mean streets a man must go who is not himself mean". Humphrey Bogart's portrayal of Marlowe fixed the character in popular culture. Chandler's influence runs through all subsequent crime writing, including the television and literary tradition of Nordic noir, whose bleak detectives inherit Marlowe's combination of integrity and melancholy.
