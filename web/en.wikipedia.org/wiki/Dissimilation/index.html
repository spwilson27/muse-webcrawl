Dissimilation

In phonology, dissimilation is a phenomenon whereby similar consonant or vowel sounds in a word become less similar. For example, English February is often pronounced without the first /r/, influenced by the second /r/; and "chimney" is sometimes heard as "chimley". Dissimilation is the opposite of assimilation.

Dissimilation
Field: Historical linguistics / phonology
Opposite: Assimilation

Types

Dissimilation can be anticipatory (a sound changes because of a later similar sound) or lag (because of an earlier one), and may affect place, manner or voicing. A celebrated case is Grassmann's law in Greek and Sanskrit, where aspirated consonants lose aspiration when another aspirate follows in the next syllable. Latin suffix -alis becomes -aris when the stem already contains an /l/ (e.g. solaris).

Causes

Explanations include avoidance of repetition in articulation and perception, and hypercorrection. Unlike assimilation, dissimilation is often sporadic and lexical rather than a regular sound change.

Examples

  • Latin peregrinus → English "pilgrim" (r…r → l…r)
  • Latin arborem → Spanish árbol
  • English "library" sometimes pronounced "libry" / with r-loss
