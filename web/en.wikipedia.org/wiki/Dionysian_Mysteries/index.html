Dionysian Mysteries

Initiation scenes from the Villa of the Mysteries at Pompeii.

The Dionysian Mysteries were initiatory rituals of ancient Greece and Rome centred on the god Dionysus (Roman Bacchus), god of wine, ecstasy and theatre. Like the better-known Eleusinian Mysteries, they promised initiates a transformed relationship with death and the hope of a blessed afterlife, and their rites were kept secret from the uninitiated.

Rites and practices

The mysteries drew on the myth of Dionysus himself — twice-born, dismembered by the Titans in the Orphic version and reborn — as a pattern of death and renewal. Initiation appears to have involved ecstatic music and dance, the handling of sacred objects (the liknon, a winnowing basket containing a phallus, among them), and ritual intoxication. Followers, called Bacchants or Maenads in mythology, formed associations known as thiasoi.

Suppression at Rome

In 186 BC the Roman Senate, alarmed by reports of the cult's secrecy and alleged crimes, issued the Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus, severely restricting Bacchic worship in Italy — an episode narrated in detail by the historian Livy.

Legacy

The great fresco cycle of the Villa of the Mysteries at Pompeii is usually interpreted as depicting Dionysian initiation, and remains the richest visual evidence for the rites. The mysteries influenced later thinking about ecstatic religion; Nietzsche's contrast of the Dionysian and Apollonian in The Birth of Tragedy drew on the figure of Dionysus as the god of ecstatic dissolution of the self.
