Nastaʿlīq script

Nastaʿlīq
Type: Abjad calligraphic style
Languages: Persian, Urdu, Punjabi (Shahmukhi), Kashmiri, Ottoman Turkish
Developed: 14th–15th century, Persia
Direction: Right to left

Nastaʿlīq (Persian: نستعلیق, from naskh + taʿlīq) is one of the main calligraphic styles used for writing the Perso-Arabic script. Developed in Persia in the 14th and 15th centuries, it is the predominant style for written Persian historically and remains the preferred script for Urdu, for which it is a defining visual feature.

Characteristics

Nastaʿlīq is renowned for its fluid, cascading appearance: words slope downward from right to left, letters within a word connect along a curving baseline, and short vertical strokes contrast with long, sweeping horizontal flourishes. Letters are written with variable depth, and words frequently stack vertically, giving the script a distinctive "hanging" quality. These features make it exceptionally beautiful but also technically demanding: it was historically difficult to typeset, and early movable-type Urdu printing often used the simpler Naskh style instead.

History

The style is traditionally attributed to the calligrapher Mir Ali Tabrizi (died c. 1420) of Tabriz, who combined elements of Naskh and Taʿlīq. From Persia it spread eastward with Persianate culture to Mughal India, where it became the courtly hand for Persian and later for Urdu, and to the Ottoman world. It remains central to the literary cultures of Iran, Pakistan, and Urdu-speaking India.

Typography and computing

Because of its complex contextual shaping and vertical stacking, Nastaʿlīq posed major challenges for digital typesetting. Modern font technology and text layout engines now render Nastaʿlīq on screens, and it is widely used in digital Urdu publishing, though designers continue to balance authentic calligraphic stacking against line-height practicality.
