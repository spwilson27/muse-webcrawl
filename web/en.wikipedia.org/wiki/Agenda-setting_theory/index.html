Agenda-setting theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Agenda-setting theory holds that the mass media influence public opinion not mainly by telling people what to think, but by telling them what to think about: the issues the media emphasise become the issues the public perceives as most important. The theory was formally introduced by Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw in their study of the 1968 United States presidential election in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, published in 1972; they found a near-perfect correlation between the issue priorities of undecided voters and the emphasis those issues received in the news media the voters used.

Levels of agenda setting

First-level agenda setting concerns the salience of issues or objects (the economy, crime, a candidate). Second-level agenda setting concerns the salience of attributes: by emphasising certain qualities of an issue or candidate, media coverage shapes how the public evaluates them — a bridge to the concept of framing. A third level, network agenda setting, studies how bundles of associated issues and attributes are transferred together from media to public. Related research examines intermedia agenda setting (elite outlets such as the New York Times setting the agenda of other media) and the question of "who sets the media's agenda" — often political actors, public relations and events themselves.

Evidence and scope

Hundreds of studies, experiments and election analyses have supported the effect, with strength depending on the issue: agenda setting works best for "unobtrusive" issues the public cannot experience directly (such as foreign affairs), and individuals high in "need for orientation" are most susceptible. In the era of social media and fragmented audiences, scholars debate how far a single public agenda still forms, while noting new dynamics such as reverse agenda setting, in which public attention online drives news coverage.
