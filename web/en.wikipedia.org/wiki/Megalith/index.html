Megalith

[Image: The trilithons of Stonehenge, England]
Stonehenge, the most famous megalithic monument

A megalith is a large stone that has been used to construct a prehistoric structure or monument, either alone or together with other stones. The word derives from the Ancient Greek megas ("great") and lithos ("stone"). Most extant megalithic structures were built during the Neolithic and Chalcolithic periods (roughly 10,000–2000 BC in different regions), though some, such as those of Indonesia and Easter Island, are much later.

Types

Single standing stones are called menhirs; groups form stone circles (as at Stonehenge and Avebury), stone rows (such as the Carnac stones in Brittany), and chambered tombs, of which the dolmen — a flat capstone supported by uprights — is the most widespread type. Passage graves such as Newgrange in Ireland (c. 3200 BC, older than Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids) and the temples of Malta (Ġgantija, c. 3600 BC) are among the earliest monumental stone buildings anywhere. Megalith-building was often associated with burial, ancestor veneration and astronomical alignments on solstices and equinoxes.

Distribution

Megalithic traditions are nearly worldwide: across Atlantic and Mediterranean Europe, the Middle East (dolmen fields of the Golan and Jordan), North Africa, India (Vidarbha and the northeast), Southeast Asia (the Plain of Jars in Laos; living megalithic cultures in Sumba and Nias), Korea (whose dolmens are UNESCO-listed and number in the tens of thousands), Japan, and the Americas. How similar forms arose independently in so many places remains debated; early twentieth-century diffusionist theories that spread all megaliths from a single source have been rejected by radiocarbon dating.
