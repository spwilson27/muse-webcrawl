Asahel Nettleton (21 April 1783 – 16 May 1844) was an American Congregational theologian and evangelist, one of the leading figures of the Second Great Awakening in New England. His revivals are credited with some 30,000 conversions, and he was a principal conservative opponent of the "new measures" revivalism of Charles Grandison Finney. He also compiled the influential hymnal Village Hymns for Social Worship (1824).

Life and ministry

Nettleton was born in Killingworth (now Clinton), Connecticut. Converted during the revivals around 1800, he abandoned plans for medical missionary work and studied at Yale under its president Timothy Dwight, graduating in 1809. Ordained in 1811, he never held a settled pastorate but worked as an itinerant evangelist across Connecticut, Massachusetts, New York and Virginia, conducting protracted town revivals characterised by quiet, orderly preaching, house-to-house visiting and careful inquiry meetings — in deliberate contrast to the emotional excitement of frontier camp meetings.

Controversy with Finney

Nettleton's declining health after 1822 limited his preaching, but he became the elder statesman of the "Old School" New England revival tradition. He opposed Finney's techniques — the anxious bench, protracted meetings with high-pressure appeals, and the underlying Arminian theology — and was a central figure in the New Lebanon Convention of 1827, called to settle the disputes between the rival revival parties. His critique helped define the divide between eastern, church-centred revivalism and the newer western methods.

Later years and legacy

In 1833 Nettleton married and settled in East Windsor, Connecticut, where he mentored young ministers. His hymnal Village Hymns went through many editions, and after his death Bennet Tyler published his biography, Memoir of the Life and Character of Rev. Asahel Nettleton, D.D. (1844). Historians regard Nettleton as the foremost American evangelist between Jonathan Edwards's era and Finney's, and his writings influenced later Reformed evangelicalism.
