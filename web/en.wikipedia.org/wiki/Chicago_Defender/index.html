The Chicago Defender is an African-American newspaper founded in Chicago in 1905 by Robert S. Abbott. One of the most influential Black newspapers in United States history, it is credited with encouraging the Great Migration of African Americans from the rural South to northern industrial cities.

History

Abbott started the paper with a small investment and built it into a national voice against lynching, Jim Crow segregation, and racial violence, using sensational headlines and a militant editorial tone. Pullman porters distributed the paper secretly throughout the South, where it was often banned. Its campaigns — including the "Great Northern Drive" of 1917 — urged Black southerners to move north for jobs and safety; more than a million did so in the decades that followed.

Influence

The Defender launched the careers of writers such as Langston Hughes and Gwendolyn Brooks, campaigned for anti-lynching legislation, and fought to integrate professional sports and the armed forces. John H. Sengstacke, Abbott's nephew, succeeded him in 1940 and founded the National Newspaper Publishers Association. The paper became a daily in 1956, later returned to weekly publication, and ended its print edition in 2019, continuing online.
