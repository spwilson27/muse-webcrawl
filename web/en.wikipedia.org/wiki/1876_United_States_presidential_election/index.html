The 1876 United States presidential election was the 23rd quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, November 7, 1876. It was one of the most contentious and controversial elections in American history. Democratic Governor Samuel J. Tilden of New York won a majority of the popular vote, but Republican Governor Rutherford B. Hayes of Ohio won the presidency by a single electoral vote after a disputed count and a controversial political bargain.

The dispute

Tilden won the popular vote by about 250,000 votes (roughly 3 percentage points) and initially appeared to hold 184 electoral votes, one short of a majority, while Hayes held 165. Twenty electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon were disputed, with rival slates of electors submitted from the three Southern states, where Reconstruction governments and Democratic "redeemers" both claimed victory amid violence and fraud.

Resolution

Congress created a 15-member Electoral Commission, which awarded all 20 disputed votes to Hayes along strict party lines, 8–7. The result was accepted by Democrats only after the informal Compromise of 1877, in which Republicans agreed to withdraw the remaining federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction, in exchange for Democratic acquiescence to Hayes's inauguration. Hayes took office on March 5, 1877.

Legacy

The election is often described as the "stolen election" of 1876. The withdrawal of federal troops ended Reconstruction and opened the way for decades of disenfranchisement of African Americans in the South under Jim Crow laws. The crisis also led to the Electoral Count Act of 1887, governing how Congress counts electoral votes.
