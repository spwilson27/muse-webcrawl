Cane Ridge Revival

The Cane Ridge Revival was a large camp meeting held in August 1801 at Cane Ridge, near Paris in Bourbon County, Kentucky, United States. Drawing crowds estimated at 10,000 to 25,000 people, it was the largest and most famous meeting of the Second Great Awakening and a landmark in the development of the Restoration Movement.

Background

The revival grew out of the "Red River Revival" of 1800 and the sacramental camp meetings pioneered in Kentucky by Presbyterian minister James McGready. Barton W. Stone, pastor of the Cane Ridge and Concord Presbyterian congregations, organized what was planned as a Presbyterian communion season; news of earlier meetings drew a vast crowd from Kentucky, Ohio, and Tennessee at a time when Kentucky's total population was only about 220,000.

The meeting

Beginning on August 6, 1801, preaching continued day and night for nearly a week from multiple stands in the woods around the log meeting house. Presbyterian, Methodist, and Baptist ministers preached simultaneously to different parts of the crowd. The gathering became famous for dramatic physical manifestations — falling, jerking, barking, dancing, and ecstatic laughter — which participants interpreted as the work of the Holy Spirit and critics as religious excess.

Consequences

Cane Ridge accelerated the spread of the camp meeting as the signature revival technique of the American frontier and swelled Methodist and Baptist membership. It also deepened doctrinal conflict: Stone and several colleagues broke with the Presbyterian Synod of Kentucky in 1803, forming the Springfield Presbytery and then dissolving it with the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery (1804), a founding document of the Restoration Movement from which the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) and Churches of Christ descend.

The log meeting house is preserved as a shrine within a stone memorial building erected in 1932.
