Philae (spacecraft)

Operator: European Space Agency / DLR

Launch: March 2, 2004 (aboard with Rosetta)

Landing: November 12, 2014, comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko

Mass: ~100 kg

Philae was a robotic lander built by the European Space Agency and the German Aerospace Center (DLR) that accompanied the Rosetta orbiter to comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko. On 12 November 2014, Philae achieved the first-ever soft landing on a comet nucleus, a landmark in space exploration. The lander was named after the island of Philae in the Nile, where an obelisk bearing a bilingual inscription helped in the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs — a counterpart to the Rosetta Stone for which the orbiter was named.

The landing

After a ten-year journey and several months orbiting the comet, Philae separated from Rosetta and descended toward the landing site Agilkia. Its anchoring harpoons and hold-down thruster failed to fire, and the lander bounced twice across the surface over a period of about two hours before coming to rest in the shadow of a cliff at a site later named Abydos, tilted and with only some of its three legs in contact. Despite the unplanned landing, Philae carried out its primary battery-powered science programme over roughly 57–64 hours, returning data from all ten instruments — including the CONSERT radar (which probed the comet's interior with Rosetta), the COSAC and PTOLEMY analysers (which sniffed organic compounds), cameras, and a drill (SD2) — before its batteries were exhausted and it fell silent on 15 November 2014.

Aftermath

Philae briefly re-established contact in June and July 2015 as the comet approached perihelion and sunlight reached its solar panels, but communications could not be sustained. Its exact location remained unknown until Rosetta's OSIRIS camera imaged it in September 2016, weeks before the orbiter's own mission-ending descent to the comet. Philae's measurements informed studies of the comet's composition, including the detection of organic molecules and the finding that its water differs in deuterium content from Earth's oceans.
