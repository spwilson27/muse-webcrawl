Sexual ethics

Sexual ethics (or sex ethics) is the branch of philosophy concerned with the moral evaluation of human sexual behaviour and relationships. It addresses questions of consent, fidelity, marriage, reproduction, sexual orientation and the social norms governing sexual conduct, and it has been shaped by religious teaching, legal tradition and changing social attitudes.

Consent

Most contemporary frameworks place informed consent at the centre of sexual ethics: sexual activity is considered permissible when all parties are competent adults who freely agree to it, and wrong when it involves coercion, deception or persons unable to consent. Laws on the age of consent and sexual assault reflect this principle, though they vary between jurisdictions.

Religious approaches

The Abrahamic religions have traditionally confined legitimate sexual activity to marriage, typically monogamous and oriented toward procreation; the Catholic Church additionally prohibits contraception under its natural law teaching. Eastern traditions vary widely: Buddhism evaluates sexual conduct by whether it causes harm or breaches commitments, while Hindu ethics tie sexuality to the householder stage of life.

Modern debates

The "sexual revolution" of the 1960s, the contraceptive pill and the decriminalisation of homosexuality reshaped Western sexual norms. Feminist writers have debated pornography and sex work; other contested topics include premarital sex, polyamory, commercial surrogacy and the ethics of dating technology. Philosophers such as Roger Scruton defended traditional accounts of desire and commitment, while others have argued for a purely consent-based framework.
