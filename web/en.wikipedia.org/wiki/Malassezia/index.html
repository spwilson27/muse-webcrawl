Malassezia

Malassezia is a genus of fungi in the family Malasseziaceae, named after the French scientist Louis-Charles Malassez. Malassezia species are lipophilic yeasts that live naturally on the skin surfaces of many warm-blooded animals, including humans, where they form part of the normal skin microbiome and feed on the lipids of sebum.

Species and biology

Around eighteen species are recognised, the most common on human skin being Malassezia globosa, M. restricta, M. sympodialis, and M. furfur. Most species cannot synthesise their own fatty acids and therefore depend on external lipid sources, which is why they cluster in sebum-rich areas such as the scalp, face, and upper trunk. Their genomes, sequenced in the 2000s, confirmed the loss of fatty-acid synthase genes.

Associated conditions

Although normally commensal, Malassezia overgrowth or host sensitivity is implicated in several common skin conditions: dandruff and seborrhoeic dermatitis, pityriasis (tinea) versicolor, Malassezia folliculitis ("fungal acne"), and possibly exacerbations of atopic dermatitis and psoriasis. In premature infants and immunocompromised patients receiving lipid infusions, M. furfur can cause bloodstream infections. In veterinary medicine, M. pachydermatis is a well-known cause of ear and skin infections in dogs.

Treatment

Conditions associated with Malassezia generally respond to topical antifungal agents such as ketoconazole, selenium sulfide, or zinc pyrithione shampoos; widespread or systemic cases may require oral azole antifungals.
