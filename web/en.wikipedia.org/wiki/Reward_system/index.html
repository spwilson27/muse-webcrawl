Reward system


Illustration placeholder: the mesolimbic pathway from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens

The reward system (the mesocorticolimbic circuit) is a group of neural structures responsible for incentive salience (i.e., "wanting"; desire or craving for a reward), associative learning (primarily positive reinforcement and classical conditioning), and positively-valenced emotions, particularly ones involving pleasure as a core component (e.g., joy, euphoria, and ecstasy).

Reward is the attractive and motivational property of a stimulus that induces appetitive behavior, also known as approach behavior, and consummatory behavior. In describing reward, the rewarding stimulus consists of the object itself; a rewarding stimulus has been described as "any stimulus, object, event, activity, or situation that has the potential to make us approach and consume it".

Anatomy

The primary reward circuitry comprises dopaminergic projections of the mesolimbic pathway from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) in the ventral striatum, together with projections to the amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and the mesocortical pathway to frontal areas. Natural rewards — food, sex, social interaction — and almost all addictive drugs activate this system and increase dopamine in the nucleus accumbens.

Wanting and liking

Research by Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson distinguishes "wanting" (incentive salience, mediated largely by dopamine) from "liking" (hedonic pleasure, mediated by small opioid "hedonic hotspots"). In addiction, sensitised wanting can persist and intensify even as liking diminishes, a dissociation central to the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction.

Learning and prediction error

Dopamine neurons encode reward prediction errors: they fire to unexpected rewards, shift to firing at reward-predicting cues, and pause when an expected reward fails to arrive. This signal, demonstrated by Wolfram Schultz and colleagues, links the reward system to reinforcement learning in both neuroscience and artificial intelligence.

History

The system was discovered serendipitously by James Olds and Peter Milner in 1954, when rats with electrodes in the septal area (near the nucleus accumbens) would repeatedly press a lever to stimulate their own brains, in some experiments to the exclusion of food.

Clinical relevance

Dysregulation of the reward system is implicated in addiction, depression (anhedonia), schizophrenia, and obesity. Treatments ranging from dopamine antagonists to opioid antagonists such as naltrexone target components of this circuitry.
