Lobopodia (from Greek lobos, lobe, and pous, foot) is a grouping of soft-bodied panarthropod animals characterised by stubby, unjointed legs called lobopods — fluid-filled limbs that end in claws and are moved by hydrostatic pressure rather than by jointed levers. The group is best known from Cambrian marine fossils such as Aysheaia, Hallucigenia and Opabinia-like forms from the Burgess Shale and Chengjiang deposits, and it encompasses the ancestry of the three living panarthropod phyla: Onychophora (velvet worms), Tardigrada (water bears) and Arthropoda.

Evolutionary significance

Lobopodians occupy a pivotal place in understanding the origin of arthropods. Fossil forms show a graded series of features — soft lobopod limbs, then limbs with sclerotised plates, then fully jointed appendages in the gilled lobopodians and radiodont relatives on the arthropod stem — documenting how the arthropod body plan was assembled. Living velvet worms are essentially terrestrial lobopodians, retaining the lobopod limbs, soft cuticle and slime glands of their Cambrian relatives. Because "Lobopodia" includes ancestors but not all descendants of the common ancestor, the group is paraphyletic and is used mainly as an informal grade name; modern classifications place its members within Panarthropoda, usually as stem-group onychophorans, tardigrades and arthropods. Famous members include the heavily armoured Diania ("walking cactus"), the spiny Hallucigenia, and the giant predatory-adjacent forms Jianshanopodia and Siberion.
