Egg hunt

An egg hunt is a traditional Easter game in which decorated eggs — real hard-boiled eggs, chocolate eggs, or plastic eggs filled with candy — are hidden outdoors or indoors for children to find. The child who collects the most eggs, or who finds a specially marked "prize egg", is declared the winner.

Origins

The egg has been a pre-Christian symbol of fertility and rebirth, and springtime egg customs were adopted into Easter celebrations as the egg came to symbolize the Resurrection and the empty tomb. Egg hunts are particularly associated with German tradition; legend credits the Protestant reformer Martin Luther with organizing egg hunts for his congregation, in which men hid eggs for women and children to find. German immigrants brought the custom, together with the Easter hare (the precursor of the Easter Bunny), to Pennsylvania in the 1700s.

Traditions

Eggs for the hunt are traditionally dyed or painted in bright colors, a practice known as pysanky in Ukrainian culture when done with elaborate wax-resist designs. Modern hunts often use plastic eggs filled with candy, small toys, or money. Many communities, churches, and schools organize public hunts; among the best known is the White House Easter Egg Roll in Washington, D.C., an annual event dating to 1878, in which children roll eggs across the South Lawn with spoons.

Records and variations

Large-scale hunts draw thousands of participants; the largest egg hunt on record took place in Winter Park, Florida, in 2007, when 9,753 children searched for 501,000 eggs. Some hunts incorporate egg rolling or egg tapping contests, flashlight hunts for older children at night, and accessible "beeping egg" hunts for visually impaired children. The phrase "Easter egg" has also been borrowed in media and computing to mean any hidden message, feature, or inside joke planted by creators for users to discover.
