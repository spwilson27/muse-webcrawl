Mercenary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mercenary is a private individual who joins an armed conflict for personal profit, is otherwise an outsider to the conflict, and is not a member of any other official military. Mercenaries fight for money or other forms of payment rather than for political interests. Beginning in the 20th century, mercenaries have increasingly come to be seen as less entitled to protections by rules of war than non-mercenaries. Indeed, the Geneva Conventions declare that mercenaries are not recognised as legitimate combatants and do not have to be granted the same legal protections as captured service personnel of the armed forces.

History

The employment of foreign soldiers is ancient: Egyptian pharaohs hired Nubian and Libyan troops, Xenophon's Anabasis recounts the march of the Greek "Ten Thousand" hired by Cyrus the Younger, and Carthage relied heavily on mercenary armies. In medieval Europe, free companies of soldiers sold their services between campaigns. Renaissance Italy's wars were fought largely by condottieri, captains under contract (condotta). Swiss pikemen — the Reisläufer — were Europe's most sought-after infantry for two centuries, and German Landsknechte imitated them. Hessian troops hired by Britain fought in the American Revolutionary War.

The rise of the nation-state and mass conscript armies in the 19th century marginalised mercenaries, but they returned prominently in the post-colonial wars in Africa in the 1960s (the Congo Crisis, Biafra) and again after the Cold War through private military companies such as Executive Outcomes, Sandline International, Blackwater (later Academi), and Russia's Wagner Group.

International law

Article 47 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977) defines a mercenary by six cumulative criteria, including being specially recruited to fight in an armed conflict, taking a direct part in hostilities, and being motivated by the desire for private gain and promised material compensation substantially in excess of that paid to combatants of similar rank in the same armed forces. A mercenary does not have the right to be a combatant or a prisoner of war. The 1989 UN Mercenary Convention criminalises the recruitment, use, financing and training of mercenaries, though it has been ratified by relatively few states and none of the major military powers.
