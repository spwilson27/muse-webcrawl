"The Overcoat" (Russian: Шинель, romanized: Shinel) is a short story by Ukrainian-born Russian author Nikolai Gogol, published in 1842. It tells of the life and death of Akaky Akakievich Bashmachkin, an impoverished, downtrodden copying clerk in St. Petersburg whose existence is transformed — and destroyed — by the acquisition of a magnificent new overcoat. Widely regarded as Gogol's masterpiece in short fiction and as a founding text of Russian literary realism, it is traditionally credited with the remark attributed to Dostoevsky: "We all come out from under Gogol's Overcoat."

Plot

Akaky Akakievich, a titular counsellor in a government department, is a meek copyist mocked by younger colleagues for his shabby appearance and total absorption in the mechanical copying of documents — work he loves for its own sake. When the Petersburg frost makes his threadbare coat useless, the tailor Petrovich declares it beyond repair and insists on making a new one for the ruinous sum of 80 roubles. By months of starvation-level saving — walking on tiptoe to spare his shoe leather, going without tea and candles — Akaky accumulates the money, and the overcoat, finished with a thick collar and sturdy lining, becomes the radiant centre of his life. His colleagues congratulate him and even host a small celebration in his honour.

That same night, walking home across a deserted square, Akaky is accosted by two men with moustaches who strip the coat from his back. Appeals to the night watchman, to a district police chief and finally to an arrogant "Person of Consequence" — a general who berates him for his importunity — all fail. Akaky, exposed to the cold without the coat, falls ill and dies, having received a letter of call to a clerkship only in delirium. His death is barely noticed at the office. Soon afterwards, rumours spread of a ghost haunting the Kalinkin Bridge, tearing overcoats from passers-by regardless of rank. The ghost at last confronts the Person of Consequence himself, seizes his splendid coat, and is never seen again.

Significance

The story combines social satire of the imperial bureaucracy with sympathy for the "little man" crushed by it, and its ending converts realism into ghost story. Vladimir Nabokov called Gogol the greatest artist Russia has yet produced and praised the story's grotesque, dreamlike depth beneath its comic surface. "The Overcoat" has been adapted repeatedly for theatre, ballet, opera and film, including a celebrated 1926 Soviet silent film, and its title phrase entered Russian as a byword for the plight of the humble clerk.
