The Harwell computer, later known as the WITCH (Wolverhampton Instrument for Teaching Computation from Harwell), is one of the earliest British digital computers. It was built at the Atomic Energy Research Establishment at Harwell, Oxfordshire, and first ran in 1951.

Design

Unlike many contemporary machines built around vacuum tubes (valves), the Harwell computer used dekatron counting tubes and telephone-style relays, giving it a reputation for great reliability though modest speed. It worked in decimal, and could run unattended for long periods; contemporary reports describe it operating over weekends without supervision, unusual for computers of the era.

Later history

In 1957 the machine was donated to Wolverhampton and Staffordshire College of Technology, where it was renamed the WITCH and used in teaching until 1973. It was later displayed at the Museum of Science and Industry in Birmingham. From 2009 it was restored at The National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park, and in 2012 it was restarted and recognised as the world's oldest original working digital computer.
