Foundation (nonprofit)

Foundations typically fund their activities from an endowment.

A foundation (also a charitable foundation) is a category of nonprofit organisation or charitable trust that typically provides funding and support for other charitable organisations through grants, while also potentially participating directly in charitable activities. Foundations include public charitable foundations, such as community foundations, and private foundations, which are typically endowed by an individual or family.

Private foundations, in the United States at least, must distribute about 5% of their assets each year for charitable purposes. Among the largest foundations in the world are the Gates Foundation, the Novo Nordisk Foundation, the Wellcome Trust, the Ford Foundation and the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation.

Types

  • Private (family/corporate) foundations — endowed by a family, individual or company; e.g. the Rockefeller Foundation (1913) and Carnegie Corporation (1911), pioneers of modern philanthropy.
  • Community foundations — pool donations from many donors in a geographic area; the first, the Cleveland Foundation, dates to 1914.
  • Corporate foundations — funded and closely tied to a parent company.
  • Operating foundations — run their own programmes rather than (or in addition to) making grants.

Legal structures

The term derives from civil-law jurisdictions: the German Stiftung, Dutch stichting and French fondation are legal persons created by dedicating assets to a purpose, with no owners or members. In common-law countries the same function is often served by a charitable trust or a nonprofit corporation. In the European Union, foundations are important funders of research, culture and social welfare; in Germany alone there are more than 20,000 foundations. Regulation generally requires purposes to be charitable or public-benefit, restricts political activity and self-dealing, and grants tax advantages to the foundation and its donors.

Criticism

Foundations have been criticised as unaccountable vehicles through which wealthy donors exercise outsized influence over public policy ("plutocracy" critiques), for perpetuity that outlives donors' intentions, and for payout rates that prioritise asset preservation. Defenders point to their role in funding long-term, high-risk or unpopular causes that governments and markets neglect.
