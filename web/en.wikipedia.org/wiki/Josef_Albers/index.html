Josef Albers (March 19, 1888 – March 25, 1976) was a German-born American artist and educator. He is considered one of the most influential teachers of the visual arts in the twentieth century, known especially for his work on color theory and his long-running painting series Homage to the Square.

Early life and the Bauhaus

Albers was born in Bottrop, Westphalia, into a family of craftsmen. He trained and worked as a schoolteacher before studying art in Berlin, Essen, and Munich. In 1920 he enrolled at the Bauhaus in Weimar, where he initially studied painting and stained glass. He joined the Bauhaus faculty in 1923, teaching the preliminary course (Vorkurs) on form and materials, and later headed the glass workshop. When the Bauhaus moved to Dessau and then Berlin, Albers remained on the faculty until the school closed under Nazi pressure in 1933.

Emigration and Black Mountain College

In 1933 Albers and his wife, the textile artist Anni Albers, emigrated to the United States at the invitation of Black Mountain College in North Carolina, where he led the art program until 1949. His teaching emphasized disciplined experimentation with materials and the perception of color, famously summarized in the dictum that he wanted students to "open eyes."

Yale and Interaction of Color

From 1950 to 1958 Albers chaired the Department of Design at Yale University. In 1963 he published Interaction of Color, a landmark study demonstrating the relativity of color perception — that a single color can appear as two different colors, and two different colors can be made to look alike, depending on context. The book became a standard text in art and design education.

Homage to the Square

Beginning in 1949 and continuing until his death, Albers produced the Homage to the Square series: more than a thousand paintings and prints based on a fixed composition of three or four nested squares in differing colors. The rigid format served as a laboratory for studying how colors interact optically, each canvas recording only the pigments used, inscribed on the back.

Legacy

Albers died in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1976. His work bridged European modernism and American abstract painting, and his pedagogy influenced generations of artists, including many associated with Op art and Minimalism. The Josef and Anni Albers Foundation, based in Bethany, Connecticut, preserves his legacy.
