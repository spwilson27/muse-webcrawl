The Later Zhao (simplified Chinese: 后赵; traditional Chinese: 後趙; pinyin: Hòu Zhào; 319–351) was a dynasty of Imperial China, one of the Sixteen Kingdoms during the Jin dynasty period. It was founded by the Shi family of the Jie ethnicity. The Jie were a Central Asian tribe, probably of Yeniseian or Iranian affiliation, who had been settled in China during the Han and Jin dynasties. At its height, Later Zhao ruled most of northern China, and its founder Shi Le and his nephew Shi Hu are remembered both for unifying the north and for the exceptional brutality of their rule.

History

Shi Le (274–333) rose from slavery to become a bandit leader and then a general under the Xiongnu-led Han-Zhao dynasty during the upheavals that destroyed Western Jin control of the north. In 319 he broke with Han-Zhao and proclaimed himself King of Zhao, establishing his capital at Xiangguo (modern Xingtai, Hebei). After destroying Han-Zhao in 329, he took the imperial title and ruled a realm stretching across the North China Plain. Shi Le is credited with administrative reforms and with patronising the Buddhist monk Fotudeng, through whom Buddhism gained influence at court.

On Shi Le's death in 333, his nephew Shi Hu seized power in a coup (334) and moved the capital to Ye (near modern Linzhang, Hebei). Shi Hu's reign (334–349) was notorious for extravagant palace building, mass conscription of labour, and extreme cruelty; hundreds of thousands are said to have died in his construction projects. He also raised taxes heavily and mobilised armies against the Eastern Jin in the south and Former Yan in the northeast, without decisive success.

After Shi Hu's death in 349, his sons fought for the throne, and the Han Chinese general Ran Min seized power in 350, founding the short-lived Ran Wei state and ordering the massacre of the Jie and other non-Han peoples in Ye. Later Zhao remnants were extinguished by 351, and its territory was divided between Former Yan and Eastern Jin. Ran Min's slaughter is remembered as one of the great atrocities of the period; the Jie largely disappear from history thereafter.
