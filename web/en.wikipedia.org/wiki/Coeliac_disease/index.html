**Coeliac disease** (spelled celiac disease in American English) is a long-term autoimmune disorder in which eating gluten — the protein complex in wheat, barley and rye — triggers an immune attack that damages the lining of the small intestine (villous atrophy), impairing absorption of nutrients. It affects roughly 1% of people in many populations, though many cases remain undiagnosed.

Symptoms vary widely: diarrhoea, bloating, abdominal pain, fatigue, weight loss, anaemia, osteoporosis, infertility, mouth ulcers, the itchy blistering skin rash dermatitis herpetiformis — or no symptoms at all. Genetic susceptibility is necessary (nearly all patients carry HLA-DQ2 or DQ8) but not sufficient. Diagnosis combines blood tests for tissue transglutaminase antibodies with confirmatory duodenal biopsy (or, in some paediatric pathways, serology alone). The only effective treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet, which heals the intestine in most patients; untreated disease raises risks of malnutrition and, rarely, intestinal lymphoma.

History

Aretaeus of Cappadocia described the condition in antiquity; the modern understanding came from Dutch paediatrician Willem Dicke, who noticed Dutch children improved during the wartime bread shortage of 1944–45 and suffered on re-exposure to wheat, leading to the identification of gluten in the 1950s.
