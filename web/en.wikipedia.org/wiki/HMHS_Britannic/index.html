HMHS Britannic

HMHS Britannic was the third and largest vessel of the White Star Line's Olympic class of ocean liners, sister ship to Olympic and Titanic. Launched on 26 February 1914, she was requisitioned by the British Admiralty before entering commercial service and converted into a hospital ship during the First World War. On 21 November 1916 she struck a naval mine in the Kea Channel of the Aegean Sea and sank in 55 minutes; 1,066 people were rescued and 30 lost their lives.

Design changes

Lessons from the loss of Titanic were incorporated into Britannic: a double hull along the engine and boiler rooms, raised watertight bulkheads, and huge gantry davits capable of launching lifeboats from both sides of the ship. She was slightly larger than her sisters and would have been the largest liner in the world had she entered passenger service.

Sinking

At 08:12 on 21 November 1916, while steaming to Mudros to embark wounded soldiers, Britannic struck a mine laid by the German submarine U-73. The explosion breached the forward holds; open portholes low on the ship — opened by nurses to air the wards — admitted water as she listed, defeating her watertight protection. Two lifeboats launched without orders were drawn into the still-turning propellers, killing 30 people; the rest of those aboard, including nurse Violet Jessop — who had also survived Titanic — were rescued.

The wreck

The wreck was located by Jacques Cousteau in 1975, lying on its starboard side in about 120 metres of water. Remarkably intact, it is the largest passenger ship on the seabed and is a popular, though technically demanding, dive site.
