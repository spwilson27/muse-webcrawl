Chiaroscuro (English: /kiˌɑːrəˈskʊəroʊ/; Italian: [kjaroˈskuːro]), in art, is the use of strong contrasts between light and dark, usually bold contrasts affecting a whole composition. It is also a technical term used by artists and art historians for the use of contrasts of light to achieve a sense of volume in modelling three-dimensional objects and figures. Similar effects in cinema, and black and white photography, are also called chiaroscuro.

In painting

Although artists had long modelled figures with light and shade — notably Leonardo da Vinci with his sfumato — the dramatic use of sharp contrasts emerged in the late Renaissance and was raised to a signature style by Caravaggio, whose religious scenes emerge from near-total darkness (the technique sometimes called tenebrism). Rembrandt van Rijn used a softer chiaroscuro to give his portraits and biblical scenes psychological depth, while Georges de La Tour, Joseph Wright of Derby and Francisco Goya each adapted it to nocturnal, candlelit or industrial subjects.

In other media

In woodcut printing, the chiaroscuro woodcut is a technique, first used in the early sixteenth century by Lucas Cranach the Elder and Ugo da Carpi, in which tone is produced by printing with several blocks in different shades. In drawing, chiaroscuro studies in charcoal or ink-wash teach the rendering of form. Film noir, expressionist cinema and much modern cinematography rely on chiaroscuro lighting, as does dramatic low-key portrait photography.
