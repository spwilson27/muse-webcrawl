The Spanish East Indies (Spanish: Indias Orientales Españolas) were the overseas territories of the Spanish Empire in Asia and Oceania, governed from Manila in the Spanish Philippines. At their widest they included the Philippine archipelago, Guam and the Mariana Islands, the Caroline Islands and Palau, together with temporary possessions in Taiwan (Formosa, 1626–1642), Ternate and Tidore in the Moluccas, and coastal enclaves of Borneo and Sulawesi.

History

Spain's Pacific empire followed Ferdinand Magellan's landfall in the Philippines in 1521 and Miguel López de Legazpi's colonisation from Mexico in 1565; Manila was founded in 1571 and became the entrepôt of the Manila galleon trade, which for 250 years exchanged Mexican silver for Chinese silk and porcelain across the Pacific to Acapulco. Until Mexican independence in 1821 the islands were administered through the Viceroyalty of New Spain, thereafter directly from Madrid.

The colonial order rested on the Catholic Church's friar estates, the encomienda and later hacienda systems, and periodic revolts by Filipinos, Chinese residents and Muslim polities of the south (the "Moro" resistance in Mindanao and Sulu was never fully subdued). Defeat in the Spanish–American War ended the empire: by the Treaty of Paris (1898) Spain ceded the Philippines, Guam and Puerto Rico to the United States, and in 1899 it sold the remaining Carolines, Marianas (except Guam) and Palau to Germany.

Legacy

Three centuries of rule left enduring marks: Roman Catholicism as the majority faith of the Philippines, Spanish loanwords and surnames, colonial churches and fortifications such as Intramuros, and the Chavacano Spanish creole of Zamboanga. The period remains foundational — and contested — in Philippine national history.
