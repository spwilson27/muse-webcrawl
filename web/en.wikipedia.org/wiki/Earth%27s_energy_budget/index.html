Earth's energy budget

Earth's energy budget (or Earth's energy balance) accounts for the balance between the energy that Earth receives from the Sun and the energy the Earth loses back into outer space. Smaller internal sources of energy, such as Earth's internal heat, are also considered, but make a tiny contribution compared to solar energy. The energy budget also accounts for how energy moves through the climate system.

Because the Sun heats the equatorial regions more than the polar regions, the received solar energy is unevenly distributed. As the various components of the climate system move energy around, the energy budget is balanced; heat is transferred from warmer to cooler areas by atmospheric and oceanic circulation, and the excess heat received near the equator is carried poleward. If the budget becomes unbalanced, the global mean temperature rises or falls.

Incoming and outgoing radiation

Of the roughly 340 watts per square metre of solar radiation that arrive at the top of the atmosphere, about 30 percent is reflected back to space by clouds, aerosols, and the surface — this fraction is known as the planetary albedo. About one fifth of the incoming radiation is absorbed in the atmosphere, and roughly half reaches and warms the surface. The warmed surface emits long-wave infrared radiation. Much of this outgoing radiation is absorbed by greenhouse gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide, and methane, which re-radiate part of it back toward the surface, keeping the planet far warmer than it would otherwise be — the natural greenhouse effect.

Energy imbalance

Measurements by instruments such as NASA's CERES satellites show that Earth is currently accumulating energy, largely because human activities have increased concentrations of greenhouse gases. The excess energy, on the order of half a watt to one watt per square metre in recent decades, is stored overwhelmingly in the oceans and drives contemporary climate change, including sea level rise and the melting of ice sheets and glaciers.
