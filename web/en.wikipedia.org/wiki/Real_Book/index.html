The Real Book

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Real Book is a compilation of lead sheets for jazz standards — single-staff melodies with chord symbols — first assembled in the mid-1970s by students at the Berklee College of Music in Boston. Circulated for decades as an illicit, photocopied "fake book", it became the common repertoire bible of working jazz musicians before being published legally by Hal Leonard in 2004.

History

Fake books had existed since the 1940s, but the original Real Book, compiled around 1974–75 by Berklee students (with input attributed in jazz lore to teachers and players including Pat Metheny and Steve Swallow), stood out for its modern repertoire — tunes by Chick Corea, Herbie Hancock, Wayne Shorter and Keith Jarrett alongside standards — and for relatively accurate chord changes based on famous recordings. Sold under the counter in music shops and passed hand to hand, it spread worldwide; two further bootleg volumes followed, despite notorious errors in some changes and melodies.

Legal editions

In 2004 the Hal Leonard Corporation issued a licensed sixth edition of Volume I, securing rights from composers and correcting many errors; legal editions of Volumes II and III and versions for B♭, E♭ and bass-clef instruments followed, alongside genre spin-offs for blues, Latin jazz and Christmas music. The name pokes fun at the "fake book": musicians could at last carry the previously forbidden book openly — though many veterans still keep their battered bootlegs.

Influence

The Real Book standardised the jazz repertoire: a tune's inclusion effectively canonised it, and its changes became the default reference at jam sessions from Boston to Tokyo. It also shaped jazz pedagogy, since students everywhere learned the same versions of "Autumn Leaves", "All the Things You Are" and "Blue Bossa" from its pages. Digital successors, such as the iReal Pro app, reproduce its lead-sheet format for the smartphone era.
