Debt restructuring

[Image: Negotiators at a sovereign debt restructuring meeting]

Debt restructuring is a process that allows a private or public company, or a sovereign entity, facing cash flow problems and financial distress to reduce and renegotiate its delinquent debts to improve or restore liquidity so that it can continue its operations. Terms typically include extending maturities, reducing interest rates, converting debt into equity, or reducing the principal owed (a "haircut" for creditors).

Corporate restructuring

Companies restructure debt either out of court, through negotiated workouts with lenders, or under court protection such as Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the United States. Restructuring is generally less costly than bankruptcy and preserves business relationships, but requires creditor agreement; "distressed exchanges" that coerce acceptance can still be treated as defaults by rating agencies.

Sovereign restructuring

Countries that cannot service their debts renegotiate with official creditors (often coordinated through the Paris Club), commercial banks, or bondholders. Famous cases include the Brady Plan restructurings of Latin American debt in the 1990s, Argentina's 2005 and 2020 exchanges after its 2001 default, and Greece's 2012 private sector involvement—the largest sovereign restructuring in history, which reduced the face value of Greek bonds by over €100 billion. Collective action clauses in bond contracts now make it easier to bind holdout creditors to agreed terms.
