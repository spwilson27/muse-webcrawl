Historiography of the United Kingdom

The historiography of the United Kingdom is the study of the historians, methods, and interpretations that have shaped understanding of the history of Britain and, after the unions, of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and its successor state. It traces how each generation has rewritten the national past in the light of its own politics, from medieval chronicles to the "four nations" history of the present day.

Medieval chronicles such as those of Matthew Paris are foundation sources of British historiography

Chronicles to the Enlightenment

British history writing begins with the chronicles of the early medieval period — Gildas, Bede, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and the great monastic historians of the twelfth century — and with mythic histories such as Geoffrey of Monmouth's History of the Kings of Britain. Tudor chroniclers and antiquaries, including Polydore Vergil and William Camden, began to test these traditions against documents. In the eighteenth century, David Hume's History of England and Edward Gibbon's work on Rome made history a literary and philosophical enterprise read across Europe.

Whig history and professionalisation

The dominant nineteenth-century interpretation, later named the "Whig interpretation of history" by Herbert Butterfield in 1931, read the past as a story of progress toward parliamentary liberty and Protestant constitutionalism; its great exponent was Thomas Babington Macaulay. Against this tradition, the later nineteenth century saw history professionalised as an academic discipline based on archival research, in the work of William Stubbs, J. R. Green, and the founders of the English Historical Review (1886) and the Victoria County History.

Twentieth-century debates

Twentieth-century historiography was transformed by Marxist and social historians — the Communist Party Historians' Group, E. P. Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class (1963), and the journals Past & Present and History Workshop — who shifted attention from high politics to society, economy, and culture "from below". Fierce scholarly debates followed over the causes of the English Civil War (the "storm over the gentry"), the standard of living during industrialisation, and the timing of class formation. From the 1970s, feminist, postcolonial, and imperial history rewrote the national story again, emphasising empire, race, and gender, while the Scottish, Welsh, and Irish dimensions of the past led J. G. A. Pocock and others to call for a "new British history" treating the archipelago as a whole.

Present themes

Contemporary British historiography is marked by the cultural and linguistic turns, public controversies over the memory of empire and slavery, and debates about how history is taught in schools and presented in museums. Popular history writing and broadcasting flourish alongside the academic field, and digitised archives have changed the evidentiary basis of research.
