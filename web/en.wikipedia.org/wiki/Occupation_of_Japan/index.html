The Occupation of Japan was the period from the end of World War II in 1945 to 1952 during which Japan was occupied and governed by the Allied Powers, principally the United States, under the authority of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers (SCAP), General Douglas MacArthur. It transformed Japan's political system, economy and society, introducing a new constitution, land reform, demilitarisation and the purge of wartime leaders, before sovereignty was restored by the Treaty of San Francisco.

Surrender and beginnings

Japan accepted the Potsdam Declaration on 14 August 1945, and the formal surrender was signed aboard USS Missouri on 2 September. Unlike Germany, Japan was governed indirectly: SCAP ruled through the existing Japanese government and retained Emperor Hirohito, who cooperated with the occupation authorities. Allied forces, ultimately numbering several hundred thousand, were stationed across the home islands.

Reforms

The early occupation pursued demilitarisation and democratisation. The armed forces were disbanded, about 200,000 military and political figures were purged, the zaibatsu conglomerates were targeted for dissolution, and a sweeping land reform transferred tenancy land to cultivators. Political prisoners were freed, women gained the vote, labour unions were legalised, and the education system and the Shinto establishment were reformed, including the disestablishment of State Shinto.

The new constitution

After Japanese drafts were judged too conservative, SCAP staff drafted a new constitution in February 1946. Promulgated on 3 May 1947, the Constitution of Japan made the emperor "the symbol of the State", vested sovereignty in the people, guaranteed extensive rights, and, in its famous Article 9, renounced war and the maintenance of war potential.

War crimes trials

The International Military Tribunal for the Far East at Tokyo tried 28 "Class A" defendants; seven, including former prime minister Hideki Tojo, were executed. Thousands more were tried in national tribunals across Asia. The emperor was not indicted, a decision that remains controversial.

The "reverse course"

From about 1947–48, Cold War priorities shifted occupation policy toward economic recovery and anti-communism: the Dodge Line austerity programme, the "red purge" of communists from public life, and, after the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, the creation of a National Police Reserve — the nucleus of the later Self-Defense Forces.

End of the occupation

The Treaty of San Francisco, signed by 48 nations in September 1951, came into effect on 28 April 1952, restoring Japanese sovereignty. The United States retained administration of the Ryukyu Islands, including Okinawa, until their reversion in 1972, and a US–Japan security treaty kept American bases in Japan.
