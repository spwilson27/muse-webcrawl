Parzival is a Middle High German verse romance written by Wolfram von Eschenbach in the first decade of the thirteenth century. It is widely considered the greatest of the German Arthurian romances and one of the masterpieces of medieval literature. The poem retells and greatly expands the story of Perceval, the Grail hero, from Chrétien de Troyes's unfinished Perceval, the Story of the Grail, though Wolfram claimed a Provençal source, "Kyot," whose existence scholars still debate.

Synopsis

The poem opens with the adventures of Parzival's father, the knight Gahmuret, who dies in the East before his son is born. Parzival is raised in forest solitude by his mother Herzeloyde, who tries to shield him from knighthood. Venturing out in naïve ignorance, he is educated in courtly conduct by Gurnemanz and marries Condwiramurs. He comes to the Grail Castle of the wounded Fisher King Anfortas but, following advice not to ask questions, fails to ask the compassionate question that would heal the king, and is cursed for his failure.

A long second movement interweaves Parzival's wanderings with the adventures of Gawan (Gawain). Guided by the hermit Trevrizent, Parzival comes to understand his guilt, his lineage, and the nature of the Grail — in Wolfram's version a miraculous stone guarded by a knightly order of "templeise." Returning to the Grail Castle, he asks the question, Anfortas is healed, and Parzival succeeds him as Grail King. His half-brother Feirefiz, a piebald Saracen knight, is baptised and marries the Grail bearer Repanse de Schoye, becoming, in the poem's genealogy, an ancestor of Prester John.

Themes and legacy

Parzival explores the tension between courtly values and spiritual growth, the ethics of speech and silence, and an unusually tolerant medieval view of the Muslim world. It is the principal source for Richard Wagner's final opera, Parsifal (1882), and it survives in more than eighty manuscripts and fragments — evidence of its immense medieval popularity.
