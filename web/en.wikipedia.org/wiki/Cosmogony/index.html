Cosmogony

Cosmogony is any model concerning the origin of the cosmos or the universe. The term (from Greek kosmos, "world," and gonos, "offspring") covers both religious creation myths and scientific theories of the early universe. It is distinguished from cosmology, which studies the universe as it exists now and does not necessarily ask how it began.

Accounts of cosmic origins span myth and physics.

Religious and mythical cosmogonies

Nearly every culture has a creation account: the Babylonian Enūma Eliš, in which the god Marduk fashions the world from the body of Tiamat; the Genesis narrative of the Hebrew Bible; Hesiod's Theogony, beginning from Chaos; Norse myth, in which the world is made from the giant Ymir; Chinese accounts of Pangu separating heaven and earth; and Hindu cosmogonies that describe cyclic creation and dissolution of the universe over immense timescales. Common motifs include creation from nothing (ex nihilo), emergence from a cosmic egg or primordial waters, and creation by a craftsman deity.

Philosophical cosmogony

Greek philosophers such as Anaximander proposed naturalistic origins for the cosmos, and Plato's Timaeus presents a demiurge ordering pre-existing matter. Atomists like Democritus held that worlds form and perish by chance collisions of atoms.

Scientific cosmogony

In modern usage, cosmogony is the scientific study of the origin of the universe and of particular structures within it: the Big Bang theory (developed from the work of Lemaître, Gamow, and others and supported by the cosmic microwave background discovered in 1965) for the universe as a whole, and the nebular hypothesis for the formation of the Solar System from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust. Scientific cosmogony is testable in principle, distinguishing it from metaphysical accounts.
