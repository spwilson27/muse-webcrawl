Probabilism

Probabilism is a doctrine in Catholic moral theology, formulated by the Dominican Bartolomé de Medina in 1577, which holds that when there is doubt about whether a law binds in a particular case, one may follow a solidly probable opinion favouring liberty even if the opposite opinion is more probable. Its maxim is often summarised as lex dubia non obligat — a doubtful law does not oblige.

Development and controversy

The doctrine was taken up by Jesuit casuists in the seventeenth century and defended as a humane way of resolving conflicts of conscience. It drew fierce criticism from Jansenists and from Blaise Pascal, whose Provincial Letters (1656–57) attacked the lax applications of probabilism by some confessors. Rival systems arose: probabiliorism (follow the more probable opinion), equiprobabilism (developed by Alphonsus Liguori, holding that liberty may be followed when opinions are roughly equal), and tutorism (always follow the safer course).

Other uses

The term is also used in epistemology for the view that knowledge does not require certainty and that beliefs should be held with degrees of probability — a position associated with the ancient Academic sceptics and revived in modern fallibilism.
