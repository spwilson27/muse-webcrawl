Rütlischwur

Image placeholder: the oath of the three confederates on the Rütli meadow above Lake Lucerne.

The Rütlischwur (German for "Rütli Oath") is the legendary oath taken on the foundation of the Old Swiss Confederacy by the representatives of the three founding cantons, Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden, traditionally dated to 1307. It is named after the Rütli, a meadow above Lake Lucerne near Seelisberg. The three men traditionally identified as the oath-takers (Eidgenossen) are Werner Stauffacher for Schwyz, Walter Fürst for Uri and Arnold von Melchtal for Unterwalden.

Legend

According to the legend, the representatives of the three cantons met on the Rütli meadow and swore to stand by each other in the struggle against the tyranny of the Habsburg bailiffs. The oath is closely associated in Swiss tradition with the story of William Tell and with the Federal Charter of 1291, the document considered the founding charter of the confederacy. The Rütli oath is famously dramatised in Friedrich Schiller's play Wilhelm Tell (1804), which includes the celebrated oath formula: "We want to be a single people of brethren, never to part in danger or distress."

Historical background

No contemporary record of the oath survives, and the story is first recorded in chronicles of the 15th century, such as the White Book of Sarnen (c. 1470) and the chronicle of Aegidius Tschudi (c. 1570), who gave the date of the oath as 8 November 1307. Historians regard the Rütlischwur as a founding myth rather than a documented event, though the alliance of the three cantons in the late 13th and early 14th centuries is historical.

Legacy

The Rütli meadow remains a symbolic site of Swiss national identity. It was purchased by the Swiss Society for Public Utility in 1860 and given to the Confederation. In 1940, General Henri Guisan gathered senior Swiss officers there for the Rütlirapport, a celebrated act of resolve to resist invasion during the Second World War.
