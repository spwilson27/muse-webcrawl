The Family Assistance Plan (FAP) was a welfare reform proposal announced by United States President Richard Nixon on August 8, 1969. It would have replaced the existing Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) programme with a guaranteed minimum income — a form of negative income tax — for every American family with children, whether or not the parents were employed. It remains the closest the United States has come to enacting a guaranteed income at the federal level.

Proposal

Designed largely by Nixon adviser Daniel Patrick Moynihan, with intellectual roots in Milton Friedman's negative income tax proposal, FAP guaranteed a family of four with no income a federal payment of $1,600 per year (about $13,000 in 2020s dollars), which with food stamps and other benefits reached roughly $2,500. Families could earn up to $3,920 and still receive partial benefits, creating an incentive to work; employable recipients (excluding mothers of young children) were required to register for work or training. The plan would roughly have doubled the welfare rolls by including the working poor — some 13 million additional people — a deliberate design to eliminate the stigma and perverse incentives of AFDC.

Legislative history

Nixon's televised address framed the existing welfare system as a "colossal failure" that broke up families and discouraged work. The bill passed the House of Representatives in April 1970 by 243–155 and again in 1971, backed by the administration and Wilbur Mills's Ways and Means Committee. In the Senate Finance Committee, however, it was attacked from both directions: conservatives, led by Russell Long, considered it too generous and objected to guaranteeing income without work; liberals and the National Welfare Rights Organization considered the floor too low and the work requirements punitive, campaigning for a much higher guarantee. Unable to reconcile the objections, the Senate let the plan die in 1972.

Legacy

Although FAP failed, elements survived: the Supplemental Security Income (SSI) programme of 1972 extended a federal income floor to the aged, blind, and disabled, and the Earned Income Tax Credit (1975) adopted its strategy of supplementing low wages. Historians and policy analysts continue to debate whether FAP's failure was a missed turning point toward a guaranteed income or inevitable given American politics; the episode is frequently cited in modern debates over universal basic income and child allowances.
