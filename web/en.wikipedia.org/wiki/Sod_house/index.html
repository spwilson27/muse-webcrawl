Sod house

A sod house in Nebraska, photographed in the 1880s
Also calledSoddy, sod shanty
RegionGreat Plains and Canadian Prairies, 1860s–early 1900s
MaterialsCut prairie sod blocks, timber or pole roof frame, earth or sod roof
Known forCheap, well-insulated shelter where timber was scarce

A sod house, or soddy, was a house built from blocks of prairie sod — dense turf of grass and roots cut from the ground — widely used by homesteaders on the treeless Great Plains of North America and the Canadian Prairies in the late nineteenth century. Where lumber had to be hauled long distances at high cost, sod was free, abundant and an excellent insulator, keeping interiors cool in summer and relatively warm in winter.

Construction

Settlers cut sod into strips with a special "grasshopper" breaking plough, then sliced the strips into blocks roughly 30 × 10 × 60 centimetres. The blocks were laid grass-side down in staggered courses, like bricks, to form walls often a metre thick at the base; a typical house might use close to an acre of sod. Roofs were the hard part: poles or scarce boards were laid across the walls, covered with brush, hay and a layer of sod, which shed most rain but could drip for days after a storm and occasionally collapsed under wet weight. Floors were usually packed earth; walls were sometimes plastered inside with clay or whitewashed.

Life in a soddy

Sod houses demanded constant maintenance — walls eroded in rain and roofs needed re-sodding — and inhabitants contended with dirt sifting from the ceiling, insects, mice and snakes in the walls. Yet many pioneer accounts recall soddies fondly for their even temperature and their strength against prairie fires and tornadoes. Photographs of proud families posed before their sod homes, popularised by photographers such as Solomon Butcher in Nebraska, became iconic images of homesteading.

Decline and survival

As railroads spread and milled lumber became cheap, frame houses displaced soddies after about 1900, though some remained occupied into the mid-twentieth century. A number of restored sod houses survive as museums in Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma, South Dakota and Manitoba, preserving a building tradition also echoed in the turf houses of Iceland and Scandinavia.
