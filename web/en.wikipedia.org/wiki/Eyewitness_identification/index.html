Eyewitness identification

Eyewitness identification is evidence supplied by a witness who can identify a person they saw at a scene. It is a powerful form of evidence in criminal trials, but decades of psychological research and DNA exonerations have shown it to be fallible: mistaken eyewitness identification has been the leading contributing factor in wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA testing in the United States.

Procedures

Identifications are obtained through lineups, photographic arrays, show-ups (presenting a single suspect), and in-court identification. Psychologists divide the factors affecting accuracy into estimator variables — such as lighting, distance, duration of view, stress, weapon focus, and cross-race effects — which are beyond the justice system's control, and system variables, such as lineup construction and instructions, which the system can improve.

Reforms

Recommended reforms include double-blind administration (the officer does not know who the suspect is), sequential rather than simultaneous presentation, unbiased instructions warning that the perpetrator may not be present, selecting fillers who resemble the description, and recording the witness's confidence immediately at the time of identification. Research by Gary L. Wells and others underpins these measures, which many US states and the Department of Justice have adopted in whole or part.
