Pyrrole


Structure of pyrrole, C4H5N

Pyrrole is a heterocyclic, aromatic, organic compound, a five-membered ring with the formula C4H4NH. It is a colourless volatile liquid that darkens readily upon exposure to air. Pyrrole was first detected by F. F. Runge in 1834 as a constituent of coal tar, and was isolated from the pyrolysate of bone oil by Anderson in 1857; its structure was established by Baeyer in 1870.

Properties

The nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet, making pyrrole a π-excessive aromatic system that is much more reactive than benzene toward electrophilic aromatic substitution, preferring attack at the 2-position. Unlike amines, pyrrole is only very weakly basic because protonation destroys its aromaticity.

Occurrence and synthesis

Four pyrrole units linked in a macrocycle form porphyrins, the basis of heme, chlorophyll and vitamin B12. Pyrrole rings also occur in bile pigments such as bilirubin and in many pharmaceuticals. Industrial synthesis proceeds from furan and ammonia over solid acid catalysts; laboratory methods include the Paal–Knorr, Knorr and Hantzsch pyrrole syntheses. Polymerisation of pyrrole gives polypyrrole, a conducting polymer.
