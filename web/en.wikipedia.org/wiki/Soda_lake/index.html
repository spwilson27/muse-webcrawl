Soda lake

A soda lake or alkaline lake is a lake on the strongly alkaline side of neutrality, typically with a pH of 9 to 12, owing to high concentrations of carbonate salts (sodium carbonate and related minerals, historically called "soda"). Soda lakes form in closed (endorheic) drainage basins in arid or semi-arid climates, where evaporation concentrates dissolved carbonates while calcium and magnesium are removed by precipitation. Many of the world's soda lakes lie in the East African Rift Valley; famous examples include Lake Natron and Lake Magadi in Kenya/Tanzania, Lake Nakuru, and, outside Africa, Mono Lake in California and Lake Van in Turkey.

Ecology

Despite their harsh chemistry, soda lakes are among the most productive aquatic ecosystems on Earth. Cyanobacteria such as Spirulina (Arthrospira) thrive in the warm, alkaline, nutrient-rich water, colouring lakes green or reddish, and support immense flocks of lesser flamingos, which filter-feed on the algae — the Rift Valley soda lakes are the principal feeding and breeding habitat of the world's lesser flamingo population. Alkaliphile microorganisms from soda lakes are studied for enzymes useful in detergents and industry, and the lakes' chemistry has been proposed as an analogue for conditions on early Earth.

Economic use

Soda lakes have been mined for millennia: natron from Egypt's Wadi Natrun was used in mummification, glass-making and cleansing; today Lake Magadi and other lakes are worked for soda ash (sodium carbonate) used in glass and chemical manufacture, and trona deposits in Wyoming's Green River Basin — an ancient soda lake bed — supply much of the world's natural soda ash.
