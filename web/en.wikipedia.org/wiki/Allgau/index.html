Allgau

The Allgau (German: Allgäu) is a region in Swabia in southern Germany, covering the southern part of Bavarian Swabia, southeastern Baden-Württemberg and parts of Austria. It stretches from the prealpine lands up to the Alps and is one of Germany's best-known holiday regions, famous for dairy farming, cheese, spa towns and winter sports.

Geography

The region has no fixed political borders but centres on the districts of Oberallgäu, Ostallgäu, Unterallgäu and Lindau in Bavaria and part of Ravensburg district in Baden-Württemberg. Principal towns include Kempten — its largest city — Kaufbeuren, Memmingen, Füssen and Oberstdorf. The Iller river runs through the Allgäu from south to north, and the landscape grades from rolling moraine hills and lakes to high Alpine peaks such as the Hochfrottspitze (2,649 m) near Oberstdorf.

Economy and culture

Historically a land of flax and linen, the Allgäu reinvented itself in the nineteenth century around dairy cattle: Carl Hirnbein promoted Alpine dairy farming and soft and hard cheeses, so that Allgäuer Bergkäse and Allgäuer Emmentaler became protected regional brands. Cattle are still driven up to and down from mountain pastures in the ceremonial Almabtrieb each autumn. Tourism rivals agriculture: Neuschwanstein Castle near Füssen, the ski resort of Oberstdorf — home to a ski flying hill and the opening of the Four Hills Tournament at the turn of the year — and Kneipp spa towns such as Bad Wörishofen draw millions of visitors.

History

Settled by Alemannic tribes and long divided among prince-abbots, imperial cities and bishoprics, the region was consolidated into Bavaria and Württemberg by the mediatisations of 1803–1806. Its relative isolation preserved distinctive dialects of Swabian and a strong regional identity that survives in the self-description of the Allgäuer as a people apart within Bavaria.
