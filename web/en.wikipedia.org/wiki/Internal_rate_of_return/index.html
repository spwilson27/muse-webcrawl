Internal rate of return

The IRR is the discount rate at which net present value equals zero.

The internal rate of return (IRR) is a measure of an investment's rate of return. In capital budgeting, it is the discount rate that makes the net present value (NPV) of all cash flows from a project equal to zero. Equivalently, it is the rate of return at which the project breaks even in present-value terms.

Calculation

For cash flows C0, C1, …, Cn over periods 0 to n, the IRR r solves Σ Ct / (1 + r)t = 0. Except in simple cases this polynomial equation must be solved numerically, for example by iteration in spreadsheet functions such as Excel's IRR. A project is conventionally accepted if its IRR exceeds the organisation's cost of capital (hurdle rate).

Problems with IRR

The IRR has well-known limitations: cash-flow streams that change sign more than once can produce multiple IRRs or none; IRR implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself; and it ignores project scale, so a small project with a high IRR may add less value than a larger project with a lower IRR. The modified internal rate of return (MIRR) addresses the reinvestment assumption.

Uses

IRR is widely used in private equity and venture capital to report fund performance, in real estate, and in comparing energy and infrastructure projects, usually alongside NPV.
