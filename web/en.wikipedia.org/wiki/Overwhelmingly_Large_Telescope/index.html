Overwhelmingly Large Telescope

The Overwhelmingly Large Telescope (OWL) was a conceptual design for an extremely large telescope proposed by the European Southern Observatory (ESO). The design called for a single-aperture optical and near-infrared telescope with a segmented primary mirror 100 metres (328 ft) in diameter. The project was studied between the late 1990s and 2005, when ESO concluded that a 100 m telescope was too ambitious and redirected its efforts toward a smaller design, which eventually became the 39-metre Extremely Large Telescope (ELT).

Design

OWL was conceived as a scaled-up version of existing segmented-mirror telescopes. Its primary mirror would have comprised 3,048 hexagonal segments, each 1.6 m across, with an identical number of segments in a flat secondary mirror. The modular, serial-production approach was intended to keep the cost — estimated at roughly €1.5 billion — far below a naive extrapolation from smaller telescopes. The design included adaptive optics correctors integrated into the optical train, with a field of view of about 10 arcminutes.

A 100 m aperture would have collected roughly 40 times more light than the largest telescopes then planned and offered diffraction-limited resolution of about a milliarcsecond in the near-infrared, sufficient in principle to image Earth-like exoplanets around nearby stars and to observe the first generation of galaxies.

Cancellation and legacy

In 2005–2006, ESO's governing bodies judged the technical and financial risks of the full OWL design unacceptable for the timescale required. The OWL science case and much of its technology work were folded into the E-ELT (European Extremely Large Telescope) programme, which initially studied apertures of 42 m before fixing on 39.3 m. Construction of the ELT began at Cerro Armazones in Chile in 2014.
