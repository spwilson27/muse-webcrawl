The Postman Always Rings Twice

The Postman Always Rings Twice is a 1934 crime novel by American writer James M. Cain. Violent and sexually frank for its time, it is regarded as one of the most important crime novels of the 20th century and a founding work of noir fiction.

Plot

Drifter Frank Chambers arrives at a rural California diner run by Greek immigrant Nick Papadakis and his young wife Cora. Frank and Cora begin a passionate affair and plot to murder Nick: a first attempt fails, but they succeed by staging a drunk-driving accident. A suspicious prosecutor and a blackmailing accomplice entangle the lovers' escape, and their mutual distrust grows. After Cora is killed in a genuine car crash while Frank is driving, Frank is convicted of her murder — a crime he did not commit — for the one he did. Cain explained the title metaphorically: fate, like the postman, always delivers its ring twice; Frank escapes justice once but not again.

Reception

The novel was banned in Boston on obscenity grounds, a notoriety that helped its success. Albert Camus acknowledged it as a model for The Stranger. It is ranked among the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century by the Modern Library.

Adaptations

The story was adapted as Luchino Visconti's Ossessione (1943), an unauthorized film now seen as a forerunner of Italian neorealism; the MGM film noir of 1946 starring Lana Turner and John Garfield; and Bob Rafelson's 1981 remake starring Jack Nicholson and Jessica Lange. Cain's other novels Double Indemnity and Mildred Pierce had similar screen afterlives.
