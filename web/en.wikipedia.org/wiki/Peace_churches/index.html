Peace churches
TraditionsQuakers (Religious Society of Friends); Mennonites and other Anabaptists; Church of the Brethren
Core beliefChristian pacifism; nonresistance; refusal of military service

The term peace churches refers to Christian churches, groups, and communities that, on theological grounds, adopt a position of principled nonviolence (pacifism) and oppose war and military service. The description is applied above all to the three "historic peace churches": the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers), the Mennonites (within the Anabaptist tradition), and the Church of the Brethren.

Origins

The designation "peace churches" was popularized at a conference held in Newton, Kansas, in 1935, where representatives of the three traditions met to coordinate their peace witness as war approached. Each tradition had, however, held pacifist convictions for centuries: Anabaptists from their 16th-century Swiss and Dutch origins (formalized in the Schleitheim Confession of 1527), Quakers from the 17th century (the Peace Testimony was presented to Charles II in 1660), and the Brethren from their founding in Schwarzenau in 1708.

Beliefs and practice

Peace churches ground their pacifism in the teachings of Jesus, especially the Sermon on the Mount, and in doctrines such as the Anabaptist principle of nonresistance. Members historically refused military service, becoming conscientious objectors, and in the United States many served instead in Civilian Public Service camps during the Second World War. The traditions also sustain extensive relief and development work, such as the Mennonite Central Committee, and active peacemaking programs.

Wider usage

The term is sometimes extended to other pacifist bodies, including the Amish, Hutterites, some Churches of Christ, and international fellowships influenced by these traditions. Peace church representatives participate in ecumenical bodies and in advocacy for conscientious objection and disarmament.
