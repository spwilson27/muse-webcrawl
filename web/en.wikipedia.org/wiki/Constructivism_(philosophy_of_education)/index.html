Constructivism (philosophy of education)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Constructivism

Learners actively construct knowledge through experience and collaboration
Core claimLearners construct their own understanding rather than passively receiving knowledge
Key figuresJean Piaget, Lev Vygotsky, John Dewey, Jerome Bruner, Seymour Papert
Related methodsDiscovery learning, inquiry-based learning, problem-based learning, scaffolding, constructionism

Constructivism is a theory in education and the philosophy of learning which holds that people actively construct their own knowledge and understanding of the world through experiencing things and reflecting on those experiences, rather than passively absorbing information transmitted by a teacher. New information is integrated with prior knowledge — assimilated into existing schemas or accommodated by restructuring them — so teaching should start from what the learner already understands.

Varieties

  • Cognitive constructivism (Piaget) — the child as lone scientist, constructing knowledge through staged interaction with the physical world; learning follows development.
  • Social constructivism (Vygotsky) — knowledge is first built socially, through language, culture and collaboration; development follows learning within the zone of proximal development.
  • Radical constructivism (von Glasersfeld) — knowledge is not a copy of reality but a viable model fitted to experience.
  • Constructionism (Papert) — learning is most effective when learners build shareable artefacts, as in Logo programming and later maker education.

Pedagogy

Constructivist classrooms favour active, inquiry-based and problem-based learning, group work, authentic tasks, and assessment embedded in activity (portfolios, projects) rather than only tests. The teacher acts as facilitator or coach, using scaffolding — temporary support withdrawn as competence grows — a practice derived from Vygotsky and Bruner. Jerome Bruner's discovery learning and the spiral curriculum are classic expressions; the approach strongly influenced science and mathematics education reform from the 1980s onward.

Criticism

Critics in cognitive science (e.g. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark, 2006) argue that minimally guided instruction overloads working memory and is less effective than explicit teaching, especially for novices — a debate framed by the cognitive revolution's legacy in instructional design. Defenders reply that well-designed constructivist teaching is guided, not unguided, and point to evidence for structured inquiry and worked-example–faded designs. The dispute remains central to developmental and educational psychology.
