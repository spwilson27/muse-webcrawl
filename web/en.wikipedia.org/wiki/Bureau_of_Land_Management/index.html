The Bureau of Land Management (BLM) is an agency within the United States Department of the Interior responsible for administering America's public lands, totaling approximately 245 million acres (about 991,000 km²), or one-eighth of the landmass of the country. Most BLM public lands are located in the western states, and the agency also administers about 700 million acres of subsurface mineral estate.

History

The BLM was created in 1946 by the merger of the General Land Office (established 1812) and the U.S. Grazing Service. Its modern mission — managing public lands for multiple use and sustained yield — was set out in the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 (FLPMA), which ended the era of homesteading and land disposal and declared that public lands generally would remain in federal ownership.

Programs

The BLM manages public lands for a wide range of uses including energy development (oil, gas, coal, geothermal, wind, and solar), livestock grazing under the Taylor Grazing Act, timber, recreation, rights-of-way, and conservation. Its National Conservation Lands include national monuments, national conservation areas, wilderness areas, wild and scenic rivers, and national scenic and historic trails. The agency also runs the Wild Horse and Burro Program, administers helium operations, and maintains the official land records and cadastral surveys of the United States.

Controversies

BLM management has been a flashpoint in debates over federal land in the West, including the "Sagebrush Rebellion" of the 1970s and the 2014 Bundy standoff in Nevada over grazing fees. The agency's headquarters was moved to Grand Junction, Colorado, in 2019–2020 and returned to Washington, D.C., in 2021.
