Must

Must (from the Latin mustum, "young wine") is freshly crushed fruit juice — usually grape juice — that contains the skins, seeds, and stems of the fruit. Making must is the first step in winemaking. Because of its high sugar content, must is also used as a sweetener in a variety of cuisines.

In winemaking

After grapes are harvested, they are crushed and pressed to release the juice. The resulting must contains sugars (principally glucose and fructose), organic acids such as tartaric and malic acid, tannins, anthocyanins (in red varieties), and wild yeasts present on the grape skins. Fermentation begins when these yeasts — or cultured yeasts added by the winemaker — convert the sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide, transforming must into wine.

For red wine, the must is fermented in contact with the skins to extract color and tannin; for white wine, the juice is usually separated from the solids before fermentation. The density of the must, measured in degrees Brix or on the Oechsle or Baumé scales, predicts the potential alcohol of the finished wine.

Culinary uses

In Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisines, must is reduced by boiling into a thick syrup. Variants include Roman defrutum and sapa, Turkish pekmez, and Italian saba and vino cotto. Grape must is also the base of mostarda in Italy and of various mustards — the word "mustard" itself derives from mustum ardens, "burning must", because the seeds were originally mixed with unfermented grape juice.

Other uses

In Roman Catholic practice, mustum is very slightly fermented grape juice that may, with permission, be used for the Eucharist by priests who cannot consume wine. In parts of Central Europe, partially fermented must is sold seasonally as a sparkling drink — Federweißer in Germany and Sturm in Austria.
