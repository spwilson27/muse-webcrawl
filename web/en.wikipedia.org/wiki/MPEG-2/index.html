MPEG-2 (also known as H.262) is a standard for the generic coding of moving pictures and associated audio information, developed by the Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG) and published in 1995 as ISO/IEC 13818. It describes a combination of lossy video compression and lossy audio data compression methods that permit storing and transmitting movies using then-available storage media and transmission bandwidth.

MPEG-2 was the core of most digital television and DVD formats. It provided the video compression for DVD-Video, for digital satellite, cable, and terrestrial broadcasting systems (DVB, ATSC, ISDB), and for early HD distribution; variants also underlie the transport streams used in Blu-ray discs.

Components

The standard is divided into parts. Part 1 (Systems) defines the Program Stream (used on DVDs) and the Transport Stream (used in broadcasting), which multiplex compressed video, audio, and data into a single stream. Part 2 (Video, identical to ITU-T H.262) defines the video codec, which extends MPEG-1 with support for interlaced video, higher resolutions, and scalable coding through "profiles" and "levels" — the Main Profile at Main Level (MP@ML) being standard-definition television, and MP@HL covering HDTV up to 1920×1080. Part 3 (Audio) extends MPEG-1 Audio (including MP3, defined in MPEG-1) to multichannel sound; Part 7 defines the more efficient AAC codec.

Compression

Like MPEG-1, MPEG-2 video compression exploits spatial redundancy within frames (using DCT-based intra coding of I-frames) and temporal redundancy between frames (using motion-compensated prediction in P-frames and bidirectional B-frames), followed by quantization, zigzag scanning, and entropy coding. Typical broadcast bitrates ranged from about 4–9 Mbit/s for standard definition. Patent licensing was administered by MPEG LA; the last MPEG-2 patents expired in most of the world by 2018.
