Aerial photography

First aerial photograph1858, by Nadar (from a balloon)
PlatformsAircraft, balloons, kites, drones, satellites
UsesMapping, reconnaissance, archaeology, surveying

Aerial photography (or airborne imagery) is the taking of photographs from an aircraft or other airborne platform. Images may be vertical, taken with the camera axis pointing straight down for mapping, or oblique, taken at an angle for pictorial and reconnaissance purposes. Aerial photographs differ from satellite imagery chiefly in their lower altitude and typically higher resolution.

History

The earliest surviving aerial photographs were taken by the French photographer Nadar from a tethered balloon over Paris in 1858 (his earliest attempts are lost; an 1860 view of Boston by James Wallace Black is the oldest surviving example in the United States). Experimenters used kites, pigeons, and rockets before aeroplanes made aerial photography practical. It matured rapidly during the First World War for military reconnaissance, and in the interwar years became a foundation of surveying, cartography, and archaeology, revealing crop marks and earthworks invisible from the ground.

Applications

Systematic vertical photography underlies photogrammetry and the production of orthophoto maps. Aerial imagery is used in land-use planning, environmental monitoring, agriculture, disaster assessment, film production, and real estate. Since the 2010s, small unmanned aerial vehicles (drones) have dramatically lowered the cost of aerial photography, making it a routine tool for hobbyists and professionals alike.
