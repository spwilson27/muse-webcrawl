Coenzyme

A coenzyme is a small, non-protein organic molecule that binds to an enzyme and is required for its catalytic activity. Coenzymes can be considered "helper molecules" that assist in biochemical transformations; they are typically regenerated during the reactions they participate in, and many are derived from vitamins.

Cofactors and prosthetic groups

Coenzymes are a subset of cofactors. Cofactors may be inorganic ions (such as iron, magnesium, or zinc) or organic molecules, and the organic ones are the coenzymes. A coenzyme that is tightly or even covalently bound to its enzyme is called a prosthetic group; a loosely bound coenzyme functions more like a cosubstrate, binding and leaving with the reaction. The enzyme without its cofactor is the apoenzyme; the complete, active complex is the holoenzyme.

Functions

Coenzymes act as carriers of chemical groups and electrons in metabolism. Important examples include:

  • NAD+ and NADP+ — electron carriers derived from niacin (vitamin B3), central to catabolism and anabolism.
  • FAD — derived from riboflavin (vitamin B2), used in the citric acid cycle and fatty acid oxidation.
  • Coenzyme A — derived from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5), carries acyl groups, most famously as acetyl-CoA.
  • Coenzyme Q (ubiquinone) — carries electrons in the mitochondrial respiratory chain.
  • Tetrahydrofolate — derived from folate, carries one-carbon units in nucleotide synthesis.
  • S-adenosylmethionine — the principal methyl-group donor in the cell.

Vitamin precursors

Many coenzymes are synthesised from vitamins, which is one reason these dietary compounds are essential. Thiamine (vitamin B1) gives thiamine pyrophosphate; vitamin B6 gives pyridoxal phosphate; vitamin B12 is itself a coenzyme in rearrangement and methylation reactions; and vitamin K functions as a coenzyme in the carboxylation of glutamate residues in blood-clotting proteins.
