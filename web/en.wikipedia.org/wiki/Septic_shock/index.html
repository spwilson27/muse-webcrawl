Septic shock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Septic shock is a potentially fatal medical condition that occurs when sepsis — organ injury or damage in response to infection — leads to dangerously low blood pressure and abnormalities in cellular metabolism. Under the Sepsis-3 definitions, septic shock is sepsis in which underlying circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality: clinically, it requires vasopressor therapy to maintain a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mmHg and a serum lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Mechanism

The condition is mainly a distributive shock: microbial products and the host inflammatory response cause widespread vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and maldistribution of blood flow, compounded in many patients by myocardial depression and intravascular volume loss. The resulting tissue hypoperfusion produces lactic acidosis and progressive failure of organs including the lungs (ARDS), kidneys, liver, and brain.

Treatment

Management follows the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines: rapid recognition, blood cultures and lactate measurement, early broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (ideally within the first hour), source control of the infection, intravenous fluid resuscitation, and vasopressors — norepinephrine first-line, with vasopressin as an adjunct — with corticosteroids for refractory shock. Patients require intensive care, and mortality remains high despite modern therapy.
