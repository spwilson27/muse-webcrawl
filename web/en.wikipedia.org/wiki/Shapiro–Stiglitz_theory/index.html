Shapiro–Stiglitz theory

The Shapiro–Stiglitz theory of efficiency wages, developed by Carl Shapiro and Joseph Stiglitz in their 1984 paper "Equilibrium Unemployment as a Worker Discipline Device" (American Economic Review), explains involuntary unemployment as the result of employers' inability to monitor workers perfectly. Stiglitz later received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in part for this body of work on asymmetric information.

The model

Because monitoring is imperfect, an employer cannot directly observe whether a worker shirks. The firm's only sanction is dismissal. To make dismissal costly, the firm pays a wage above the market-clearing level — an efficiency wage — so that losing the job means losing a premium and facing a spell of unemployment before finding another job at the going wage. The condition that workers prefer effort to shirking (the "no-shirking condition") depends on the wage, unemployment benefits, the probability of being caught, and the economy-wide unemployment rate.

Implications

In equilibrium, all firms pay the premium, yet unemployment persists: the pool of unemployed workers is precisely what disciplines the employed. The theory thus gives a microeconomic foundation for involuntary unemployment consistent with rational behaviour, without relying on wage rigidities or unions, and it explains why similar workers may earn different wages in different industries — firms with costlier monitoring pay more.
