The Supreme Federal Court (Portuguese: Supremo Tribunal Federal, STF) is the highest court of Brazil, serving principally as the nation's constitutional court. Its eleven justices (ministros) are appointed by the President of Brazil and confirmed by the Federal Senate; they must be Brazilian-born citizens between 35 and 65 years of age with notable legal knowledge and unblemished reputation, and they serve until compulsory retirement at 75.

Jurisdiction

The Court's jurisdiction is defined by the 1988 Constitution. It has original jurisdiction over direct actions of unconstitutionality (ADI), declaratory actions of constitutionality, and cases involving the President, Vice President, members of Congress, and other high authorities, who enjoy privileged forum for criminal trials before the STF. On appeal it decides extraordinary appeals presenting constitutional questions; since a 2004 reform, such appeals require a showing of "general repercussion." Its binding precedents (súmulas vinculantes) bind lower courts and the public administration.

History

The Court succeeded the Supremo Tribunal de Justiça of the Empire of Brazil; it was created by decree in 1890 and installed on 28 February 1891 under the first republican constitution, modeled partly on the U.S. Supreme Court. During the military regime (1964–1985) its independence was curtailed; an institutional act in 1969 forcibly retired three justices. The 1988 "Citizen Constitution" greatly expanded access to the Court and its role in Brazilian public life. The Court sits in a modernist building designed by Oscar Niemeyer in the Praça dos Três Poderes in Brasília.

Prominence and controversy

Televised sessions (TV Justiça) and plenary livestreams have made individual justices public figures. The Court has decided major cases on same-sex unions, affirmative action quotas, the mensalão corruption trial (2012), the January 8, 2023 riots in Brasília, and inquiries into disinformation networks. Critics accuse it of judicial activism and expansive use of individual (monocratic) decisions; defenders credit it with defending democratic institutions.

Relation to other courts

Brazil's judiciary also includes the Superior Court of Justice (STJ), the final court for non-constitutional federal law, and specialized labor, electoral, and military superior courts. The STF heads the system only on constitutional matters.
