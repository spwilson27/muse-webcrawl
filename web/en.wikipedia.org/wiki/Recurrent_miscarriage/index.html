Recurrent miscarriage

Recurrent miscarriage, also called recurrent pregnancy loss or habitual abortion, is generally defined as two or more consecutive (or, in some definitions, three or more) pregnancy losses. It affects approximately 1–2% of women of reproductive age, a rate higher than expected by chance alone, suggesting underlying causes in many couples.

Causes

Recognised causes include parental chromosomal rearrangements (such as balanced translocations), uterine anomalies, antiphospholipid syndrome, endocrine disorders including poorly controlled diabetes and thyroid disease, and certain thrombophilias. Chromosomal abnormalities in the embryo remain the single most common explanation for individual losses. In a large proportion of couples — often around half — no cause is found despite investigation.

Evaluation and management

Guidelines from bodies such as ESHRE and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists recommend karyotyping of pregnancy tissue and parents, uterine imaging, screening for antiphospholipid antibodies, and thyroid assessment. Treatment is cause-specific: antiphospholipid syndrome is managed with low-dose aspirin and heparin; surgical correction may help selected uterine anomalies. Supportive care in early pregnancy, including access to early pregnancy units, itself improves outcomes.

Prognosis

Even without a found cause, the chance of a successful subsequent pregnancy is generally good — roughly 60–70% — though it declines with increasing maternal age and number of previous losses.
