Magan (civilisation)

Magan (Sumerian: 𒈖𒆠 Maganki) was an ancient region known from Mesopotamian cuneiform texts of the third millennium BC as a source of copper and diorite and as a trading partner of Sumer, Akkad and later Babylonia. It is generally identified with the area of present-day Oman, and possibly parts of the United Arab Emirates, on the southeastern Arabian Peninsula.

Trade with Mesopotamia

Mesopotamian inscriptions describe ships of Magan docking at Akkad and Ur. Sargon of Akkad boasted that ships from Magan, Meluhha (the Indus region) and Dilmun (Bahrain) moored in his capital. Magan supplied copper from the mines of the Al Hajar Mountains, hard diorite stone used for royal statuary such as the statues of Gudea of Lagash, and other goods; in return it received textiles, oil, grain and manufactured goods from Mesopotamia.

Archaeology

The culture corresponding to Magan is associated with the Umm an-Nar period (c. 2600–2000 BC) in Oman, characterised by distinctive circular collective tombs, fortified oasis settlements and copper production. Later, in the Wadi Suq and Iron Age periods, direct references to Magan in Mesopotamian texts decline as trade routes and political conditions changed.
