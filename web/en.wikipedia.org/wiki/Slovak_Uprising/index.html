The Slovak National Uprising (Slovak: Slovenské národné povstanie, abbreviated SNP) was a military uprising organised by the Slovak resistance during World War II. Launched on 29 August 1944 from Banská Bystrica, it aimed to overthrow the collaborationist regime of Jozef Tiso, expel German forces, and restore Czechoslovakia. It was one of the largest armed resistance revolts in Nazi-occupied Europe.

Background

Since 1939 Slovakia had existed as a German client state under President Jozef Tiso and his Slovak People's Party, which deported most of the country's Jews to German extermination camps in 1942. Resistance centred on the Slovak National Council, uniting democratic exiles' supporters and communists in the 1943 "Christmas Agreement." Officers of the Slovak Army, coordinated by Lt. Col. Ján Golian from the Banská Bystrica command, prepared a coup to coincide with a Soviet advance through the Carpathians.

Course of the uprising

The uprising began prematurely after Slovak partisans ambushed and killed a German military mission (the Martin incident) on 27–28 August, prompting Germany to occupy Slovakia. On 29 August Golian issued the coded order to resist. Insurgents held a liberated zone in central Slovakia of roughly 20,000 km² with some 47,000 regular troops, supplemented by partisans including Soviet, French and Jewish units, and an airlift of supplies to Tri Duby airfield; Allied airmen and a U.S. OSS mission were present. The Czechoslovak government-in-exile sent Gen. Rudolf Viest to take command in October.

However, the companion plan failed: the two best-equipped Slovak divisions in the east, meant to open the Carpathian passes, were disarmed by the Germans without fighting, and the Soviet Dukla offensive ground forward too slowly. German battle groups, including SS units, compressed the pocket; Banská Bystrica fell on 27 October 1944, and Viest and Golian were captured (both were later executed at Flossenbürg).

Aftermath

Insurgents who escaped continued guerrilla warfare in the mountains through the winter until liberation in spring 1945. Reprisals by Einsatzgruppe H and Hlinka Guard emergency units murdered thousands, including some 900 victims at Kremnička and Nemecká, and deportations of Slovakia's remaining Jews resumed. After the war, the uprising — its anniversary, 29 August, is a Slovak national holiday — became a cornerstone of Slovak national memory, though its interpretation was politically contested under communism and after 1993. The other "Slovak Uprising" sometimes referenced in older literature is the Slovak volunteer campaigns of 1848–49 during the Hungarian Revolution.
