Bouillabaisse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Marseille bouillabaisse with its saffron broth, rouille croutons and whole fish]

Bouillabaisse is the great fish soup-stew of Marseille: a saffron-scented broth of Mediterranean rockfish, shellfish and aromatics, served in two acts — first the broth, ladled over garlic-rubbed croutons spread with fiery rouille; then the fish themselves, presented whole and filleted at the table. It began as the Provençal fisherman's way with the unsellable catch, simmered in seawater-adjacent simplicity on the shore, and became one of French cuisine's most codified luxuries: in 1980 a group of Marseille chefs published a Bouillabaisse Charter fixing the canonical ingredients and method.

The rules of the pot

The charter insists on the fish that make the dish: rascasse (scorpionfish) above all, plus conger, weever, gurnard and others of the rocky shore, with the broth built on olive oil, fennel, orange peel, saffron and pastis-scented depth. The name is an instruction in Provençal: boui-abaisso — boil it, then lower the heat — because the broth must first roll hard to emulsify the oil, then settle. Rouille ("rust", for its colour) is non-negotiable: a pounded sauce of garlic, chilli, saffron and bread or potato, whisked with the broth. Cheap versions with frozen white fish are, by Marseille standards, simply other soups — the Mediterranean is full of fine ones, from Italian brodetto to Greek kakavia and Catalan suquet.

From shore pot to grand dish

Bouillabaisse's climb from poverty to ceremony tracks Marseille's own history: port workers' wives made it from the rockfish no merchant wanted; 19th-century restaurants on the Vieux-Port gentrified it; Paris and the world followed. It remains a dish where the argument is part of the pleasure — over price, over authenticity, over whether potatoes belong (the charter says yes, sliced into the broth) — conducted in the serious tone French cities reserve for their edible monuments.
