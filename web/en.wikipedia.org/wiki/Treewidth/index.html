Treewidth

In graph theory, the treewidth of an undirected graph measures how close the graph is to being a tree. It is defined through a tree decomposition: a tree whose nodes are subsets ("bags") of the graph's vertices, such that every edge has both endpoints in some bag, and the bags containing any given vertex form a connected subtree. The width of a decomposition is the size of its largest bag minus one, and the treewidth is the minimum width over all decompositions.

Properties

Trees themselves have treewidth 1; series-parallel graphs have treewidth at most 2; cliques on k vertices have treewidth k−1. Graphs of bounded treewidth exclude large grid minors, a connection made precise by the grid minor theorem of Robertson and Seymour.

Algorithms

Many problems that are NP-hard in general — such as graph coloring, Hamiltonian path, and problems expressible in monadic second-order logic (by Courcelle's theorem) — can be solved in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth by dynamic programming over a tree decomposition. Computing the treewidth itself is NP-hard, but fixed-parameter tractable algorithms and effective heuristics exist. Treewidth also underlies the use of junction trees in probabilistic graphical models.
