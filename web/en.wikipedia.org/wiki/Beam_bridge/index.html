Beam bridge

Beam bridges, also known as stringer bridges, are the simplest structural forms for bridge spans, supported by an abutment or pier at each end. No moments are transferred throughout the support, hence their structural type is known as simply supported. The simplest beam bridge is a log, plank or stone slab laid across a stream; modern beam bridges are built of steel or reinforced concrete girders, often as box girders, and include most highway overpasses and viaducts.

Design

The deck load is carried by one or more longitudinal beams (stringers or girders) resting on bearings at the supports. The beams work in bending: the top fibres compress and the bottom fibres stretch, so depth and material furthest from the neutral axis — as in an I-beam or hollow box — give the most strength for the least weight. Long crossings are made from many short simply-supported spans in sequence, as in the Lake Pontchartrain Causeway in Louisiana, one of the longest bridges in the world at nearly 24 miles (38 km), which is essentially thousands of beam spans joined end to end.

Variations

Beam bridges may be continuous over intermediate piers to reduce deflection, or built as cantilever-and-suspended-span (Gerber) arrangements. Prestressing concrete girders extends economical spans well beyond those of ordinary reinforced concrete. Because they are cheap, quick to erect and easy to maintain, beam bridges are by far the most common type of bridge worldwide.
