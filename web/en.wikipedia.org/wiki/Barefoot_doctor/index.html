Barefoot doctor

Barefoot doctors were rural medical workers in the People's Republic of China from the mid-1960s to the early 1980s. Typically farmers who received short — often three to six months' — training combining basic Western medicine with traditional Chinese medicine, they provided primary care, sanitation, vaccination, family planning and health education in their own villages as part of the Cooperative Medical System financed by agricultural collectives. The name arose because farmers in the rice-growing south often worked barefoot in the paddies.

History

The programme was greatly expanded after Mao Zedong's June 26 Directive of 1965 criticising the urban bias of the Ministry of Health, and at its height in the mid-1970s China had well over a million barefoot doctors, credited with major gains in rural life expectancy and control of infectious and parasitic diseases such as schistosomiasis. The model influenced the World Health Organization's primary-health-care philosophy set out in the 1978 Alma-Ata Declaration.

End of the programme

With the dismantling of the communes after 1978, the collective financing collapsed, and in 1985 the title was officially abolished: practitioners who passed examinations became "village doctors", while others reverted to farming or became fee-charging private practitioners, a transition that left lasting gaps in rural health coverage that later reforms have tried to fill.
