Asclepeion

An asclepeion (Ancient Greek: Ἀσκληπιεῖον, Asklepieion; Latin: aesculapium) was a healing temple or sanctuary dedicated to Asclepius, the Greek god of medicine, in the ancient Greek and Roman world. Part pilgrim shrine, part sanatorium, asclepieia combined religious ritual with regimen, bathing, diet and rest, and were staffed by priest-physicians. Several hundred are known across the Mediterranean world.

The cult and the cure

The most famous asclepieia were at Epidaurus in the Argolid — the cult's legendary birthplace, recognised by UNESCO — and on the island of Kos, associated with Hippocrates, as well as at Pergamon, Corinth and Athens. Pilgrims seeking a cure would purify themselves, make sacrifices and offerings, and then sleep overnight in a dormitory called the abaton in a rite known as incubation (enkoimesis). During sleep the god was expected to appear in a dream to heal the supplicant directly or to prescribe a treatment, which the priests then interpreted and applied. Grateful patients left votive offerings — often models of healed body parts — and inscriptions at Epidaurus, the iamata, record dozens of miracle cures.

Treatments

Beyond dream therapy, asclepieia offered baths, massage, exercise in the sanctuary gymnasium, diet, herbal remedies and sometimes surgery, and the sanctuaries featured theatres, stadia and libraries as part of a holistic regimen of body and mind. Non-venomous snakes, sacred to Asclepius, roamed the temples and featured in the healing ritual; the god's staff entwined by a single snake remains a symbol of medicine today.

History

The cult of Asclepius spread from the fifth century BCE, reaching Athens in 420 BCE and Rome in 293 BCE, when a temple was founded on Tiber Island during a plague. The sanctuaries flourished in the Roman imperial period — the Pergamon asclepeion was a fashionable spa where the physician Galen trained and the orator Aelius Aristides was a celebrated long-term patient — before declining with the suppression of pagan cults in late antiquity.
