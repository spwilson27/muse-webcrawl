Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis is a type of autoimmune encephalitis caused by antibodies against the NR1 subunit of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor in the brain. It is the most common autoimmune encephalitis and disproportionately affects young women and children.

Symptoms

The illness often begins with a flu-like prodrome, followed by prominent psychiatric symptoms — anxiety, agitation, hallucinations, delusions and catatonia — that frequently lead to initial psychiatric admission. Patients then develop seizures, memory loss, abnormal movements (especially orofacial and limb dyskinesias), speech disturbance, decreased consciousness and autonomic instability, sometimes requiring prolonged intensive care.

Cause and diagnosis

In many women the disorder is paraneoplastic, triggered by an ovarian teratoma containing neural tissue; it can also follow herpes simplex encephalitis. Diagnosis is confirmed by detecting IgG anti-NMDA receptor antibodies in cerebrospinal fluid. EEG often shows a characteristic "extreme delta brush" pattern. MRI is frequently normal or non-specific.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment consists of tumour removal where present and immunotherapy: corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin and plasma exchange first-line, with rituximab or cyclophosphamide for non-responders. With treatment, about 80% of patients recover substantially, though recovery may take months to years; relapses occur in a minority. The condition was brought to public attention by Susannah Cahalan's 2012 memoir Brain on Fire.
