Posidonia oceanica

Posidonia oceanica

Common name: Neptune grass
Family: Posidoniaceae
Habitat: Mediterranean Sea
Depth: 1–35 m

Posidonia oceanica, commonly known as Neptune grass or Mediterranean tapeweed, is a seagrass species endemic to the Mediterranean Sea. It forms large underwater meadows that are among the most productive and ecologically important ecosystems in the Mediterranean, supporting fisheries, stabilising sediment and storing carbon.

Description

The plant has long, ribbon-like green leaves up to about a metre long, growing from creeping rhizomes buried in sandy sediment. It flowers only rarely, in autumn, and its olive-like floating fruits — sometimes called "olives of the sea" — disperse by water. Dead leaves accumulate on beaches as fibrous balls (egagropili) and banquettes that protect shores from erosion.

Ecology and age

Posidonia grows extremely slowly, spreading clonally by rhizomes. A vast clonal colony near the Balearic Islands, about 8 km across, has been estimated from genetic markers to be among the oldest and largest living organisms on Earth, possibly around 100,000 years old. The meadows shelter hundreds of species and act as nurseries for commercial fish.

Conservation

The species is in decline due to coastal development, trawling, anchoring and warming waters, and is protected under the EU Habitats Directive and the Barcelona Convention. The seagrass meadows around Ibiza and Formentera form part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
