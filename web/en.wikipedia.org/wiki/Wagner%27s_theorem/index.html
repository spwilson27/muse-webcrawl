Wagner's theorem

In graph theory, Wagner's theorem is a characterization of planar graphs formulated by Klaus Wagner in 1937. It states that a finite graph is planar if and only if its minors include neither the complete graph K5 nor the complete bipartite graph K3,3. A minor of a graph is formed by deleting vertices and edges and contracting edges.

Relation to Kuratowski's theorem

Wagner's theorem is closely related to Kuratowski's theorem, which characterizes planar graphs by forbidden subdivisions of K5 and K3,3 rather than minors. The two results are equivalent for these particular graphs: a graph contains a subdivision of K3,3 if and only if it contains K3,3 as a minor, and similarly, a K5 subdivision/minor relationship holds because K5 has maximum degree four.

Generalizations

Wagner's work on graph minors laid the groundwork for the Robertson–Seymour theorem, which states that any family of graphs closed under minors can be characterized by a finite set of forbidden minors. Forbidden-minor characterizations now exist for many other graph properties, such as having bounded genus or not containing a given graph as a minor.
