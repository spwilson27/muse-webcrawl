The Vienna Circle (Wiener Kreis) was a group of philosophers, scientists, and mathematicians centered on the University of Vienna who met regularly from 1924 to 1936 and founded logical positivism. Inspired by Ernst Mach, Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus, and Einstein's relativity, they sought to reconstruct philosophy on rigorous logical and empirical foundations, eliminating metaphysics as cognitively meaningless.

History

The group coalesced around Moritz Schlick, appointed in 1922 to the Vienna chair in the philosophy of the inductive sciences. Regular Thursday-evening meetings came to include Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath, Hans Hahn, Friedrich Waismann, Herbert Feigl, Kurt Gödel (an occasional participant), and visitors such as Karl Popper (a critical associate) and Willard Van Orman Quine. Their 1929 manifesto, The Scientific Conception of the World: The Vienna Circle, announced the movement publicly, and the journal Erkenntnis (founded 1930) became its organ.

Ideas

The Circle's signature doctrine was the verifiability principle: a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytic (true by definition, as in logic and mathematics) or empirically verifiable in principle. Metaphysical, theological, and ethical statements were declared cognitively meaningless — at best expressions of attitude. Allied projects included the logical analysis of science's language, the unity of science movement (Neurath's International Encyclopedia of Unified Science), and the reduction of theoretical terms to an observational basis.

Dissolution

The Nazi rise to power scattered the group: Carnap left for Prague in 1931 and then the United States, Neurath fled after the 1934 Austrian civil war, and Schlick was murdered on the university steps in 1936 by a deranged former student. The Anschluss of 1938 ended the Circle, but its émigrés transformed Anglophone philosophy of science.

Influence

As logical empiricism, the Circle's program dominated philosophy of science for decades; its critiques — by Popper, Quine, Kuhn, and the Circle's own members — define the field's modern agenda. Its emphasis on logical analysis also propelled analytic philosophy.
