Hot hatch

A hot hatch is a high-performance version of a mass-produced hatchback car. The term originated in the United Kingdom in the early 1980s but had been in use for each individual car's name — such as the "GTI" suffix — earlier. Most hot hatches are front-wheel drive with a four-cylinder engine, though all-wheel-drive examples have become common.

Origins

Early examples include the 1973 Simca 1100 Ti and the 1976 Volkswagen Golf GTI, which is widely credited with defining the genre, followed by the 1984 Peugeot 205 GTi — both combining an economical family hatchback body with a tuned engine, uprated suspension, and sporty trim. The Renault 5 Turbo added a mid-engined rally variant to the era.

Development

By the 2000s, turbocharging pushed outputs beyond 250 horsepower in cars such as the Ford Focus RS, Renault Mégane RS, and Honda Civic Type R, while all-wheel-drive models like the Volkswagen Golf R and the Toyota GR Yaris brought rally-bred hardware to the segment. "Hyper hatches" such as the Mercedes-AMG A 45 S exceed 400 horsepower. The type's appeal rests on combining everyday practicality with sports-car performance at a comparatively low price.
