Stephen Joshua Sondheim (March 22, 1930 – November 26, 2021) was an American composer and lyricist. Regarded as one of the most important figures in twentieth-century musical theatre, Sondheim is credited with reinventing the American musical with shows that combine sophisticated music, intricate lyrics, and unusually dark or ambivalent subject matter.

Early life and mentorship

Sondheim was born in New York City and, after his parents' divorce, lived near Oscar Hammerstein II, who became his mentor and surrogate father; Sondheim famously said that if Hammerstein had been a geologist, he would have become a geologist. He studied music at Williams College and privately with composer Milton Babbitt. His professional break came as lyricist for West Side Story (1957) and Gypsy (1959), after which he resolved to write both music and lyrics.

Career

His first score as composer-lyricist, A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum (1962), won the Tony for Best Musical. After the commercial failures of Anyone Can Whistle (1964) and Do I Hear a Waltz? (1965), he began a legendary collaboration with director Hal Prince: Company (1970), Follies (1971), A Little Night Music (1973, containing his most famous song, "Send in the Clowns"), Pacific Overtures (1976), Sweeney Todd (1979), and Merrily We Roll Along (1981). With book writer-director James Lapine he created Sunday in the Park with George (1984, Pulitzer Prize), Into the Woods (1987), and Passion (1994). Assassins (1990) and the late Road Show (2008) completed a major canon unmatched in the form.

Style and legacy

Sondheim's songs are marked by dense internal rhyme, contrapuntal ensembles, and a refusal of easy sentiment — "content dictates form," he wrote, in his two volumes of collected lyrics, Finishing the Hat (2010) and Look, I Made a Hat (2011). He won eight Tony Awards (plus a Lifetime Achievement Award), an Academy Award (for "Sooner or Later" from Dick Tracy), and the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2015. The Stephen Sondheim Theatre on Broadway bears his name, and his death in 2021 prompted theatres on Broadway and in the West End to dim their lights in tribute.
