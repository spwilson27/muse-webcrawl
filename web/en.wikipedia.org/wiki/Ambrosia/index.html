Ambrosia

In the ancient Greek myths, ambrosia (Greek: ἀμβροσία, "immortality") is the food or drink of the Greek gods, often depicted as conferring longevity or immortality upon whoever consumed it. It was brought to the gods in Olympus by doves and served by Hebe or Ganymede.

In mythology

Ambrosia is closely related to nectar, the other substance of the gods; in Homer the distinction is usually that nectar is the drink and ambrosia the food, though the terms are sometimes reversed or used interchangeably. Gods who lacked ambrosia and nectar would lose their immortality. The substance was also used as an ointment: Thetis anointed the body of Patroclus with ambrosia to preserve it, and ambrosia was said to cleanse and beautify the skin of goddesses. Mortals granted ambrosia could become immortal, as nearly happened to Demophon in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.

Interpretations

Scholars have speculated about the earthly origins of the concept, proposing honey, olive oil, or other prized foods as models. The word derives from the Greek a- ("not") and mbrotos ("mortal"), meaning "not mortal" or "immortal".

Other uses

The name "ambrosia" has been applied to various earthly things: the ragweed genus Ambrosia, ambrosia beetles and their fungal food, a fruit salad popular in the American South, and beebread in beekeeping. In modern fantasy fiction, ambrosia appears frequently as a divine or healing food.
