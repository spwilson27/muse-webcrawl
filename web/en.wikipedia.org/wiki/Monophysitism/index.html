Monophysitism

Monophysitism is a Christological doctrine, derived from the Greek monos ("one") and physis ("nature"), holding that Jesus Christ has only one nature — either divine, or a unified divine-human nature — rather than the two natures, divine and human, affirmed by the Council of Chalcedon in 451. The term was applied by Chalcedonians, often polemically, to opponents of the council's definition.

The Council of Chalcedon (451) defined Christ as one person in two natures, rejecting both Nestorianism and extreme Monophysitism.

Origins

The controversy grew out of the Alexandrian tradition of Cyril of Alexandria, whose formula "one nature of God the Word incarnate" (mia physis tou Theou Logou sesarkōmenē) was taken up by later theologians. In its extreme form, taught by the archimandrite Eutyches, the human nature of Christ was said to be absorbed into the divine "like a drop of honey in the sea", a teaching condemned at Chalcedon.

After Chalcedon

Many Christians in Egypt and Syria rejected the council, which they regarded as a betrayal of Cyril and a slide toward Nestorianism. Imperial attempts at compromise — such as Zeno's Henotikon (482) — failed to restore unity, and by the 6th century separate hierarchies had formed. These churches, today the Oriental Orthodox communion (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara churches), generally reject the label "Monophysite" and prefer Miaphysitism, affirming one united nature of Christ "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation".

Modern dialogue

In the 20th century, official dialogues between the Oriental Orthodox and the Eastern Orthodox, and with the Roman Catholic Church, concluded that the ancient disputes were substantially matters of terminology and politics rather than of faith, and agreed statements on Christology have been issued by both sides.
