Sapper

For other uses, see Sapper (disambiguation).


Combat engineers constructing a pontoon bridge

A sapper, also called a combat engineer, pioneer or field engineer, is a soldier who performs a variety of military engineering duties in support of land forces, especially under combat conditions. The word derives from the French sape, a trench or approach dug toward an enemy fortress during a siege, from Italian zappa, a spade or hoe.

Role

Sappers specialise in enabling the movement of friendly forces and denying movement to the enemy. Their tasks include:

  • constructing and breaching fortifications, trenches and obstacles;
  • laying and clearing minefields;
  • demolitions, including destroying bridges, roads and enemy structures, and breaching defences with explosive charges;
  • building temporary bridges (such as pontoon and Bailey bridges), roads and airfields;
  • constructing defensive positions, camouflage and field sanitation and water supply.

Because they frequently work ahead of the main body under fire, sappers are trained as infantry as well as engineers, and the work is considered among the most dangerous in an army.

History

The sapping approach — digging zig-zag trenches toward a besieged fortress while protected by gabions and earthworks — was systematised in the siege warfare of the seventeenth century, notably by Vauban, Louis XIV's engineer. Sappers digging the parallels at a siege were exposed targets, and the role became a byword for hazardous duty.

Modern engineer corps descend from these specialists. In the British Army, "Sapper" is both the generic term for soldiers of the Royal Engineers and the corps' equivalent of the rank of private; the French sapeur survives in the Foreign Legion's pioneer platoons. In the First World War vast armies of engineers dug the trench systems of the Western Front and tunnelled beneath enemy lines to plant mines, as at the Battle of Messines in 1917. In the Second World War combat engineers became essential to amphibious landings and river crossings, and specialised assault engineer vehicles were developed.
