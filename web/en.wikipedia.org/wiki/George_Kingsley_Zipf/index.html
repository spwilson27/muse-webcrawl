George Kingsley Zipf (January 7, 1902 – September 25, 1950) was an American linguist and philologist who studied statistical occurrences in different languages. He is best known for Zipf's law, the empirical observation that the frequency of a word in a text is approximately inversely proportional to its rank in the frequency table, and for his broader principle of least effort, proposed as a unifying explanation of human behaviour.

Career

Zipf was born in Freeport, Illinois, and educated at Harvard, where he took his PhD in German philology in 1930 after study in Bonn and Berlin. He spent his career at Harvard, first in the German department and later as a lecturer in linguistics and university professor. His early statistical work appeared in Selected Studies of the Principle of Relative Frequency in Language (1932) and The Psycho-Biology of Language (1935), which introduced the frequency–rank relation that now bears his name (anticipated in part by Jean-Baptiste Estoup and Felix Auerbach, among others).

Zipf's law and least effort

Zipf's law states that if words in a large corpus are ranked by frequency, the frequency of the r-th word is roughly proportional to 1/r: the second most common word occurs about half as often as the first, the third about a third as often, and so on. Similar rank-size regularities were found by Zipf and others in city populations, income distributions, and many other domains. In his final book, Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort (1949), Zipf argued that such patterns arise because individuals and systems organise their behaviour to minimise the average rate of work — the "principle of least effort" — balancing the speaker's economy (fewer, versatile words) against the hearer's economy (precise, distinct words).

Legacy

Zipfian distributions remain a benchmark in quantitative linguistics, information theory, and the study of complex systems, and Zipf's law is a standard test for models of language and random generation. Zipf died in 1950 at the age of 48. The generalised family of discrete distributions bearing his name (the Zipf and Zipf–Mandelbrot distributions) is used in fields from bibliometrics to network traffic modelling.
