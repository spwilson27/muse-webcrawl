Charanga (Cuba)

Charanga
OriginCuba, early 20th century
Typical instrumentsEuropean classical flute, violins, piano, güiro, timbales, congas
Associated genresDanzón, mambo, cha-cha-chá

Charanga is a traditional Cuban dance music ensemble, derived from the French charanga francesa orchestras that played French contredanses in Cuba in the early 19th century. The classic charanga combines European instrumentation — wooden five-key (later Boehm-system) flute and violins — with African-derived percussion such as güiro, timbales, and later congas and bass.

History

Charangas became the standard orchestra for the danzón, Cuba's national dance. In the late 1930s, Orquesta Aragón and Arcaño y sus Maravillas developed the nuevo ritmo that led to the mambo; in the early 1950s, charanga violinist and composer Enrique Jorrín created the cha-cha-chá from charanga repertoire. Famous charangas include Orquesta Aragón (founded 1939 and still active), Charanga Habanera (a leading timba band formed in 1988), and Los Van Van's charanga-influenced songo format.

Style

The charanga sound is marked by the flute's virtuoso improvisations (charanga flute solos are a hallmark), the violins' rhythmic guajeos, and syncopated percussion. Musicians such as flautists José Fajardo and Richard Egües became celebrated exponents of the style.
