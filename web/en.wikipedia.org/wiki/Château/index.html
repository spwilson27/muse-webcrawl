Château

A château in the Loire Valley, France.

A château (plural: châteaux) is a manor house or residence of the lord of the manor, or a fine country house of nobility or gentry, with or without fortifications, originally — and still most frequently — in French-speaking regions of Europe. The word derives from the Latin castellum, a diminutive of castra (fortified place), via Old French chastel.

Concept

The term is loosely applied to grand residences far beyond France, but in its strict French usage a château is a structure that, whatever its size, bears the name and historically belonged to a seigneur. Not every French country house is a château: a maison de campagne or manoir lacks the seigneurial association. Historically, châteaux ranged from fortified medieval strongholds such as the Château de Chenonceau's predecessors, through Renaissance palaces of the Loire Valley — Chambord, Blois, Amboise — to elegant 17th- and 18th-century houses surrounded by formal gardens.

Wine châteaux

In the Bordeaux wine region, the word château is used for a wine-producing estate, whether or not a grand house stands on it. Famous examples include Château Pétrus, Château Margaux and Château Lafite Rothschild. The usage spread to wine estates elsewhere in France and, by imitation, to wineries in other countries.

Regional usage

In Belgium and Switzerland the term is also common (French: château; German equivalents include Schloss). In Canada, particularly in Quebec, it was adopted for grand railway hotels such as the Château Frontenac in Quebec City and the Château Laurier in Ottawa, built in the "château style" of architecture.
