Dugout canoe

A dugout canoe or simply dugout is a boat made from a hollowed-out tree trunk. Other names for this type of boat are logboat and monoxylon. Monoxylon (μονόξυλον) (pl: monoxyla) is Greek for "single piece of wood". Construction of a dugout begins with the selection of a log of suitable dimensions. Sufficient wood must be removed to make the vessel light and buoyant, but strong enough to support one or more occupants and any cargo. Specialised forms of dugouts can also include stronger types such as the pirogue.

Dugout canoes are among the oldest types of boat known to archaeologists. The oldest known example is the Pesse canoe, found in the Netherlands and dated to around 8040–7510 BC. Dugouts have been used by indigenous peoples on every inhabited continent, and they remain in use today in parts of Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas for fishing and transport.

Construction

Traditionally, the interior of the log was hollowed out using controlled fire, which charred the wood so it could be scraped away with stone, shell, or metal adzes. The process could take weeks. Builders often selected softwoods such as pine or balsa, or hardwoods such as teak and mahogany, depending on local availability. Some dugouts were fitted with outriggers for stability at sea, notably in the Pacific, while others were expanded by spreading the hull with heat and water to increase the beam.

Regional forms

In Europe, logboats were used from the Mesolithic through the medieval period; the Hanson Log Boat, found in Derbyshire, England, dates to the Bronze Age. In West Africa, large dugouts served riverine trade, and in the Amazon basin dugouts remain a primary means of transport. The Māori of New Zealand built large war canoes (waka taua) from single tōtara logs, some exceeding 30 metres. In North America, dugouts were used by peoples of the Pacific Northwest, the Southeast, and the Caribbean, where the Taíno word canoa gave rise to the English word "canoe".
