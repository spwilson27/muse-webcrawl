Arboreal locomotion

Arboreal locomotion
HabitatTree canopies and branches
FormsBrachiation, climbing, leaping, bridging, gliding, quadrupedal walking on branches
Example animalsPrimates, sloths, squirrels, koalas, many birds and snakes

Arboreal locomotion is the movement of animals through trees. Arboreal habitats pose mechanical challenges — discontinuous, narrow, compliant and steeply angled supports — and species that live in them have evolved a wide range of anatomical and behavioural solutions, from grasping hands and prehensile tails to flight membranes.

Forms

Brachiation

Brachiation is arm-over-arm swinging beneath branches, practised most perfectly by gibbons and siamangs, whose long arms, short thumbs, and ball-and-socket wrist joints let them move rapidly through the canopy; spider monkeys use a semi-brachiating gait assisted by a prehensile tail.

Climbing and clinging

Vertical clinging and leaping is characteristic of small primates such as tarsiers and indriids, which cling to trunks and launch between them with elongated hind limbs. Sloths move slowly beneath branches in a suspended posture with hook-like claws. Squirrels climb head-up and head-down using sharp claws and reversible hind feet.

Gliding and parachuting

Flying squirrels, colugos, sugar gliders, and the paradise tree snake flatten or spread membranes to glide between trees, trading height for distance.

Adaptations

Common adaptations include opposable digits and nails (or claws) for grip, enhanced depth perception and balance organs, low centres of mass, friction pads, and tails used as counterweights or fifth limbs. Arboreal snakes concentrate weight and use the scales' grip to cantilever across gaps.

Evolutionary significance

The arboreal habits of early primates are central to theories of primate origins: grasping extremities, forward-facing eyes, and increased brain size have all been linked to life in the trees — whether for leaping between fine branches to catch insects (the visual predation hypothesis) or for exploiting flowering-plant resources (the angiosperm coevolution hypothesis).
