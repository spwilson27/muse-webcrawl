B. B. King

Riley Benjamin King (September 16, 1925 – May 14, 2015), known professionally as B. B. King, was an American blues singer-songwriter, guitarist, and record producer. Dubbed the "King of the Blues", he is regarded as one of the most influential blues musicians of all time, and one of the "Three Kings of the Blues Guitar", alongside Albert King and Freddie King.

Life and career

King was born on a cotton plantation in Berclair, Mississippi, and raised in Kilmichael and Indianola. He worked as a tractor driver and sang in gospel groups before moving to Memphis in 1946, where he worked as a disc jockey at WDIA — earning the nickname "Blues Boy", shortened to "B. B." — and recorded for Sam Phillips. His breakthrough came in 1951 with "Three O'Clock Blues".

Through the 1950s King toured relentlessly, reportedly playing 342 shows in 1956. His album Live at the Regal (1965) became a landmark live recording, and in 1969 his single "The Thrill Is Gone" won a Grammy Award and brought him to mainstream audiences. He opened for the Rolling Stones, collaborated with Eric Clapton on Riding with the King (2000), and maintained a touring schedule of some 200 concerts a year well into old age. His guitars, a succession of Gibson ES-355s, were all named "Lucille", after a woman over whom a fight started a fire at a dance hall in Twist, Arkansas.

Legacy

King won 15 Grammy Awards, was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1987, and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2006. His expressive single-string soloing, using vibrato and string bending, shaped generations of blues and rock guitarists.
