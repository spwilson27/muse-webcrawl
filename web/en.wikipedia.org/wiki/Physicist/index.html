Physicist

A physicist is a scientist who specializes in the field of physics, which encompasses the interactions of matter and energy at all length and time scales in the physical universe. Physicists generally are interested in the root or ultimate causes of phenomena, and usually frame their understanding in mathematical terms. Physicists work across a wide range of research fields, spanning all length scales: from sub-atomic and particle physics, through biological physics, to cosmological length scales encompassing the universe as a whole.


Albert Einstein (1879–1955) is often regarded as one of the greatest physicists of all time.

The field generally includes two types of physicists: experimental physicists who specialize in the observation of natural phenomena and the development and analysis of experiments, and theoretical physicists who specialize in mathematical modeling of physical systems to rationalize, explain and predict natural phenomena.

Physicists can apply their knowledge towards solving practical problems or to developing new technologies (also known as applied physics or engineering physics).

History

The study of what came to be known as physics developed out of natural philosophy. Figures such as Galileo Galilei, Johannes Kepler and Isaac Newton established classical mechanics; the 19th century unified electricity and magnetism (James Clerk Maxwell) and developed thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. The revolutions of relativity (Albert Einstein) and quantum mechanics (Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger, Paul Dirac and others) transformed the field in the early 20th century. The word "physicist" was coined in the 19th century, commonly attributed to William Whewell.

Education

Physicists typically hold a bachelor's degree in physics or a related subject, and research physicists usually hold a PhD. Undergraduate curricula include classical mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, quantum mechanics, and laboratory work, supported by substantial mathematics.

Employment

The largest employers of physicists are universities and academic institutions, government laboratories, and industry. Many physics graduates also work in engineering, computing, finance, medicine (medical physics), and education. Professional bodies such as the American Physical Society and the Institute of Physics represent physicists and accredit degrees.

Honours and awards

The highest honour awarded to physicists is the Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded annually since 1901 by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. Other notable awards include the Max Planck Medal, the Dirac Medal, and the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics.
