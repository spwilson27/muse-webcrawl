Russian symbolism was an intellectual and artistic movement predominant at the end of the 19th and beginning of the 20th century in the Russian Empire. It arose largely independently of, though influenced by, the French symbolist movement, and became the founding current of the "Silver Age" of Russian culture, transforming poetry, prose, theatre, music, and the visual arts. Unlike its French counterpart, Russian symbolism was driven as much by philosophy and religious thought — above all the writings of Vladimir Solovyov — as by aesthetics.

Origins

The movement's manifesto is generally considered to be Dmitry Merezhkovsky's lecture and book On the Causes of the Decline and on the New Trends in Contemporary Russian Literature (1892–93), which proclaimed mystical content, symbols, and the expansion of artistic sensitivity as the elements of a new art. Nikolai Minsky and Zinaida Gippius were among its early theorists, and Valery Bryusov's anthologies Russian Symbolists (1894–95) introduced younger readers to the new poetry.

The two generations

Elder symbolists

The first generation — Bryusov, Konstantin Balmont, Fyodor Sologub, Gippius, and Merezhkovsky — emphasised aesthetic innovation and the doctrine of "art for art's sake," often in a Decadent register. Bryusov, organiser of the influential journal Vesy (The Balance, 1904–09) and the publishing house Scorpion, became the movement's chief impresario.

Younger symbolists

Around 1900 a younger generation — Alexander Blok, Andrei Bely, and Vyacheslav Ivanov — turned symbolism towards a mystical philosophy of history. Deeply influenced by Solovyov's doctrine of Sophia, the Divine Wisdom and "Eternal Feminine," and by expectations of apocalyptic transformation, they saw the symbol not as decoration but as a real link between worlds ("a realibus ad realiora" — "from the real to the more real," in Ivanov's phrase). Blok's Verses About the Beautiful Lady (1904) and Bely's novel Petersburg (1913–14, often cited as the movement's prose masterpiece) exemplify this phase.

Decline and legacy

By 1910 the movement fragmented: Blok declared a "crisis of symbolism," and the younger poets Nikolai Gumilev and Osip Mandelstam founded Acmeism in reaction, while Futurists such as Vladimir Mayakovsky attacked the symbolists outright. Nevertheless, symbolism's renovation of Russian poetic language, its theory, and its fusion of literature with philosophy and music shaped all subsequent Russian modernism.
