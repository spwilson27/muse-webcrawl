Fat Tuesday is the traditional name for the day before Ash Wednesday, the first day of Lent in the Western Christian tradition. It is more commonly known in English as Shrove Tuesday and in French as Mardi Gras (literally "Fat Tuesday"). The name reflects the custom of using up rich foods such as eggs, butter, milk and meat before the Lenten fast, which traditionally abstains from such foods for the forty days of Lent.

Names and traditions

In Britain and Ireland the day is popularly Pancake Day, because pancakes were a way to use up eggs, milk and sugar before Lent; pancake races, most famously at Olney in Buckinghamshire (held since 1445 by tradition), mark the day. In many Catholic countries the day crowns the carnival season: the Carnival of Venice, the Rhineland Karneval, Fasching in Austria and Bavaria, and festivities across Latin America, most famously the Rio Carnival and the Barranquilla Carnival in Colombia.

Mardi Gras in the United States

In the United States "Mardi Gras" is most strongly associated with New Orleans, Louisiana, where French colonial tradition developed into elaborate parades organised by "krewes" such as Rex (founded 1872) and Zulu, with floats, marching bands and the throwing of beads and doubloons to crowds. Mobile, Alabama, claims the oldest organised US Mardi Gras (dating to 1703), and celebrations are also held across the Gulf Coast, in St. Louis, and elsewhere. Traditional foods include the king cake, a ring-shaped pastry containing a small hidden figurine or bean.

Religious observance

"Shrove" derives from shrive, to hear confession: Christians traditionally confessed their sins before Lent. Many churches ring the "pancake bell" and hold services of absolution; the following day, Ash Wednesday, begins the penitential season.
