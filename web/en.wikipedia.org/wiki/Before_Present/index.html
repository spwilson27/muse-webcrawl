Before Present

Before Present (BP) is a time scale used in archaeology, geology and other scientific disciplines to specify when events occurred relative to a fixed reference point. By convention, "the present" is AD 1950, chosen because radiocarbon dating was invented in the late 1940s and because atmospheric nuclear weapons testing after 1950 artificially altered the proportion of carbon-14 in the atmosphere, disturbing the basis of the method. A date of, for example, 10,000 BP therefore means 10,000 years before 1950 — approximately 8050 BC.

Radiocarbon years

Dates quoted in BP are frequently uncalibrated radiocarbon years rather than calendar years. Because the atmospheric concentration of carbon-14 has varied over time, a radiocarbon age must be converted using a calibration curve (such as the IntCal curves) to obtain a calendar age. To avoid confusion, calibrated dates are often written "cal BP" or expressed as BCE/CE. Radiocarbon years also conventionally use the Libby half-life of 5,568 years rather than the more accurate 5,730 years, with the discrepancy absorbed by calibration.

Usage

BP is standard in Quaternary science for events of the last ~50,000 years, the practical limit of radiocarbon dating. For older material, ages are simply given in years ago, ka (thousands of years) or Ma (millions of years). In contexts not involving radiocarbon, some disciplines define BP differently — for example, ice-core chronologies may use "b2k", years before AD 2000 — so the reference point should always be checked.

History

The convention emerged with Willard Libby's development of radiocarbon dating in 1949, with 1950 adopted as the epoch of the scale in the 1950s as journals standardised reporting practice. The term is sometimes backronymed or miswritten as "Before Physics", referring to the nuclear tests; this is a folk etymology, though it reflects the real reason 1950 was chosen as the cutoff.
