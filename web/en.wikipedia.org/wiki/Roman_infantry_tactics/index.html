Roman infantry tactics

Roman infantry tactics were the battle methods employed by the heavy infantry of the Roman Republic and Empire. Built around the disciplined legion, these tactics combined missile volleys, a short sword optimized for close combat, and formations that could be adapted to terrain and enemy, giving Roman armies a consistency of performance unmatched in the ancient world.

The manipular legion

By the middle Republic the legion was organized in three lines of heavy infantry: the young hastati in front, the seasoned principes behind them, and the veteran triarii in reserve, screened by light-armed velites. The lines were arranged in a checkerboard (quincunx) pattern so that a hard-pressed front line could retire through the gaps of the line behind and fresh troops come forward in its place.

Weapons and the standard engagement

The classical legionary carried the heavy javelin or pilum, the short thrusting sword (gladius) and a large curved shield (scutum). The standard engagement began with skirmishing, followed at close range by a volley of pila intended to kill, wound and encumber enemy shields. The legionaries then closed at a run, punching with the shield boss and thrusting with the gladius from behind the protection of the scutum.

Formations

  • Testudo (tortoise) — shields overlapped overhead and on all sides against missiles, used in sieges.
  • Wedge — a triangular thrust to break an enemy line at one point.
  • Orb — a circular all-round defence used when isolated.
  • Repellere equites — a kneeling shield wall with pila angled upward against cavalry.

The cohort legion

After the Marian reforms the legion was reorganized into ten cohorts of roughly 480 men, all equipped alike, usually deployed in two or three lines. Auxiliary infantry and cavalry covered the flanks. Roman practice stressed entrenchment — a fortified camp every night — relentless drill, and rapid tactical reinforcement of threatened sectors, which together made the system resilient even when individual battles began badly.
