Leukotriene

Leukotrienes are a family of eicosanoid inflammatory mediators produced in leukocytes by the oxidation of arachidonic acid and the essential fatty acid eicosapentaenoic acid by the enzyme 5-lipoxygenase. Their name, coined from leukocyte and triene (three conjugated double bonds), reflects their discovery in white blood cells and their characteristic structure.

Biosynthesis

5-Lipoxygenase converts arachidonic acid to the unstable epoxide leukotriene A4 (LTA4). From LTA4, cells produce leukotriene B4 (LTB4) or the cysteinyl leukotrienes LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4, formed by conjugation with glutathione and subsequent trimming of the peptide. The cysteinyl leukotrienes account for the activity once called "slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis" (SRS-A).

Function

LTB4 is a potent chemoattractant that recruits neutrophils to sites of inflammation. The cysteinyl leukotrienes act on CysLT receptors in the airways to cause bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, mucus secretion, and eosinophil recruitment, and they play a central role in asthma and allergic rhinitis. Leukotrienes are far more potent bronchoconstrictors than histamine.

Clinical significance

Leukotriene receptor antagonists such as montelukast and zafirlukast, and the 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor zileuton, are used in the treatment of asthma and seasonal allergies. Leukotriene pathways are also implicated in anaphylaxis, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, and cardiovascular inflammation.
