Crimes against humanity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crimes against humanity are certain serious offences committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against any civilian population, pursuant to or in furtherance of a state or organisational policy. Enumerated in the Rome Statute (Article 7), they include murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, imprisonment, torture, sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance and apartheid, and other inhumane acts of a similar character. Unlike war crimes, they may occur in peacetime; unlike genocide, no intent to destroy a protected group is required, though persecution targets groups discriminatorily.

History

The phrase gained legal form in response to the Armenian massacres (the 1915 Allied declaration spoke of "crimes against humanity and civilisation") and was codified in the Nuremberg Charter (1945) and applied by the ICTY, ICTR and ICC. A dedicated global treaty on crimes against humanity has been in preparation at the UN (the General Assembly resolved in 2024 to move towards negotiations), aiming to do for these crimes what the Genocide Convention did for genocide: impose prevention and inter-state cooperation duties.
