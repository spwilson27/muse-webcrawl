Hartmann's operation, also known as Hartmann's procedure or a proctosigmoidectomy, is a surgical operation in which the sigmoid colon and the upper rectum are removed, the end of the descending colon is brought out as an end colostomy, and the rectal stump is closed off and left inside the pelvis.

History

The procedure was described in 1921 by the French surgeon Henri Hartmann for the treatment of obstructing cancer of the rectosigmoid colon. It was intended to reduce operative mortality compared with more extensive resections of the period.

Indications

Today Hartmann's operation is most often performed as an emergency procedure for complicated diverticulitis with perforation or abscess, obstructing or perforated colorectal cancer, traumatic bowel injury, or ischaemic colitis — situations in which creating an immediate bowel join (anastomosis) would carry a high risk of leakage. In a second operation months later, the colostomy may be reversed and continuity restored, although many patients never undergo reversal.

Technique

Through a laparotomy or laparoscopy, the diseased segment is resected, the rectal stump is stapled or sewn closed, and the proximal colon is exteriorised through the abdominal wall as a stoma. Care is taken to preserve blood supply and to identify the stump for later reversal.
