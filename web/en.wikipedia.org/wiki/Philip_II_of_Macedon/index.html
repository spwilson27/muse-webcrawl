Philip II of Macedon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Gold larnax and royal tomb at Vergina (ancient Aegae), attributed to Philip II]

Philip II of Macedon (382–336 BC) was king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon from 359 BC until his assassination in 336 BC. A military and diplomatic genius, he transformed a weak, threatened kingdom into the dominant power of Greece: he rebuilt the Macedonian army around the sarissa pike phalanx and combined arms, used gold from Mount Pangaeum, marriages and bribery as instruments of statecraft, defeated Athens and Thebes at Chaeronea (338 BC), and organised the Greek states in the League of Corinth for a planned invasion of Persia. His son and successor, Alexander the Great, executed that plan with the instrument Philip had forged.

The making of Macedon

Philip came to power after his brother Perdiccas III and 4,000 men fell to the Illyrians. As hostage in Thebes in his youth he had studied Epaminondas' tactics; he now created a professional army — phalangites with 18-foot sarissas, companion cavalry, hypaspists, engineers with torsion catapults — drilled and marched year-round. Victory over Illyrians, Thracians and the Phocians (in the Third Sacred War) and seizure of the Pangaeum gold mines made Macedon rich and feared; Demosthenes' Philippics warned Athens in vain.

Hegemony and assassination

At Chaeronea (338 BC) Philip destroyed the allied Athenian-Theban army (the Theban Sacred Band died where it stood) yet treated Athens leniently, preferring willing partners for the Persian war. The League of Corinth (337 BC) elected him hegemon for the invasion. In October 336 BC, at the wedding of his daughter in Aegae, Philip was stabbed by his bodyguard Pausanias — for reasons ancient sources and modern historians still dispute. Alexander, aged twenty, inherited army, plan and enemies.
