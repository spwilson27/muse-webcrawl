Woburn Abbey, near Woburn in Bedfordshire, England, is the family seat of the Duke of Bedford. The present house, occupying the site of a Cistercian abbey granted to John Russell, 1st Earl of Bedford, in 1547 after the Dissolution of the Monasteries, was largely rebuilt in the mid-18th century to designs by Henry Flitcroft, with interiors and the Chinese Dairy added by Henry Holland.

The house holds one of England's great private art collections — including works by Rembrandt, Van Dyck, Gainsborough, Reynolds and Canaletto — and its deer park and safari park are major visitor attractions. Woburn is also celebrated in natural history as the home of the herd that saved Père David's deer from extinction.

History

Woburn Abbey was founded as a Cistercian house in 1145. Granted to the Russells at the Dissolution, it was rebuilt in stages; the surviving west front dates from Flitcroft's remodelling for the 4th Duke from 1744, and the 6th Duke employed Humphry Repton to landscape the grounds. A full wing — the east front — was demolished in 1950 after dry rot was discovered, halving the house.

Architecture and interiors

The Palladian west front faces the park across a lake. State rooms include the saloon, the state dining room and the Long Gallery, hung with 24 views of Venice by Canaletto commissioned by the 4th Duke. The French architect Henry Holland added the Chinese Dairy and riding school in the 1780s.

Collections

Highlights include the Armada Portrait of Elizabeth I (attributed to George Gower), Van Dyck portraits of the Russell family, silver by Paul de Lamerie, Sèvres porcelain and one of the finest private libraries in England.

The park and animals

The 3,000-acre deer park holds nine species of deer. In 1894–1901 the 11th Duke assembled the last surviving Père David's deer (milu) from European zoos at Woburn; all animals of the species alive today descend from this herd, and deer from Woburn founded the reintroduced population in China. Woburn Safari Park, opened in 1970, was among Britain's first drive-through reserves.

Public opening

Hastings Russell, 12th Duke, and especially Hugh Percy "Ian" Russell, 13th Duke, opened the house to the public in the 1950s, pioneering the commercial stately-home business; the safari park and antiques centre followed.
