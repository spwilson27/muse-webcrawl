Court of last resort

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A court of last resort (or final court of appeal, supreme court, or court of cassation in civil-law usage) is the highest court in a jurisdiction, whose decisions on questions of law bind all other courts and ordinarily cannot be appealed. It ensures uniformity of legal interpretation across the system and typically selects its cases, hearing only those raising important or unsettled points of law.

Designs differ: some states fuse constitutional and ordinary appeals in one supreme court (the United States), others separate a constitutional court (Germany, Italy, Spain, and much of the post-1989 world on the Kelsenian model) from a supreme court for civil and criminal cassation. Historically, final courts could sit outside the state: the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London long served (and for some Caribbean and Pacific states still serves) as the final court for Commonwealth jurisdictions, and the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court and the Caribbean Court of Justice illustrate regional final courts. Above the national level, courts such as the Court of Justice of the European Union and the European Court of Human Rights decide questions within their treaty spheres.
