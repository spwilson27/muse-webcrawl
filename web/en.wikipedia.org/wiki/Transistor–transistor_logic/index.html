Transistor–transistor logic

Transistor–transistor logic (TTL) is a logic family built from bipolar junction transistors. Its name signifies that transistors perform both the logic function (the first "transistor") and the amplifying function (the second "transistor"), as opposed to earlier resistor–transistor logic and diode–transistor logic. TTL integrated circuits were widely used in computers, industrial controls, test equipment, and consumer electronics through the 1970s and 1980s, most famously in the 7400 series introduced by Texas Instruments in 1964.

Implementation

A basic TTL gate uses a multiple-emitter transistor at its input: when any input is low, base current is diverted away from the following stage, turning the output transistor off. The output stage is a "totem pole" pair that can actively pull the output both high and low, giving good fan-out (typically ten standard loads). Standard TTL operates from a tightly regulated 5 V supply and defines a low input as below 0.8 V and a high input as above 2.0 V, providing modest noise margins.

Subfamilies

Variants trade speed against power: low-power TTL (74L), high-speed TTL (74H), Schottky TTL (74S), low-power Schottky (74LS), and advanced families (74AS, 74ALS, 74F). The 74LS series became the workhorse of digital design in the late 1970s. CMOS families such as 74HC later displaced TTL in most new designs because of their far lower static power consumption, though TTL logic levels remain a reference standard.

History

TTL was invented in 1961 by James L. Buie of TRW and popularized by Texas Instruments' 7400 series. It succeeded diode–transistor logic in minicomputers and early microprocessor systems, and millions of TTL chips were used in equipment ranging from the PDP-11 to arcade games.
