War of the currents

The war of the currents was a series of events surrounding the introduction of competing electric power transmission systems in the late 1880s and early 1890s. It grew out of a commercial rivalry between Thomas Edison's direct current (DC) system, marketed by the Edison Electric Illuminating Company, and the alternating current (AC) system championed by George Westinghouse and based in part on patents of Nikola Tesla.

Contemporary commentary on the rivalry between Edison's DC and Westinghouse's AC

The dispute

Edison's DC systems operated at low voltage and could serve only customers within about a mile of a generating station, because DC voltage could not then be efficiently raised for long-distance transmission. AC, using transformers, could be transmitted at high voltage over long distances and stepped down for use, but it required higher voltages that opponents portrayed as deadly. Edison's camp conducted a public campaign against AC, including public electrocutions of animals, and lobbied for legal limits on voltage. Edison's associate Harold Brown promoted the first electric chair — powered by a Westinghouse AC generator — to associate AC with execution; it was first used in 1890 on William Kemmler.

Resolution

AC's technical and economic advantages prevailed. Westinghouse won the contract to light the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893 and then the contract to harness Niagara Falls, delivering power to Buffalo in 1896. Edison's own company, reorganised as General Electric, itself moved into AC. AC became the worldwide standard for power grids, although DC has since returned for special uses such as high-voltage direct-current transmission links and electronics.
