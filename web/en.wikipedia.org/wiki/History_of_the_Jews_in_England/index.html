History of the Jews in England

The history of the Jews in England begins with the arrival of Jewish communities following the Norman Conquest in 1066. Jews from Rouen in Normandy settled in London and other towns, working chiefly as moneylenders, a role permitted to them while Christians were barred from usury.

Medieval period

Under the early Norman and Angevin kings, Jews lived under royal protection but also faced heavy taxation and periodic violence. The coronation of Richard I in 1189 was followed by anti-Jewish riots, and in 1190 the Jewish community of York was massacred at Clifford's Tower. The Statute of the Jewry (1275) under Edward I restricted moneylending, and in 1290 Edward issued the Edict of Expulsion, banishing all Jews from England — the first general expulsion of Jews from a European kingdom. A small number of converts remained at the Domus Conversorum in London.

Readmission

For over three and a half centuries there was no officially recognised Jewish community in England, though individual conversos and merchants visited. In the 1650s, the Amsterdam rabbi Menasseh ben Israel petitioned Oliver Cromwell for readmission. Although no formal act was passed, Jews were quietly permitted to resettle from 1656, and a synagogue was established in Creechurch Lane, London, followed by the Bevis Marks Synagogue in 1701.

Emancipation and immigration

During the nineteenth century, legal disabilities were gradually removed; Lionel de Rothschild took his seat in Parliament in 1858 after the Jews Relief Act allowed a modified oath. Between 1881 and 1914, roughly 120,000–150,000 Eastern European Jews fled pogroms in the Russian Empire and settled in Britain, many in London's East End, Leeds, and Manchester. The Aliens Act 1905 was the first modern immigration control partly aimed at this influx.

Twentieth century

In the 1930s, some 70,000–80,000 Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany and Austria reached Britain, including about 10,000 children on the Kindertransport. After 1948 many British Jews supported the new state of Israel. The community, numbering around 290,000 in recent censuses, is concentrated in London, Manchester, and Leeds, and has produced prominent figures in politics, science, and the arts.
