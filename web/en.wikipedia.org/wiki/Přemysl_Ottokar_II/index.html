Přemysl Ottokar II

Přemysl Ottokar II (Czech: Přemysl Otakar II.; c. 1233 – 26 August 1278), known as "the Iron and Golden King", was King of Bohemia from 1253 until his death. A member of the Přemyslid dynasty, he built the largest territorial empire of any medieval Bohemian ruler, extending at its height from the Sudetes to the Adriatic Sea: at various times he ruled Bohemia, Moravia, Austria, Styria, Carinthia, Carniola and parts of Friuli and Slovenia, and he founded or refounded numerous towns. His ambition brought him into fatal conflict with Rudolf of Habsburg, and he was killed at the Battle on the Marchfeld in 1278, an event that cleared the way for the rise of the House of Habsburg in Austria.

Rise to power

Ottokar was the second son of King Wenceslaus I. As a young man he was installed as Margrave of Moravia and in 1247 briefly led a rebellion against his father before being reconciled. When the Babenberg duke Frederick II of Austria died childless in 1246, the Austrian lands became a prize; Ottokar marched in and was acclaimed duke by the Austrian nobility in 1251, legitimising his rule by marrying Frederick's sister Margaret, a widow some 26 years his senior. On his father's death in 1253 he became King of Bohemia. His claim to Austria was contested by Béla IV of Hungary, but Ottokar's victory at the Battle of Kressenbrunn in 1260 confirmed his possession of Austria and added Styria to his domains.

Expansion

Ottokar twice campaigned with the Teutonic Order against the pagan Prussians (1255 and 1267); the city of Königsberg ("King's Mountain"), founded in 1255, was named in his honour. In 1269 he inherited Carinthia and Carniola, and in 1272 he advanced to the Adriatic, becoming the most powerful prince in the Holy Roman Empire. At home he was an energetic founder of towns — including České Budějovice (Budweis) and Leoben — promoted German colonisation and mining (the silver of Kutná Hora enriched the Bohemian crown), and supported the building of castles and churches in the early Gothic style. His wealth from Bohemian silver and gold earned him the "golden" half of his epithet; "iron" referred to his military power.

Conflict with Rudolf and downfall

Passed over for the imperial crown in 1273, when the electors chose the comparatively obscure Count Rudolf of Habsburg as King of the Romans, Ottokar refused to recognise the election and refused to surrender the former Babenberg lands, which Rudolf claimed as reverted imperial fiefs. Placed under the imperial ban in 1275 and invaded in 1276, Ottokar was forced by the Treaty of Vienna to renounce Austria, Styria, Carinthia and Carniola, keeping only Bohemia and Moravia, and to do homage for them. Humiliated but undefeated, he rebuilt alliances and invaded Austria again in 1278. The two armies met on 26 August 1278 at the Battle on the Marchfeld near Dürnkrut, one of the largest cavalry battles of the Middle Ages. Ottokar was defeated and killed — by some accounts murdered after surrendering — and his embalmed body was displayed in Vienna before eventual burial in St Vitus Cathedral, Prague.

Legacy

Ottokar's death transferred Austria to the Habsburgs for nearly 650 years and stands as one of the turning points of Central European history. In Czech tradition he is remembered as one of the greatest Bohemian kings, a builder and warrior whose empire briefly made Bohemia a European great power; his son Wenceslaus II succeeded him as a child under regency. He figures in Dante's Purgatorio and is the subject of Franz Grillparzer's tragedy König Ottokars Glück und Ende (1825).
