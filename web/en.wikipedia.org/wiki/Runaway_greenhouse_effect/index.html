Runaway greenhouse effect
FieldPlanetary science, climatology
Key exampleVenus

A runaway greenhouse effect occurs when a planet's atmosphere contains greenhouse gases in an amount sufficient to block thermal radiation from leaving the planet, preventing the planet from cooling and having liquid water on its surface. In such a state, warming feeds on itself: as the surface heats, oceans evaporate and add water vapour — itself a greenhouse gas — to the atmosphere, trapping still more heat until the oceans are lost entirely.

Mechanism

The process is governed by the balance between absorbed sunlight and outgoing longwave radiation. A pure steam atmosphere has a ceiling on how much infrared radiation it can emit to space (the Komabayashi–Ingersoll limit). If absorbed solar radiation exceeds this limit, the surface cannot reach equilibrium and warms without bound; water reaches the upper atmosphere, where ultraviolet light splits it (photodissociation) and the hydrogen escapes to space, permanently drying the planet.

Venus

Venus is believed to have undergone a runaway greenhouse early in its history. Although it receives about twice the sunlight of Earth, its surface today is 737 K (464 °C), hot enough to melt lead, under a dense carbon-dioxide atmosphere with a pressure of about 92 bar, and its water is essentially gone — the heavy-to-light hydrogen isotope ratio in its atmosphere is strong evidence of massive past water loss.

Earth's future

As the Sun gradually brightens over the next billion years or so, Earth is expected eventually to cross the threshold into a "moist greenhouse" and ultimately a runaway greenhouse, evaporating the oceans. Climate models indicate that human-caused greenhouse-gas emissions, while dangerous to civilisation and ecosystems, are far too small to trigger a true Venus-style runaway on Earth; the term is sometimes used loosely in popular writing for strong self-reinforcing warming feedbacks, which scientists distinguish from the strict planetary-science meaning.
