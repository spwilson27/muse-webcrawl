A kilowatt-hour (symbolised kW·h or kWh) is a unit of energy equal to the work done or energy transferred when a power of one kilowatt (1,000 watts) is sustained for one hour. Since energy in the SI is measured in joules and one watt is one joule per second, one kilowatt-hour is exactly 3.6 megajoules (1,000 W × 3,600 s = 3,600,000 J). It is the standard unit in which electrical energy is sold to consumers and displayed on utility bills worldwide.

Usage

Households are billed for electricity in kilowatt-hours (often simply called "units" in Britain, India and elsewhere). A typical modern home in developed countries consumes on the order of a few thousand kWh per year. Everyday examples: a 100 W light bulb left on for ten hours uses 1 kWh; a 2 kW kettle boiled for half an hour uses 1 kWh; electric vehicle battery capacities are quoted in kWh (commonly 40–100 kWh). Larger multiples used by the energy industry include the megawatt-hour (MWh), gigawatt-hour (GWh) and terawatt-hour (TWh) for national generation statistics.

Common confusions

The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not of power: it must not be confused with the kilowatt (kW), the rate of energy transfer. News reports occasionally write "kilowatts per hour", which is incorrect in this context. Because the hour is not an SI unit, the kilowatt-hour is technically a non-SI unit, but it is accepted for use with the SI and is entrenched by a century of electrical engineering practice dating to the commercialisation of electricity supply in the 1880s–1890s.

Conversions

1 kWh equals 3.6 MJ, about 3,412 British thermal units (Btu), about 859.8 kilocalories, or 0.034 therms of natural gas equivalent. The cost per kWh varies widely by country and tariff, from a few US cents in energy-rich regions to over 40 cents in parts of Europe, a figure that prompts public interest in household consumption measured in this unit.
