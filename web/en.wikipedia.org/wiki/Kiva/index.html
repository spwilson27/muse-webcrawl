Kiva

A kiva is a space used by Pueblo peoples for rites and political meetings, many of them associated with the kachina belief system. Among the modern Hopi and most other Pueblo peoples, a kiva is a large round room (chambers of other shapes are also known) with a roof; the form, usually round and above ground, contrasts with earlier square and fully subterranean examples. Kivas are central to Pueblo ceremonial life and community organization.


A reconstructed great kiva at Aztec Ruins, New Mexico

Origins and development

Kivas developed from the pit houses of the Ancestral Puebloans. The simplified form, often called a "round room", evolved from about AD 1000 in Chaco Canyon and spread throughout the Pueblo world. Features characteristic of kivas include a sipapu (a small hole in the floor symbolizing the place of emergence from the underworld), a fire pit, a ventilator shaft, and roof entry by ladder through a hatch — the ladder poles protruding from a kiva roof remain an iconic image of the Southwest. The largest examples, "great kivas", could be 20 metres or more in diameter; the great kiva at Casa Rinconada in Chaco Canyon is a famous example.

Use

Traditionally, kivas served as men's houses and ceremonial chambers, where societies prepared for dances and rituals, wove textiles, and conducted community business. At Spanish contact beginning in the 1540s, missionaries suppressed kiva ceremonies, and kiva use briefly diminished; after the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, however, kivas returned to prominence. Modern kivas remain in active use among the Hopi, Zuni, and Rio Grande pueblos, where access is restricted during ceremonies and photography is prohibited.
