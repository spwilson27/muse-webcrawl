Succession to the Crown Act 2013

The Succession to the Crown Act 2013 (c. 20) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that amended the laws of succession to the British throne in accordance with the Perth Agreement of 2011. The Act replaced male-preference primogeniture with absolute primogeniture for those born after 28 October 2011, removed the disqualification arising from marriage to a Roman Catholic, and repealed the Royal Marriages Act 1772, replacing its consent requirements with a narrower provision affecting only the first six persons in line to the throne.

The Act modernised the rules of succession agreed by the Commonwealth realms at Perth in 2011.

Background

At the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in Perth, Western Australia, in October 2011, the sixteen Commonwealth realms that shared Elizabeth II as head of state agreed to change the rules of succession. Because the Statute of Westminster 1931 required the realms to legislate in concert, each realm passed its own legislation; the United Kingdom's Act came into force on 26 March 2015, once all realms' laws were in place.

Provisions

The Act provides that succession is not determined by gender for persons born after 28 October 2011, so that an elder daughter inherits ahead of a younger son. It removed the bar on a person in the line of succession marrying a Roman Catholic, though the monarch must still not themselves be a Roman Catholic. It repealed the Royal Marriages Act 1772 and instead requires only the six persons next in line to obtain the sovereign's consent to marry, failing which they (but not their descendants) are disqualified. It also removed the disqualification based on descent from George II for persons whose marriages had been void under the old Act but treated as never having been required to seek consent.

Effects

The changes affected the order of succession: for example, Princess Charlotte, born in 2015, retained her place ahead of her younger brother Prince Louis, born in 2018 — the first time a princess was not displaced by a younger brother. The removal of the marriage disqualification restored persons such as George Windsor, Earl of St Andrews, to the line of succession.
