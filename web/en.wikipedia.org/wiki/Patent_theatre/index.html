Patent theatre

The patent theatres were the theatres in London that held a royal patent granting them the exclusive right to perform spoken drama. The system originated with the Restoration of Charles II in 1660 and survived, in modified form, until the Theatres Act 1843 abolished the monopoly. For nearly two centuries the patent theatres dominated the legitimate English stage and shaped the repertory, architecture, and economics of British theatre.

The Theatre Royal, Drury Lane — one of the two original patent houses.

Origins

When Charles II was restored to the throne in 1660, he granted letters patent to two courtiers, Thomas Killigrew and Sir William Davenant, authorizing each to form a theatre company. Killigrew's King's Company performed at the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, while Davenant's Duke's Company performed at Lincoln's Inn Fields and later Dorset Garden. These two companies — and their successor houses — held a legal duopoly over serious spoken drama in London.

The Licensing Act and expansion

The Licensing Act 1737, provoked by the satirical plays of Henry Fielding, tightened government control by requiring the Lord Chamberlain's approval of new plays and reinforcing the patent monopoly. Theatres without patents — the so-called "minor" houses — were restricted to burlettas, pantomime, melodrama, and musical entertainments, restrictions they often evaded by creative staging. A third patent was granted in 1766 to Samuel Foote's Haymarket Theatre for summer performances. Outside London, provincial theatres royal, such as those at Bath and Norwich, received patents by Act of Parliament from 1768 onward.

Abolition

By the 19th century the monopoly was widely seen as an anachronism that stifled new writing. The Theatres Act 1843 ended the patent theatres' exclusive right to perform drama, opening all theatres to the full repertory, though the Lord Chamberlain's censorship of plays survived until 1968.

Legacy

The patent houses — above all Drury Lane and Covent Garden — became enormous venues seating over 3,000 spectators, encouraging spectacle and star performances over intimate drama. The distinction between "legitimate" and "illegitimate" theatre, created by the patent system, left a lasting mark on English theatrical genres.
