The Alaouite dynasty (also spelled Alawite or Alaoui; Arabic: العلويون, al-ʿAlawiyyūn) is the current ruling royal family of Morocco. Claiming descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his grandson Hasan ibn Ali, the Alaouites rose to power in the oasis region of Tafilalt in south-eastern Morocco in the 17th century and, under Moulay Rashid and his brother Moulay Ismail, reunified the country after the collapse of the Saadian sultanate. They have ruled Morocco continuously ever since, surviving the French and Spanish protectorates (1912–1956), making them one of the longest-reigning dynasties in the Islamic world.

Rise to power

The family, sharifs settled at Sijilmasa in the Tafilalt, gained local authority from 1631 amid Saadian decline. Moulay Rashid defeated rival powers, took Fes in 1666 and Marrakesh in 1668, and is regarded as the dynasty's effective founder as ruler of all Morocco. His brother and successor Moulay Ismail (r. 1672–1727) built the imperial capital at Meknes, created the Black Guard army of sub-Saharan soldiers, campaigned against the Ottoman regency of Algiers and the European enclaves, and exchanged embassies with Louis XIV's France.

18th and 19th centuries

Ismail's death was followed by decades of succession struggles among his sons, until Mohammed III (r. 1757–1790) restored order, founded Essaouira and developed trade with Europe and the young United States — Morocco was among the first states to recognise American independence. In the 19th century the sultans contended with growing European pressure: defeat by France at Isly (1844), war with Spain (1859–1860), and mounting debt and "protection" abuses that culminated in the Algeciras Conference (1906) and the Treaty of Fes (1912), which made Morocco a French protectorate (with a Spanish zone in the north).

Protectorate and modern monarchy

Under the protectorate the sultans were retained as figureheads. Sultan Mohammed V's alignment with the nationalist movement led France to depose and exile him in 1953, an act that galvanised resistance; he returned triumphantly in 1955 and Morocco regained independence in 1956. Mohammed V took the title of King in 1957. His son Hassan II (r. 1961–1999) ruled through decades of political turbulence, and his grandson Mohammed VI has reigned since 1999, presiding over constitutional reforms in 2011 that modestly expanded the elected government's powers while the king retains the religious title Commander of the Faithful and decisive executive authority.
