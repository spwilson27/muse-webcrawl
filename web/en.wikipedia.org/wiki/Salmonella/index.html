Salmonella

Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped (bacillus), Gram-negative bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. It is a major cause of foodborne illness worldwide, causing salmonellosis (gastroenteritis) and, in the case of Salmonella Typhi, typhoid fever.

Taxonomy

The genus is named after American veterinary scientist Daniel Elmer Salmon, whose associate Theobald Smith isolated the organism in 1885. Only two species are recognised, S. enterica and S. bongori, but there are more than 2,600 serovars, including Typhi, Typhimurium and Enteritidis, distinguished by surface antigens under the Kauffmann–White scheme.

Disease

Non-typhoidal salmonellosis follows ingestion of contaminated poultry, eggs, produce or water, with diarrhoea, fever and cramps 6 hours to 6 days later, usually resolving in a week; severe cases occur in infants, the elderly and the immunocompromised. Typhoidal serovars invade the bloodstream and cause enteric fever. Salmonella can survive in dry foods and in carrier animals and humans.

Prevention

Control relies on cooking food thoroughly, avoiding cross-contamination, refrigeration, pasteurisation, farm hygiene and vaccines against typhoid fever. Antimicrobial-resistant strains are a growing concern.
