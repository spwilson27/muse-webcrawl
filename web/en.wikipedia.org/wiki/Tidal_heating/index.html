Tidal heating

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tidal heating (also known as tidal working or tidal flexing) occurs through the tidal friction processes: orbital and rotational energy is dissipated as heat in the crust of a planet or moon. When an object is in an elliptical orbit, the tidal forces acting on it are stronger near periapsis than near apoapsis. The resulting varying deformation of the body over the course of its orbit causes internal friction, which heats its interior. Because the energy is drawn from the orbit, tidal heating also gradually circularizes the orbit unless other interactions intervene.

Role in planetary science

Tidal heating is responsible for the extraordinary volcanic activity of Jupiter's moon Io, the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. Io's orbit is kept eccentric by an orbital resonance with Europa and Ganymede, so the tidal flexing never ceases; the heat produced is estimated at around 100 terawatts, far exceeding the heat from radioactive decay.

The same process is believed to maintain subsurface liquid-water oceans beneath the icy crusts of Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus. At Enceladus, tidal heating is concentrated near the south pole, where geysers of water vapor and ice particles erupt from fractures known as "tiger stripes." Tidal heating also shapes the geology of many other icy satellites and is an important consideration in the habitability of exoplanets and exomoons, since it can supply heat far from a star, including in the "habitable zone" calculations for moons of giant planets.

Theory

The rate of tidal heating depends strongly on the size of the body and the eccentricity of its orbit, and inversely on a high power of the distance to the primary. It is commonly expressed in terms of the tidal Love number k2 and the tidal quality factor Q, which characterize how readily the body deforms and how much of the tidal energy is dissipated per cycle. For a synchronously rotating body in an eccentric orbit, the heating rate scales approximately with the square of the eccentricity and the seventh power of the body's radius divided by the sixth power of the semi-major axis, making tidal effects extremely sensitive to orbital distance.
