Offshore drilling

Offshore drilling is a mechanical process where a wellbore is drilled below the seabed. It is typically carried out in order to explore for, and subsequently extract, petroleum and natural gas that lie in rock formations beneath the seabed. Most commonly, the term is used to describe drilling activities on the continental shelf, though the term can also be applied to drilling in lakes, inshore waters and inland seas.

History

Some of the first offshore wells were drilled from piers extending over the waters of Summerland, California, in the late 1890s. The first offshore well drilled out of sight of land was completed in 1947 off the coast of Louisiana in the Gulf of Mexico by Kerr-McGee. From there the industry expanded rapidly, into the North Sea in the 1960s and later into deepwater provinces off Brazil, West Africa and in the Gulf of Mexico.

Rigs and platforms

Offshore drilling is performed from a variety of structures depending on water depth. In shallow water, jackup rigs rest legs on the seabed; in deeper water, semi-submersible platforms and drillships maintain position by anchors or dynamic positioning. Fixed platforms and compliant towers are used for long-term production, as are floating production systems such as FPSOs (floating production, storage and offloading vessels), tension-leg platforms and spar platforms. Modern drillships can operate in water depths exceeding 3,000 metres.

Environmental concerns and disasters

Offshore drilling carries risks of oil spills and blowouts. Major incidents include the Ixtoc I spill in the Gulf of Mexico in 1979 and the Deepwater Horizon oil spill of 2010, in which the semi-submersible rig exploded and sank, killing 11 workers and releasing millions of barrels of oil. Routine discharges, seismic surveys, and the climate impact of the hydrocarbons produced are also subjects of controversy, and offshore drilling policy is politically contested in many coastal nations.
