Speech perception

Speech perception is the process by which the sounds of language are heard, interpreted, and understood. The study of speech perception is closely linked to the fields of phonology, phonetics, and psycholinguistics. A central puzzle of the field is the lack of invariance: there is no simple one-to-one mapping between the acoustic signal and the phonemes listeners perceive, because the same phoneme sounds different depending on its neighbours (coarticulation), the speaker, and the rate of speech — yet listeners perceive speech effortlessly and robustly.

Categorical perception

One of the most influential findings is categorical perception: along a synthetic acoustic continuum, such as voice onset time varying from /ba/ to /pa/, listeners hear an abrupt category boundary rather than a gradual change, and discriminate sounds across the boundary far better than equally spaced sounds within a category. Categorical perception is strongest for stop consonants; vowels are perceived more continuously. Infants only a few months old already show categorical perception, and infants attune their perception to the phoneme categories of their native language during the first year of life.

Theories

  • The motor theory of speech perception (Liberman and colleagues) proposes that listeners perceive speech by reference to the articulatory gestures that produce it, not the raw sounds.
  • Direct realist theory similarly holds that listeners directly perceive articulatory gestures as the objects of perception.
  • General auditory approaches argue that speech is processed by ordinary auditory mechanisms, with no speech-specific module.
  • Modern Bayesian and predictive models treat perception as probabilistic inference combining acoustic evidence with lexical and contextual expectations.

Phenomena and findings

The McGurk effect demonstrates that vision influences speech perception: seeing lips articulate one syllable while hearing another can produce the perception of a third syllable. The phonemic restoration effect shows that listeners "hear" phonemes replaced by noise as if they were present, guided by context. Listeners also normalise rapidly for different talkers and exploit lexical knowledge, as shown by the Ganong effect, in which ambiguous sounds are categorised toward the interpretation that makes a real word. The neural processing of speech involves the superior temporal gyrus and a dual-stream architecture: a ventral stream for comprehension and a dorsal stream linking sound to articulation.
