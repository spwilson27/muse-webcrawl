The Rinzai school (Japanese: 臨済宗, Rinzai-shū) is one of the three main schools of Zen Buddhism in Japan, alongside Sōtō and Ōbaku. It is the Japanese transmission of the Chinese Linji school of Chan, named for the Tang-dynasty master Linji Yixuan, famous for his iconoclastic teaching methods — shouts, blows and paradoxical encounters meant to jolt students into direct realization.

History

Zen of the Linji line was first brought to Japan by Myōan Eisai in 1191, and was firmly established during the Kamakura period by Chinese émigré masters fleeing the Mongol conquest. Patronized by the shogunate and the warrior class, Rinzai temples of Kyoto's Five Mountains (Gozan) system became centres of Chinese learning, ink painting, calligraphy, tea culture and garden design, profoundly shaping Japanese aesthetics. The reformer Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769) revitalized Rinzai in the Edo period, systematizing kōan training; nearly all modern Rinzai lineages descend from him.

Practice

Rinzai emphasizes kenshō — seeing into one's true nature — typically pursued through intensive meditation on kōans (paradoxical questions or cases such as "What is the sound of one hand?") under a teacher's guidance in private interviews (dokusan), combined with strict zazen and periods of intensive retreat (sesshin). It contrasts with the Sōtō school's emphasis on "just sitting" (shikantaza) and gradual cultivation. In the modern era, teachers such as D. T. Suzuki introduced Rinzai Zen to the West.
