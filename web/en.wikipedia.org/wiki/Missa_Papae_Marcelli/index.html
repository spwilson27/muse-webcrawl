Missa Papae Marcelli, or Pope Marcellus Mass, is a mass by Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina. It is his best-known mass, and is frequently taught in music history courses. It is generally attributed to the pontificate of Pope Marcellus II, reigning for three weeks in 1555, either having been composed for that Pope, or in his honour.

The legend

According to a famous story first printed in 1607 by Agostino Agazzari and popularised by Palestrina's biographer Baini (1828), the mass was written to persuade the Council of Trent that polyphonic music need not obscure the words of the liturgy, after the cardinals — in some versions a commission under cardinals Carlo Borromeo and Vitellozzi in 1564–65 — considered banning polyphony from the Church altogether. Palestrina's mass, the story runs, "saved" church music. Modern scholarship treats the tale as largely legendary: the Council never in fact proposed a ban, its canon on music (1562) merely required that the words be intelligible and the style free of the "lascivious", and the mass's clear declamation may instead reflect the influence of earlier reform-minded settings by composers such as Jacobus de Kerle.

Music

The mass is written for six voices except for the final Agnus Dei, which is for seven. Unlike most Renaissance masses it is mostly freely composed, without a cantus firmus or extensive parody of a model. Its style is noted for smooth, predominantly stepwise voice-leading, careful handling of dissonance, and — crucially for its legend — syllabic, often chordal declamation of the text in the Gloria and Credo, while the Kyrie, Sanctus and Agnus Dei unfold in flowing imitative polyphony.
