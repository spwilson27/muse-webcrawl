H I region

An H I region is a cloud in the interstellar medium composed of neutral atomic hydrogen (H I), in addition to the local abundance of helium and other elements. (H is the chemical symbol for hydrogen, and "I" is the Roman numeral.) It is the radiation signature of un-ionised hydrogen atoms. These regions are non-luminous, save for emission of the 21 cm (1,420 MHz) region spectral line. This line has a very low transition probability, so it requires large volumes of hydrogen gas for collisions to excite it; nevertheless the line's transparency lets astronomers map the distribution of neutral hydrogen throughout the Milky Way and other galaxies.

Characteristics

H I regions have temperatures of roughly 60–100 K in their cold phase and up to ~8,000–10,000 K in the warm neutral medium, with typical densities from about 0.1 to a few tens of atoms per cubic centimetre. Because H I emits no visible light, the clouds are seen as dark silhouettes only when they lie in front of brighter emission; their structure is instead surveyed at radio wavelengths. Giant H I complexes can span hundreds of light-years and contain thousands of solar masses of gas.

The 21 cm line

The 21 cm line arises from the hyperfine transition of hydrogen — a spin flip between the parallel and antiparallel states of the electron and proton. Predicted theoretically by Hendrik C. van de Hulst in 1944, it was first detected in 1951 by Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell at Harvard, with near-simultaneous confirmations in the Netherlands and Australia. Because the line is Doppler-shifted by motion, observations of 21 cm emission have been used to trace the rotation curves of galaxies — key evidence for dark matter — and to chart the spiral structure of the Milky Way, including surveys such as HI4PI. Large red-shifted surveys of the 21 cm line are also a central tool of cosmology, probing the epoch of reionisation.

Relation to H II regions

H I regions contrast with H II regions, where hydrogen is ionised by ultraviolet light from hot young stars and glows visibly. In star-forming complexes, molecular clouds (dominated by H2) are surrounded by shells of atomic H I, which in turn border the ionised H II gas — a layered structure tracing the action of stellar radiation on the interstellar medium.
