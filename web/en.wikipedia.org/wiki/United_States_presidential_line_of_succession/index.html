United States presidential line of succession

The United States presidential line of succession is the order in which officials of the United States federal government discharge the powers and duties of the office of President of the United States if the incumbent president becomes incapacitated, dies, resigns, or is removed from office (by impeachment by the House of Representatives and subsequent conviction by the Senate) during their term of office.

The succession is set by the Presidential Succession Act of 1947, codified at 3 U.S.C. § 19, and is also governed by Article II, Section 1, Clause 6 of the Constitution and the Twentieth and Twenty-fifth Amendments.

History

The first succession law, the Presidential Succession Act of 1792, placed the president pro tempore of the Senate and then the Speaker of the House after the vice president. The Act of 1886 moved to cabinet officers only, ordered by the date their departments were created. The 1947 Act, championed by President Harry S. Truman, restored the congressional leaders — but placed the elected Speaker ahead of the president pro tempore — before the cabinet, which follows in the order of departmental creation. Officers who are not constitutionally eligible to be president (for example, a naturalized citizen serving as a cabinet secretary) are skipped.

Nine vice presidents have succeeded to the presidency upon the death or resignation of the president, most recently Gerald Ford in 1974. No official further down the line has ever been required to serve as acting president.
