A brass band is a musical ensemble generally consisting entirely of brass instruments plus a percussion section. The standardised British-style brass band — about 28 players using cornets rather than trumpets, tenor horns, baritones, euphoniums and tubas, all (except trombones) reading treble clef — developed in the industrial communities of northern England in the 19th century and remains a vigorous amateur music tradition spread across the United Kingdom, the Commonwealth and beyond.

History

Brass bands flourished from the 1830s as instrument makers mass-produced valved brass and employers — collieries, mills and factories — sponsored works bands. Contesting began in earnest with the Belle Vue contest of 1853 and the national contest at the Crystal Palace in 1860, creating a competitive culture of graded sections and set test pieces. Famous works bands such as Black Dyke (1855), Foden's and Grimethorpe Colliery became institutions of their communities.

Instrumentation

The British band seats cornets in E♭ and B♭, a flugelhorn, tenor horns, baritones, euphoniums, tenor and bass trombones, and E♭ and B♭ tubas ("basses"), plus two to four percussionists. Conical-bore instruments give the ensemble its mellow blend. Conductors traditionally stand for contest and concert performance alike.

Culture

Contests — culminating in the British Open and the National Brass Band Championships of Great Britain, and the street marches of Whit Friday in Saddleworth and Tameside — remain the movement's lifeblood, and a sizeable repertoire of original test pieces and arrangements sustains it. The tradition also produced the distinct New Orleans brass band of parades and jazz funerals, and Salvation Army bands worldwide.
