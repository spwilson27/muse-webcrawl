Absolute music

Absolute music
PeriodConcept developed in the 19th century
OppositeProgram music
Key advocatesEduard Hanslick, Johannes Brahms

Absolute music (sometimes abstract music) is music that is not explicitly "about" anything; in contrast to program music, it is non-representational. The notion of absolute music developed at the end of the 18th century in the writings of German authors on early Romanticism, such as Wilhelm Heinrich Wackenroder, Ludwig Tieck and E. T. A. Hoffmann, but the term was not coined until 1846, when Richard Wagner used it in a programme for Beethoven's Ninth Symphony — ironically, as a term of criticism.

History

The idea that purely instrumental music could be the highest form of the art gained ground in the early 19th century, reversing the older view that vocal music was supreme. Eduard Hanslick, in his treatise On the Musically Beautiful (1854), argued that music's content consists solely of "tonally moving forms" and that it cannot represent definite emotions or ideas. Hanslick became a champion of Brahms in the so-called "War of the Romantics" against Wagner and Liszt, advocates of programmatic and symphonic-poem forms.

Debate

Critics of the concept, including Wagner, held that Beethoven's Ninth Symphony — with its choral finale — demonstrated the limits of purely instrumental music. Modern scholars such as Carl Dahlhaus have examined how the idea of absolute music shaped concert culture and the canon of "serious" music, while others note that even supposedly absolute works often carry titles, associations and expressive conventions.
