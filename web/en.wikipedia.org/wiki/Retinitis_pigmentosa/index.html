Retinitis pigmentosa

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited disorders in which the light-sensitive cells of the retina — first the rods, then the cones — progressively degenerate, leading to loss of peripheral vision, night blindness and, in many cases, eventual severe visual impairment. It affects roughly 1 in 4,000 people worldwide and is one of the commonest causes of inherited blindness. The name derives from the characteristic pigment deposits ("bone spicule" pigmentation) visible in the retina on examination.

Genetics and course

RP is genetically highly heterogeneous: mutations in more than 100 genes can cause it, following autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive or X-linked patterns. It may occur alone or as part of a syndrome, as in Usher syndrome (with congenital hearing loss). Symptoms usually begin with difficulty seeing at night in adolescence, followed by constriction of the visual field ("tunnel vision") as rods die; central cone vision is often preserved into middle age. Diagnosis combines visual field testing, electroretinography, retinal imaging and genetic testing.

Treatment

For most forms there is no cure, and management centres on low-vision aids, mobility training and monitoring for treatable complications such as cataract and macular oedema. A landmark exception is biallelic RPE65 disease, for which the gene therapy voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna), approved from 2017, delivers a working copy of the gene to the retina. Retinal prostheses and stem-cell and gene-editing therapies remain under investigation.
