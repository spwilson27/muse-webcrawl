Palladium is a chemical element with the symbol Pd and atomic number 46. It is a rare, lustrous silvery-white metal discovered in 1802 by the English chemist William Hyde Wollaston. It is named after the asteroid Pallas, which was itself named after the Greek goddess Athena's epithet. Palladium, platinum, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium, and osmium form the platinum group metals, which have similar chemical properties but palladium has the lowest melting point and is the least dense of them.

Characteristics

Palladium is a soft, ductile metal that does not tarnish in air at standard temperatures. It has the unusual property of absorbing up to 900 times its own volume of hydrogen at room temperature, a feature exploited in hydrogen purification and storage research. Its electron configuration, [Kr] 4d¹⁰, is unusual in having a completely filled d subshell with no electrons in the outer s orbital.

Applications

The largest use of palladium today is in catalytic converters for automobiles, which convert harmful exhaust pollutants into less toxic substances. The metal is also used in electronics (multilayer ceramic capacitors), dentistry, hydrogen purification, groundwater treatment, jewellery — where it is alloyed to produce white gold — and as a catalyst in cross-coupling reactions such as the Suzuki, Heck, and Negishi reactions, whose development was recognized by the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Occurrence and production

Palladium is obtained commercially as a by-product of nickel and copper refining and from platinum deposits. Major producers include Russia (Norilsk), South Africa (Bushveld Complex), Canada, and the United States (Stillwater, Montana). Palladium prices have at times exceeded those of gold, driven by demand from the automotive sector and constrained supply.

History

Wollaston isolated palladium from South American platinum ore in 1802 and announced it anonymously in 1803, offering samples for sale through a London shop — a marketing stunt that initially drew accusations of fraud before he revealed himself as the discoverer in 1805.
