Eudoxus of Cnidus

Eudoxus modelled planetary motions with nested homocentric spheres.

Eudoxus of Cnidus (c. 408 – c. 355 BC) was a Greek astronomer, mathematician, scholar and student of Plato. None of his writings survive complete, but his contributions—reported by later authors such as Archimedes, Aristotle and Diogenes Laërtius—were foundational for Greek geometry and astronomy.

Mathematics

Eudoxus developed the theory of proportion preserved in Book V of Euclid's Elements, which handled incommensurable magnitudes rigorously and effectively defined equality of ratios for any magnitudes, anticipating the modern real numbers. He also invented the method of exhaustion, a precursor of integral calculus used to compute areas and volumes—famously proving that the volume of a cone is one-third that of the enclosing cylinder. Archimedes credited Eudoxus with these results.

Astronomy

To explain retrograde motion, Eudoxus proposed the first geometrical model of the heavens: each planet, the Sun and the Moon were carried on sets of nested spheres rotating about different axes, all centred on Earth—twenty-seven spheres in total. Callippus and Aristotle expanded the system. Eudoxus also produced a star catalogue and calendar (the Phaenomena, versified by Aratus) and a geographical work, Tour of the Earth.

Life

Born at Cnidus in southwestern Anatolia, Eudoxus studied at Tarentum with the Pythagorean Archytas, visited Egypt, and attended Plato's Academy at Athens. He later taught at Cyzicus and Athens before returning to Cnidus, where he died around 355 BC. The lunar crater Eudoxus is named for him.
