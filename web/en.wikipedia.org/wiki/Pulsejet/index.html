Pulsejet


The Argus As 109-014 pulsejet that powered the German V-1 flying bomb.

A pulsejet engine (or pulse jet) is a type of jet engine in which combustion occurs in pulses. A pulsejet engine can be made with few or no moving parts, and is capable of running statically (that is, it does not need to have air forced into its intake, typically by forward motion). The best known example is the Argus As 109-014 engine used on the German V-1 flying bomb of the Second World War.

Design

The classic valved pulsejet consists of a long tube with a set of spring-loaded one-way valves (a reed or shutter valve grid) at the intake. Fuel is injected and ignited; the resulting pressure rise closes the valves and accelerates the exhaust out of the tailpipe, producing thrust. As the exhaust gases leave, the pressure in the tube drops below atmospheric, the valves open, fresh air and fuel are drawn in, and the cycle repeats—typically tens of times per second, producing the characteristic buzzing sound. The valveless pulsejet uses carefully shaped intake and exhaust geometry instead of mechanical valves.

History

Early designs were patented in the early 20th century, with contributions from inventors in Russia, France, and Germany. Paul Schmidt's work in Germany led to the Argus engine for the V-1, which flew at about 640 km/h (400 mph). After the war, pulsejets were used on target drones and helicopters (as tip jets) and became popular with hobbyists because of their mechanical simplicity.

Characteristics

Pulsejets are extremely simple and cheap to construct, but they are noisy, vibrate heavily, have poor fuel efficiency, and the intake valves wear out quickly. They work best at low speeds and are unsuitable for most crewed aircraft. Modern research has explored pulse detonation engines as a related concept with potentially higher efficiency.
