Transgender

Transgender (often abbreviated as trans) is an umbrella term for people whose gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth. Transgender people may identify as men or women whose identity does not match their assigned sex (trans men and trans women), or as non-binary, genderqueer or otherwise outside the male–female binary. The opposite of transgender is cisgender, describing people whose gender identity matches their assigned sex.

Terminology and identity

The term gained wide currency in the 1990s through the activism of writers such as Leslie Feinberg and has since been adopted by medical bodies, human-rights organisations and the United Nations. Some transgender people pursue social transition (changes of name, pronouns and presentation), legal transition (amended identity documents) or medical transition (hormone therapy or surgeries); others do not. Not all gender-nonconforming people identify as transgender, and being transgender is distinct from sexual orientation — trans people may be heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, asexual or use other labels.

History and society

Gender-variant people are documented in many cultures and historical periods, from the hijra of South Asia to Two-Spirit roles in some Indigenous North American cultures and the Institut für Sexualwissenschaft's early 20th-century research in Berlin, destroyed by the Nazis in 1933. Modern milestones include Christine Jorgensen's widely reported transition in 1952 and the participation of trans people in the 1969 Stonewall riots. Surveys suggest trans people form a small but growing share of younger populations, and their civil rights — covering healthcare access, legal recognition and protection from discrimination — remain contested in many countries.
