Afterimage

An afterimage is an image that continues to appear in the eyes after exposure to the original image has ceased. Afterimages occur because the photoreceptor cells of the retina adapt to a stimulus: when the stimulus is removed, the temporarily fatigued receptors respond less strongly than their neighbours, and the visual system interprets the imbalance as an image — typically in complementary colours.

Types

A positive afterimage retains the original colours and brightness and lasts only a fraction of a second; it underlies the persistence of vision that makes cinema and animation appear continuous. A negative afterimage appears after longer fixation (around 20–30 seconds) on a bright or coloured stimulus: looking away to a neutral surface, one sees a ghostly image in opponent colours — red yields cyan, green yields magenta, blue yields yellow. This supports the opponent-process theory of colour vision.

Mechanism

Sustained stimulation bleaches photopigments in the cones and reduces their sensitivity; ganglion cells comparing signals across opponent channels then signal the "opposite" colour until the receptors recover. Abnormally persistent or intrusive afterimages (palinopsia) can be a symptom of migraine, medication effects or neurological disease and differ from the ordinary physiological phenomenon.

Demonstrations

Classic demonstrations include flag illusions (staring at an inverted-colour flag, then seeing the correct colours on a white wall) and lilac-chaser displays. Afterimages have been studied since antiquity and were described by Aristotle and, in detail, by Goethe and Ewald Hering.
