Mousse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview

A mousse (French for "foam" or "froth") is a soft prepared food that incorporates air bubbles to give it a light and airy texture. In cooking, the aeration is usually achieved by folding in whipped cream or beaten egg whites. Mousses may be sweet or savoury, served hot or chilled, and range from light and fluffy to rich and dense.

Sweet mousses

Chocolate mousse is the best-known version, traditionally made by folding whipped cream and/or a meringue into melted chocolate, sometimes set with a little gelatine and flavoured with coffee, liqueur or vanilla. Fruit mousses are made from puréed fruit with cream and gelatine. Recipes for chocolate mousse appear in French cookery from the late 19th century, and the dessert became widely popular in the 20th century; the painter Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec is fondly (if dubiously) credited with a mayonnaise-like chocolate concoction he called "chocolate mayonnaise".

Savoury mousses

Savoury mousses are made from puréed meat, fish, shellfish, foie gras or vegetables, lightened with cream and set with gelatine. Salmon mousse and ham mousse were staples of mid-century dinner parties, often piped or moulded. Related preparations include the forcemeat-based mousseline, in which cream is worked into a very fine purée.

Other uses of the word

The French word is also applied to foamed cosmetics and hair products: hair mousse is a styling foam dispensed from an aerosol can, popularised in the 1980s, that adds volume and hold. In brewing and wine, mousse describes the foam on a glass of beer or the froth of a sparkling wine.
