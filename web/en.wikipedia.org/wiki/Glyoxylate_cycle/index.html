Glyoxylate cycle

The glyoxylate cycle, a variation of the tricarboxylic acid cycle, is an anabolic pathway occurring in plants, bacteria, protists, and fungi. The glyoxylate cycle centers on the conversion of acetyl-CoA to succinate for the synthesis of carbohydrates. In microorganisms, the glyoxylate cycle allows cells to use two carbons (C2 compounds), such as acetate, to satisfy cellular carbon requirements when simple sugars such as glucose or fructose are not available. The cycle is generally not present in animals, with the exception of nematodes at the early stages of embryogenesis.

In recent years, the detection of the enzymes unique to the glyoxylate cycle, malate synthase and isocitrate lyase, in some animal tissue has raised questions regarding the evolutionary relationship of enzymes in bacteria and animals and suggests that animals encode alternative enzymes of the cycle that differ in function from known malate synthase and isocitrate lyase in non-animal species.

Similarities with the citric acid cycle

The glyoxylate cycle uses three of the five enzymes associated with the citric acid cycle: citrate synthase, aconitase, and malate dehydrogenase. The two cycles differ in that in the glyoxylate cycle, isocitrate is converted by isocitrate lyase into succinate and glyoxylate, instead of into alpha-ketoglutarate. This bypasses the decarboxylation steps that take place in the citric acid cycle, allowing simple carbon compounds to be used in the later synthesis of macromolecules, including glucose. Glyoxylate is subsequently combined with acetyl-CoA to produce malate by malate synthase. Malate is also produced in parallel from succinate by the action of succinate dehydrogenase in the cycle and fumarase.

Role in gluconeogenesis

Lipids are an important energy source stored as triglycerides in many tissues. During the beta-oxidation of fatty acids, acetyl-CoA is generated. In seeds, these stored fats are converted into carbohydrates via the glyoxylate cycle during seed germination, allowing the growing seedling to produce its own food until its leaves are mature enough to photosynthesize. Organelles involved in this conversion include glyoxysomes, which contain the key enzymes of the cycle in plants.

Function in organisms

The glyoxylate cycle allows bacteria such as Escherichia coli to grow on acetate as the sole carbon source. It is also important in pathogenic fungi such as Candida albicans and in the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, making the unique enzymes of the cycle attractive drug targets, since the cycle is absent from the mammalian hosts of these pathogens.
