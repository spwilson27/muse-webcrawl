Orientalism is a 1978 book by Edward W. Said that has been highly influential in postcolonial studies and other fields. In the work, Said examines the scholarly and artistic tradition by which Europeans constructed the "Orient" — the Middle East, Asia, and North Africa — as an exotic, backward, and essentially static Other against which the West defined itself. He argues that this discourse of Orientalism was inseparable from the imperial power exercised by Britain and France (and later the United States) over the regions it described.

Argument

Drawing on Michel Foucault's concept of discourse and Antonio Gramsci's idea of cultural hegemony, Said contends that Orientalist scholarship did not merely misunderstand the East but produced a body of knowledge that made domination possible: "the Orient was almost a European invention." He traces the tradition from Aeschylus and Dante through nineteenth-century philologists, administrators, and novelists such as Flaubert and Kipling, to modern American area studies and media portrayals of Arabs and Islam. Orientalism, he writes, is "a style of thought based upon an ontological and epistemological distinction made between 'the Orient' and (most of the time) 'the Occident.'"

Reception and influence

Orientalism is regarded as a founding text of postcolonial studies, spawning wide-ranging debates in literary studies, history, anthropology, art history, and Middle East studies. Admirers credit it with exposing the politics embedded in supposedly neutral scholarship; critics, including Bernard Lewis and Ibn Warraq, charged Said with selective evidence, ignoring German and Russian Orientalism, and homogenising the West. Said responded to his critics in a 2003 preface and in the companion essay "Orientalism Reconsidered," maintaining that his target was a system of representations rather than individual scholars.
