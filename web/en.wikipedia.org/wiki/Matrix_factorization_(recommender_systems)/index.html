Matrix factorization (recommender systems)

Matrix factorization is a class of collaborative filtering algorithms used in recommender systems. Matrix factorization algorithms work by decomposing the user–item interaction matrix into the product of two lower-dimensionality rectangular matrices. This family of methods became widely known during the Netflix Prize challenge for its effectiveness at predicting user ratings for films.

Basic idea

The sparse matrix of observed user ratings R is approximated as the product of a user latent-factor matrix and an item latent-factor matrix, R ≈ P·Qᵀ, where each user and each item is represented by a vector of latent factors. The predicted rating of a user for an item is the dot product of their vectors. The factors are learned by minimizing a regularized squared error over the observed ratings, typically with stochastic gradient descent or alternating least squares, using only the known entries.

History

The approach descends from information-retrieval techniques such as latent semantic analysis and singular value decomposition applied to sparse matrices. Simon Funk's incremental SVD algorithm, published on his blog in 2006 during the Netflix Prize, popularized learning latent factors directly from the observed ratings with regularization. Subsequent refinements — biases for users and items, temporal dynamics, and implicit feedback — were central to the winning solutions of the Netflix Prize and were described by Koren, Bell and Volinsky (2009).

Extensions

Variants include non-negative matrix factorization, probabilistic matrix factorization, SVD++, factorization machines, and models for implicit feedback (clicks, views, purchases) based on confidence-weighted loss functions. Matrix factorization is also widely used as a building block of hybrid recommenders combining content and behavioral data.
