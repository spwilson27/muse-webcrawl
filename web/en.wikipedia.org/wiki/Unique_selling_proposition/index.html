Unique selling proposition

Mid-century advertising, such as the campaigns of Rosser Reeves, was built around a single repeated unique claim.

The unique selling proposition (or unique selling point, USP) is a marketing concept first proposed as a theory to explain successful advertising campaigns of the early 1940s. The term was coined by Rosser Reeves of the Ted Bates advertising agency. A USP is the specific benefit or feature, unique to a product or brand, that is presented to consumers as the reason to buy: in Reeves's formulation each advertisement must say to the reader, "Buy this product, and you will get this specific benefit"; the proposition must be one the competition either cannot or does not offer; and it must be strong enough to move the mass millions.

History

Reeves developed the idea while creating campaigns noted for hard-sell repetition of a single claim, such as M&M's "Melts in your mouth, not in your hand" and Colgate's cleansing claim. He set out the theory in his 1961 book Reality in Advertising. The concept became a standard element of marketing textbooks and business planning, and is closely related to later ideas of positioning and differentiation.

Use and criticism

In contemporary practice, businesses are advised to define a clear USP to distinguish themselves in crowded markets, and the term is used generally for any distinctive advantage of a product, service, organisation or even a job candidate. Critics note that genuinely unique features are quickly imitated, that many purchases are driven by price, habit or brand rather than a rational unique claim, and that the concept fits packaged goods better than services or platforms.
