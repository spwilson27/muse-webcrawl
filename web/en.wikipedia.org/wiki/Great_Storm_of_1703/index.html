Great Storm of 1703

The Great Storm wrecked much of the Channel fleet.

The Great Storm of 1703 was a destructive extratropical cyclone that struck central and southern England on 26 November 1703 (Old Style; 7 December New Style). One of the most severe storms ever recorded in the British Isles, it caused an estimated 8,000 to 15,000 deaths, the great majority of them at sea, and enormous damage on land. Contemporary writers, including Daniel Defoe, interpreted the storm as a divine judgement and chronicled it in detail.

The storm

After a week of unsettled weather, the storm reached its peak in the night of 26–27 November. Winds estimated at well over 120 mph tore roofs from houses, toppled church spires and chimneys — falling masonry killed many in their beds — and uprooted an estimated 4,000 oak trees in the New Forest alone. In London, some 2,000 chimney stacks collapsed and the lead roof of Westminster Abbey was rolled up "like a scroll". The Eddystone Lighthouse off Plymouth was destroyed with its designer, Henry Winstanley, inside.

At sea the losses were catastrophic: the Royal Navy lost about 13 ships, including four ships of the line wrecked on the Goodwin Sands, where around 1,200 men drowned in sight of shore. Merchant losses in the Channel and the Thames were heavier still, and the storm surge flooded low-lying land around Bristol and the Severn estuary, drowning livestock and villagers.

Aftermath

Queen Anne ordered a national day of fasting and humiliation in January 1704. Defoe's The Storm (1704), compiled from advertisements soliciting eyewitness accounts, is often described as a pioneering work of journalism. The disaster also reduced a generation of naval timber and ships during the War of the Spanish Succession, and it remains a benchmark for storm severity in southern England, compared in modern times to the Great Storm of 1987.
