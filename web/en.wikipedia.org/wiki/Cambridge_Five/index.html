Cambridge Five

The ring was recruited among students at the University of Cambridge.

The Cambridge Five was a ring of spies in the United Kingdom who passed information to the Soviet Union during the Second World War and the early Cold War. All five were recruited while students at the University of Cambridge in the 1930s and went on to hold sensitive positions in British intelligence, the Foreign Office and other institutions. Their identities were a matter of speculation for decades.

Members

  • Kim Philby — MI6 officer and head of its Soviet counter-intelligence section; fled to Moscow in 1963.
  • Donald Maclean — Foreign Office diplomat; fled with Burgess in 1951.
  • Guy Burgess — MI6 and Foreign Office; fled in 1951.
  • Anthony Blunt — MI5 officer and later Surveyor of the Queen's Pictures; confessed in 1964 and was publicly exposed in 1979.
  • John Cairncross — civil servant and codebreaker at Bletchley Park; identified as the "fifth man" in 1990.

Exposure

The defection of Burgess and Maclean in May 1951, warned by Philby, triggered a long investigation that gradually unravelled the ring. The extent of the damage — which included atomic secrets and the identities of Western agents — made the case one of the most notorious espionage scandals of the twentieth century and a lasting symbol of the penetration of the British establishment.

In culture

The Cambridge spies have been the subject of numerous books, plays and films, including Alan Bennett's An Englishman Abroad and A Question of Attribution, and John le Carré's novel Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy, partly inspired by Philby.
