Islamic democracy

Islamic democracy refers to a political ideology and family of political systems that seek to combine democratic governance — elections, popular sovereignty, rule of law and civil liberties — with the principles and values of Islam. Supporters argue that concepts in Islamic tradition, above all shura (consultation), ijma (consensus) and the election of early caliphs, provide indigenous foundations for representative government, while critics contend that sovereignty in classical Islamic political theory belongs to God rather than the people.

Concepts

Thinkers associated with Islamic democracy and related reformist currents include Abul A'la Maududi — who coined the term "theodemocracy" for a system in which the people govern as God's vicegerents within the limits of divine law — Rashid Rida, Muhammad Iqbal, Fazlur Rahman, Rachid Ghannouchi of Tunisia's Ennahda party, and Abdolkarim Soroush in Iran. Key debates concern the status of Sharia as a source of legislation, the rights of women and non-Muslims, freedom of expression, and whether a clerical body may veto elected legislatures, as Iran's Guardian Council does.

Examples

Several states have been described as Islamic democracies or hybrid systems. The Islamic Republic of Iran combines elected institutions — a president and parliament — with clerical oversight by a Supreme Leader and Guardian Council. Pakistan's constitution declares it an Islamic republic with parliamentary government. Indonesia, the world's largest Muslim-majority country, is a democracy whose Pancasila ideology is not formally Islamic but accommodates religion. Malaysia is a constitutional monarchy with Islam as the religion of the federation. Following the Arab Spring of 2011, Tunisia under Ennahda was often cited as the clearest experiment in reconciling Islamism with democratic pluralism, while Turkey under the AKP was long presented as a model of "Muslim democracy" before concerns over democratic backsliding grew.

Criticism

Secular critics argue that enshrining religious law limits equal citizenship, while some Islamist critics reject democracy itself as an imposition of human legislation over God's sovereignty. Political scientists note that outcomes vary widely and that the label often says more about constitutional symbolism than about the depth of democratic practice.
