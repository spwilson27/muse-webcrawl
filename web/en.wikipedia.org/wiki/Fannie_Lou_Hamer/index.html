Fannie Lou Hamer

Fannie Lou Hamer (born Fannie Lou Townsend; October 6, 1917 – March 14, 1977) was an American voting and women's rights activist, community organizer, and a leader in the civil rights movement. She was the co-founder and vice-chair of the Freedom Democratic Party, which she represented at the 1964 Democratic National Convention.

Hamer also organized Mississippi's Freedom Summer along with the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). She was also a co-founder of the National Women's Political Caucus, an organization created to recruit, train, and support women of all races who wished to seek election to government office.

Early life

Hamer was born in Montgomery County, Mississippi, the youngest of 20 children. Her parents were sharecroppers, and she began working in the fields when she was only six years old. She attended school in a one-room schoolhouse until the sixth grade. Hamer was sterilized without her consent in 1961 by a white doctor who performed a hysterectomy during surgery to remove a uterine tumor; such forced sterilizations of Black women were so common they were nicknamed "Mississippi appendectomies."

Activism

On August 31, 1962, Hamer traveled with a group of fellow activists to the Indianola courthouse to attempt to register to vote. The attempt failed, and on the way home their bus was stopped and fined by police. Upon returning home she was fired from her job as a plantation timekeeper and forced to leave the plantation where she had lived and worked for 18 years.

In June 1963, Hamer and several other civil rights workers were arrested in Winona, Mississippi, and brutally beaten in jail, leaving her with permanent kidney damage and a blood clot that nearly caused her to lose the vision in one eye. She famously testified: "All of this is on account of we want to register, to become first-class citizens."

Freedom Democratic Party and the 1964 convention

In 1964 Hamer co-founded the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP), which challenged the state's all-white Democratic Party delegation at the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City. Her televised testimony before the convention's Credentials Committee, in which she described the violence she had suffered for trying to vote, brought national attention to the civil rights struggle in Mississippi. When asked whether she sought equal rights, she replied: "I am sick and tired of being sick and tired," a phrase that became her signature statement.

Later life and legacy

Hamer continued her activism, running for Congress and working on economic development programs in her community, including the Freedom Farm Cooperative. She died of complications of hypertension and breast cancer in 1977. In 1993 she was inducted posthumously into the National Women's Hall of Fame. In January 2025, she was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom.
