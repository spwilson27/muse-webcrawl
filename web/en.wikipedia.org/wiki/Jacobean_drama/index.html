The era

Jacobean drama refers to the plays and theatrical culture of England during the reign of James I (1603–1625), continuing the vitality of the Elizabethan theatre while darkening its tone. Whereas Elizabethan drama balanced optimism and ambition, Jacobean plays grew more cynical, dissecting court corruption, sexual appetite, and political decay. The King's Men, now under royal patronage, performed at both the Globe and the indoor Blackfriars Theatre, the latter's candlelit intimacy favouring darker, more intimate work.

Revenge tragedy

The revenge tragedy reached its peak in the Jacobean period. John Webster's The White Devil (1612) and The Duchess of Malfi (c. 1613) paired sumptuous verse with lurid Italianate settings, corruption, and poison. Thomas Middleton's The Revenger's Tragedy (1607, sometimes attributed to Cyril Tourneur) turned revenge into grotesque satire, while Women Beware Women and The Changeling (the latter with William Rowley) explored female agency and damnation.

City comedy and tragicomedy

City comedy satirised London's merchants, gallants, and whores: Middleton's A Chaste Maid in Cheapside (1613) and Ben Jonson's Volpone (1606), Epicoene (1609), and The Alchemist (1610) remain the form's peaks. Tragicomedy, pioneered by Francis Beaumont and John Fletcher in Philaster and A King and No King, blended threatened tragedy with providential rescue, and became the dominant fashionable genre of the decade.

The court masque

The court masque was the spectacular ceremonial entertainment of James I's court, written chiefly by Ben Jonson and designed by Inigo Jones, whose perspective scenery and machinery transformed stage spectacle. Queen Anne sponsored The Masque of Blackness (1605) and The Masque of Beauty (1608). Jonson also invented the satirical antimasque of grotesque figures preceding the noble main masque.

Major playwrights

  • Ben Jonson (1572–1637): the era's dominant classicist, author of the great comedies and the Jonsonian masque.
  • John Webster (c. 1580–c. 1634): master of the dark Jacobean tragedy.
  • Thomas Middleton (1580–1627): prolific across tragedy, city comedy, and pamphlet-like realism.
  • Beaumont and Fletcher: creators of the fashionable tragicomedy.
  • John Ford (1586–1639): whose 'Tis Pity She's a Whore (1633) carried Jacobean themes into Caroline theatre.

Legacy

Jacobean drama's preoccupation with corruption and power made it a touchstone for later political theatre. Its plays were revived extensively from the Restoration onward, and the Webster and Middleton tragedies remain staples of the modern repertoire.
