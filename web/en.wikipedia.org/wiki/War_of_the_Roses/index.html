War of the Roses

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: White and red roses, the badges of York and Lancaster]

The Wars of the Roses (1455–1487) were a series of civil wars fought in England between two rival branches of the royal House of Plantagenet — Lancaster (symbolised by a red rose) and York (a white rose) — for the throne of England. Sparked by the weakness and mental illness of Henry VI, the wars saw the crown change hands repeatedly amid battles such as St Albans, Towton (1461, perhaps the bloodiest battle on English soil) and Bosworth Field (1485), where Richard III was killed and the Tudor dynasty began under Henry VII. The wars thinned the old nobility and, in tradition, ended England's medieval age.

Course

Richard, Duke of York, protector during Henry VI's incapacity, claimed the throne through a senior line; after his death at Wakefield (1460), his son took London and was crowned Edward IV after the carnage of Towton. Edward reigned (1461–1470, 1471–1483) apart from Henry VI's brief restoration (the Readeption, 1470–71), which ended at Barnet and Tewkesbury, where Henry's son was killed and Henry himself died — probably murdered — in the Tower. Edward's death (1483) brought the disappearance of his sons, the 'Princes in the Tower', and the usurpation of Richard III.

Bosworth and aftermath

In August 1485 the exiled Lancastrian claimant Henry Tudor landed in Wales, defeated and killed Richard III at Bosworth, and was crowned Henry VII. His marriage to Elizabeth of York united the roses; victory over the last Yorkist rising at Stoke (1487) is conventionally taken as the wars' end. Shakespeare's history plays fixed the wars' popular image; modern historians note that fighting was intermittent and England's government and economy less devastated than tradition suggests.
