Goodhart's law

ProposerCharles Goodhart (1975)
Popular phrasingMarilyn Strathern (1997)
FieldEconomics; statistics; policy
RelatedCampbell's law; Lucas critique; Cobra effect

Goodhart's law is an adage often stated as: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." It is named after the British economist Charles Goodhart, who first articulated the idea in a 1975 article on monetary policy in the United Kingdom, writing that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern gave the law its now-standard phrasing in 1997, in the context of audit culture in British universities.

Explanation

The law describes what happens when a proxy measure — chosen because it correlates with some underlying quantity that is hard to observe directly — is instead optimised for its own sake. Agents subject to the target then game, distort or manipulate the measure, breaking its correlation with the thing it was meant to track. Examples frequently cited include teaching to the test in education, hospital waiting-time targets met by reclassifying patients, Soviet factories producing useless quantities of goods measured by weight or count, and monetary aggregates that stopped predicting inflation once central banks targeted them.

Related ideas

Goodhart's law is closely related to Campbell's law, formulated by social scientist Donald T. Campbell in 1979, which emphasises the corruption of social indicators under decision-making pressure, and to the Lucas critique in economics. It is widely invoked in discussions of performance metrics, standardised testing, academic publishing rankings and machine learning, where optimising a proxy objective can produce reward hacking.
