Taberna

PeriodRoman antiquity
MeaningShop, stall, workshop or tavern
FeaturesWide street opening, masonry counter, mezzanine loft (pergula)
Notable examplesPompeii and Ostia Antica

A taberna (plural tabernae) was a type of shop or stall in ancient Rome, and by extension the taverns and workshops that filled the street fronts of Roman cities. Originally the word denoted a hut or booth of boards (tabulae), but in the urban architecture of the late Republic and Empire it came to mean the standard single-room commercial unit embedded in the ground floor of houses and apartment blocks.

Layout

The characteristic taberna opened onto the street through a very wide doorway, closed at night by wooden shutters that slotted into a groove in the threshold and counter. Many food and drink shops had an L-shaped masonry counter faced with marble or reused amphorae, with large earthenware jars (dolia) sunk into it for wine and foodstuffs. A wooden mezzanine, the pergula, reached by a ladder or narrow stair, provided storage or cramped living quarters for the shopkeeper (tabernarius), who often lived and worked in the one space.

Types and social role

Tabernae housed bakers, butchers, fullers, metalworkers, booksellers and money-changers, as well as eating and drinking establishments: the popina and caupona served hot food and wine to the urban poor, who largely lacked kitchens at home, making the taberna the fast-food outlet and social hub of the Roman street. Elite writers looked down on taverns as dens of gambling and vice, and emperors periodically tried to restrict what cooked food they could sell. Well-preserved examples line the streets of Pompeii and Ostia, where painted signs and surviving counters show how thoroughly commerce colonized the Roman street frontage.

Legacy

The word survives in "tavern" and in the Romance languages (Spanish taberna, Italian taverna), and also in the biblical "tabernacle" tradition via the Latin tabernaculum, a diminutive of taberna.
