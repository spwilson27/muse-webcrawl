Participatory culture

Participatory culture is a culture in which private individuals (the public) do not act only as consumers but also as contributors and producers of content. The term is most often associated with media scholar Henry Jenkins, who contrasted it with older models of passive spectatorship: in a participatory culture, fans and audiences create fan fiction, remix video, build game modifications, annotate texts, and otherwise reshape the media they receive, circulating their work through social networks and digital platforms.

Jenkins's framework

Jenkins developed the concept across works including Textual Poachers (1992) and Convergence Culture (2006). A 2009 white paper, Confronting the Challenges of Participatory Culture, written with colleagues for the MacArthur Foundation, defined a participatory culture as one with relatively low barriers to artistic expression and civic engagement, strong support for creating and sharing creations, and informal mentorship in which experienced members pass knowledge to novices. Members believe their contributions matter and feel some degree of social connection with one another.

Forms of participation

Jenkins's forms
AffiliationsMemberships in online communities (social networks, forums)
ExpressionsProducing new creative forms — fan videos, mods, mashups
Collaborative problem-solvingWorking together to complete tasks and build knowledge (wikis, alternate reality games)
CirculationsShaping the flow of media (blogging, podcasting)

Examples of participatory culture include Wikipedia's volunteer editors, fan-fiction archives, YouTube creators, open-source software contributors, and video-game modding communities. Jenkins and others argue that participation raises questions about access (the "participation gap"), about the ethics and skills young people need, and about how commercial media industries incorporate — and sometimes exploit — fan labour.
