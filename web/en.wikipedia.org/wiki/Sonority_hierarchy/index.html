Sonority hierarchy

A sonority hierarchy or sonority scale is a ranking of speech sounds (or phone types) by their sonority, an acoustic property related to loudness and openness of the vocal tract. For example, ranking vowels above glides, glides above liquids, liquids above nasals, and nasals above obstruents yields the scale shown below.

Sonority sequencing principle

The sonority hierarchy is used to explain syllable structure through the sonority sequencing principle (SSP): in a well-formed syllable, sonority rises steadily from the onset toward the nucleus (peak) and falls toward the coda. For example, in the English word plant, sonority rises from /p/ to /l/ to the vowel /æ/ and then falls through /n/ to /t/. Apparent violations of the SSP, such as sibilant-initial clusters (/sp/, /st/, /sk/), are a longstanding puzzle in phonology.

Definitions of sonority

Sonority has been defined acoustically in terms of intensity, articulatorily in terms of the degree of stricture or openness of the vocal tract, and perceptually in terms of audibility at a distance. No single phonetic correlate has proven entirely adequate, and sonority is sometimes treated as a primitive of phonological theory.

Use in phonology

Sonority scales are used to state constraints on possible syllables and consonant clusters across languages, to explain patterns of epenthesis and deletion, and in models of the syllable such as Optimality Theory accounts of cluster phonotactics.
