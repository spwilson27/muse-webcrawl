Patriarchy

Patriarchy is a social system in which positions of dominance and privilege are primarily held by men. In its original and narrower sense it denotes rule by the father — the male head of a household or clan — while in feminist theory the term is used more broadly for social structures and ideologies that establish or maintain male authority in political leadership, moral authority, property, and the family.

History and anthropology

Patriarchal arrangements appear in many recorded societies, from ancient Mesopotamia and classical Greece and Rome to traditional China and India. Nineteenth-century theorists such as Johann Jakob Bachofen and Friedrich Engels debated whether patriarchy was universal or had succeeded an earlier matriarchal stage; Engels, in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884), linked patriarchy to the rise of private property and inheritance. Anthropologists today generally treat systems of kinship and authority as varied, with patrilineal descent and male household headship widespread but not universal.

Feminist theory

From the second-wave feminism of the 1960s and 1970s, patriarchy became a central analytic concept. Kate Millett's Sexual Politics (1970) and Gerda Lerner's The Creation of Patriarchy (1986) treated it as a historically constructed system rather than a natural order. Later scholars, including bell hooks and Sylvia Walby, refined the concept — Walby identifying structures such as paid work, household production, culture, sexuality, violence, and the state — and intersectional approaches examine how patriarchy interacts with race, class, and colonialism.

Criticism and debate

Critics of the concept argue that it can be imprecise, that it may obscure women's agency and variations across societies, or that it functions more as a political slogan than an explanatory tool. Others defend it as indispensable for naming persistent patterns of male advantage.
