Inland taipan

Inland taipan
Scientific name: Oxyuranus microlepidotus
Family: Elapidae
Range: Channel Country, south-western Queensland and north-eastern South Australia
Also called: Western taipan, fierce snake

The inland taipan (Oxyuranus microlepidotus), also called the western taipan or fierce snake, is a highly venomous snake endemic to semi-arid regions of central east Australia. Based on the median lethal dose of its venom in mice, it is considered the most venomous snake in the world, yet it is shy and reclusive, living far from most human settlement, and no recorded fatalities are attributed to it where antivenom and first aid have been available.

Description and habitat

The inland taipan averages about 1.8 metres in length. Its colour varies seasonally — darker in winter, paler in summer — helping it regulate body temperature. It inhabits the deep cracks of dry black-soil plains in the Channel Country where the borders of Queensland and South Australia meet, sheltering underground and emerging to hunt.

Diet and venom

It specialises in mammals, especially the long-haired rat, whose population booms after rain; in plague years taipans thrive and in lean years they decline. The venom is a potent cocktail of neurotoxins, procoagulants, and other toxins evolved to subdue warm-blooded prey quickly; a single bite yields enough venom, by laboratory measures, to kill many times the snake's own mass in prey. The species was known to Aboriginal people for millennia but was described scientifically in 1879 and then rarely seen until its "rediscovery" in 1972.
