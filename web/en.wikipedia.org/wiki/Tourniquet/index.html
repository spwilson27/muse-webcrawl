Tourniquet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tourniquet is a device that is used to apply pressure to a limb or extremity in order to stop the flow of blood. It may be used in emergencies, in surgery, or in post-operative rehabilitation. A simple tourniquet can be made from a stick and a rope (or leather belt), but the use of makeshift tourniquets has been much reduced over time due to the acceptance of commercial tourniquets designed for first-response bleeding control and for surgical bloodless operative fields.

Emergency use

In emergency medicine, a tourniquet is applied proximal to a site of severe extremity bleeding that cannot be controlled by direct pressure. Modern military experience, particularly from the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, demonstrated that early tourniquet application dramatically improved survival from catastrophic limb haemorrhage, leading to the widespread issue of commercial devices such as the Combat Application Tourniquet (CAT) and the SOF Tactical Tourniquet. Civilian first-aid guidance, including the "Stop the Bleed" campaign in the United States, now teaches tourniquet use to the public.

A tourniquet should be tightened until bleeding stops and the distal pulse is absent; a loosely applied tourniquet can worsen bleeding by obstructing venous return while allowing arterial inflow. Once applied in the field, a tourniquet is generally not loosened until the casualty reaches surgical care, and the time of application should be recorded.

Surgical use

In orthopaedic and plastic surgery, pneumatic tourniquets — inflatable cuffs similar to a blood-pressure cuff — are used to create a bloodless operative field in limb surgery. Safe use requires attention to cuff pressure and duration; continuous application is typically limited to about two hours to reduce the risk of nerve injury and tourniquet pain. Tourniquets are also used in intravenous regional anaesthesia (the Bier block), where the cuff isolates the anaesthetised limb from the systemic circulation.

Other uses

A light constricting band, sometimes loosely called a tourniquet, is used in phlebotomy to make veins more prominent for blood drawing or intravenous cannulation; medical stylets and elastic bands (such as the Esmarch bandage) are used to exsanguinate a limb before surgery. In hairdressing the term also appears in "hair tourniquet syndrome", where a strand of hair strangulates an infant's digit.

History

Compression devices for controlling bleeding date to antiquity; the Roman physician Galen described their use. The screw tourniquet was developed by Jean Louis Petit in 1718, and the word derives from the French tourner, "to turn", referring to the twisting of the windlass or screw. Joseph Lister used tourniquets in surgery in the 1860s, and Friedrich von Esmarch introduced the elastic bandage technique in 1873. Harvey Cushing introduced the pneumatic cuff in 1904.

Risks

Improper or prolonged tourniquet use can cause nerve damage, muscle injury, compartment syndrome, and, in the worst cases, loss of the limb. Venous tourniquets applied too loosely may increase bleeding. For these reasons, training emphasises applying tourniquets only for life-threatening limb bleeding, placing them "high and tight" when the bleeding site is unclear, and documenting the time of application.
