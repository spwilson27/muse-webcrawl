Rivers of the United Kingdom

The rivers of the United Kingdom drain the islands of Great Britain and Ireland's northeastern portion within Northern Ireland, along with numerous smaller islands. Owing to the United Kingdom's position near the western edge of Europe, receiving the prevailing moist westerly winds, rainfall is abundant in many upland regions, and the country possesses a dense network of relatively short rivers. The longest river entirely within the United Kingdom is the River Severn, while the River Shannon in Ireland (mostly in the Republic of Ireland) is the longest in the British Isles.

Major rivers by region

In England, the Thames flows through London and has been central to the country's history, trade, and settlement. The Severn rises in the Cambrian Mountains of Wales and flows through Shrewsbury, Worcester, and Gloucester before entering the Bristol Channel, which has one of the largest tidal ranges in the world and a famous tidal bore. The Trent drains much of the Midlands, joining the Ouse to form the Humber.

In Scotland, the Tay carries the greatest volume of water of any river in the United Kingdom. The Clyde powered Glasgow's shipbuilding industry, and the Spey is famed for salmon fishing and whisky distilleries along its banks. The Tweed forms part of the border between Scotland and England.

In Wales, besides the Severn and Wye, notable rivers include the Dee, the Tywi, the Teifi, and the Conwy. In Northern Ireland, the Bann flows through Lough Neagh, the largest lake in the British Isles by surface area, and the Foyle and Lagan drain the northwest and Belfast respectively.

Use and management

British rivers have been used for transport, milling, drinking water, irrigation, and hydroelectric power (especially in the Scottish Highlands). Many lowland rivers are heavily modified by embankments, weirs, and canal connections from the era of the Industrial Revolution, when rivers fed the canal network that moved coal and manufactured goods. Flooding is a recurring concern, as are pollution and the ecological status of waterways, which are monitored under environmental regulation by bodies such as the Environment Agency in England, Natural Resources Wales, the Scottish Environment Protection Agency, and the Northern Ireland Environment Agency.

Rivers of the United Kingdom support Atlantic salmon, brown trout, otters, kingfishers, and a wide range of invertebrates. Chalk streams, found mainly in southern and eastern England, are a globally rare habitat type sustained by groundwater from chalk aquifers.
