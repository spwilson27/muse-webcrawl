River Neckinger

River Neckinger
LocationSouth London, England
MouthRiver Thames at St Saviour's Dock, Bermondsey
StatusSubterranean (culverted) except at its tidal mouth

The River Neckinger is a reduced subterranean river that flows through south London to its mouth at St Saviour's Dock in Bermondsey, where it joins the River Thames. Almost entirely culverted since the 19th century, it is one of the lost rivers of London, though its final tidal channel remains visible at low tide.

Course

The Neckinger rose from springs in the area of modern Southwark and Elephant and Castle, and flowed eastwards roughly along the line of New Kent Road, through the district around Abbey Street and Jamaica Road in Bermondsey, to the Thames at St Saviour's Dock, east of Tower Bridge. Its former course still influences local street names and boundaries.

History

The river's lower course bordered Bermondsey Abbey, the great Cluniac monastery of St Saviour founded in the 11th century, whose precinct lay beside the stream. The area along the Neckinger became notorious in the 19th century as Jacob's Island, the rookery immortalised by Charles Dickens in Oliver Twist as the squalid refuge where Bill Sikes meets his end falling from a rooftop into the tidal ditch ("Folly Ditch") fed by the Neckinger. Following the cholera epidemics and sanitary reforms of the mid-19th century, the district was cleared, the river was bricked over and converted into a sewer, and only the dock inlet at its mouth remained open.

Name

The peculiar name is usually explained by reference to the "devil's neckcloth" or "neckinger"—slang for a hangman's noose: pirates and other criminals were traditionally hanged at the river's mouth on the Thames foreshore, at execution places associated with the tidal stairs nearby. The name is recorded in forms such as "Neckinger" by the 18th century.
