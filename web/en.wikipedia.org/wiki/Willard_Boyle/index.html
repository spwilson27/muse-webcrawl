Willard Sterling Boyle (19 August 1924 – 7 May 2011) was a Canadian physicist and co-inventor of the charge-coupled device (CCD). He shared a quarter of the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics with George E. Smith 'for the invention of an imaging semiconductor circuit — the CCD sensor' (half of the prize went to Charles Kuen Kao for optical fibres).

Boyle, a Nova Scotian who served in the Royal Canadian Navy and took his PhD at McGill, joined Bell Labs in 1953, working on the first continuously operating ruby laser and semiconductor injection lasers. In an hour-long brainstorming session in October 1969, Boyle and Smith sketched the CCD: an array of tiny capacitors that store photo-generated charge and shuffle it, bucket-brigade style, to a readout — the basis of nearly all digital imaging, from the Hubble Space Telescope to phone cameras and medical scanners.

Legacy

The CCD turned light directly into data and made modern astronomy possible: every pixel of the Event Horizon Telescope-era detectors' ancestors, the deep-field images of Hubble, and the survey cameras mapping the sky are its descendants. Boyle retired to Nova Scotia, where he sailed and, by family accounts, applied the same ingenuity to winning arguments about local weather instruments.
