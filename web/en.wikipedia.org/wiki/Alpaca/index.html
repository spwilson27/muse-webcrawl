The alpaca (Lama pacos) is a species of South American camelid mammal. Traditionally, alpacas were kept in herds that grazed on the level heights of the Andes of Southern Peru, western Bolivia, Ecuador, and southwestern Colombia. Alpacas are closely related to the llama, and genetic studies indicate that they descend principally from the wild vicuña, domesticated between five and six thousand years ago.

Characteristics

An adult alpaca generally stands about 81–99 cm (32–39 in) at the withers and weighs 48–90 kg (106–200 lb). Alpacas are covered in a thick fleece of long, silky fibre that occurs naturally in more than twenty recognised colours, more than any other fibre-producing animal. Two breed types are distinguished by fleece character: the Huacaya, whose dense, crimped fleece resembles sheep's wool, and the Suri, whose fibre grows in long, lustrous locks.

Behaviour

Alpacas are social herd animals that communicate through body posture and a repertoire of sounds, most commonly a soft humming. They are well known for spitting as a defence and as a means of establishing herd hierarchy; the spit is regurgitated stomach contents. Alpacas are induced ovulators and are typically bred once a year, producing a single cria after a gestation of about eleven and a half months.

Uses

Alpacas are bred primarily for their fibre, which is warmer, lighter, and less prickly than sheep's wool, contains no lanolin, and is used for high-quality garments, blankets, and knitwear. In Peru, where the great majority of the world's roughly 4.5 million alpacas live, alpaca meat is also consumed, and alpacas have become popular worldwide as pasture companions, guard animals for sheep, and therapy animals.
