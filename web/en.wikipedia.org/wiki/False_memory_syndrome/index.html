False memory syndrome

In psychology, false memory syndrome (FMS) describes a condition in which a person's identity and interpersonal relationships are affected by a memory they believe to be true but which is actually false and strongly believed in. The term was coined in the early 1990s in connection with the False Memory Syndrome Foundation, founded in 1992 by parents who had been accused, on the basis of memories their adult children reported recovering in therapy, of child sexual abuse that the accused parents maintained never occurred.

Background

During the 1980s and early 1990s, a movement in psychotherapy promoted the idea that symptoms such as depression, eating disorders and anxiety might indicate repressed memories of childhood abuse. Some therapists used techniques such as hypnosis, guided imagery, dream interpretation and age regression to help patients "recover" such memories. Critics, including cognitive psychologists such as Elizabeth Loftus, argued that these techniques were highly suggestive and could create vivid, confidently held memories of events that never happened.

Research

Experimental research on memory has shown that human recollection is reconstructive rather than a literal replay of the past. In "lost in the mall" studies and related paradigms, researchers were able to implant detailed false memories of plausible childhood events in a substantial minority of participants. Misleading post-event information (the misinformation effect) can also alter recollection. This body of work underlies concerns that suggestive therapeutic techniques could produce false memories.

Controversy

False memory syndrome is not recognised as a diagnosis in the DSM-5. The concept remains controversial: while there is broad agreement that false memories can occur, clinicians and researchers disagree about the prevalence of recovered memories of abuse and about the reliability of such memories when they emerge in therapy. The debate, sometimes called the "memory wars", had major legal consequences, contributing to acquittals, retracted accusations, and lawsuits against therapists.
