Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Goethe in the Roman Campagna (1787) by Tischbein: the poet in a white cloak seated among ancient ruins, the most famous portrait of Germany's national writer]

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (28 August 1749 – 22 March 1832) was a German poet, playwright, novelist, scientist, statesman and theatre director, the towering figure of German literature and a universal genius of European culture. His works span the Storm and Stress of his youth, Weimar Classicism, and the world-literature of his old age; his Faust is Germany's national poem.

Goethe's fame began with Götz von Berlichingen (1773) and the epistolary novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774), which made him a European celebrity at 24. His mature works include the Wilhelm Meister novels, the West-Eastern Diwan (1819), the autobiography Poetry and Truth, the Roman Elegies, and Faust Part One (1808) and Part Two (1832, published posthumously).

Weimar and Italy

Summoned to the Weimar court in 1775, Goethe spent a decade as minister and administrator before fleeing incognito to Italy (1786–88) — a rebirth as artist among classical art and landscape. Back in Weimar he directed the court theatre, formed with Schiller the alliance at the heart of Weimar Classicism (the ballad year 1797, the journal Die Horen), and pursued science: morphology, the metamorphosis of plants, and his controversial Theory of Colours (1810), which he prized above his poetry.

Old age and world literature

The last decades brought the Marienbad Elegy (1823, after his rejected proposal to the teenage Ulrike von Levetzow), the completion of Faust Part Two in a sealed packet for posthumous publication, and the conversations recorded by Eckermann that fixed his image as Europe's sage. His coinage Weltliteratur — world literature — named the global republic of letters he saw coming. He died in Weimar in 1832; his reported last words, 'More light!', are probably legend.
