William Gilbert Grace (18 July 1848 – 23 October 1915) was an English amateur cricketer who was important in the development of the sport and is considered one of its greatest-ever players. Universally known as "W. G.", he played first-class cricket for a record-equalling 44 seasons, from 1865 to 1908, during which he captained England, Gloucestershire, the Gentlemen, Marylebone Cricket Club (MCC), the United South of England Eleven and several other teams.

A right-handed batsman and bowler, Grace dominated the sport during his career. His technical innovations and enormous influence made modern batting possible: he is held to have invented modern batsmanship, combining orthodox strokeplay with powerful hitting. An outstanding all-rounder, he also excelled as a fielder and, early in his career, as an athlete, winning a hurdles title at the National Olympian Games in 1866.

Career

Grace made his first-class debut in 1865 and quickly became the outstanding player of his era. In 1871 he scored 2,739 runs at an average of 78.25, a season unmatched for decades. He was the first batsman to score 100 first-class centuries, reaching the milestone in 1895, and the first to pass 2,000 runs in a season. In his career he scored 54,896 first-class runs and took 2,864 wickets. He played 22 Test matches for England, captaining the side, and made his Test debut in 1880, scoring England's first Test century on home soil.

Legacy

With his full beard, large frame and commanding personality, Grace became one of the best-known men in England and a national institution. Despite his nominally amateur status he earned more money from cricket than any professional of his day. The W. G. Grace Memorial Gates at Lord's commemorate him. Wisden described his achievements as "without parallel" and his influence on the game as incalculable.
