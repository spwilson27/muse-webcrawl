Early Irish law

Also known asBrehon law; Fénechas
RegionIreland
PeriodEarly medieval period to the 17th century
Principal textSenchas Már

Early Irish law, also known in English as Brehon law (from breitheamh, "judge"), is the body of law that governed daily life in early medieval Ireland. In Irish it was called Fénechas, "the law of the Féni" (the free men of Ireland). The laws survive in a large corpus of Old and Middle Irish legal texts, mostly written down between the 7th and 9th centuries, and the system remained in use, especially in Gaelic-controlled areas, until it was displaced by English law in the early 17th century.

Texts and jurists

The most important compilation is the Senchas Már ("Great Tradition"), a collection of dozens of tracts covering status, injury, property, contracts and crime, traditionally attributed to a commission convened by Saint Patrick. Other major texts include Uraicecht Becc on social rank and the Bretha Nemed school texts on the privileged classes. Law was in the hands of a professional class of jurists (brithemain) trained in hereditary legal schools; there was no central court system or state enforcement — judgments were secured through sureties, distraint of property, and the pressure of public opinion.

Characteristics

Early Irish law was strongly status-based: a person's legal capacity, compensation values and oath-worth depended on rank, measured partly by an "honour price" (lóg n-enech). Compensation rather than corporal or capital punishment was the norm: offences were remedied by fines such as the éraic (body-fine) for killing, payable by and to the kin-group. The law tracts give detailed rules on cattle, land, marriage (recognising several grades of union), fosterage, clientship, beekeeping and even the liability of cats and dogs. Women had limited but real property rights, and could obtain divorce on specified grounds.

Decline

After the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169, English common law spread in the areas under English control, while Brehon law persisted in Gaelic lordships. Successive statutes, culminating in the Tudor conquest and judgments such as the Case of Tanistry (1608), declared the Irish laws void and completed their suppression in the early 17th century.
