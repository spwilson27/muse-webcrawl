British Guiana was a British colony on the northern coast of South America, formed in 1831 by the union of the colonies of Demerara-Essequibo and Berbice, which Britain had taken from the Netherlands. It is now the independent nation of Guyana. Its capital was Georgetown, at the mouth of the Demerara River.

History

The Dutch had established trading posts and sugar colonies along the Essequibo, Demerara and Berbice rivers from the early 17th century. Britain occupied the colonies during the wars with revolutionary and Napoleonic France and formally received them under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1814. In 1831 the settlements were united as British Guiana.

The colony's sugar economy rested first on enslaved African labour and, after emancipation in the 1830s, on indentured labourers brought principally from India, as well as from Madeira, China and Africa. The resulting society — Afro-Guyanese, Indo-Guyanese, Amerindian and smaller European and Chinese communities — shaped the colony's politics for generations.

Boundary disputes

The western boundary with Venezuela was contested throughout the colonial period: the Schomburgk Line survey of the 1840s claimed territory Venezuela regarded as its own, and although an arbitral tribunal awarded most of the disputed land to Britain in 1899, Venezuela later revived the claim. A boundary with Brazil in the south was settled by arbitration in 1904.

Towards independence

Universal adult suffrage came in 1953, when the People's Progressive Party under Cheddi Jagan won the first elections; the constitution was suspended months later amid British fears of communist influence. After further constitutions, elections and the split of the movement along ethnic lines, British Guiana became independent on 26 May 1966 as Guyana, with Forbes Burnham as its first prime minister.
