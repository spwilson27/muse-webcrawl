The Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF) is a wide-field sky astronomical survey using a new camera attached to the Samuel Oschin Telescope at Palomar Observatory in California, United States. Commissioned in 2018, it systematically scans the entire northern sky every two to three nights, searching for transient astronomical events — objects that change in brightness or position — such as supernovae, variable stars, asteroids, and comets.

Instrument

The ZTF camera has a 47-square-degree field of view — roughly 100 times the area of the full moon in a single exposure — made up of 16 CCDs totalling 576 megapixels. Mounted on the 1.2-metre Samuel Oschin Schmidt telescope, the same instrument used for earlier Palomar sky surveys, it can image the whole visible northern sky in a single night of observing, producing on the order of a terabyte of data per night and hundreds of thousands of transient alerts.

Science

ZTF has discovered thousands of supernovae, including rare superluminous and pair-instability candidates, and it classifies more supernovae spectroscopically than any other survey. It found the first asteroid known to orbit entirely within the orbit of Venus (2020 AV2), identified numerous near-Earth asteroids, and provided early detections enabling multi-messenger follow-up of gravitational-wave events. It is also a pathfinder for the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time, testing alert distribution and broker systems such as ALeRCE and ANTARES.

The facility is named after Fritz Zwicky, the Swiss astronomer who pioneered supernova surveys at Palomar and coined much of the vocabulary of transient astronomy. It is operated by a partnership led by the California Institute of Technology with funding from the US National Science Foundation and international partners.
