Archdiocese

An archdiocese (also known as an archbishopric) is a diocese of a church that is administered by an archbishop rather than a bishop. Because of their historical importance, size, or population, certain dioceses are elevated to the rank of archdiocese, and the bishops who govern them bear the title of archbishop. The term is used particularly in the Roman Catholic Church, and in modified form in the Anglican Communion, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and other historic episcopal churches.

Structure and governance

An archdiocese is typically the principal see of an ecclesiastical province, a grouping of surrounding dioceses known as suffragan dioceses. The archbishop of a metropolitan archdiocese is called a metropolitan archbishop and exercises limited supervisory functions over the suffragan sees of the province, including convening provincial councils and hearing certain appeals. Other archdioceses are non-metropolitan and are immediately subject to the Holy See.

The governing church of an archdiocese is its cathedral, which houses the archbishop's cathedra or throne. Administration is normally carried out through a chancery, with auxiliary bishops and a vicar general assisting the archbishop.

History

The rank developed in the late Roman Empire, when the bishops of major metropolitan cities — the metropolitans — acquired oversight of the churches of their provinces. Councils such as Nicaea (325) and Chalcedon (451) regulated the rights of metropolitans. Over the Middle Ages the archiepiscopal office became closely associated with the pallium, a woollen band conferred by the pope as a sign of metropolitan authority.

Examples

Well-known Roman Catholic archdioceses include New York, Paris, Cologne, Canterbury (Anglican), Westminster, Manila, and São Paulo. The Archdiocese of Manila, founded as a diocese in 1579 and raised to an archdiocese in 1595, is the oldest in the Philippines. In the United States there are more than thirty metropolitan archdioceses.
