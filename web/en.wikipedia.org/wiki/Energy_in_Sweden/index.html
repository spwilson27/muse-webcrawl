Energy in Sweden

Energy in Sweden is characterised by one of the world's most decarbonised electricity systems. Sweden generates nearly all its electricity from hydropower, nuclear power, and wind, and has among the lowest carbon emissions per unit of electricity in Europe. The country aims for net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2045.

Electricity supply

Hydropower from the great northern rivers (such as the Lule älv) supplies roughly 40% of electricity in an average year; nuclear reactors at Ringhals, Forsmark, and Oskarshamn provide about 30%; wind power has grown past 20%. District heating networks, fuelled largely by biomass and waste heat, warm most urban buildings. A carbon tax introduced in 1991 — among the world's highest — helped drive fossil fuels out of heating.

History and policy

Oil shocks in the 1970s prompted both the nuclear build-out and conservation drives. A 1980 referendum voted to phase out nuclear power, but the policy was gradually reversed; in 2010 parliament allowed replacement reactors, and recent governments have embraced nuclear expansion as essential for electrifying steel, transport, and industry — including fossil-free steel projects such as HYBRIT in the north.

Consumption

Sweden's per-capita electricity use is high by European standards, reflecting cold climate, extensive electrified industry (mining, pulp and paper, steel), and growing data-centre demand. The grid is interconnected with Norway, Finland, Denmark, Germany, Poland, and the Baltic states, with Sweden usually a net exporter.
