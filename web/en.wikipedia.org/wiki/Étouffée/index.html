Étouffée is a dish of Louisiana Cajun and Creole cuisine consisting of seafood — most famously crawfish — smothered in a thick, richly seasoned sauce and served over rice. The name comes from the French étouffée, "smothered" or "suffocated", describing the cooking method.

Preparation

The dish begins with a roux — butter-based in most étouffées rather than the oil roux of gumbo — into which the Cajun "holy trinity" of onion, celery, and bell pepper is cooked down. Stock, seasonings, and the shellfish are then added and gently simmered until the seafood is bathed in a glossy, gravy-like sauce; the result is ladled over white rice and finished with green onions and hot sauce.

History and variations

Crawfish étouffée is associated with the Breaux Bridge area of Acadiana, the self-styled "crawfish capital of the world", where it was popularised in the 1920s and 1930s and spread to New Orleans. Cajun versions tend to be darker and tomato-free, while Creole versions may include tomato. Shrimp, crab, and even chicken étouffées are common variants.
