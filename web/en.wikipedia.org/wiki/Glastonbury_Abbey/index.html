Glastonbury Abbey was a monastery in Glastonbury, Somerset, England. Its ruins, a grade I listed building and scheduled ancient monument, are open as a visitor attraction. The abbey was founded in the 7th century and enlarged in the 10th century by Abbot Dunstan. It was one of the richest and most powerful monasteries in England before its suppression in the Dissolution of the Monasteries in 1539.

Legends

Glastonbury is wrapped in legend. Medieval tradition held that Joseph of Arimathea came to Glastonbury and founded the first church in Britain, and that his staff took root as the Glastonbury Thorn. In 1191 the monks announced the discovery of the grave of King Arthur and Guinevere, and Glastonbury became identified with the Isle of Avalon, a claim that drew pilgrims and royal patronage.

History

After a devastating fire in 1184 destroyed the old church, the abbey was rebuilt on a grand scale; the Lady Chapel was consecrated in 1186. King Arthur's purported remains were reburied before Edward I in 1278. At the Dissolution, Abbot Richard Whiting refused to surrender the abbey and was executed on Glastonbury Tor in November 1539; the abbey's estates were seized and its buildings quarried for stone.

Today

The surviving ruins include the great church's crossing and choir walls, St Mary's Chapel, and the 14th-century Abbot's Kitchen, one of the best-preserved medieval kitchens in Europe. The site hosts concerts, exhibitions and reenactments and remains a place of pilgrimage for Christians and others.
