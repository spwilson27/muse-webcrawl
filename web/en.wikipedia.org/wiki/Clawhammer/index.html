Clawhammer

Clawhammer, sometimes called frailing, is a distinctive style of playing the five-string banjo associated with American old-time music. Unlike the fingerpicking style used in bluegrass, clawhammer uses the back of the fingernail (usually the index or middle finger) to strike the melody strings in a downward motion, while the thumb catches the fifth string or a melody note in a rhythmic pattern often summarised as "bum-ditty".

Clawhammer technique on an open-back banjo.

Technique

The hand is held in a loose claw shape. The basic stroke consists of the nail striking a melody string downward, followed by a brush across the strings, with the thumb then plucking the short fifth string to complete the measure. A characteristic advanced technique is "drop thumbing", in which the thumb also plays melody notes on lower strings. Clawhammer is typically played on an open-back banjo, often with the strings at a higher action and sometimes with a "frailing scoop" carved into the neck.

History

The style descends from West African lute-playing traditions brought to the Americas by enslaved musicians, in which a short drone string is played by the thumb — a technique documented in the Caribbean and the American South. Nineteenth-century minstrel performers spread the "stroke style" of banjo playing. In the twentieth century, clawhammer became the rhythmic heart of old-time string bands in Appalachia, with noted players including Wade Ward, Kyle Creed, Fred Cockerham, and Tommy Jarrell of North Carolina. Grandpa Jones popularised the style on the Hee Haw television programme, and it remains central to the old-time revival.
