Naturalistic fallacy

The naturalistic fallacy is an alleged error in moral philosophy: the attempt to define the ethical term "good" in terms of natural properties such as pleasure, desire, or evolutionary fitness, or to derive statements about what ought to be purely from statements about what is. The term was introduced by the British philosopher G. E. Moore in his 1903 book Principia Ethica.

Moore's open-question argument

Moore argued that "good" is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property, comparable to the colour yellow: just as one cannot define yellow to someone who cannot see it, good cannot be analysed into other terms. His test was the open question argument: for any natural property proposed as the definition of good — say, pleasure — it remains an intelligible, "open" question whether things having that property are in fact good. If the proposed definition were correct, the question would be closed and trivial, as "Are bachelors unmarried?" is. Since it is not, the identification fails.

Relation to Hume's law

The naturalistic fallacy is closely related to, but distinct from, David Hume's observation (the is–ought problem) that normative conclusions cannot be deduced from purely descriptive premises. Moore's fallacy concerns the definition of moral terms; Hume's concerns the logic of inference.

Debate

Later philosophers have disputed whether the alleged fallacy is genuine. Ethical naturalists such as the Cornell realists argue that moral properties might be identical to natural properties in the way water is identical to H2O — an identity discoverable empirically rather than by definition. Others, including J. L. Mackie and the non-cognitivists, rejected Moore's non-naturalism while retaining doubts about defining good naturalistically.
