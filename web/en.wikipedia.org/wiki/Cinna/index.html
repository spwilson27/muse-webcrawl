Gaius Helvius Cinna was a Roman poet of the first century BC, a leading figure among the neoteric poets (poetae novi) and a friend of Catullus. He is famous both for his lost masterpiece, the epyllion Zmyrna, and for his lynching by an angry mob that mistook him for one of Julius Caesar's assassins.

Cinna was killed by the crowd after Caesar's funeral in 44 BC.

Life and death

Cinna came from northern Italy, like Catullus, and probably travelled to Bithynia with him in the entourage of Memmius in 57 BC. In 44 BC he was a tribune of the plebs and a supporter of Julius Caesar. After Caesar's assassination on the Ides of March, Cinna attended the dictator's funeral — where the crowd, inflamed by Mark Antony's speech, seized him in the street and tore him to pieces, mistaking him for Lucius Cornelius Cinna, the praetor who had approved the assassination. The sources stress the tragic irony: Cinna protested in vain that he was a poet and Caesar's friend. Shakespeare dramatised the scene in Julius Caesar, where the mob cries "Tear him for his bad verses!" — and Cinna replies, "I am Cinna the poet... I am not Cinna the conspirator."

Works

Cinna's Zmyrna told the story of Myrrha (Zmyrna), who fell in love with her own father Cinyras. Catullus, in poem 95, praises it as a work of nine years of labour, destined to be read centuries later "at the distant waves of the Saetabis", while the swollen productions of hack poets would die at home. The poem is lost apart from a couple of lines, but ancient commentators needed glossaries to explain its dense, learned allusions — the essence of the neoteric aesthetic. Cinna also wrote epigrams, a propempticon (farewell poem) for Asinius Pollio, and possibly love poems.
