Economic history

[Image: an old ledger book and brass scales on a merchant's desk, with coins and a quill]

Economic history is the study of history using methodological tools from economics, or with a special attention to economic phenomena — how economies worked in the past, how they grew or failed, and how trade, money, technology, institutions and living standards changed over time. It asks why some societies became rich and others remained poor, and what ordinary people's material lives were actually like.

Economic historians draw on price and wage series, tax and customs records, probate inventories, national accounts reconstructed for past centuries, and the methods of the 'new economic history' (cliometrics), which applies statistics and economic theory to historical questions.

Major themes

Central topics include the Neolithic transition to agriculture, the commercial expansion of the medieval world, the causes and timing of the Industrial Revolution, the Great Divergence between Western Europe and Asia, the spread of globalisation in the nineteenth century, the Great Depression, and the post-1945 'golden age' of growth and its end.

Money and finance have their own long history: coinage, banking in medieval Italy, joint-stock companies, financial crises from tulip mania to 2008, and the gold standard and its successors.

Debates

Classic debates include the standard-of-living controversy during industrialisation, the role of slavery and empire in European growth, the causes of the Great Depression, and competing explanations — geography, institutions, culture, technology — for persistent global inequality.

The field bridges history and economics departments; its Nobel-recognised practitioners include Robert Fogel and Douglass North (1993), honoured for renewing economic history with theory and quantitative methods.
