Plumage

Breeding plumage of an Indian peafowl

Plumage (Latin: pluma, "feather") is the layer of feathers that covers a bird, including their pattern, colour, and arrangement. Differences in plumage between species, sexes, ages, and seasons are central to bird identification and behaviour.

Functions

Plumage provides insulation and waterproofing, streamlines the body for flight, and serves in communication: camouflage in most females and juveniles, and often bright advertisement in courting males – an outcome of sexual selection described by Charles Darwin. Colours arise from pigments (melanins, carotenoids, porphyrins) and from structural effects, including the iridescence of hummingbirds and starlings.

Plumages and moult

Birds replace their feathers in regular moults. Many species have a cryptic non-breeding (basic) plumage and a brighter breeding (alternate) plumage acquired by a partial pre-breeding moult; ducks undergo an "eclipse" plumage while flightless. Young birds pass through downy, juvenile, and immature plumages before attaining adult dress, in large gulls over as many as four years. The Humphrey–Parkes terminology standardises the naming of these plumages and moults.

Abnormalities and variation

Plumage abnormalities include albinism and leucism (loss of pigment), melanism (excess dark pigment), and xanthochromism. Within species, geographic variation in plumage underlies many subspecies, and hybridisation can produce intermediate patterns.
