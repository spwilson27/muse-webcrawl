Peafowl

Peafowl is a common name for two bird species of the genus Pavo and one species of the closely related genus Afropavo within the tribe Pavonini of the family Phasianidae (the pheasants and their allies). Male peafowl are referred to as peacocks, and female peafowl are referred to as peahens, even though both sexes are often referred to colloquially as "peacocks".

Species

The three recognised species are the Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus), native to the Indian subcontinent and the national bird of India; the green peafowl (Pavo muticus) of Southeast Asia, which is endangered; and the Congo peafowl (Afropavo congensis), endemic to the Congo Basin and only described scientifically in 1936.

The peacock's train

The male Indian peafowl is famed for its enormous train of elongated upper-tail coverts, patterned with iridescent eyespots (ocelli), which it fans out and vibrates ("train-rattling") in courtship display. Charles Darwin considered the train a puzzle for natural selection and used it to develop his theory of sexual selection: the ornamental feathers evolve because peahens preferentially mate with males bearing elaborate displays. The train is shed (moulted) each year after the breeding season.

Behaviour and culture

Peafowl are forest and scrub birds that forage on the ground for seeds, insects, and small animals, roosting in trees at night. The Indian peafowl has been kept in captivity for millennia and introduced to parks and estates worldwide; feral populations exist in several countries. Peacocks figure prominently in Hindu mythology — as the mount of Kartikeya and an attribute of Krishna — and in the symbolism of many cultures, where they represent beauty, pride, and immortality.
