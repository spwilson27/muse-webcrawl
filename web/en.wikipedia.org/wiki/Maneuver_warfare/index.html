Maneuver warfare

Maneuver warfare is a military strategy that seeks to defeat an enemy by incapacitating its decision-making and cohesion through movement, surprise and shock, rather than by destroying its forces through attrition. It emphasises tempo, initiative and striking at critical vulnerabilities instead of engaging the enemy's strength head-on.

Concepts

Maneuver warfare contrasts with attrition warfare, which measures success by the destruction of enemy forces and matériel. The maneuver approach aims to render the enemy incapable of effective resistance by dislocating it — attacking command and control, logistics and morale so that its formations become disorganised and unable to react. Key ideas include Boyd's OODA loop (observe–orient–decide–act), decentralised command and mission-type orders (Auftragstaktik), in which subordinates are given intent rather than detailed instructions, and the concentration of force at a decisive point (Schwerpunkt).

History

The philosophy has roots in the campaigns of commanders from Napoleon to the German blitzkrieg operations of the Second World War, in which fast-moving armoured thrusts bypassed and encircled enemy forces. Theorists such as B. H. Liddell Hart, with his "indirect approach", and later John Boyd influenced its formal adoption. The United States Marine Corps made maneuver warfare its official doctrine in the 1989 manual Warfighting (FMFM-1), and similar concepts underlie the US Army's AirLand Battle doctrine.

Practice

Maneuver warfare demands well-trained forces, reliable communications, combined arms coordination and logistics capable of sustaining rapid movement. Critics note that it still requires sufficient combat power, and that maneuver alone cannot succeed where forces must seize and hold ground or where the enemy cannot be dislocated.
