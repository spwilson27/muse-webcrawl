Stereotomy (architecture and construction)

Stereotomy (from the Greek stereos, solid, and tomē, cutting) is the art and science of cutting three-dimensional solids—above all stone—into predetermined shapes and sizes so that they can be assembled into vaults, arches, stairs and other architectural elements. It is the branch of stonemasonry concerned with the geometrical problem of shaping each block (voussoir) of a complex masonry structure so that all joints fit exactly.

History

Stereotomic techniques were highly developed in Gothic and Renaissance stone construction. From the 16th century onward, French treatises systematised the discipline: Philibert de l'Orme's Premier tome de l'architecture (1567) and Girard Desargues' work on stone cutting helped found descriptive geometry, later codified by Gaspard Monge. The trait—the full-scale geometrical drawing from which templates were taken—was a guarded craft secret of master masons and the compagnonnage guilds.

Technique

The stereotomer begins with the geometry of the finished structure (for example a skewed arch, a trumpet vault or a helical stair) and projects each joint surface onto drawings from which the true shape of every face of every block can be derived. Templates of wood, zinc or cardboard transfer these shapes to the rough stone, which is then dressed by hand or, today, by CNC milling.

Modern use

Although largely displaced by concrete and steel, stereotomy survives in restoration of historic masonry and has seen renewed interest in contemporary architecture, where robotic fabrication makes complex dry-stone vaulting feasible again.
