Commune

A commune is an intentional community of people living together, sharing common interests, often having common values and beliefs, as well as shared property, possessions, resources, and in some cases, shared work, income, or assets. In addition to the communal economy, consensus decision-making, non-hierarchical structures, and ecological living have become important core principles for many communes. The word derives from the Latin communis, "common, public."

History

Communal experiments are ancient, from early Christian communities and monastic orders to the Hutterites and other Anabaptist groups. The 19th century saw a wave of utopian communities inspired by thinkers such as Charles Fourier, Robert Owen (New Harmony, Indiana, 1825), and Étienne Cabet, as well as religious communities like the Shakers and the Oneida Community. The 20th century brought the Israeli kibbutz movement, the collectivisation-era communes of China (the people's communes, 1958–1983, a state-imposed rather than voluntary form), and a flowering of communes during the 1960s–70s counterculture in North America and Europe.

Types

  • Religious communes — organised around shared faith (Hutterite colonies, monasteries, Bruderhof).
  • Secular/egalitarian communes — income-sharing communities such as Twin Oaks and East Wind in the United States, members of the Federation of Egalitarian Communities.
  • Countercultural and artistic communes — from Drop City to Christiania in Copenhagen.
  • Therapeutic and service communities — shared living organised around care or rehabilitation.

Characteristics and challenges

Most voluntary communes are small (often under 50 adults), share a common treasury to varying degrees, and make decisions by consensus. Common failure modes include disputes over work contribution, leadership, money, and the departure of founding members; long-lived communes tend to develop clear membership processes, labour systems, and conflict-resolution practices.

Relationship to related forms

Communes overlap with cohousing (where households remain economically independent), ecovillages (emphasising sustainability), and the kibbutz (which historically combined communal property with collective child-rearing). "Commune" is also the name of the smallest administrative division in several countries (France, Italy, Switzerland, Vietnam, and others).
