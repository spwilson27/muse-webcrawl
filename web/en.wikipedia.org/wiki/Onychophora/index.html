Onychophora (from Ancient Greek ὄνυχ-, onych-, "claw" + φέρειν, pherein, "to carry"), commonly known as velvet worms or more ambiguously as peripatus, is a phylum of elongate, soft-bodied, many-legged animals. In appearance, velvet worms have variously been compared to worms with legs, caterpillars, and slugs. They prey upon other invertebrates, which they catch by ejecting an adhesive slime from glands near their mouths.

Onychophorans are exclusively terrestrial and live in humid environments across the tropics and the temperate Southern Hemisphere. Together with the tardigrades and arthropods, they form the clade Panarthropoda.

Anatomy

Velvet worms are segmented animals with cylindrical, flattened bodies ranging from about 0.5 to 20 cm in length. They bear between 13 and 43 pairs of stubby, unjointed legs called lobopods, each tipped with a pair of claws. Their skin is covered in fine papillae giving a velvety texture, and they must keep it moist to avoid desiccation. They breathe through numerous small tracheal openings that cannot be closed, which restricts them to humid habitats. The head bears a pair of antennae, simple eyes, and jaws; a pair of specialised appendages, the oral papillae, house the openings of the slime glands.

Slime

To capture prey and defend themselves, velvet worms squirt a rapidly hardening adhesive slime from their oral papillae, reaching targets up to tens of centimetres away. The slime is a protein-based fluid that forms stiff nanofibres when drawn out by the prey's struggles, entangling it. Velvet worms then feed by injecting digestive saliva and sucking up the liquefied tissues.

Reproduction

Reproductive modes vary widely: most species are placental viviparous, nourishing embryos in a uterus, while others lay eggs or are ovoviviparous. Males deposit spermatophores on the female's body, and in some species the female stores sperm for extended periods. Some Australian species engage in elaborate courtship, and males of Euperipatoides place their sperm packets on the female's head region.

Evolution and fossils

Fossil onychophoran-like animals, the lobopodians, are known from the Cambrian period, including forms such as Aysheaia and the armoured Hallucigenia. The modern crown group appears to have evolved on land and has remained morphologically conservative. Molecular and morphological evidence places Onychophora as the sister group of arthropods within Panarthropoda, making velvet worms important for understanding the origin of arthropod limbs, segmentation, and terrestrialisation.

Conservation

Many velvet worm species have tiny distributions and are vulnerable to habitat destruction; several are listed as endangered or critically endangered on the IUCN Red List. Because they cannot tolerate drying, deforestation and climate change pose particular threats.
