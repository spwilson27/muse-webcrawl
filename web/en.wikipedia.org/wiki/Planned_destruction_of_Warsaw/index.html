Planned destruction of Warsaw

The planned destruction of Warsaw refers to Nazi Germany's campaign to systematically raze the Polish capital during the Second World War, culminating after the suppression of the Warsaw Uprising in 1944. The destruction was not merely a by-product of fighting: it implemented long-standing plans, dating from before the war, to demolish Warsaw and replace it with a small German provincial town, erasing the city as a centre of Polish culture and national identity.

Background: the Pabst Plan

Before the invasion of Poland, German planners had sketched schemes for the "Germanisation" of conquered territories. The so-called Pabst Plan, drawn up under chief architect Friedrich Pabst, envisaged demolishing most of Warsaw, deporting or exterminating its Polish population, and building a new German town (the "New German City of Warsaw") for some 130,000 inhabitants on a fraction of the site. The historic Old Town was to be reduced and Germanised, and a monumental party hall raised over the Vistula escarpment.

Destruction during the occupation

Warsaw suffered heavy damage in the siege of September 1939 and in air raids, but the most thorough destruction came later. After the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943, German forces levelled the entire ghetto district. Following the failure of the Warsaw Uprising of August–October 1944, Adolf Hitler ordered that the city be completely destroyed. SS and police units under commanders including Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski and Heinz Reinefarth, together with special demolition detachments (Brandkommandos and Sprengkommandos), went street by street with flamethrowers and explosives, burning libraries, archives, museums, churches, palaces, and ordinary apartment blocks.

Extent of the damage

By January 1945, when the Red Army entered the city, an estimated 85% of Warsaw's buildings had been destroyed — including some 25% lost in 1939 and the ghetto fighting, roughly 35% during the Uprising, and a further share in the deliberate demolition of October 1944 to January 1945. Cultural losses were catastrophic: the Royal Castle, the National Library's collections, and hundreds of historic monuments were burned or blown up.

Aftermath

After the war, despite the scale of destruction, the Polish government decided to rebuild the capital. The Old Town was reconstructed in meticulous detail, aided by pre-war architectural records and the vedute paintings of Bernardo Bellotto, and was later inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as an outstanding example of near-total reconstruction of a historic urban ensemble.
