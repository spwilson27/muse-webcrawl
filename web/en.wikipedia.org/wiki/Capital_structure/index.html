Capital structure

Capital structure describes the mix of debt and equity a firm uses to finance its operations.

In corporate finance, capital structure is the particular combination of debt and equity used by a company to finance its overall operations and growth. Debt takes the form of bonds, loans and other obligations that must be repaid, while equity consists of common stock, preferred stock and retained earnings. The choice of capital structure affects a firm's cost of capital, risk and value.

Modigliani–Miller theorem

In 1958 Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller showed that, under idealized assumptions (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, perfect information), a firm's value is independent of its capital structure. Relaxing those assumptions changes the conclusion: because interest payments are usually tax-deductible, debt can add value through a tax shield, but expected costs of financial distress rise with leverage, implying an optimal trade-off.

Theories

The trade-off theory holds that firms balance the tax benefits of debt against the costs of possible bankruptcy. The pecking order theory proposes that, because managers know more than outside investors, firms prefer internal funds first, then debt, and issue equity only as a last resort. Agency theory examines conflicts between shareholders, managers and creditors that financing choices can create or mitigate.

Measurement

Leverage is commonly measured by debt-to-equity or debt-to-assets ratios. Rating agencies and lenders monitor capital structure closely, and loan agreements often impose covenants limiting additional borrowing. In bankruptcy, the priority of claims follows a hierarchy: secured creditors first, then unsecured creditors, preferred shareholders, and finally common shareholders.
