Fourth Aliyah

The Fourth Aliyah refers to the fourth wave of Jewish immigration (aliyah) to Mandatory Palestine, between 1924 and 1928 (some date it to 1929). Roughly 80,000–100,000 immigrants arrived during this period, considerably more than in the preceding Third Aliyah, and the character of immigration changed markedly.

Composition and causes

Unlike the earlier, largely socialist-pioneer waves, the Fourth Aliyah was dominated by middle-class families from Poland and other parts of Eastern Europe, many of them merchants and shopkeepers bringing modest capital. Two push factors were decisive: the economic policies of Polish finance minister Władysław Grabski, whose taxes and restrictions squeezed Jewish businesses (leading the wave to be nicknamed the "Grabski Aliyah"), and the United States Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and Immigration Act of 1924, which sharply closed American doors to Eastern European immigrants.

Impact

The newcomers settled mainly in the cities, especially Tel Aviv, whose population roughly doubled, and they stimulated private commerce, small industry, construction, and banking. An economic boom in 1925 gave way to a severe crisis in 1926–27, with unemployment and a substantial counter-stream of emigration, before recovery toward the end of the decade. The wave consolidated the urban, bourgeois sector of the Yishuv alongside the labour movement that had dominated earlier immigration.
