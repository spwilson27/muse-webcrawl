Earth's core

Earth's core is the very hot, very dense centre of our planet. The ball-shaped core lies beneath the cool, brittle crust and the mostly solid mantle. It is composed primarily of an iron–nickel alloy and is divided into a liquid outer core, about 2,260 km thick, and a solid inner core with a radius of about 1,220 km. The core-mantle boundary lies roughly 2,890 km below the surface.

Properties and formation

Temperatures at the inner-core boundary reach about 6,000 °C, comparable to the surface of the Sun, yet extreme pressure keeps the inner core solid. The core formed early in Earth's history, about 4.5 billion years ago, as dense iron and nickel sank during planetary differentiation. Seismology is the principal tool for studying the core: S-waves do not pass through the liquid outer core, and P-wave velocities and reflections reveal the inner-core boundary, discovered by Inge Lehmann in 1936.

Geodynamo

Convection in the liquid outer core, powered by cooling of the Earth and the gradual growth (freezing) of the inner core, sustains the geodynamo that generates Earth's magnetic field. The solid inner core is thought to rotate slightly relative to the mantle, and its growth releases light elements that help drive the convection.
