Chardonnay (pronounced [ʃaʁdɔnɛ]) is a green-skinned grape variety used in the production of white wine. The variety originated in the Burgundy wine region of eastern France, but is now grown wherever wine is produced, from England to New Zealand. For new and developing wine regions, growing Chardonnay is seen as a "rite of passage" and an easy entry into the international wine market.

The Chardonnay grape itself is neutral, with many of the flavors commonly associated with the wine being derived from such influences as terroir and oak. It is vinified in many different styles, from the lean, crisply mineral wines of Chablis, France, to the New World wines with oak and tropical fruit flavors. In cool climates (such as Chablis and the Côte de Beaune), Chardonnay wine tends to be medium to light in body with noticeable acidity and flavors of green plum, apple, and pear. In warmer locations, the flavors become more citrus, peach, and melon, while in very warm locations more fig and tropical fruit notes such as banana and mango come out.

History

For a long time the relationship of Chardonnay to Pinot noir and Pinot blanc was unknown to ampelographers. In 1999, DNA fingerprinting research at the University of California, Davis showed that Chardonnay was the result of a cross between Pinot noir and Gouais blanc.

Viticulture

Chardonnay is an early budding vine, which makes it susceptible to spring frosts. It is a vigorous, productive variety that ripens relatively early and adapts to a wide range of soils and climates, though it performs best on limestone, chalk, and marl soils such as those found in Burgundy.

Wine regions

By 2002, there were over 344,000 acres (140,000 hectares) of Chardonnay planted worldwide. In France, most Chardonnay is grown in Burgundy, where it produces the white wines of Chablis, the Côte de Beaune, and Mâconnais, and in Champagne, where it is one of the three principal grape varieties used in the sparkling wine.
