Cuttlebone

Cuttlebone, also known as cuttlefish bone, is a hard, brittle internal structure (an internal shell) found in all members of the family Sepiidae, commonly known as cuttlefish, within the cephalopods. In other cephalopod families it is called a gladius.

Cuttlebone is primarily composed of aragonite (a form of calcium carbonate). It is a chambered, gas-filled shell used for buoyancy control. Its microscopic structure consists of numerous layers of material with many small chambers. The cuttlebone provides the cuttlefish with rigidity and a degree of protection as well as buoyancy.

Structure and buoyancy

Unlike the shell of the nautilus, the cuttlebone is wholly internal. It consists of two layers: an outer shield and an inner array of chambered lamellae supported by pillars. The animal adjusts the ratio of gas to liquid within the chambers by osmosis, allowing neutral buoyancy at different depths. Because the cuttlebone is gas-filled, it imposes a depth limit: the common cuttlefish's cuttlebone implodes at a depth of around 200 metres, and the animal generally lives above this.

Human uses

In the past, cuttlebones were ground up to make polishing powder used by goldsmiths, and added to toothpaste and antacids. Today, cuttlebones are commonly used in the pet trade as a calcium supplement and beak conditioner for caged birds, and as food for pet snails, hermit crabs, reptiles and some invertebrates. They are also used as moulds by jewellers and silversmiths: the soft, heat-resistant material can be carved, and molten metal poured into the two halves of a cuttlebone mould reproduces fine detail. Cuttlebones wash up on beaches in large numbers after storms and are collected commercially in some regions.
