Beer in Belgium includes some of the most distinctive and varied beers in the world. Belgium produces a vast range of styles from a relatively small brewing industry, and Belgian beer culture was added to UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2016.

History

Brewing in the region dates to the Middle Ages, when monasteries refined recipes and sold beer to travellers. Guilds of brewers formed in cities such as Bruges, Leuven and Brussels. The 19th and 20th centuries saw consolidation, the dominance of pilsner-style lagers such as Stella Artois and Jupiler, and the survival — later revival — of traditional speciality styles. Interbrew, formed in Leuven, grew by merger into today's AB InBev, the world's largest brewer, while hundreds of small and farmhouse breweries continued or were founded.

Styles

  • Lambic — spontaneously fermented wheat beer from the Pajottenland and Zenne valley, including gueuze (blended young and old lambics) and kriek (with cherries).
  • Trappist beer — brewed within Cistercian monasteries; Belgian examples include Westmalle, Chimay, Orval, Rochefort, Westvleteren and Achel.
  • Abbey beer — abbey-style ales brewed commercially under licence or in the monastic tradition, e.g. Leffe and Grimbergen.
  • Saison — dry, highly carbonated farmhouse ale from Wallonia.
  • Flemish red and oud bruin — sour, oak-aged ales of West and East Flanders.
  • Witbier — spiced wheat beer flavoured with coriander and orange peel, e.g. Hoegaarden.
  • Strong golden ale and tripel — pale, strong ales such as Duvel and Westmalle Tripel.

Culture

Beer is served in cafés in its own branded glass, often with elaborate pouring rituals. Beer festivals, such as the Zythos Beer Festival, and specialist bars offering hundreds of varieties sustain a connoisseur culture. Beer is also used in cooking, notably in stoofvlees (Flemish beef stew).
