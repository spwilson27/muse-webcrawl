Dislocation

In materials science, a dislocation or Taylor's dislocation is a linear crystallographic defect or irregularity within a crystal structure that contains an abrupt change in the arrangement of atoms. The movement of dislocations allow atoms to slide over each other at low stress levels and is known as glide or slip. The crystalline order is restored on either side of a glide dislocation but the atoms on one side have moved by one position. The crystalline order is not fully restored with a partial dislocation.

History

The theory describing the elastic fields of the defects was originally developed by Vito Volterra in 1907. In 1934, Egon Orowan, Michael Polanyi and G. I. Taylor, almost simultaneously, proposed that the low stresses observed to produce plastic deformation could be explained in terms of the theory of dislocations. Dislocations were first directly observed in crystals by transmission electron microscopy in the 1950s.

Mechanisms

Edge dislocations

An edge dislocation can be pictured as the termination of a half-plane of atoms in the middle of a crystal. The surrounding planes bend around the termination. Edge dislocations move in response to shear stress applied perpendicular to the line.

Screw dislocations

A screw dislocation can be pictured as a spiral ramp of atomic planes wound around the dislocation line. It moves in response to shear stress applied parallel to the line and converts one set of planes into a helicoid.

Mixed dislocations

Most dislocations found in real materials are "mixed", meaning they have aspects of both edge and screw character along a curved dislocation line.

Burgers vector

The magnitude and direction of the lattice distortion caused by a dislocation is expressed in terms of a Burgers vector, b, obtained by making a circuit around the dislocation line (the Burgers circuit) and recording the closure failure. For an edge dislocation the Burgers vector is perpendicular to the line direction; for a screw dislocation it is parallel.

Dislocation motion

Dislocations can move if the atoms from one of the surrounding planes break their bonds and rebond with the atoms at the terminating edge. Motion by glide occurs on the slip plane containing both the dislocation line and the Burgers vector; motion perpendicular to that plane, called climb, requires diffusion of atoms or vacancies and is important in high-temperature creep.

Role in plasticity and hardening

Plastic deformation of crystalline materials occurs primarily by dislocation motion. As dislocations multiply (for example by Frank–Read sources) and interact, they entangle and impede one another, raising the stress needed for further deformation — the phenomenon of work hardening. Obstacles such as grain boundaries, precipitates, and solute atoms are exploited in alloy design to strengthen metals by hindering dislocation motion.
