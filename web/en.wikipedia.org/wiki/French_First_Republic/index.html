The French First Republic (French: Première République française) was founded on 21 September 1792 during the French Revolution, when the newly elected National Convention abolished the monarchy. It lasted until the declaration of the First French Empire on 18 May 1804, when Napoleon Bonaparte was proclaimed Emperor.

National Convention (1792–1795)

The Republic was proclaimed the day after the French victory at Valmy. The Convention put Louis XVI on trial and executed him on 21 January 1793. Facing foreign invasion, civil war in the Vendée and economic crisis, the Convention governed through the Committee of Public Safety, dominated from mid-1793 by Maximilien Robespierre. Under the emergency measures known as the Reign of Terror, thousands were guillotined as enemies of the Revolution. The revolutionary government also introduced the levée en masse, the metric system and the French Republican calendar. The fall of Robespierre in the Thermidorian Reaction (July 1794) ended the Terror.

The Directory (1795–1799)

The Constitution of 1795 created a five-member Directory as executive, with a bicameral legislature. The regime was plagued by corruption, inflation, royalist and Jacobin conspiracies — suppressed in turn with military help — and coups d'état (Fructidor 1797, Floreal 1798, Prairial 1799). Abroad, republican armies carried the war into the Low Countries, Germany, Switzerland and Italy, where Napoleon Bonaparte's campaigns made him a national hero.

The Consulate (1799–1804)

Napoleon's coup of 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799) replaced the Directory with the Consulate, in which Napoleon as First Consul held effective power. The Consulate produced lasting reforms — the Bank of France, the prefectoral system, the Concordat of 1801 with the Church, and the Civil Code (Code Napoléon) of 1804 — while steadily concentrating authority. In 1802 Napoleon was made Consul for life; in 1804 the Senate proclaimed him Emperor, ending the Republic formally, though coins continued to bear the name of the "République française" for some years after.

Legacy

The First Republic established enduring symbols of French republicanism — the tricolour, the Marseillaise, and the motto Liberté, égalité, fraternité — and served as the model invoked by the later Second, Third, Fourth and Fifth Republics.
