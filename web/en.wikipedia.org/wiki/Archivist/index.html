Archivist

An archivist is an information professional who assesses, collects, organizes, preserves, maintains control over, and provides access to records and archives that have long-term value. Archivists keep records that have enduring significance — for their administrative, legal, fiscal, or historical information — including documents, photographs, maps, audio-visual materials, and born-digital files. The archivist's work underpins accountability, scholarship, and the preservation of cultural memory.

Duties

Core duties include appraisal (deciding which records merit permanent retention), acquisition and accessioning, arrangement and description according to principles such as provenance and original order, preservation (controlling storage environments and conserving damaged items), and reference and outreach to researchers and the public. Modern practice is increasingly concerned with electronic records, digital preservation, metadata standards such as ISAD(G) and EAD, and managing access restrictions arising from privacy and data-protection law.

History of the profession

Keepers of records have existed since ancient states first stored clay tablets and papyri, but the modern profession emerged in the nineteenth century with the opening of national archives to researchers and the formulation of the principle of provenance by French and Prussian archivists. Landmark manuals by Muller, Feith, and Fruin (1898) and by Sir Hilary Jenkinson (1922) codified archival theory, and the twentieth century saw the founding of national archives and professional associations such as the Society of American Archivists (1936).

Education and settings

Archivists typically hold a graduate degree in history, library and information science, or archival studies, and may be certified by bodies such as the Academy of Certified Archivists. They work in government archives, universities, corporations, religious organizations, museums, and community archives.
