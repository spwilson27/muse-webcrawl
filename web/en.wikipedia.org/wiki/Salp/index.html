A salp (plural salps) is a planktonic tunicate of the order Salpida. Salps are barrel-shaped, gelatinous, and nearly transparent marine animals that swim by rhythmic contractions of their muscular walls, pumping water through their bodies — a form of jet propulsion among the most efficient in the animal kingdom. Despite their jellyfish-like appearance they are chordates, more closely related to vertebrates than to jellyfish.

Anatomy and feeding

The salp body is a hollow barrel of translucent tunic, open at both ends, with bands of muscle encircling it. As water is pumped through the body it passes an internal mucous net on which phytoplankton and other particles are trapped and conveyed to the gut. Salps can strain particles as small as bacteria, making them unusually effective grazers; they continuously replace their feeding nets, discarding clogged ones. Individuals range from a few millimetres to more than 10 cm in length, with some chains of joined individuals extending several metres.

Life cycle

Salps have a complex two-part life cycle with an obligatory alternation of generations. The solitary, asexual oozoid buds off a stolon that develops into chains of dozens to hundreds of blastozooids. Blastozooids are sequential hermaphrodites, starting female and later becoming male; each fertilised egg develops within a parent until released as a solitary oozoid. Under favourable conditions this cycle produces explosive population growth: salps can form enormous swarms covering thousands of square kilometres, sometimes outcompeting krill and copepods.

Ecological role

Salps are increasingly studied for their role in the biological pump. Their dense faecal pellets and the carcasses of dead salps sink rapidly — sometimes hundreds of metres per day — exporting carbon from surface waters to the deep ocean. In the Southern Ocean, shifts between krill-dominated and salp-dominated years (notably involving Salpa thompsoni) alter food availability for penguins, seals, and whales, and the increasing abundance of salps in some regions has been linked to warming waters and changes in sea ice.
