Tropicália, also known as Tropicalismo, was a Brazilian artistic movement that arose in the late 1960s and encompassed music, poetry, theatre, cinema and the visual arts. It merged Brazilian popular and folk traditions with foreign influences — rock and roll, psychedelia, concrete poetry and avant-garde art — in a deliberately irreverent style that both celebrated and satirised Brazilian culture under the military dictatorship.

Origins

The movement took its name from Tropicália (1967), an installation by the artist Hélio Oiticica exhibited at the Museum of Modern Art in Rio de Janeiro, and from the song "Tropicália" by Caetano Veloso. Its ideas drew on Oswald de Andrade's 1928 Manifesto Antropófago (Cannibalist Manifesto), which proposed that Brazilian culture should "devour" foreign influences and transform them into something new.

The musical movement crystallised at the 1967 Festival of Brazilian Popular Music, where Veloso performed "Alegria, Alegria" and Gilberto Gil performed "Domingo no Parque" (with the rock band Os Mutantes), both accompanied controversially by electric guitars. In 1968 the participants released the collective manifesto album Tropicália: ou Panis et Circencis, featuring Veloso, Gil, Gal Costa, Tom Zé, Nara Leão, Os Mutantes and the arranger Rogério Duprat.

Repression and exile

The tropicalists' provocative performances and coded criticism of the regime made them targets after the dictatorship hardened with Institutional Act No. 5 in December 1968. Veloso and Gil were arrested, imprisoned, and in 1969 sent into exile in London, where they remained until 1972 — effectively ending the movement's core phase, though its members continued to shape Brazilian music for decades.

Legacy

Tropicália profoundly influenced Música popular brasileira (MPB) and later Brazilian rock, and has been cited as an influence by international artists including David Byrne, Beck, and the members of Talking Heads and Animal Collective. The movement has been the subject of major exhibitions and retrospectives in Brazil, Europe and the United States.
