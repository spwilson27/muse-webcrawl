Closed-circuit television

Closed-circuit television (CCTV), also known as video surveillance, is the use of video cameras to transmit a signal to a specific place, on a limited set of monitors. It differs from broadcast television in that the signal is not openly transmitted. CCTV is used for surveillance of areas such as banks, shops, airports, military installations, streets, and private homes, for traffic monitoring, industrial process observation, and security.

History

The first CCTV system was installed by Siemens AG at Test Stand VII in Peenemünde, Germany, in 1942, to observe the launch of V-2 rockets. In the United States, the first commercial system, Vericon, was marketed from 1949. CCTV spread through banks and shops in the 1960s and 1970s, and from the 1990s digital video recorders and later networked IP cameras replaced analogue tape systems, adding motion detection, remote viewing, and automated video analytics such as face and number-plate recognition.

Uses and prevalence

CCTV is used in crime prevention and detection, traffic enforcement, crowd management at events, monitoring of industrial processes too dangerous for constant human presence, and in retail to deter theft. Some countries have exceptionally high camera densities: the United Kingdom became known for extensive public-space CCTV from the 1990s onward, and Chinese cities operate some of the world's largest surveillance networks, with estimates of hundreds of millions of cameras nationwide.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about privacy, function creep, the cost-effectiveness of cameras in preventing crime (evidence is strongest for car parks and targeted schemes), and the chilling effects of mass surveillance and automated face recognition. Supporters point to successes in identifying suspects, locating missing persons, and providing evidence after offences. Regulation varies widely: many jurisdictions impose signage, retention, and data-protection requirements on operators under laws such as the EU's GDPR.
