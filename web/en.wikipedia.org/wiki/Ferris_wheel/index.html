A Ferris wheel (also called a big wheel, observation wheel, or, in the context of the very highest examples, a giant wheel) is an amusement ride consisting of a rotating upright wheel with multiple passenger-carrying components (commonly referred to as passenger cars, cabins, capsules, pods, tubs or gondolas) attached to the rim in such a way that as the wheel turns, they are kept upright, usually by gravity. Some of the largest modern Ferris wheels have cars mounted on the outside of the rim and electric motors that independently rotate each car to keep it upright.

History

Early "pleasure wheels" with wooden frames and hand-turned rotation appeared at fairs in the 17th and 18th centuries. The modern form was designed and constructed by George Washington Gale Ferris Jr., a Pittsburgh bridge engineer, for the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893, where it was intended to rival the Eiffel Tower of the Paris exposition of 1889. The original Ferris Wheel stood 80.4 metres (264 ft) tall, carried 36 cars each holding up to 60 passengers, and could carry more than 2,000 people at once. It was dismantled, rebuilt in St. Louis for the 1904 Louisiana Purchase Exposition, and dynamited in 1906.

Giant observation wheels

From the late 20th century, Ferris wheels evolved into landmark observation attractions: the Wiener Riesenrad in Vienna (1897, 64.75 m) is one of the oldest surviving; the London Eye (2000, 135 m) popularised the cantilevered observation wheel with enclosed capsules; the Singapore Flyer (2008, 165 m) and the High Roller in Las Vegas (2014, 167.6 m) followed; and Ain Dubai (2021, 250 m) became the tallest of all. Most giant wheels rotate slowly and continuously, completing one revolution in about 20–40 minutes, with passengers boarding moving capsules.

Design

The wheel is supported by towers or a single cantilevered support, driven by motors acting on the rim or by friction wheels, and the cabins hang from the rim so that gravity keeps them level — a principle unchanged since 1893, though spokes, rims, and bearings are now engineered like bridge structures. Smaller travelling wheels remain a fixture of fairgrounds worldwide.
