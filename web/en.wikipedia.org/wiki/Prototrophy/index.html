Prototrophy

Prototrophy is a term used in microbiology and genetics to describe an organism that has the same nutritional requirements as the wild type from which it was derived. A prototroph can synthesise all of the organic compounds it needs for growth — amino acids, nucleotides, vitamins and other metabolites — from a minimal medium containing only a carbon source and inorganic salts.

Prototrophy is defined in contrast to auxotrophy. An auxotroph carries a mutation that disables a biosynthetic pathway, so it can only grow when the missing nutrient is supplied in the medium. Auxotrophic mutants are widely used as genetic markers in laboratory strains of bacteria and yeast.

Terminology

The words derive from the Greek protos ("first") and trophe ("nourishment"): a prototroph is "nourished in the first way", i.e. like the original wild type. The terms were introduced into microbial genetics in the mid-twentieth century, as researchers such as Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum began isolating nutritional mutants of Escherichia coli and Neurospora crassa to study gene function.

Detection

Prototrophs can be distinguished from auxotrophs by replica plating: colonies are transferred from a rich medium to a minimal medium, and only prototrophs (or revertants) continue to grow. Restoration of prototrophy — for example by reversion of a mutation or by horizontal gene transfer — is a common selectable outcome in genetic crosses, because prototrophs can be recovered simply by plating on minimal medium.
