Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States

Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States, 221 U.S. 1 (1911), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States in which the Court found Standard Oil to be an illegal monopoly under the Sherman Antitrust Act and ordered its dissolution into 34 separate companies. The decision also established the rule of reason as the governing standard for interpreting the Act: only unreasonable restraints of trade are prohibited.

Background

John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, organised as a trust in 1882 and reincorporated as a New Jersey holding company in 1899, controlled the overwhelming majority of American oil refining and transportation. Muckraking journalism, notably Ida Tarbell's The History of the Standard Oil Company (1904), documented its secret railroad rebates, predatory pricing and espionage. The federal government filed suit in 1906 under President Theodore Roosevelt. A federal circuit court ruled against Standard Oil in 1909, and the company appealed.

Decision

On May 15, 1911, Chief Justice Edward Douglass White delivered the opinion of the Court. The Court held that the Sherman Act, read in light of the common law, condemns contracts and combinations that unreasonably restrain trade — the "rule of reason." Applying that standard, the Court catalogued Standard Oil's history of coercion and concluded that its power and conduct manifested an intent to monopolise. The combination was ordered dissolved within six months.

Justice John Marshall Harlan concurred in the result but sharply dissented from the rule-of-reason reasoning, warning that the Court was amending the statute by judicial legislation.

Aftermath

Standard Oil was broken into 34 companies, among them Jersey Standard (later Exxon), Socony (later Mobil), Standard Oil of California (Chevron), Standard Oil of Indiana (Amoco) and Continental Oil (Conoco). Paradoxically, the dissolution vastly increased Rockefeller's fortune, since he held shares in all the successor companies, whose combined value rose sharply. The rule of reason announced in the case remains the default framework of American antitrust law, applied in cases from professional sports to modern technology platforms.
