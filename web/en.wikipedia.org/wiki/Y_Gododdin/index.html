Y Gododdin (Welsh: [ə ɡɔˈdɔðɪn]) is a medieval Welsh poem consisting of a series of elegies to the men of the Brittonic kingdom of Gododdin and their allies who fell at the Battle of Catraeth, conventionally dated to around AD 600. It is traditionally attributed to the bard Aneirin and survives in the late 13th-century manuscript known as the Book of Aneirin. It is one of the most famous and controversial works of early Welsh literature, and possibly the earliest surviving literary work from Scotland.

Content

The poem laments a warband of some 300 picked warriors, feasted for a year at Din Eidyn (Edinburgh) by Mynyddog Mwynfawr, king of Gododdin, before riding south to attack Catraeth — usually identified with Catterick in Yorkshire — held by the Anglian kingdoms of Deira and Bernicia. Nearly all were killed. The stanzas praise individual heroes' valour in formulaic but vivid language. One stanza mentions a warrior fighting "though he was not Arthur", often cited as the earliest reference to King Arthur, though its authenticity as part of the original poem is debated.

Transmission and debate

The Book of Aneirin contains two versions of the text in different hands, copied around 1265. Scholars debate how much of the poem, transmitted orally and in lost manuscripts for centuries, genuinely dates from around 600: the text contains archaic features consistent with an early origin, but also later modernisation of spelling and language. Kenneth Jackson, Ifor Williams and John Koch have produced influential editions and reconstructions; Koch argued the poem could substantially date to the late 6th century.

Legacy

Y Gododdin stands at the head of the Welsh heroic tradition and remains a touchstone of Welsh and Scottish national literature, commemorating the Brittonic "Old North" (Yr Hen Ogledd).
