Rapid eye movement sleep

Rapid eye movement sleep
Also calledREM sleep, paradoxical sleep, desynchronized sleep
Share of adult sleep~20–25%
Key featuresRapid eye movements, vivid dreaming, muscle atonia, low-voltage mixed-frequency EEG
Discovered1953, by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman

Rapid eye movement sleep (REM sleep, or REMS) is a unique phase of sleep in mammals and birds, characterised by random rapid movement of the eyes, low muscle tone throughout the body, and a propensity to dream vividly. Because the brain's activity resembles wakefulness while the body is paralysed, it is also called paradoxical sleep.

Physiology

During REM sleep the EEG shows low-amplitude, mixed-frequency activity similar to wakefulness, with bursts of eye movements, irregular heart and breathing rates, and muscle atonia — near-total paralysis mediated by the pons, which prevents the sleeper from acting out dreams. PGO waves and hippocampal theta rhythm are also prominent.

Sleep cycles

Humans cycle between non-REM and REM sleep roughly every 90 minutes; REM periods lengthen across the night, with the first period only minutes long and the last up to an hour. Adults spend about 20–25% of sleep in REM; infants spend far more.

Function

Proposed functions include consolidation of procedural and emotional memory, brain development in infancy, and processing of emotional experiences. REM deprivation leads to a rebound increase in REM sleep.

Disorders

Narcolepsy involves intrusions of REM phenomena into wakefulness; REM sleep behavior disorder features loss of atonia and acting out of dreams, and is strongly associated with later Parkinson's disease. SSRIs suppress REM sleep.
