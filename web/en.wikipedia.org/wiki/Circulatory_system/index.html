Circulatory system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The circulatory system (cardiovascular system) is the organ system that circulates blood through the body, delivering oxygen, nutrients and hormones to cells and removing carbon dioxide and waste. It consists of the heart and the blood vessels: arteries, which carry blood away from the heart; veins, which return it; and capillaries, the microscopic networks where exchange with tissues happens.

In humans and other vertebrates the system is closed and double: a pulmonary circuit to the lungs and a systemic circuit to the body, each driven by one side of the heart. William Harvey demonstrated the circulation experimentally in 1628 (De Motu Cordis), overturning the Galenic model that had stood for over a millennium. The lymphatic system, which returns tissue fluid to the blood and houses much of the Immune system, is closely associated.

Blood pressure and flow

The heart's pumping generates blood pressure, measured as systolic over diastolic (a typical healthy reading near 120/80 mmHg). Vessel diameter, blood volume and vessel elasticity regulate pressure and distribution; persistently high pressure (hypertension) damages arteries and organs and is a major risk factor for stroke and heart disease.

Disorders

Atherosclerosis — fatty plaques in artery walls — underlies most heart attacks and many strokes. Other disorders include aneurysms, varicose veins, deep-vein thrombosis and congenital malformations of the heart and great vessels.
