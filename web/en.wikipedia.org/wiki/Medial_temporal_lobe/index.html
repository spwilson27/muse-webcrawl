Medial temporal lobe


Medial surface of the temporal lobe

The medial temporal lobe (MTL) is a region of the cerebral cortex on the medial surface of the temporal lobe. It comprises the hippocampal formation — including the hippocampus proper, dentate gyrus, and subiculum — together with the surrounding entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices, and (in many usages) the amygdala. The MTL is essential for the formation and consolidation of declarative memory.

Function and memory

The importance of the MTL was established by the case of Henry Molaison (patient H.M.), who in 1953 underwent bilateral medial temporal lobe resection for epilepsy and afterwards could not form new long-term declarative memories, while retaining working memory and procedural learning. Subsequent research distinguishes roles within the system: the hippocampus supports episodic memory and spatial navigation (including place cells), the perirhinal cortex supports familiarity judgements, and the parahippocampal cortex processes spatial scenes and context.

Clinical significance

The entorhinal cortex and hippocampus are among the first structures damaged in Alzheimer's disease, producing its characteristic early memory loss. Medial temporal structures are also implicated in temporal lobe epilepsy, herpes simplex encephalitis, and Korsakoff syndrome. Atrophy of the MTL can be assessed on MRI and is used as a biomarker in dementia diagnosis.
