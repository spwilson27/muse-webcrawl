Triangulum is a small constellation in the northern sky. Its name is Latin for "triangle", derived from its three brightest stars, which form a long and narrow triangle. Known to the ancient Babylonians and Greeks, it was one of the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy in the Almagest, and remains one of the 88 modern constellations. The ancient Greeks sometimes called it Deltoton, from the shape of the Greek letter delta.

Stars

The constellation's brightest stars are Beta Trianguli (magnitude 3.00), a white subgiant about 127 light-years away, and Alpha Trianguli (magnitude 3.42), whose traditional name, Caput Trianguli, means "head of the triangle". Gamma Trianguli completes the triangle's figure. Triangulum also contains notable double stars such as Iota Trianguli, popular with amateur astronomers for its contrasting colours.

Deep-sky objects

Triangulum is best known for the Triangulum Galaxy (Messier 33), a spiral galaxy about 2.7 million light-years away and the third-largest member of the Local Group after Andromeda and the Milky Way. Under exceptionally dark skies it can be glimpsed with the naked eye, making it one of the most distant objects visible without a telescope. The constellation also hosts the bright emission nebula NGC 604 within M33—one of the largest known H II regions—and several fainter galaxies including NGC 672 and IC 1727.

Mythology

The Greeks associated the triangle variously with the island of Sicily (Trinacria, the "three-cornered" island), sacred to Demeter, and with the Nile Delta. In Babylonian star catalogues, the stars were linked with the Plough. A separate, obsolete constellation, Triangulum Minus, was created by Hevelius from stars of Triangulum but is no longer recognised; the southern counterpart is Triangulum Australe.
