Lawrence textile strike

The Lawrence textile strike, also known as the Bread and Roses strike, was a strike of immigrant workers in Lawrence, Massachusetts, in 1912 led by the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW). Triggered by a reduction in pay accompanying a reduction in working hours, the strike spread rapidly through the city's textile mills, uniting workers of more than 40 nationalities.

Background

Lawrence was a major center of the woolen textile industry, dominated by the American Woolen Company. Most mill workers were recent immigrants—Italians, Poles, Lithuanians, Franco-Belgians, Syrians, and others—who worked long hours for low wages in dangerous conditions. When a new Massachusetts law reduced the working week for women and children from 56 to 54 hours, the mill owners cut wages accordingly, sparking the walkout on January 12, 1912.

The strike

The IWW sent organizers Joseph Ettor and Arturo Giovannitti to lead the strike. Some 20,000–25,000 workers struck. The strikers pioneered tactics such as the moving picket line and mass singing. When authorities tried to prevent strikers' children from being sent to supporters in other cities—the famous "children's exodus"—police violence at the train station drew national outrage and a congressional investigation.

Outcome

On March 12, 1912, the mill owners conceded a 15 percent wage increase, overtime pay, and no discrimination against strikers. The victory demonstrated that immigrant, unskilled workers—long considered unorganizable—could win strikes, and it became a landmark in American labor history. The slogan "Bread and Roses," associated with the strike, symbolized workers' demands for both fair wages and dignity.
