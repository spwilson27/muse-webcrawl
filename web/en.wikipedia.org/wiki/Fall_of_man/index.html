The fall of man, the fall of Adam, or simply the Fall, is a term used in Christianity to describe the transition of the first man and woman from a state of innocent obedience to God to a state of guilty disobedience. The doctrine of the Fall comes from a biblical interpretation of Genesis, chapters 1–3. At first, Adam and Eve lived with God in the Garden of Eden, but the serpent tempted them into eating the fruit from the tree of knowledge of good and evil, which God had forbidden. After doing so, they became ashamed of their nakedness and God expelled them from the Garden to prevent them from eating from the tree of life and becoming immortal.

The narrative

In Genesis 2–3, God places Adam in Eden and creates Eve from his side. The serpent, described as more crafty than any other beast, persuades Eve that eating the forbidden fruit will open her eyes and make the couple "like God, knowing good and evil". Eve eats and gives the fruit to Adam. Their eyes are opened; realising their nakedness, they sew fig leaves together. God pronounces curses: pain in childbirth for the woman, toil and thorns for the man, enmity between the serpent and humanity, and mortality — "for you are dust, and to dust you shall return". Cherubim and a flaming sword then guard the way to the tree of life.

Interpretations

Original sin

Though the phrase "original sin" does not appear in Genesis, Christian theology — developed especially by Augustine of Hippo in the 4th–5th centuries — holds that Adam's sin is inherited by all humanity, corrupting human nature and requiring redemption through Christ, who is portrayed in the New Testament as the "second Adam". Eastern Orthodoxy speaks of "ancestral sin", emphasising mortality rather than inherited guilt.

Judaism and Islam

Judaism does not generally teach inherited sin; Adam and Eve's disobedience is a primal human failure with lasting consequences but each person remains responsible for their own deeds. In Islam, Adam's lapse (eating from the tree after Iblis's deception) is forgiven after repentance; there is no doctrine of original sin, though Adam and Eve are sent down to earth as part of God's plan.

Symbolic readings

Many modern interpreters read the story symbolically — as a myth of maturation, consciousness and moral responsibility — and note parallels with other ancient Near Eastern narratives.

In art and literature

The Fall is one of the most depicted episodes in Western art, from medieval manuscripts to Masaccio's Brancacci Chapel fresco and Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling. John Milton's epic poem Paradise Lost (1667) is the most famous literary retelling in English.
