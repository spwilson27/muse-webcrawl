Jean-François Lyotard (10 August 1924 – 21 April 1998) was a French philosopher, sociologist and literary theorist of the post-structuralist generation. He is best known for The Postmodern Condition: A Report on Knowledge (1979), the book that did more than any other to define "postmodernism" for philosophy and the humanities, in which he famously defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives".

Life

Lyotard was born in Versailles and studied philosophy at the Sorbonne, where he befriended Gilles Deleuze. After graduating he taught philosophy in secondary schools, including in Constantine, Algeria (1950–52), an experience that sharpened his political engagement. From 1954 he was a member of the revolutionary Marxist group Socialisme ou Barbarie, writing extensively on the Algerian war of independence, before leaving organised militancy in the mid-1960s. He took part in the events of May 1968 and taught at the experimental University of Paris VIII (Vincennes) from 1970, later becoming professor emeritus there; he also held visiting posts in North America, including at the University of California, Irvine and Emory University.

Work

Lyotard's early book Discourse, Figure (1971) opposed structuralism by defending the disruptive force of the figural and of desire in art and language. Libidinal Economy (1974), his most provocative work, treated capitalism through the flows of libidinal energy and remains controversial even among his admirers.

The Postmodern Condition

Commissioned as a report on the state of knowledge in advanced societies, The Postmodern Condition (1979) argued that the grand legitimating stories of modernity — the Enlightenment narrative of human emancipation through reason, the speculative narrative of the unity of knowledge, and the Marxist narrative of liberation through revolution — had lost their credibility. Knowledge in computerised societies is increasingly legitimated instead by performativity: efficiency and the optimisation of the system. Against totalising narratives, Lyotard defended the plurality of small, local "language games" (adapting Wittgenstein) and their irreducible diversity.

Later philosophy

In The Differend (1983), often considered his most rigorous work, Lyotard analysed wrongs that cannot be justly resolved because the parties' claims belong to incommensurable "phrase regimens" — a differend rather than a litigation — taking the Holocaust and the denier's demand for impossible proof as a central, grave example. Other later works include The Inhuman (1988), essays criticising the dehumanising logic of techno-science and development, and writings on aesthetics and the sublime, on Kant, and on his friend the philosopher's contemporaries. He died of leukaemia in Paris in 1998 while preparing a conference on his work.
