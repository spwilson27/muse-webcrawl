Prodromal schizophrenia

The prodrome of schizophrenia is the period of declining function and emerging symptoms that precedes the onset of frank psychosis. During this phase individuals may experience attenuated psychotic symptoms, social withdrawal, deteriorating school or work performance, depressed mood, anxiety, and disturbances of sleep and concentration. Because many of these features are non-specific, identifying a true prodrome prospectively is difficult, and the term is often applied retrospectively after a first psychotic episode.

Clinical features

Early signs commonly reported include suspiciousness, unusual perceptual experiences, odd beliefs, disorganised thinking of mild degree, and a loss of interest in social activities. Basic symptoms — subtle disturbances in thought, perception and motor function described by Gerd Huber — may be present years before diagnosis. Functional decline, measured by instruments such as the Global Assessment of Functioning scale, is a key marker.

Identification and risk

Clinics for people at clinical high risk (CHR) or ultra-high risk (UHR) use structured interviews such as the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes (SIPS) and the Comprehensive Assessment of At-Risk Mental States (CAARMS). Roughly a fifth to a third of identified high-risk individuals transition to a psychotic disorder within two to three years. Genetic risk, obstetric complications, cannabis use, and childhood trauma are among the factors associated with higher risk.

Intervention

Interventions in the prodromal phase generally emphasise psychosocial treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy, family support, and treatment of comorbid anxiety and depression. Antipsychotic medication is not routinely recommended for people who have not developed psychosis, because of side effects and the high rate of false positives.
