Spanish Inquisition

The Spanish Inquisition (Spanish: Inquisición española), formally the Tribunal of the Holy Office of the Inquisition, was established in 1478 by the Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, with papal authorisation, and was not definitively abolished until 1834. Unlike earlier medieval inquisitions, it operated under the control of the Spanish crown. Its original purpose was to police the orthodoxy of conversos — Jews (and later Muslims) who had converted to Christianity — suspected of secretly practising their former faiths; its remit later extended to Protestants, bigamists, blasphemers, homosexuals, and practitioners of superstition, as well as censorship of books.

History

The Inquisition's first tribunal opened in Seville in 1480; Tomás de Torquemada became the first Grand Inquisitor in 1483 and organised the institution's procedures. Its early decades coincided with the Alhambra Decree (1492) expelling unconverted Jews from Spain and, after 1502 and the 1520s, the forced conversion and eventual expulsion (1609–1614) of the Moriscos. Trials followed an inquisitorial procedure: secret accusations, confiscation of property, limited defence, and the use of torture under regulated conditions. Sentences were proclaimed at public ceremonies called autos-da-fé; those condemned to death were "relaxed" to the secular arm for execution.

Scale and historiography

Modern archival research suggests the Inquisition tried on the order of 150,000 people over three and a half centuries, executing a few thousand — far fewer than the millions depicted in the "Black Legend" of Protestant and Enlightenment polemic, though revisionist debates continue. Its deeper impact lay in censorship (the Indexes of prohibited books), the policing of limpieza de sangre (purity of blood), and a climate of religious conformity.
