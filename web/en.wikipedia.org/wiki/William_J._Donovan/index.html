William Joseph Donovan (January 1, 1883 – February 8, 1959) was an American soldier, lawyer, intelligence officer, and diplomat. He is best known for serving as the head of the Office of Strategic Services (OSS), the precursor to the modern Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), during World War II. He is regarded as the founding father of the CIA, and a statue of him stands in the lobby of its headquarters in Langley, Virginia.

A decorated soldier, Donovan is the only person to have received all four of the United States' highest awards for valor: the Medal of Honor, the Distinguished Service Cross, the Distinguished Service Medal, and the National Security Medal. He was also a recipient of the Silver Star and Purple Heart, as well as decorations from a number of other nations.

Early life and education

Donovan was born in Buffalo, New York, to Irish Catholic immigrants. He attended St. Joseph's Collegiate Institute and Niagara University before transferring to Columbia University, where he played football and graduated in 1905. He went on to earn a law degree from Columbia Law School and became a successful lawyer in Buffalo, later opening a prominent practice on Wall Street.

Military career

Donovan served with the New York National Guard on the Mexican border in 1916 under General John J. Pershing. During World War I he commanded a battalion of the famous "Fighting 69th" (the 165th Infantry Regiment) and was awarded the Medal of Honor for leading an assault near Landres-et-St. Georges, France, in October 1918, continuing to direct his men despite being wounded.

Office of Strategic Services

In 1941 President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed Donovan Coordinator of Information, and in June 1942 the office was reorganized as the OSS. Donovan built an organization that conducted espionage, sabotage, propaganda, and guerrilla warfare behind enemy lines in Europe and Asia. After the war the OSS was dissolved, but its model directly inspired the creation of the CIA in 1947.

Later life and legacy

After the war Donovan returned to law practice and served as ambassador to Thailand from 1953 to 1954. He died in Washington, D.C., in 1959 and is buried at Arlington National Cemetery. Known as "Wild Bill," Donovan remains one of the most celebrated figures in American intelligence history.
