Lorica hamata

The lorica hamata is a type of chain mail armour used by soldiers of the Roman Republic and Empire. Made of interlocking iron rings, it was in service for roughly six centuries — longer than any other Roman body armour — and was worn by legionaries, auxiliaries, and later by soldiers of the late Roman and Byzantine worlds.

Construction

A typical lorica hamata comprised some 30,000 or more iron rings arranged in alternating rows of solid punched rings and riveted rings, each about 5–7 mm in diameter. The shirt generally reached mid-thigh, weighed around 9–10 kg, and often included shoulder doubling — extra mail panels fastened at the chest with hooks, in the Greek style — particularly for cavalry. Mail offered good flexibility and coverage and was highly resistant to slashing, though less so to thrusting and arrows at close range.

History

The Romans adopted mail from the Celts, probably by the 3rd century BC. It was the standard armour of Republican legionaries and remained widespread after the introduction of the plate lorica segmentata in the early Empire, especially among auxiliaries. When segmentata fell out of use in the 3rd century AD, mail again became the principal armour, and it continued in use into the medieval period, when it was known simply as mail or hauberk.

Production and maintenance

Mail was labour-intensive and expensive to produce, but durable and easily repaired by replacing damaged rings. Soldiers cleaned it by tumbling it in barrels of sand, and a padded undergarment (subarmalis) was worn beneath to absorb blows and prevent chafing.
