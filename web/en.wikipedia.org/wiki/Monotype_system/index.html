Monotype system

The Monotype system is a system for printing by hot-metal typesetting developed by the Lanston Monotype Machine Company, based on patents of Tolbert Lanston, from the 1890s onward. Unlike the rival Linotype machine, which casts whole lines of type (slugs), the Monotype casts individual letters and assembles them into justified lines, making corrections easy.

Operation

The system consists of two separate machines. The Monotype keyboard is operated like a typewriter; it punches holes in a wide paper tape, encoding each character and the spacing needed to justify the line. The tape is then fed into the Monotype caster, which reads it backwards and casts individual pieces of type from molten type metal, using matrices held in a matrix case. Because keyboarding and casting are separate, one caster could be served by several keyboards, and the tape could be stored and reused for reprints.

Use and legacy

Monotype excelled at complex work — books, tables, mathematics, and fine typography — where the ability to correct single letters mattered. The British Monotype Corporation became a major type foundry, commissioning faces such as Times New Roman (with Linotype), Gill Sans, and Perpetua under the influence of Stanley Morison. Hot-metal Monotype production declined with phototypesetting and digital typesetting, though the machines retained a following among fine-press printers.
