Fugaku (Japanese: 富岳) is a petascale supercomputer at the RIKEN Center for Computational Science in Kobe, Japan. Developed jointly by RIKEN and Fujitsu, it is the successor to the K computer. Fugaku began full operation in 2021 and was the world's fastest supercomputer from June 2020, when it debuted at the top of the TOP500 list, until it was surpassed by the American Frontier system in 2022.

History

On 23 May 2019, RIKEN announced that the supercomputer would be named Fugaku, an alternative name for Mount Fuji. The name was chosen to symbolize the machine's high performance and broad user base. In November 2019, the Fugaku prototype won first place at the Green500 energy-efficiency ranking. The system was delivered to the RIKEN campus in Kobe between February and May 2020 and was pressed into early service for research on the COVID-19 pandemic, including simulations of droplet spread and the effectiveness of masks.

Fugaku began shared use in March 2021. It topped the TOP500 list four consecutive times (June 2020, November 2020, June 2021 and November 2021), achieving an HPL benchmark performance of 442 petaflops — roughly three times faster than its nearest competitor at debut.

Hardware

Fugaku is built around the Fujitsu A64FX processor, a 48-core system-on-chip based on the ARMv8.2-A architecture with Scalable Vector Extensions (SVE) — the first production CPU to implement SVE. The full system comprises 158,976 nodes and 7,299,072 cores, interconnected by Fujitsu's proprietary Tofu Interconnect D network. It uses high-bandwidth HBM2 memory and consumes roughly 30 megawatts of power.

Unlike most modern supercomputers, Fugaku achieves its performance without GPU accelerators, relying instead on the efficiency of its CPU vector units. It also led the HPCG and HPL-AI benchmark rankings during its period at the top.

Software and applications

Fugaku runs a lightweight Linux-based environment with the McKernel light-weight kernel on compute nodes. Its applications span disaster prevention (earthquake and tsunami simulation), weather and climate modelling, drug discovery, materials science, and fundamental physics. During the COVID-19 pandemic, its simulations of respiratory droplet dispersion in offices, restaurants and public transport received wide public attention in Japan.
