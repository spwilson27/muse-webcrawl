Core competency

A core competency is a concept in management theory introduced by C. K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel in their 1990 Harvard Business Review article "The Core Competence of the Corporation." It describes a harmonized combination of multiple resources and skills that distinguish a firm in the marketplace and underpin its ability to enter new markets.

Criteria

Prahalad and Hamel proposed three tests for identifying a core competence: it should provide potential access to a wide variety of markets; it should make a significant contribution to the perceived customer benefits of the end product; and it should be difficult for competitors to imitate. Examples frequently cited include Honda's expertise in engines and power trains, Canon's in optics and imaging, and 3M's in adhesives and materials.

The corporation as a tree

The authors compared the diversified corporation to a tree: core products are the trunk and major limbs, business units the smaller branches, end products the leaves and fruit, and core competencies the root system that provides nourishment and stability. Firms that think of themselves only as portfolios of businesses risk "tying" themselves to shrinking markets and starving the roots.

Influence and criticism

The concept strongly influenced strategic management and the resource-based view of the firm, encouraging outsourcing of non-core activities. Critics note that competencies are often identified only in hindsight, after success, and that over-commitment to existing competencies can produce "core rigidities" that block adaptation to change.
