Found object

Found object
FrenchObjet trouvé
Associated movementsDada, Surrealism, Pop art, assemblage
Key exponentMarcel Duchamp ("readymades")

A found object (a loan translation from the French objet trouvé), or found art, is art created from undisguised, but often modified, items or products that are not normally considered materials from which art is made, often because they already have a non-art function. Pablo Picasso first publicly utilised the idea when he pasted a printed image of chair caning onto his painting Still Life with Chair Caning (1912).

Readymades

Marcel Duchamp is thought to have perfected the concept in the years 1913–1917 with his readymades: ordinary manufactured objects designated as art by the artist's choice and signature. The most famous is Fountain (1917), a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt", submitted to and rejected by the Society of Independent Artists in New York — a gesture that questioned the definition of art itself. Duchamp's earlier Bicycle Wheel (1913) and Bottle Rack (1914) pioneered the form.

Later developments

The concept fed into Surrealist objects, the assemblages of artists such as Kurt Schwitters and Robert Rauschenberg's "combines", Pop art's use of commercial imagery, and later practices including arte povera and installation art. Found objects continue to raise questions about authorship, context, and the institutional framing of art.
