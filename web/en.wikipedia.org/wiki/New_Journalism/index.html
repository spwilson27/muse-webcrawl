New Journalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New Journalism is a style of news writing and journalism, developed in the 1960s and 1970s, that uses literary techniques unconventional in standard news writing at the time. The term was codified with its current meaning by Tom Wolfe in a 1973 collection of journalism articles he published as The New Journalism, which included works by Wolfe, Truman Capote, Hunter S. Thompson, Norman Mailer, Joan Didion, Terry Southern, Robert Christgau, Gay Talese and others.

Articles in the New Journalism style tended not to shy away from controversial topics and often employed literary techniques such as scene-by-scene construction, extensive dialogue, interior points of view and the recording of everyday details ("status details"), while still aiming at factual accuracy through intensive reporting and immersion in the subject. Because the writers combined the techniques of fiction with long-form reportage, the style is closely related to creative non-fiction and the nonfiction novel.

Techniques

In the introduction to his 1973 anthology, Wolfe identified four devices borrowed from realist fiction that distinguished the new style:

  1. Scene-by-scene construction — telling the story by moving from scene to scene rather than through historical narrative.
  2. Full dialogue — recording dialogue in full, rather than quotation fragments, to establish character.
  3. Third-person point of view — presenting scenes through the eyes and interior thoughts of a character, based on interviews about what the subject was thinking and feeling.
  4. Status details — the recording of everyday gestures, habits, manners, furnishings, clothing and other symbolic details that reveal social status and character.

Major works and practitioners

  • Truman Capote, In Cold Blood (1966), the "nonfiction novel" of the Clutter murders in Kansas.
  • Gay Talese, "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold" (Esquire, 1966), a profile written without interviewing its subject.
  • Joan Didion, Slouching Towards Bethlehem (1968).
  • Norman Mailer, The Armies of the Night (1968) and Miami and the Siege of Chicago (1968).
  • Hunter S. Thompson's "gonzo journalism", culminating in Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas (1971), made the reporter himself the protagonist.
  • Tom Wolfe, The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test (1968) and The Right Stuff (1979).

The movement flourished in magazines such as Esquire, New York (co-founded by Wolfe's editor Clay Felker), Harper's, Rolling Stone and The Village Voice.

Criticism

Critics charged that the style tempted writers toward embellishment, composite characters and mind-reading that could not be verified; Dwight Macdonald dismissed parts of it as "parajournalism". Others objected that Wolfe's label exaggerated the novelty of the form, citing earlier literary reporters from Stephen Crane and George Orwell to John Hersey, whose Hiroshima (1946) anticipated many of the techniques.

Legacy

New Journalism fundamentally shaped modern long-form and narrative non-fiction, magazine profiles and immersive feature writing; elements of the style are now standard in quality journalism and in the book-length narrative nonfiction that followed it.
