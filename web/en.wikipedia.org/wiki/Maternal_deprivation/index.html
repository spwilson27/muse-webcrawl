Maternal deprivation

Maternal deprivation is a concept in developmental psychology referring to the harmful effects on a young child of the absence, loss, or disruption of a continuous relationship with a mother or primary caregiver. The term is most closely associated with the psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby, whose 1951 World Health Organization report, Maternal Care and Mental Health, argued that infants and young children need a warm, intimate, and continuous relationship with their mother (or permanent mother substitute) in which both find satisfaction and enjoyment.

Background and evidence

Bowlby drew on studies of children raised in institutions and hospitals, including the work of René Spitz on "hospitalism" and wartime research on evacuated and orphaned children. He proposed that prolonged separation or deprivation in the early years could lead to an "affectionless character", delinquency, and lasting difficulties in forming relationships.

Criticism and development

Later researchers, notably Michael Rutter in Maternal Deprivation Reassessed (1972), distinguished deprivation (loss of an established bond) from privation (never having formed a bond), and emphasised that outcomes depend on many factors, including the reasons for separation, the quality of substitute care, and the child's other relationships. Bowlby's ideas developed into attachment theory, and the research spurred major reforms in the care of hospitalised children and the move away from large residential nurseries toward foster care and family-based placements.
