Color reproduction

Color reproduction is the process of duplicating the colours of an original scene, object, or image in another medium — for example in print, photography, television, or computer displays — such that the reproduction is perceived to match the original. The science of colour reproduction underpins technologies including colour printing, colour photography, and electronic imaging.

A colour checker chart used for calibrating reproduction systems.

Principles

Colour reproduction rests on the trichromatic theory of colour vision: the human eye has three types of cone cells sensitive to long, medium, and short wavelengths. Because of this, most colours can be matched — or approximately reproduced — by mixing just three primary stimuli. Two spectra that appear identical under given viewing conditions despite differing physically are called metamers, and metameric matching is the basis for reproducing a scene's colours with a limited set of dyes, phosphors, or inks.

Additive and subtractive reproduction

Additive reproduction, used in displays and projectors, combines red, green, and blue light (the RGB colour model). Subtractive reproduction, used in printing and colour photography, employs cyan, magenta, and yellow colorants (often with black, CMYK) that selectively absorb wavelengths from white light. Each system has a colour gamut — the range of colours it can produce — and colours outside the gamut must be mapped or clipped, a process handled by colour management systems and ICC profiles.

History

James Clerk Maxwell demonstrated the principle in 1861 with the first durable colour photograph, made by combining three black-and-white separations through red, green, and blue filters. Subsequent milestones included the Autochrome colour plate (1907), Technicolor motion pictures, Kodachrome film (1935), halftone process printing, and modern digital imaging pipelines with standardised colour spaces such as sRGB.

Evaluation

Reproduction quality is assessed by colour difference metrics such as ΔE in the CIELAB colour space, by gamut volume, and by how the match behaves under different illuminants (metamerism failure). Accurate reproduction also depends on standardised viewing conditions, since perceived colour shifts with illumination and surround.
