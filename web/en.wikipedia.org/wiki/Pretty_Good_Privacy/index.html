Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) is an encryption program that provides cryptographic privacy and authentication for data communication. PGP is used for signing, encrypting, and decrypting texts, e-mails, files, directories, and whole disk partitions, and to increase the security of e-mail communications. It was created by Phil Zimmermann in 1991.

Design

PGP combines symmetric-key encryption with public-key cryptography. A message is compressed and encrypted with a one-time session key using a fast symmetric algorithm such as CAST5, Triple DES, or AES; the session key is then encrypted with the recipient's public key (typically RSA or Diffie–Hellman/ElGamal). Digital signatures are produced by hashing the message and signing the hash with the sender's private key. Because PGP could not rely on central certification authorities, it introduced the web of trust, a decentralized model in which users endorse the binding between a public key and an identity by signing each other's keys.

History and legal controversy

Zimmermann released PGP for free in 1991, and it spread worldwide via the young Internet. United States export law then classified strong encryption software as a munition, and Zimmermann became the target of a criminal investigation after PGP appeared abroad. The investigation was dropped in 1996 without indictment. To demonstrate the absurdity of the restrictions, the PGP source code was published as a printed book—exportable under the First Amendment—and scanned back into compilable form overseas.

OpenPGP and successors

The PGP format was standardized as OpenPGP (RFC 2440, later RFC 4880), implemented by software including the free GNU Privacy Guard (GnuPG/GPG) and, after 2021, maintained forks such as LibrePGP. Commercial PGP passed through several owners, including Network Associates, PGP Corporation, Symantec (2010), and Broadcom. Despite declining use in general e-mail, OpenPGP remains widely used for signing software releases and in tools for developers and journalists.
