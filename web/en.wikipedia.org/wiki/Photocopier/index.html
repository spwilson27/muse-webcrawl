A photocopier (also called a copier or copy machine) is a machine that makes copies of documents and images quickly and cheaply on paper. Most photocopiers use xerography, a dry electrophotographic process invented by Chester Carlson, who demonstrated it in 1938 and patented it in 1942.

How it works

In a xerographic copier, a photoconductive drum is given a uniform electrostatic charge. Light reflected from the original document discharges the drum where the paper is white, leaving a charged latent image of the dark areas. Fine toner powder is attracted to the charged areas, then transferred to a sheet of paper and fused by heat and pressure. The landmark Xerox 914 of 1959 — the first successful plain-paper copier — automated this cycle and revolutionised office work; the word "xerox" became a generic verb for copying in some countries. From the 1990s, analogue copiers were displaced by digital machines that scan the original once and print from memory, usually combined with printer, scanner and fax functions in a multifunction device.

Impact and issues

Photocopiers transformed offices, education, publishing and political activism by making document reproduction cheap and private; they also raised copyright concerns, security risks (digital copiers store images of copied pages on internal hard drives), and health questions about toner dust and ozone emissions, since mitigated by design. Counterfeit deterrence features in modern machines refuse to copy banknotes and other protected documents.
