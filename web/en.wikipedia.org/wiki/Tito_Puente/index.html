Tito Puente

Ernesto Antonio "Tito" Puente Jr. (April 20, 1923 – June 1, 2000) was an American musician, songwriter, bandleader, and record producer of Puerto Rican descent. He is best known for his dance-oriented mambo and Latin jazz compositions that helped advance his career for over 50 years. Puente is often credited as "The Musical Pope", "El Rey de los Timbales" (The King of the Timbales), and simply "The King of Latin Music".

Early life

Puente was born in Harlem Hospital in New York City to Puerto Rican parents and grew up in Spanish Harlem. As a child he took piano lessons and studied percussion; his neighbours included Machito, whose Afro-Cubans he would later join. He served in the U.S. Navy during the Second World War, playing saxophone in a ship's band, and afterward studied composition and orchestration at the Juilliard School.

Career

In 1949 Puente formed his own orchestra, the Piccadilly Boys, soon renamed the Tito Puente Orchestra. At the Palladium Ballroom he became a central figure of the mambo craze alongside Machito and Tito Rodríguez—the "Big Three". His 1958 album Dance Mania is considered a landmark of the genre. His composition "Oye Como Va" (1963) achieved far wider fame when Santana recorded a rock version in 1970.

A prolific recording artist, Puente released more than 100 albums. He won five Grammy Awards, and his album Mambo Diablo (1985) and the Latin-jazz recordings of the 1980s and 1990s brought him renewed acclaim. He appeared on children's television, including Sesame Street, and played a cameo in the film The Mambo Kings (1992).

Death and legacy

Puente died on June 1, 2000, in New York following heart surgery. He was posthumously awarded the Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2003, and his influence on salsa and Latin jazz remains immense; he helped popularise the timbales as a front-line solo instrument.
