Nicholas I of Russia

Nicholas I (6 July 1796 – 2 March 1855) was Emperor of Russia from 1825 until his death in 1855. A younger brother of Alexander I of Russia, he came to the throne amid the Decembrist revolt and ruled for thirty years as Europe's most consistent champion of autocracy and legitimacy — "the gendarme of Europe" — suppressing the Polish November Uprising (1830), intervening against the Hungarian revolution (1849), and codifying the ideology of "Orthodoxy, Autocracy, and Nationality". His reign ended amid the Crimean War, whose exposure of Russian backwardness forced his son's Great Reforms.

Accession and system

Because Constantine Pavlovich had secretly renounced the succession, Alexander's death produced a fortnight of confusion exploited by liberal guards officers — the Decembrists — whose revolt on Senate Square (14 December 1825) Nicholas suppressed by artillery. He interrogated the conspirators himself and drew the lesson of his reign: order above all. His instruments were the Third Section political police and the Corps of Gendarmes (1826), strict censorship, Speransky's great codification of the laws (1832), Kankrin's financial stabilisation and Kiselyov's reform of the state peasantry — competence within autocracy, reform without representation.

Foreign policy

Nicholas fought Persia (1826–28) and Turkey (1828–29), crushed Poland in 1831, and in 1849 sent 200,000 troops to save Austria from Hungarian revolution — the act that earned him the "gendarme" title and, he believed, a debt of gratitude Austria repaid in 1854 with "astonishing ingratitude" (in the phrase attributed to Schwarzenberg). His pressure on the decaying Ottoman Empire — the "sick man of Europe", his own famous phrase in conversations with the British ambassador — led through miscalculation into the Crimean War, in which Britain and France invaded Crimea itself.

Death

Nicholas died in March 1855, of pneumonia tradition says aggravated by despair at the war's course, with Sevastopol under siege. He left his heir the famous warning embodied in the reign's collapse: the system had preserved the forms of power while the empire's railways, rifles and finances fell a generation behind its rivals.
