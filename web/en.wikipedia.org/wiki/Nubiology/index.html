Nubiology

Nubiology is the interdisciplinary study of the history, culture, languages, and archaeology of Nubia, the region along the Nile in present-day southern Egypt and northern Sudan. The field combines Egyptology, archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, and history, and is concerned with the Nubian kingdoms of Kush, Kerma, Nobatia, Makuria, and Alodia, as well as the modern Nubian peoples.

History of the field

Interest in Nubia grew during the nineteenth century as European travellers and archaeologists documented its temples and pyramids. The field was transformed by the UNESCO salvage campaign of the 1960s, when the construction of the Aswan High Dam threatened Nubian monuments with flooding; international teams excavated sites and relocated temples such as Abu Simbel and Philae. The discovery of the royal cemeteries at Qustul and the work of excavators at Kerma, Meroë, and Napata established Nubia as home to an autonomous civilization rather than merely an Egyptian dependency.

Scope

Nubiologists study the Nubian languages (including Old Nubian, written in a modified Coptic script), monumental architecture such as the pyramids of Meroë and the temples of Jebel Barkal, funerary customs, trade with Egypt and the Mediterranean world, and the conversion of the Nubian kingdoms to Christianity in the sixth century CE and their later Islamization.
