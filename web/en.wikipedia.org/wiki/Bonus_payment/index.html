Bonus payment

A bonus payment is a form of compensation paid by an employer to an employee in addition to regular salary or wages. Bonuses may reward individual or company performance, mark a special occasion such as a holiday or profitable year, or serve as an incentive to encourage particular behaviour. They are a common element of total compensation in many industries, most famously in finance, where annual bonuses can exceed base salary for senior staff.

Types of bonus

Performance bonuses are tied to individual, team, or company results measured against targets. A signing bonus is offered to a new employee as an inducement to join, common in professional sport and executive hiring, while a retention bonus rewards an employee for staying through a critical period. Other forms include holiday bonuses (such as the "thirteenth month" salary customary in many countries), profit-sharing payments, referral bonuses for introducing new hires, and spot bonuses for exceptional work.

Rationale and criticism

Employers use bonuses to motivate effort, align employee interests with company performance, and keep fixed salary costs flexible. Economists of principal–agent theory note that incentive pay can improve performance on measurable tasks but may distort behaviour toward what is measured, encourage excessive risk-taking, or crowd out intrinsic motivation. Large bonuses in the financial sector became politically controversial after the 2008 financial crisis, when institutions that had received public bailouts continued to pay substantial bonuses, prompting regulatory reforms such as the European Union's cap on bankers' bonuses relative to fixed pay.
