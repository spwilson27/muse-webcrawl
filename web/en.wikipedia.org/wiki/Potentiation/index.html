Potentiation

Long-term potentiation strengthens synapses between neurons.

In pharmacology and neuroscience, potentiation refers to an enhancement of effect. The term is used in several related senses.

Pharmacology

Potentiation occurs when the effect of a drug is increased by another agent that has little or no effect of its own — a form of synergy. A classic example is the combination of clavulanic acid, which has weak antibacterial activity itself, with amoxicillin: the clavulanate inhibits bacterial beta-lactamase enzymes, thereby potentiating the antibiotic. Potentiation is clinically important both for therapeutic combinations and for dangerous interactions, such as the mutual potentiation of alcohol and sedative drugs, which can cause fatal respiratory depression.

Neuroscience

In neurophysiology, potentiation describes the strengthening of a synapse's response following activity. The best-known form is long-term potentiation (LTP), a persistent strengthening of synapses based on recent patterns of activity, widely studied in the hippocampus as a cellular mechanism underlying learning and memory. Shorter-lived forms include post-tetanic potentiation, which lasts for seconds to minutes after a burst of high-frequency stimulation.

Other uses

  • In immunology, potentiation can describe the enhancement of an immune response.
  • In homeopathy, "potentisation" refers to the serial dilution and succussion of remedies — a usage unrelated to the pharmacological sense and without scientific support.
