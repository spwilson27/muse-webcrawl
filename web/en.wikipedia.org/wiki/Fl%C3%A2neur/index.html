Flâneur

Flâneur
OriginFrench, from flâner ("to stroll")
Associated figuresCharles Baudelaire, Walter Benjamin
Setting19th-century Paris

Flâneur (French: [flɑnœʁ]) is a French noun referring to a man who saunters around observing society. The term, derived from the Old Norse verb flana, "to wander with no purpose," was developed into a cultural figure in 19th-century Paris, notably in the writings of Charles Baudelaire and later Walter Benjamin.

Meaning

The flâneur is an idler and explorer of the city, a detached but keenly observant stroller who moves through the urban crowd without joining it. Baudelaire presented the flâneur as an artist-poet of modern life, finding aesthetic delight in the fleeting scenes of the metropolis. Benjamin, writing in the 20th century, analysed the flâneur as a symptom of capitalist modernity and the Paris arcades.

Cultural influence

The concept has been widely taken up in literature, sociology, urban studies and photography, where it informs ideas about walking, observation and modern urban experience. The feminine form, flâneuse, has been discussed by writers examining women's experience of the city.
