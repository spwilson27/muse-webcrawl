Scarcity

Scarcity
FieldEconomics
Related conceptsOpportunity cost, resource allocation, supply and demand

Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem of having seemingly unlimited human wants in a world of limited resources. Because resources — land, labour, capital, time — are finite, society cannot produce everything its members want, and choices must be made about how resources are allocated. The economist Lionel Robbins defined economics itself in terms of scarcity: "the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."

Scarcity and choice

Scarcity forces trade-offs. Every decision to use a resource for one purpose means forgoing its use for another; the value of the best forgone alternative is the opportunity cost. Economic systems — market, command, traditional, or mixed — are different mechanisms for resolving scarcity through allocation.

Scarce vs. free goods

Goods that are scarce (economic goods) command a positive price when traded; free goods, available in quantities exceeding demand at zero price — historically air, sometimes water — lie outside the economic problem until circumstances change. Scarcity is relative to demand, not an absolute shortage: a good can be physically abundant yet economically scarce in a particular place or time.

Artificial scarcity and critiques

Scarcity can also be created deliberately, for example through monopoly restriction, intellectual property limits, or marketing. Critics of mainstream economics, including ecological economists, question whether human wants are truly unlimited and emphasise that some scarcities are socially constructed or distributional rather than physical.
