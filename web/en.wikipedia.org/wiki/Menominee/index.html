Menominee

The Menominee Indian Reservation lies in northeastern Wisconsin, within the tribe's ancestral homeland.

The Menominee (Menominee: Omāēqnomenēwak, "People of the Wild Rice") are a federally recognized tribe of Native Americans in the United States, historically based in present-day Wisconsin and the Upper Peninsula of Michigan. Their traditional name derives from the wild rice (manoomin) that grows along the rivers and lakes of their homeland and remains central to their culture and spirituality.

History

Menominee oral tradition places the tribe's origin at the mouth of the Menominee River, where the Creator transformed a great bear into the first Menominee. For thousands of years the Menominee occupied a vast territory of some ten million acres covering much of what is now northern Wisconsin and the western Upper Peninsula.

Contact with Europeans began in 1634, when the French explorer Jean Nicolet arrived in the Green Bay region. The Menominee became important partners in the fur trade. Through a series of treaties in the nineteenth century, the tribe ceded most of its land to the United States. The Treaty of 1854 established the Menominee Indian Reservation, roughly 234,000 acres of forested land along the Wolf River — among the most heavily forested counties in the eastern United States.

Termination and restoration

In 1954, under the federal policy of Indian termination, Congress passed the Menominee Termination Act, ending the tribe's federal recognition and converting the reservation into Menominee County. Termination proved economically and socially disastrous: tribal assets were liquidated, a hospital and schools closed, and poverty deepened. The tribe organized a celebrated campaign, led by figures such as Ada Deer, for restoration. On December 22, 1973, President Richard Nixon signed the Menominee Restoration Act, restoring federal recognition — the first tribe to be restored after termination.

Culture and language

Wild rice harvesting remains a defining cultural practice, as do sturgeon fishing traditions on the Wolf River. The Menominee language, an Algonquian language, is severely endangered; as of the early twenty-first century only a small number of fluent elder speakers remained, and the tribe has invested heavily in immersion programs and language revitalization. The tribe's sustainably managed forest, harvested under principles developed by Chief Oshkosh in the nineteenth century, is internationally renowned as a model of sustainable forestry.
