Free trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Free trade is a trade policy that does not restrict imports or exports — no tariffs, quotas, subsidies or regulations applied in a way that discriminates between domestic and foreign goods and services. Its intellectual foundation is the theory of comparative advantage (Ricardo, 1817): countries gain by specialising where their relative efficiency is greatest and trading, even if one country is more efficient at everything. Free trade is implemented unilaterally, by treaty (free trade agreements and customs unions) and multilaterally (the GATT/WTO system).

History and politics

Nineteenth-century Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws (1846) made it free trade's champion; the post-1945 order institutionalised liberalisation through successive GATT rounds, regional integration (EEC/EU, NAFTA/USMCA) and China's WTO accession (2001). The distributive politics — aggregate gains, concentrated losses (the "China shock" literature on manufacturing communities) — fuel recurring protectionist reactions, populist campaigns and, in the 2020s, an explicit turn to industrial policy, export controls and "de-risking" among the US, EU and China. Economists remain broadly pro-free-trade with qualifications (adjustment assistance, national-security carve-outs), while the political consensus that sustained it has visibly fractured.
