Ship of Theseus

Ship of Theseus
FieldMetaphysics, philosophy of identity
First describedPlutarch, Life of Theseus
Key questionDoes an object that has had all of its components replaced remain fundamentally the same object?

The Ship of Theseus is a thought experiment about identity over time that raises the question of whether an object that has had all of its components replaced remains fundamentally the same object. The concept is one of the oldest in Western philosophy, having been discussed by Heraclitus, Plato, and others before Plutarch recorded the story from which it takes its name.

The paradox

Plutarch, in his Life of Theseus, wrote that the ship in which the hero Theseus had sailed was preserved by the Athenians for centuries, who took away the old planks as they decayed and put in new timber, so that the ship became a standing illustration among philosophers of the question of growth and identity: some holding that it remained the same ship, others that it was not.

The 17th-century philosopher Thomas Hobbes added a further twist: suppose the discarded planks are collected and built into a second ship. Which, if either, is the original Ship of Theseus — the continuously repaired ship, or the ship reassembled from the original material?

Proposed resolutions

  • Continuity of form and function: identity resides in the structure and history rather than the matter; the repaired ship is the original, and the reassembled planks merely a copy.
  • Material continuity: identity follows the matter; the reassembled ship has the stronger claim.
  • Four-dimensionalism: objects are extended through time as well as space; the two ships share earlier temporal parts but diverge, so both can be continuations of the original in different respects.
  • Identity as convention: there is no deep fact of the matter — "same ship" simply follows whatever criteria a given context makes relevant.
  • Mereological essentialism: any change of parts destroys the original object entirely.

Applications

The puzzle is applied to organisms (whose cells and atoms are continually replaced), to personal identity, to restored buildings and refitted ships, to companies, and to software and digital files. In biology, gradual cell turnover raises the same question of what makes an organism the same over a lifetime.
