Remainder (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Property conveyances create remainders

In property law of the United Kingdom and the United States and other common law countries, a remainder is a future interest given to a person (who is referred to as the transferee or remainderman) that is capable of becoming possessory upon the natural end of a prior estate created by the same instrument. This is called an interest in remainder. A future interest following a particular (preceding) estate, where the preceding estate is a fee simple, is called a reversion.

For example, if a landowner conveys land "to A for life, then to B", A has a life estate, and B has a remainder which will become possessory when A dies. The third-party recipient is B, the "remainderman".

Types

A vested remainder is one where the remainderman is ascertained and the interest is not subject to any condition precedent other than the natural termination of the prior estate. A contingent remainder is one where the remainderman is unascertained (e.g., "to A's heirs") or is subject to a condition precedent (e.g., "to B if B reaches 21"). Vested remainders may be indefeasibly vested, vested subject to open (where the class of takers can still expand), or vested subject to complete defeasance.

History

The doctrine of remainders developed in medieval English land law as a means of keeping land within a family through settlements. The Rule in Shelley's Case and the doctrine of worthier title historically converted certain remainders in heirs into other interests, though both rules have been abolished or greatly curtailed in most modern jurisdictions. The Rule Against Perpetuities applies to contingent remainders, requiring them to vest, if at all, within the perpetuity period.

Distinction from reversion

A remainder is created in favor of a third party by the same instrument that creates the prior estate, while a reversion is the interest retained by the grantor when the grantor conveys a lesser estate than the grantor holds.
