The 1948 United States presidential election was the 41st quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, 2 November 1948. Incumbent Democratic President Harry S. Truman defeated Republican challenger Thomas E. Dewey in one of the greatest upsets in American political history. Nearly every poll, pundit and newspaper had predicted a comfortable Dewey victory; the Chicago Daily Tribune famously printed the headline "Dewey Defeats Truman" before the result was known.

Background

Truman had succeeded Franklin D. Roosevelt on the latter's death in April 1945. His approval ratings collapsed amid post-war inflation, strikes and the Republican capture of Congress in 1946. The Democratic Party split three ways: former Vice President Henry Wallace ran as the Progressive Party candidate attacking Truman's Cold War policies from the left, while Southern segregationists ("Dixiecrats") nominated South Carolina Governor Strom Thurmond in protest at the civil rights plank Truman had forced through the Democratic convention and his desegregation of the armed forces.

Campaign

Truman embarked on a 31,000-mile (50,000 km) "whistle-stop" rail tour, delivering hundreds of speeches from the rear platform of his train car Ferdinand Magellan, attacking the Republican "Do-Nothing Congress". Dewey, confident of victory, ran a cautious, high-minded campaign of vague platitudes about unity and avoided controversial specifics — a strategy later blamed for his defeat.

Truman carried 28 states, several of them — Ohio, Illinois and California among them — by margins under one percentage point. Democrats also regained control of both houses of Congress. The failure of the polls in 1948, attributed to quota sampling, early cessation of polling and undecided voters breaking late for Truman, led to lasting reforms in survey methodology. Truman's victory enabled him to pursue his Fair Deal programme and confirmed American commitments in the early Cold War, including the Marshall Plan and the forming of NATO.
