Yellowfin tuna

The yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) is a species of tuna found in the pelagic waters of tropical and subtropical oceans worldwide. It is one of the largest tuna species, reaching weights of over 180 kg (400 lb), though it is smaller than the Atlantic and Pacific bluefin tunas and the bigeye tuna. The yellowfin is a popular sport fish and is heavily fished commercially, forming a major part of the global canned tuna market where it is sold as "light tuna".

Yellowfin tuna

Description

The yellowfin tuna has a torpedo-shaped body built for speed, with a dark metallic blue back and a silvery belly. Its second dorsal fin and anal fin, as well as the finlets between those fins and the tail, are bright yellow, giving the species its name. Adults can reach 2.4 m (7.9 ft) in length and weigh over 180 kg. Like other tunas, yellowfin are warm-blooded in part, maintaining body temperatures above the surrounding water through a countercurrent heat-exchange system called the rete mirabile, which allows sustained high-speed swimming.

Distribution and habitat

Yellowfin tuna occur worldwide in tropical and subtropical seas, generally between 45°N and 45°S, but are absent from the Mediterranean Sea. They are epipelagic and occupy the upper 100 m of the water column, often schooling with dolphins, porpoises and other tuna species. Yellowfin frequently associate with floating objects such as logs, buoys and whale sharks, a behaviour exploited by purse-seine fisheries using fish aggregating devices (FADs).

Biology

Yellowfin feed on fish, squid and crustaceans, and are themselves preyed upon by sharks, billfish and larger tunas. They are fast-growing, reaching maturity at around two years of age, and females spawn multiple times during warm months, releasing millions of eggs. Most individuals live six to seven years. Their migration patterns are complex and stock-specific, with populations in the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific oceans managed as separate stocks.

Fisheries

Yellowfin is one of the most commercially important tuna species, with annual catches exceeding one million tonnes. It is caught mainly by purse seine and longline fisheries. The species is a mainstay of the canned tuna industry and is also prized fresh for sashimi, though bigeye and bluefin are generally preferred for high-end sushi. Overfishing has affected some stocks, particularly in the Indian Ocean, and regional fisheries management organisations set catch limits and FAD controls to maintain sustainability.
