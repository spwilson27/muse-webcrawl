Necessity (criminal law)

Necessity defense

Type: Affirmative defense (justification)

Also called: Choice of evils

Related: Duress, self-defense

In criminal law, necessity is a defense in which a defendant argues that they committed a crime to prevent a greater harm from occurring — a "choice of evils". The classic illustration is breaking into a cabin in a blizzard to avoid freezing to death.

Elements

Common requirements include: an imminent and greater harm to be averted, no reasonable legal alternative, a direct causal link between the crime and averting the harm, and that the defendant did not create the emergency. Most jurisdictions and the Model Penal Code (§3.02) refuse the defense for homicide; the English case R v Dudley and Stephens (1884), involving cannibalism after a shipwreck, famously rejected necessity as a defense to murder.

Applications

Necessity has been raised, usually unsuccessfully, in protest cases (trespass at weapons facilities, abortion-clinic blockades) and considered in medical and emergency contexts. It is distinguished from duress, where the threat comes from another person.
