Strain theory (sociology)

Strain theory is a theory of sociology and criminology, most associated with Robert K. Merton, that states that society puts pressure on individuals to achieve socially accepted goals — such as the "American Dream" of wealth and success — though it lacks the means to do so. This disjunction between goals and means produces strain, which may lead individuals to commit crimes, for example selling drugs or engaging in theft to gain financial security.

Merton's typology of adaptations to the goals–means disjunction.

Merton's formulation

Building on Émile Durkheim's concept of anomie, Merton argued in his 1938 essay "Social Structure and Anomie" that deviance results when a culture emphasises goals (especially material success) more strongly than the legitimate means of achieving them. He proposed five modes of individual adaptation, defined by whether the person accepts or rejects the cultural goals and the institutionalised means:

Later developments

Richard Cloward and Lloyd Ohlin extended strain theory with the concept of differential opportunity — access to illegitimate means is also unequally distributed. Albert Cohen applied strain to working-class delinquent subcultures ("status frustration"). Robert Agnew's general strain theory (1992) broadened the sources of strain beyond economic goals to include the loss of valued stimuli and the presence of negative stimuli, such as abuse or bullying, and examined the emotions (especially anger) linking strain to deviance.

Criticism

Critics note that strain theory explains economically motivated and lower-class crime better than white-collar or expressive crime, that most people experiencing strain do not offend, and that Merton's account assumes an unrealistic consensus about cultural goals.
