German Wine Route

The German Wine Route (German: Deutsche Weinstraße) is the oldest of Germany's tourist wine routes, established in 1935. It runs for about 85 kilometres through the Palatinate wine region in Rhineland-Palatinate, along the eastern edge of the Palatinate Forest, from the German Wine Gate at Schweigen-Rechtenbach on the French border in the south to Bockenheim an der Weinstraße in the north.

History

The route was created in 1935, during a period of economic difficulty and a record grape harvest, to promote Palatinate wine and tourism. Its ceremonial entrance, the German Wine Gate (Deutsches Weintor) at Schweigen-Rechtenbach, was built in 1936–37 — a massive stone gatehouse in the monumental style of the period. The northern end at Bockenheim is marked by the Haus der Deutschen Weinstraße, built in the 1990s. The opening of the route in October 1935 was accompanied by a motorcade of hundreds of vehicles from Bockenheim to Schweigen.

Route and sights

The road passes through a chain of wine villages at the foot of the Haardt mountains, including Bad Bergzabern, Edenkoben, Maikammer, Neustadt an der Weinstraße (the route's largest town, site of Hambach Castle), Deidesheim, Wachenheim, Bad Dürkheim (home of the Dürkheimer Wurstmarkt, one of the world's largest wine festivals, and a giant wine barrel restaurant), Freinsheim, and Grünstadt. The sheltered, mild climate — among the warmest in Germany — supports not only vines but almond, fig, and even lemon trees; the pink almond blossom along the route is a celebrated spring event.

Wine

The Palatinate is Germany's second-largest wine region, and the route is particularly associated with Riesling, grown here in great variety on sandstone and limestone soils, as well as Dornfelder and other red varieties, Weißburgunder (Pinot blanc), and Grauburgunder (Pinot gris). Numerous estates, wine taverns (Straußwirtschaften), and annual village wine festivals line the road.
