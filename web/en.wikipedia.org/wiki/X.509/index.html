X.509

X.509 is an International Telecommunication Union (ITU) standard defining the format of public key certificates. X.509 certificates are used in many Internet protocols, including TLS/SSL, which underlies HTTPS, the secure protocol for browsing the web. A certificate binds a public key to an identity such as a hostname or organisation, and is signed by a certificate authority (CA) so that relying parties can verify the binding.

Structure

An X.509 certificate contains, among other fields, a version number, a serial number, the signature algorithm identifier, the issuer name, a validity period, the subject name, the subject's public key information, and the CA's digital signature. Version 3 certificates support extensions, including Subject Alternative Names, key usage constraints and Basic Constraints identifying CA certificates. Certificates are encoded using ASN.1 DER and are commonly distributed in PEM format.

PKI and validation

X.509 is the certificate format of public key infrastructure (PKI). A client validates a certificate by building a chain of signatures up to a self-signed root certificate in its trust store, checking expiry and revocation status (via CRLs or OCSP). The Internet profile of X.509 is specified in RFC 5280.

History

The standard was first published in 1988 as part of the X.500 directory recommendations. Version 3, with extensibility, was standardised in 1996 in response to the needs of Internet use.
