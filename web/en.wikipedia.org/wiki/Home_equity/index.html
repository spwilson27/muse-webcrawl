Home equity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Home equity is the market value of a homeowner's unencumbered interest in their real property — that is, the difference between the home's fair market value and the outstanding balance of all liens on the property, most commonly a mortgage. Equity increases as the borrower pays down the mortgage principal and as the property appreciates in value; it decreases if the property falls in value or if the owner borrows further against it.

Calculation

Home equity equals the current market value of the property minus the total of all outstanding loans secured by it. For example, a home worth $400,000 with a remaining mortgage balance of $250,000 gives the owner $150,000 of equity. If house prices fall below the mortgage balance, the owner is in negative equity (sometimes called being "underwater").

Building equity

Equity normally grows through three channels: the initial down payment, scheduled mortgage repayments that reduce principal (and any additional lump-sum payments), and appreciation of the property's market value. It can also be increased by home improvements that raise the property's value.

Borrowing against equity

Homeowners can access equity through a home equity loan (a lump-sum second mortgage), a home equity line of credit (HELOC), or a cash-out refinancing. Because such loans are secured by the property, they usually carry lower interest rates than unsecured borrowing, but the home is at risk of foreclosure if payments are not made.
