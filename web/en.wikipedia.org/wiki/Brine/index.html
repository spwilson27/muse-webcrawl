Brine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Salt crystallising at the edge of a shallow brine evaporation pond
Salt crystallising at the edge of a shallow brine evaporation pond

Brine is a high-concentration solution of salt (usually sodium chloride) in water — from about 3.5% (seawater) up to saturation (about 26%). Natural brines occur in salt lakes, deep aquifers and hydrothermal systems; industrial brines are made or mined for food preservation (pickling, cheese, curing), chlor-alkali electrolysis, de-icing, oilfield operations and the extraction of lithium from salar brines in South America's 'lithium triangle'.

Occurrence and use

Brine is the world's chemical feedstock of first resort: solution-mined and evaporated for salt since prehistory (and the source of the salt taxes of empires), electrolysed for chlorine and caustic soda, and pumped as the carrier from which lithium, bromine, potash and magnesium are extracted. In food, brining both preserves and transforms — feta rests in brine, olives are cured in it, and pre-brined poultry is a modern supermarket staple.

Environmental issues

Desalination's reject brine — hotter and saltier than the sea — stresses coastal ecosystems at outfall scale; oilfield produced waters and salar lithium pumping (whether it draws down fresh aquifers at the Atacama is fiercely studied and litigated) make brine both resource and waste problem, sometimes in the same basin.
