Marxist humanism

Marxist humanism is a body of thought and political tradition that interprets Marx's philosophy as centred on human freedom, self-activity, and the overcoming of alienation, drawing especially on Karl Marx's early writings, above all the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, published in 1932.

Origins

The publication of Marx's early manuscripts revealed a "young Marx" concerned with estranged labour and species-being, in contrast to the structural readings of orthodox Marxism. Thinkers such as Raya Dunayevskaya in the United States, the Praxis School in Yugoslavia, Erich Fromm, and the French existential Marxists developed humanist readings. Dunayevskaya, who founded the organisation News and Letters Committees, called her distinctive philosophy "Marxist-Humanism" and placed the self-movement of oppressed groups — workers, Black Americans, women, and youth — at its centre.

Key themes

Marxist humanists emphasise alienation under capitalism (see Marx's theory of alienation), praxis as the unity of theory and practice, the dialectic of Hegel as reinterpreted by Marx, and opposition to bureaucratic "state-capitalist" societies that claimed Marxist legitimacy. The current opposed Louis Althusser's "theoretical anti-humanism", which treated humanism as ideology and the early Marx as pre-scientific.

Influence

Marxist humanism influenced the New Left, the Yugoslav Praxis philosophers and their journal, dissidents in Eastern Europe, and liberation movements. It remains a minority but persistent current within Marxist thought.
