Transportation Security Administration

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) is an agency of the United States Department of Homeland Security (DHS) that has authority over the security of transportation systems within and connecting to the United States. It was created in response to the September 11 attacks, which revealed weaknesses in existing airport security measures conducted by private firms.

History

The TSA was established by the Aviation and Transportation Security Act, signed on 19 November 2001, and moved from the Department of Transportation to the newly created DHS in 2003. It federalized passenger and baggage screening at U.S. commercial airports, deploying tens of thousands of Transportation Security Officers, and expanded into rail, mass transit, pipelines, and cargo security.

Programs

Notable programs include TSA PreCheck, a trusted-traveler scheme offering expedited screening; the Federal Air Marshal Service; airport checkpoint technologies such as millimeter-wave body scanners and computed-tomography carry-on scanners; and the REAL ID enforcement regime for identification at checkpoints, which took full effect in May 2025. The agency screens more than two million passengers on peak days across about 440 federalized airports.

Criticism

The TSA has been criticized over privacy, the effectiveness and cost of its screening technology, and covert-testing results showing high failure rates in detecting test weapons, though the agency says its layered security system mitigates individual weaknesses.
