Poor Law Amendment Act 1834

The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 (4 & 5 Will. 4. c. 76), sometimes known as the New Poor Law, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom passed by the Whig government of Earl Grey. It completely replaced the earlier legislation based on the Poor Relief Act 1601 and attempted to fundamentally change the system of alleviating poverty in England and Wales. The Act established a Poor Law Commission to oversee the national operation of the system, grouped parishes into Poor Law unions administered by boards of guardians, and prescribed that relief for the able-bodied be given only in workhouses operating on the principle of less eligibility.

Background

By the early 1830s the cost of poor relief had become a national political issue. The Speenhamland system of wage supplements, the Swing riots of 1830, and population pressure convinced ministers that the Old Poor Law encouraged idleness and burdened ratepayers. In 1832 the government appointed a Royal Commission into the Operation of the Poor Laws, dominated by the utilitarian Edwin Chadwick and the economist Nassau William Senior. Its 1834 report blamed the allowance system for pauperism and recommended centralised control, the abolition of outdoor relief for the able-bodied, and the creation of well-regulated workhouses in which conditions would deter all but the genuinely destitute.

Provisions

The Act provided for:

  • A central Poor Law Commission for England and Wales, with power to issue orders and regulations;
  • The amalgamation of parishes into unions, each administered by a board of guardians elected by ratepayers, with ex officio magistrates;
  • The construction or adaptation of a union workhouse, with relief for able-bodied paupers and their families to be given only within it;
  • The application of the workhouse test and the doctrine of less eligibility: the pauper's situation "shall not be made really or apparently so eligible as the situation of the independent labourer of the lowest class";
  • Reforms of bastardy law placing responsibility on the mother, and changes intended (largely unsuccessfully) to reform the laws of settlement.

Implementation and opposition

Assistant commissioners rolled the system out first in the rural south, but in the industrial north the Act provoked a mass Anti-Poor Law movement, with riots, short-time committees, and resistance led by figures such as Richard Oastler and Joseph Rayner Stephens; the new system fed into the grievances of Chartism. In practice the Commission could not abolish outdoor relief: most paupers continued to be relieved at home, and workhouse populations consisted mainly of the elderly, the sick, and children. The 1845 Andover workhouse scandal, in which starving inmates were found gnawing the bones they were set to crush, discredited the Commission, which was replaced by the Poor Law Board in 1847, answerable to Parliament.

Legacy

The 1834 Act shaped British social welfare for a century: its deterrent philosophy, institutional architecture (hundreds of union workhouses), and administrative structures survived until the boards of guardians were abolished in 1929 and the Poor Law itself was repealed by the National Assistance Act 1948. Historians continue to debate its effects — credited by its framers with cutting relief expenditure, it is remembered in popular culture, notably through Dickens's Oliver Twist (1838), as a byword for bureaucratic cruelty toward the poor.
