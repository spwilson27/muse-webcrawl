Mezcal

Mezcal (from Nahuatl mexcalli, "oven-cooked agave") is a Mexican distilled alcoholic beverage made from the heart of the agave plant. It is produced under a denomination of origin that covers nine states, above all Oaxaca, and is characterised by the smoky flavour imparted by roasting the agave in earthen pits before fermentation and distillation.

Production

Mature agave plants — most commonly espadín (Agave angustifolia), but also wild species such as tobalá, madrecuixe and tepeztate — are harvested for their hearts, or piñas. Traditionally the piñas are cooked for several days in conical stone-lined pits heated with hot rocks and covered with earth, then crushed, often by a stone wheel (tahona) pulled by a horse or ox. The mash is fermented in open wooden vats and distilled in copper or, for mezcal ancestral, clay pot stills. The master distiller is called a maestro mezcalero.

Classification

Since 2016 Mexican regulations distinguish three categories: industrial mezcal, mezcal artesanal and mezcal ancestral, the last restricted to the most traditional tools and methods. Styles include joven (unaged), reposado (rested in wood) and añejo (aged). A worm of the gusano de maguey (a moth larva) is sometimes added to bottles, a practice that began as a mid-twentieth-century marketing device.

Relation to tequila

Tequila is legally a type of mezcal: it is made from blue Weber agave in a defined region around Tequila, Jalisco, using more industrialised processes. As a saying puts it, all tequila is mezcal, but not all mezcal is tequila. Mezcal has enjoyed a rapid growth in international popularity since the 2010s, raising concerns about sustainability and the pressures on wild agave populations.
