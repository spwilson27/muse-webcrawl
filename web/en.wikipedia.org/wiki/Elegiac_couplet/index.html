Elegiac couplet
FormDactylic hexameter + dactylic pentameter
TraditionAncient Greek and Latin poetry

The elegiac couplet is a poetic form used by Greek lyric poets and extensively by Roman poets for a wide range of subjects. It consists of a line of dactylic hexameter followed by a line of dactylic pentameter (two half-lines of 2½ feet).

Use in antiquity

Early Greek elegy (Archilochus, Tyrtaeus, Mimnermus, Solon) covered war, politics and love; Hellenistic poets like Callimachus refined it. In Rome, Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius and above all Ovid made the couplet the vehicle of love elegy, while Ovid also used it for the Fasti, Tristia and epigrams. Martial's epigrams are largely in elegiacs.

Later use

The form was imitated in German by Goethe and Schiller (the Xenien) and adapted loosely in English; Coleridge and others experimented with English hexameter-pentameter couplets. Despite the name, elegiacs were not confined to lament — though the metre's falling close suited mourning and epitaph.
