Expulsion of the Acadians

The Expulsion of the Acadians (French: Le Grand Dérangement, "the Great Upheaval") was the forced removal by the British of the Acadian people — French-speaking Catholic settlers — from the present-day Canadian Maritime provinces between 1755 and 1764, during and after the French and Indian War. More than 11,500 Acadians were deported, their homes and farms burned, and their lands given to New England Planters and later Loyalists. Many thousands died of disease, starvation or shipwreck.

Background

Acadia had changed hands repeatedly between France and Britain before the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) ceded peninsular Nova Scotia to Britain. The Acadians, numbering about 15,000 by the 1750s, wished to remain neutral — calling themselves the "neutral French" — and refused an unconditional oath of allegiance to the British Crown, fearing they would be forced to bear arms against France and against their Mi'kmaq allies. On the eve of the Seven Years' War, Governor Charles Lawrence and the Nova Scotia Council judged their neutrality untenable.

The deportations

After the capture of Fort Beauséjour in June 1755, the deportation began. At Grand-Pré on 5 September 1755, Colonel John Winslow read the deportation order to Acadian men and boys assembled in the church, declaring their lands, houses and livestock forfeit to the Crown. Families were embarked on transport ships for the Thirteen Colonies, where many were received coldly and held as virtual prisoners. Further campaigns in 1758 removed the populations of Île Saint-Jean (Prince Edward Island) and Île Royale (Cape Breton); three transports were lost at sea with heavy loss of life, including the Duke William and Violet, which sank in December 1758 with some 750 Acadians aboard.

Aftermath and diaspora

Deportees scattered to the American colonies, England and France; some fled to Quebec or hid with Mi'kmaq assistance. Beginning in 1764 Acadians were permitted to return in small groups, resettling along the coasts of present-day New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, though their fertile dykelands had been taken. Others made their way to French Louisiana, arriving notably in waves organised from France in 1785; there their descendants became the Cajuns. Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847) made the expulsion famous worldwide. In 2003 the Canadian government issued a Royal Proclamation acknowledging the wrongs of the deportation, and 13 July was designated a day of commemoration.
