Feminist literary criticism

Feminist literary criticism is literary analysis informed by feminist theory, examining how literature represents women, how texts by women have been received and marginalised, and how gender shapes both writing and reading. It emerged as a distinct critical movement alongside the second-wave feminism of the late 1960s and 1970s.

Development

Precursors include Virginia Woolf's A Room of One's Own (1929), with its argument that a woman needs money and a room to write fiction, and Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949). Foundational works of the modern movement include Kate Millett's Sexual Politics (1970), which attacked the sexism of male canonical authors; Elaine Showalter's A Literature of Their Own (1977) and her concept of "gynocriticism", the study of women's writing as its own tradition; and Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar's The Madwoman in the Attic (1979), which read nineteenth-century women writers through figures such as Bertha Mason, the confined double of Jane Eyre.

Approaches

Feminist criticism has included the recovery of forgotten women authors and the re-evaluation of the canon; the analysis of stereotypes and images of women in male-authored texts; psychoanalytic, Marxist and poststructuralist variants; French feminist theory associated with Hélène Cixous, Luce Irigaray and Julia Kristeva; and, from the 1980s, critiques from Black, postcolonial and lesbian feminists — such as bell hooks and Chandra Talpade Mohanty — who challenged the movement's focus on white, middle-class experience. Its methods and questions have become a standard part of literary studies.
