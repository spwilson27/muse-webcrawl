Wolf interval

In music theory, the wolf interval (sometimes wolf fifth) is a particularly discordant musical interval produced when the notes available in a tuning system cannot be played in all keys without producing an out-of-tune interval — most famously in quarter-comma meantone temperament.

Origin

In meantone tunings, most fifths are narrowed slightly to make major thirds pure. Because the circle of twelve fifths does not close (it falls short by the meantone comma), the remaining "fifth" — typically between G♯ and E♭ on a standard keyboard — must absorb the discrepancy, becoming so wide and dissonant that it was said to "howl like a wolf".

In quarter-comma meantone this wolf fifth is about 737 cents, far from the just fifth of 702 cents, and unusable in practice; keys requiring it were avoided. Other temperaments such as Pythagorean tuning have their own wolf (a very narrow fifth).

Historical practice

Keyboard players coped by limiting modulation to a set of well-tempered keys, splitting keys (split sharps) to add extra notes, or adopting circulating temperaments such as Werckmeister and later equal temperament, which distribute the comma so no single wolf remains — at the cost of no interval except the octave being perfectly just.
