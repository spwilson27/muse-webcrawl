Bioelectrogenesis

The electric eel generates strong electric fields using specialized electrocytes.

Bioelectrogenesis is the generation of electricity by living organisms, a phenomenon studied in the field of electrophysiology. Electrogenesis is known in certain bacteria and in some animals, most famously the electric fishes, and is distinct from electroreception, the ability to sense electric fields.

Mechanism

In animals, electricity is produced by specialized cells called electrocytes, which are modified muscle or nerve cells stacked in series within an electric organ. Like other cells, electrocytes maintain an electrochemical gradient across their membranes using ATP-driven ion pumps. When triggered by a nervous signal, ion channels open and the cells discharge in near synchrony; because thousands of electrocytes are arranged in series and parallel, their small individual voltages sum to produce large external discharges.

Electric fish

Strongly electric fish include the electric eel (Electrophorus), which can produce discharges of several hundred volts used to stun prey and deter predators, the electric catfish, and torpedo rays. Many more species are weakly electric, generating low-voltage fields used for electrolocation and communication in murky water. Charles Darwin cited electric organs as an apparent difficulty for his theory, since they seemed to require many coordinated intermediate steps.

Microbial electrogenesis

Certain bacteria, such as species of Geobacter and Shewanella, transfer electrons to external surfaces as part of their metabolism. This extracellular electron transfer is exploited in microbial fuel cells, which use bacteria to generate electric current from organic matter in wastewater or sediments.
