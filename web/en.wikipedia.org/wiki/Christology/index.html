Christology

Christology (from Greek Χριστός Khristós and -λογία, -logia), literally "the understanding of Christ," is the study of the nature (person) and work of Jesus Christ. It studies Jesus Christ's humanity and divinity, and the relation between these two natures — and the role he plays in salvation.

Development

Early Christians, working within Jewish monotheism, confessed Jesus as Lord and applied divine titles to him. Disputes over how Jesus could be both God and man produced the great Christological controversies of the 4th and 5th centuries. The Council of Nicaea (325) affirmed that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father, against Arianism. The Council of Ephesus (431) condemned Nestorius and affirmed Mary as Theotokos ("God-bearer").

Chalcedonian definition

The Council of Chalcedon (451) defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human, united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation" — the doctrine of the hypostatic union. Churches that rejected Chalcedon, holding instead to a miaphysite formula, form today's Oriental Orthodox communion.

Approaches

Scholars distinguish Christology "from above" (beginning with Christ's divinity and the Logos) from Christology "from below" (beginning with the historical Jesus and his human life). Modern Christology also engages the "quest for the historical Jesus" and the diversity of New Testament Christologies.
