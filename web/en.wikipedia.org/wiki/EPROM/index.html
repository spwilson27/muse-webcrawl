EPROM

An EPROM (erasable programmable read-only memory) is a type of programmable read-only memory (PROM) chip that retains its data when its power supply is switched off. Data can be erased by exposing the chip to strong ultraviolet light through a quartz window on top of the package, and then reprogrammed. EPROMs were invented by Dov Frohman of Intel in 1971.

Operation

An EPROM cell consists of a floating-gate transistor. Programming applies a relatively high voltage to trap electrons on the floating gate, changing the transistor's threshold voltage so that a bit reads as 0 or 1. Because the gate is electrically isolated, the charge can remain for ten years or more. Erasing requires ultraviolet light (typically at 253.7 nm from a mercury-vapor lamp) to give the trapped electrons enough energy to escape; erasure takes roughly 15–30 minutes, and the whole chip is erased at once — selective erasure is not possible. The quartz window is usually covered with an opaque label to prevent accidental erasure by sunlight or fluorescent light.

Applications and successors

EPROMs such as the Intel 1702 (256 bytes, 1971) and the widely used 27xx series (e.g., 2716, 2764, 27256) stored firmware in early microcomputers, embedded systems, and video game cartridges. One-time programmable (OTP) versions in windowless plastic packages lowered costs. Electrically erasable EEPROM and, later, flash memory largely displaced EPROMs because they can be erased and rewritten in-circuit without ultraviolet light or removal from the board.
