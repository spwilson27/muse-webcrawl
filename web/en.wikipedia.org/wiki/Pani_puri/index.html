Pani puri

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pani puri (also called golgappa, puchka, gupchup, or phuchka, depending on region) is a popular street food from the Indian subcontinent. It consists of a small, round, hollow, crisp-fried puri that is cracked open, filled with a mixture of potatoes, chickpeas or sprouted beans, onions, and spices, then dunked in flavoured water (pani) — typically a tangy, spicy blend of mint, coriander, green chilli, and tamarind — and eaten whole in one bite.

Regional names and variations

In Delhi and Haryana it is golgappa; in West Bengal, Bangladesh, and Assam it is puchka or fuchka, usually with a tamarind-heavy water and a mashed potato filling often made with dried white peas; in Odisha it is gupchup; in Mumbai it is pani puri and may use ragda (white pea curry) as the filling. Variations include sweetened water, yoghurt-topped dahi puri, and the sev-covered sev puri. Regional legend in the Magadha region associates an early version with Draupadi of the Mahabharata, who is said to have improvised the dish from leftover dough and vegetables.

Preparation

The puris are rolled from a stiff dough of semolina or wheat flour and deep-fried so that they puff into hollow spheres. The vendor (chaatwala) assembles each piece to order, adjusting the balance of spicy, sour, and sweet waters to the customer's taste; customers customarily eat standing at the stall, one puri at a time, as the shell softens within seconds of filling.

Cultural presence

Pani puri is among the most beloved items of Indian chaat (savoury snack) culture, sold by street vendors and sweet shops alike, and widely sold frozen or as ready-to-fry kits by food companies for home preparation worldwide.
