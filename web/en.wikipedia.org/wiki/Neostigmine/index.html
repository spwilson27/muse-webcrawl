Neostigmine

Neostigmine, sold under the brand name Prostigmin among others, is a medication used to treat myasthenia gravis, Ogilvie syndrome (acute colonic pseudo-obstruction), and urinary retention without obstruction. In anaesthesia it is widely used at the end of surgery to reverse the effects of non-depolarising neuromuscular blocking agents such as rocuronium and vecuronium. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Drug classAcetylcholinesterase inhibitor (parasympathomimetic)
RoutesIntravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, oral
First synthesised1931 (by Aeschlimann and Reinert)
Legal statusPrescription only

Mechanism of action

Neostigmine is a reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor: it carbamylates the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine, thereby increasing acetylcholine levels at cholinergic synapses, including the neuromuscular junction. As a quaternary ammonium compound it is poorly absorbed from the gut and does not significantly cross the blood–brain barrier, so its effects are mainly peripheral.

Medical uses

In anaesthesia, neostigmine is co-administered with an antimuscarinic such as glycopyrrolate or atropine to counteract its unwanted muscarinic effects (bradycardia, salivation, bronchoconstriction) while reversing muscle relaxants. In myasthenia gravis it improves muscle strength by enhancing neuromuscular transmission. It is also used for paralytic ileus and acute colonic pseudo-obstruction, where it stimulates gut motility.

Side effects

Common adverse effects are muscarinic: nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, increased salivation and bronchial secretions, miosis, and slow heart rate. Excessive dosing can cause a cholinergic crisis with worsening muscle weakness. Neostigmine is the chemical analogue of physostigmine, shortened and modified for peripheral use.
