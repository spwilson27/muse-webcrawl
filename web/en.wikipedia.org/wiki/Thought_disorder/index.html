Thought disorder

A thought disorder (TD) is a disturbance in the organisation and processing of thoughts that affects language, communication, and thought content. People with a thought disorder may have trouble organising their thoughts or expressing them coherently, resulting in speech that is hard to follow. Thought disorder is a hallmark feature of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders, but it can also occur in mood disorders, dementia, and other conditions.

Types

Many specific phenomena are grouped under thought disorder, among them:

  • Derailment (loose associations) — speech that drifts from one topic to another with only loose or no connections.
  • Tangentiality — answers that veer off-topic and never answer the question asked.
  • Flight of ideas — rapid, pressured shifts of topic, typical of mania.
  • Circumstantiality — overly detailed speech that eventually reaches its point.
  • Word salad (schizophasia) — completely incoherent strings of words.
  • Neologisms — invented words; clanging — speech driven by word sounds rather than meaning.
  • Poverty of speech (alogia) — greatly reduced speech output.
  • Thought blocking — abrupt halts in the train of thought.

Formal thought disorder

Clinicians distinguish formal thought disorder — abnormalities in the form and organisation of thought, inferred from speech — from disorders of thought content such as delusions. Rating instruments such as Andreasen's Thought, Language and Communication (TLC) scale quantify positive thought disorder (e.g., derailment, illogicality) and negative thought disorder (e.g., poverty of speech and content). Mild thought disorder also occurs in healthy people under stress or fatigue, and assessment is sensitive to culture and language.

Causes and treatment

Thought disorder is most strongly associated with schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, where it predicts poorer functioning. It reflects disturbances in semantic processing, executive function, and working memory. Treatment targets the underlying condition, usually with antipsychotic medication and psychosocial interventions; speech and communication often improve as psychosis resolves.
