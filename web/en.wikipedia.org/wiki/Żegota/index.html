Żegota (pronounced [ʐɛˈɡɔta]) was the codename of the Council to Aid Jews (Polish: Rada Pomocy Żydom), an underground organisation of the Polish Underground State during the German occupation of Poland in the Second World War. Active from December 1942 until 1945, it was the only government-sponsored organisation in German-occupied Europe set up specifically to rescue Jews during the Holocaust, providing money, false documents, housing and medical care to Jews in hiding.

Żegota's document workshop produced thousands of forged Kennkarten and certificates for Jews living under assumed identities.

Origins

Żegota grew out of the Provisional Committee to Aid Jews, founded in September 1942 by the writer Zofia Kossak-Szczucka and the democratic activist Wanda Krahelska-Filipowicz after the mass deportations from the Warsaw Ghetto to Treblinka that summer. On 4 December 1942 the Government Delegation for Poland formalised the work as the Council to Aid Jews, codenamed "Żegota" — a fictitious Polish surname chosen as cover. Its council brought together representatives of Polish democratic, Catholic, socialist and peasant parties alongside the Jewish National Committee and the socialist Bund, an unusual cross-party and Polish–Jewish cooperation.

Activities

Funded largely by the Polish government-in-exile (with additional support from Jewish organisations abroad), Żegota maintained departments for housing, finance, medicine, false documents and propaganda, and regional councils in Kraków, Lwów, Wilno and other cities. Its children's section in Warsaw, headed by social worker Irena Sendler, smuggled some 2,500 Jewish children out of the ghetto and placed them in Polish families, orphanages and convents, recording their real names on slips of paper buried in jars so families might be reunited after the war. In Warsaw alone Żegota is estimated to have assisted several thousand Jews directly, and tens of thousands received its documents or funds across occupied Poland. Helpers faced the death penalty — in occupied Poland, aid to Jews was punishable by death for the rescuer's entire household — and many Żegota members were arrested and murdered, including its first chairman, Julian Grobelny, who died of tuberculosis in hiding in 1944.

Recognition

After the war, Żegota's work was commemorated in Poland and Israel; many of its members, including Sendler, were recognised by Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations. A monument to Żegota stands in Warsaw near the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews.
