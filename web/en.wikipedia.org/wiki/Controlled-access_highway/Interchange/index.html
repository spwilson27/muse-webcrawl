Interchange (road)

In the field of road transport, an interchange is a road junction that typically uses grade separation, and one or more ramps, to permit traffic on at least one highway to pass through the junction without directly crossing any other traffic stream. Interchanges are the defining access points of controlled-access highways: unlike a simple intersection, an interchange allows vehicles to transfer between roads without stopping for crossing traffic.

Types

Common configurations include:

  • Cloverleaf interchanges, with four loop ramps for left turns — compact but subject to weaving between adjacent loops;
  • Stack interchanges, with directional ramps on multiple levels, offering high capacity at high construction cost;
  • Diamond interchanges, where a minor road crosses over or under a freeway with simple ramps, often signalized at the surface street;
  • Trumpet interchanges, used where a freeway terminates at another road;
  • Diverging diamond interchanges, which temporarily cross traffic to the opposite side of the road to simplify left turns;
  • Roundabout interchanges, combining ramps with a roundabout on the crossing road.

System and service interchanges

A system interchange connects two freeways, with all movements free-flowing, whereas a service interchange connects a freeway to a lower-class road such as an arterial. Interchanges are spaced according to context — around one mile apart in dense urban areas and several miles apart in rural areas — balancing access against the turbulence caused by merging and diverging traffic.

History

Grade-separated junctions appeared with the earliest motorways in the 1920s and 1930s; the first cloverleaf in the United States opened in 1929 in Woodbridge, New Jersey. Interchange design has since been standardized in guides such as the AASHTO "Green Book".
