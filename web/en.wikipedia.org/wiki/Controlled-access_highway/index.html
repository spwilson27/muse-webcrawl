Controlled-access highway

A controlled-access highway is a type of highway that has been designed for high-speed vehicular traffic, with all traffic flow — ingress and egress — regulated. Common English terms are freeway, motorway, and expressway. Such roads provide an unhindered flow of traffic, with no traffic signals, intersections at grade, or direct property access.

Design features

Controlled-access highways are characterized by:

  • Opposing directions of travel separated by a median or barrier;
  • Access only via grade-separated interchanges with entrance and exit ramps;
  • No direct driveway connections to adjacent properties;
  • Prohibition of slower modes such as pedestrians, bicycles, and horse-drawn vehicles;
  • Multiple lanes, wide shoulders, and gentle grades and curves designed for sustained high speeds.

History and terminology

The first purpose-built high-speed roads included Italy's Autostrada dei Laghi (opened 1924) and Germany's AVUS and later Reichsautobahn network. In the United States, parkways in the New York area preceded the Interstate Highway System, authorized in 1956, which standardized freeway design nationwide. The United Kingdom uses the term "motorway," and special regulations apply to such roads; in Australia "freeway" and "motorway" are both used.

Safety and impact

Per vehicle-kilometre, controlled-access highways are generally the safest class of road because they eliminate crossing traffic and head-on collisions. Criticisms include land consumption, noise, community severance, and induced demand for car travel.
