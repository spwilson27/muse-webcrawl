Interstate Highway System

The Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways, commonly known as the Interstate Highway System, is a network of controlled-access highways that forms part of the National Highway System in the United States. Authorized by the Federal Aid Highway Act of 1956, the system was championed by President Dwight D. Eisenhower, influenced by his experiences with the 1919 Army transcontinental convoy and the German autobahnen. Federal funding covered 90% of construction costs, with states owning and operating the roads.

Network and standards

The system totals about 46,876 miles (75,440 km), making it the second-longest controlled-access network in the world after China's. Interstates must meet uniform standards: controlled access, at least two lanes in each direction separated by a median or barrier, design speeds of 50–70 mph depending on terrain, and grade-separated interchanges. Routes are identified by the red-white-and-blue shield. Primary routes have one- or two-digit numbers — odd numbers run north–south increasing eastward, even numbers east–west increasing northward — while three-digit routes are auxiliary spurs and loops of a parent route.

History

Construction began in 1956 and the original planned network was declared complete in 1992 with the opening of I-70 through Glenwood Canyon, Colorado, though additions continue. The final cost was far above the original $25 billion estimate. Though Interstates make up only a little over 1% of U.S. road mileage, they carry roughly a quarter of all vehicle traffic.

Impact

The system transformed American commerce, commuting, and suburbanization, provided for military mobility, and standardized road design nationwide. It also drew criticism for displacing urban neighborhoods — disproportionately low-income and minority communities — during freeway construction, prompting freeway revolts in many cities and later removals of some urban segments.
