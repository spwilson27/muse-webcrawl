Gonzo journalism

Gonzo journalism is a style of journalism that is written without claims of objectivity, often including the reporter as part of the story using a first-person narrative. The word "gonzo" is believed to have been first used in 1970 to describe an article about the Kentucky Derby by Hunter S. Thompson, who later popularized the style.

Characteristics

Gonzo journalism involves an approach to accuracy that concerns the reporting of the writer's personal experiences and emotions, as opposed to traditional journalism which favours a detached style and relies on facts or quotations that can be verified by third parties. It favours style over accuracy and uses personal experience and social critique. The use of sarcasm, humour, exaggeration, and profanity is common.

Key works

Thompson's Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas (1971) and Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72 are the canonical examples. The term became associated with Thompson's illustrator Ralph Steadman, whose grotesque drawings accompanied much of the work. Gonzo influenced later forms of participatory and immersive reporting and much blog- and memoir-driven nonfiction.
