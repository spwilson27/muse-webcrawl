Michael Scot

Michael Scot (Latin: Michaelus Scotus; c. 1175 – c. 1232) was a Scottish mathematician, scholar and physician who worked at the court of the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II in Sicily. He was among the most important translators of Arabic scientific works into Latin in the early 13th century and acquired a lasting popular reputation as a wizard.

Life and work

Scot studied at Oxford and Paris and worked in Toledo, Spain, where he learned Arabic and translated works of Aristotle and his commentators, including Averroes, as well as al-Bitruji's treatise on astronomy. Around 1227 he joined Frederick II's court as astrologer and physician. Revised translations associated with him — notably of Aristotle's books on animals and of Averroes' commentaries — shaped Latin scholastic natural philosophy. He also wrote original works on astrology, divination and physiognomy, later printed as the Liber particularis, Liber de signis and related treatises, and is sometimes credited with an early Latin engagement with Fibonacci's mathematics at court.

Legend

After his death Scot's knowledge of Arabic learning and astrology transformed in folklore into necromantic power: Dante placed him among the diviners in the Inferno, Boccaccio treated him as a magician, and in Scottish tradition "Michael Scot the wizard" performed feats such as splitting the Eildon Hills. Sir Walter Scott drew on these tales in The Lay of the Last Minstrel.
