An air raid shelter is a structure built to protect people from enemy air raids and bomb blast. Shelters range from small household garden structures and indoor steel tables to deep underground public bunkers capable of holding thousands. They became a prominent feature of civil defense during the Second World War, when strategic bombing campaigns against cities such as London, Berlin, Hamburg, and Tokyo made mass civilian protection a state priority.

British shelters

In Britain, the government distributed the Anderson shelter — named after Sir John Anderson — from 1939. Made of corrugated steel panels bolted together and half-buried in gardens under a covering of earth, an Anderson shelter could house up to six people and was supplied free to low-income households; about 3.6 million were erected or distributed. Because many households lacked gardens, the indoor Morrison shelter, a heavy steel-topped table under which a family could sleep, was introduced in 1941. In London, residents famously sheltered in Underground stations, and eight deep-level shelters were built beneath tube lines, each intended for up to 8,000 people; several were later used for storage, and one at Clapham South became a hostel and archive.

Shelters in Germany and elsewhere

Nazi Germany built massive reinforced-concrete flak towers (Flaktürme) in Berlin, Hamburg, and Vienna that combined anti-aircraft artillery with shelter space for tens of thousands; their walls proved so resistant that several survive today. Purpose-built public bunkers, cellar shelters, and the Luftschutzkeller system were administered under the Reich Air Protection League. In Japan, most civilians relied on neighborhood trenches and dugouts, which offered little protection during the firebombing of 1945. During the Cold War, air raid shelter programs merged into civil defense against nuclear attack, producing the fallout shelter programs of the United States and the comprehensive Swiss and Swedish shelter systems, under which Switzerland built places for its entire population.

Modern use

Air raid shelters remain in use where populations face missile and bombing threats: Israeli homes and public buildings include protected spaces (mamad), Kyiv's metro stations served as shelters during the Russian invasion of Ukraine from 2022, and Finland maintains a large network of civil defense shelters. Historic shelters in Britain and Germany are preserved as museums and memorials.
