Grey goo (also spelled gray goo) is a hypothetical global catastrophic scenario involving molecular nanotechnology in which out-of-control self-replicating machines consume all biomass on Earth while building many more of themselves—a scenario that has been called ecophagy ("eating the environment", more literally, "eating the habitation").

The original idea assumed machines were designed to have this capability, while popularizations assumed that machines might somehow gain this capability by accident.

History

The term was coined by molecular nanotechnology pioneer Eric Drexler in his 1986 book Engines of Creation. Drexler warned that self-replicating assemblers, if released and lacking in safeguards, could theoretically out-compete natural life. In 2004, Drexler stated that the scenario was unlikely, since practical nanotechnology development had moved toward designs that do not involve free-ranging replicators.

Risk assessment

Self-replicating machines of the macroscopic variety are sometimes called "clanking replicators". Analysts generally consider a grey goo apocalypse unlikely with foreseeable technology, though the scenario remains a popular cautionary tale in discussions of existential risk and nanotechnology governance. Prince Charles famously expressed concern about grey goo in 2003, prompting a Royal Society review of nanoscience.

In fiction

Grey goo scenarios appear in science fiction such as Michael Crichton's novel Prey (2002) and numerous video games, often as an analogue to runaway artificial intelligence or invasive biological organisms.
