The activated sludge process is a type of biological wastewater treatment in which a suspended culture of microorganisms — the activated sludge — is mixed with sewage or industrial wastewater under aerated conditions so that the microbes oxidise dissolved and colloidal organic pollutants. It is the most widely used method of secondary treatment worldwide.

Aeration basins, where air is bubbled through wastewater mixed with activated sludge flocs.

Process

Primary-treated wastewater enters an aeration basin, where air (or oxygen) is supplied and the liquid mixes with recycled sludge containing dense flocs of bacteria and protozoa. Microorganisms metabolise organic matter (measured as BOD), converting it to carbon dioxide, water and new biomass. The mixture then flows to a secondary clarifier, where the flocs settle: most settled sludge is returned to the aeration basin (return activated sludge), the excess is wasted for further processing (waste activated sludge), and the clarified effluent is discharged or further treated.

History and variants

The process was developed in England in 1913–1914 by Edward Ardern and W. T. Lockett at Davyhulme, Manchester. Variants include extended aeration, sequencing batch reactors (SBR), oxidation ditches, membrane bioreactors (MBR), and configurations for biological nutrient removal of nitrogen and phosphorus.
