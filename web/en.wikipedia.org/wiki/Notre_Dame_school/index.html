Notre Dame school

The Notre Dame school is the term music historians give to the group of composers working at or near the Cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris from about 1160 to 1250, and to the polyphonic music they produced. It marks the first great flowering of composed, notated polyphony in Western music, centred on the forms of organum, conductus, and the early motet.

Principal composers

Almost all knowledge of the composers comes from an anonymous English student of the late 13th century, known as Anonymous IV, who names Léonin (Leoninus, fl. c. 1150–1201), composer of the Magnus liber organi ("Great Book of Organum"), and his successor Pérotin (Perotinus, fl. c. 1200), who revised Léonin's work and composed organa for three and four voices, such as Viderunt omnes and Sederunt principes (c. 1198–1199).

Music and innovations

The school's most important innovation was rhythmic notation: the system of rhythmic modes allowed the precise coordination of multiple independent voices for the first time in the West. The later Notre Dame repertory replaced the chant-bearing tenor during melismatic passages with rhythmically patterned segments in discant style (the clausula), and excerpts of clausulae with newly added texts gave rise to the motet, the dominant learned genre of the later Middle Ages.

Manuscripts

The repertory survives chiefly in three great manuscripts: the Wolfenbüttel codices W1 and W2 and the Florence manuscript (Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Pluteo 29.1).
