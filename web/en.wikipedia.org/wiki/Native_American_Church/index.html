Native American Church

Incorporated: 1918, Oklahoma
Sacrament: Peyote (Lophophora williamsii)
Membership: Estimated 250,000–300,000
Legal protection: American Indian Religious Freedom Act amendments (1994)

The Native American Church (NAC), also called the Peyote Religion or the Peyote Road, is a Native American religious movement that combines Indigenous spiritual traditions with elements of Christianity, centred on the sacramental use of the peyote cactus in all-night prayer meetings. It is among the most widespread Indigenous religions in North America, with adherents in many tribes across the United States, Canada and Mexico.

Beliefs and practice

Meetings, usually held in a tipi from sundown to sunrise, are led by a "roadman" and involve prayer, singing with a water drum and gourd rattle, contemplation, and the eating of peyote, which contains the psychedelic alkaloid mescaline. Teachings emphasise moral living — the "Peyote Road" of sobriety, family responsibility, hard work and care for others — and the church has been widely credited with helping members overcome alcoholism.

History and law

Peyote use spread from Mexico into the southern Plains in the late nineteenth century; the church was formally incorporated in Oklahoma in 1918 to protect the practice. Despite the U.S. Supreme Court's 1990 Employment Division v. Smith decision allowing Oregon to deny benefits to peyote users, Congress amended the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1994 to protect the ceremonial use of peyote by members of federally recognised tribes.
