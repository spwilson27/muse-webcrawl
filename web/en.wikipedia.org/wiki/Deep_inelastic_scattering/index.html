Deep inelastic scattering

An electron probes a proton by exchanging a high-energy virtual photon, revealing its quark constituents.

Deep inelastic scattering is a process in particle physics in which a high-energy lepton — an electron, muon or neutrino — scatters off a nucleon (proton or neutron) with large energy and momentum transfer, breaking the nucleon apart. Because the exchanged virtual photon has a very short wavelength, it resolves the nucleon's internal structure. Experiments in deep inelastic scattering at SLAC in the late 1960s provided the first direct evidence that protons contain point-like constituents, the quarks.

Kinematics

The process is described by the Lorentz-invariant variables Q², the squared four-momentum transfer, and the Bjorken scaling variable x, the fraction of the nucleon's momentum carried by the struck constituent in the infinite-momentum frame, together with the inelasticity y. Cross-sections are expressed through structure functions F1(x, Q²) and F2(x, Q²), which encode the momentum distributions of quarks and gluons inside the nucleon — the parton distribution functions.

Scaling and QCD

The SLAC experiments of 1967–1973, led by Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall and Richard Taylor — who shared the 1990 Nobel Prize in Physics for the work — found that the structure functions depended almost entirely on x and hardly at all on Q², a phenomenon called Bjorken scaling. This was precisely what the quark–parton model predicted if the proton's charge was carried by nearly free point-like particles. Later, more precise experiments at CERN and at the HERA electron–proton collider at DESY (1992–2007) revealed small systematic violations of scaling, which are quantitatively explained by quantum chromodynamics through the DGLAP evolution equations, as quarks radiate gluons and gluons split into quark–antiquark pairs.

Deep inelastic scattering remains the principal source of information on parton distribution functions, which are indispensable inputs for predictions at hadron colliders such as the Large Hadron Collider. A future Electron–Ion Collider in the United States is designed to extend these studies to three-dimensional imaging of the nucleon's quark and gluon structure.
