Carbon farming is the set of agricultural methods aimed at sequestering atmospheric carbon dioxide into the soil and in crop roots, wood and leaves. The goal is to increase the rate at which carbon is drawn down and stored in soils and vegetation, thereby mitigating climate change, while often improving soil health, water retention and yields. Carbon farming is a component of regenerative agriculture and is closely related to climate-smart agriculture.

Methods

Practices commonly classed as carbon farming include:

  • Cover cropping and reduced tillage — keeping living roots in the ground and minimising soil disturbance to build soil organic matter.
  • Agroforestry and silvopasture — integrating trees with crops or livestock, storing carbon in woody biomass as well as soils.
  • Managed grazing — rotational or adaptive multi-paddock grazing intended to stimulate grassland root growth.
  • Biochar — incorporating charcoal produced by pyrolysis of biomass, which can persist in soils for centuries.
  • Compost and manure application, mulching, and restoration of degraded land and wetlands.

Carbon credits and policy

Carbon farmers may earn income by selling carbon credits to buyers seeking to offset emissions, with sequestration verified under various voluntary standards. Australia's Carbon Farming Initiative (2011), later folded into the Emissions Reduction Fund, was among the first national schemes to credit soil and vegetation carbon. The European Union has developed a Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) certification framework, and such practices feature in "4 per 1000" international initiatives promoting soil carbon.

Criticism and uncertainty

Scientists caution that soil carbon gains can saturate, may be reversed by later land-use change (a permanence problem), and are difficult and costly to measure precisely at farm scale. Some analyses argue the total mitigation potential of soil sequestration is modest relative to emissions from fossil fuels, and that offset claims risk overstating benefits.
