The Cape Town Treaty, formally the Convention on International Interests in Mobile Equipment, is an international treaty concluded in 2001 under the auspices of UNIDROIT (the International Institute for the Unification of Private Law) and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). It is designed to standardise transactions involving movable property — most importantly aircraft — by creating an international legal framework for security interests, leasing and conditional sale of high-value mobile equipment that routinely crosses national borders.

The treaty makes the rights of financiers and lessors more predictable and enforceable across jurisdictions, thereby reducing the risk and cost of financing mobile equipment. Its economic benefits, particularly for airlines in developing countries that can borrow more cheaply against their aircraft, are widely considered substantial.

Structure: Convention and protocols

The treaty consists of a base Convention, which contains general rules (including the creation of an "international interest" in mobile equipment, default remedies, and an electronic registry system), and equipment-specific protocols that adapt the rules to each category of asset. The Convention applies only together with a protocol, and the protocol prevails in case of conflict.

  • Aircraft Protocol (Cape Town, 2001) — covers airframes, aircraft engines and helicopters. By far the most successful; ratified by more than 80 states.
  • Luxembourg Rail Protocol (2007) — covers railway rolling stock; entered into force in 2024.
  • Berlin Space Protocol (2012) — covers space assets such as satellites; not yet in force.
  • Pretoria Protocol on Mining, Agricultural and Construction (MAC) equipment (2019) — the first extension beyond transport equipment; not yet in force.

The International Registry

A central innovation is the asset-based electronic International Registry (for aircraft, operated in Dublin, Ireland, under ICAO supervision), in which international interests in uniquely identifiable equipment can be registered and searched, 24 hours a day. Priority among competing interests is generally determined by the order of registration — a radical simplification over national filing systems based on the debtor's location.

Default remedies and insolvency

On default, the Convention gives creditors remedies including taking possession, selling or leasing the equipment, and (under the Aircraft Protocol) deregistering and exporting the aircraft. Contracting states choose from declaration options — notably "Alternative A" and "Alternative B" insolvency regimes. Alternative A, a "hard" rule resembling section 1110 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code, requires the insolvency administrator within a fixed waiting period either to cure all defaults and perform the agreement or to surrender the equipment. States adopting Alternative A with a short waiting period qualify their airlines for the "Cape Town discount" on export-credit financing under the OECD Aircraft Sector Understanding.

Adoption

The Convention entered into force on 1 March 2006. The United States ratified in 2004 (implementing it through the Cape Town Treaty Implementation Act), the European Union acceded in 2009, and major aviation states including China, India, the United Arab Emirates and Brazil are parties. The United Kingdom ratified in 2015 after long deliberation. With more than 80 contracting states covering the great majority of world aviation, the Cape Town system is regarded by the aviation finance industry as one of the most successful modern commercial-law treaties.
