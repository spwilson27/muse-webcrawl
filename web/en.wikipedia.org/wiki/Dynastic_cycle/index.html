Dynastic cycle

The dynastic cycle is a classic model of Chinese political history: a recurring pattern in which a dynasty is founded by an energetic ruler, reaches a peak of prosperity and stability, declines through corruption and weak leadership, loses the Mandate of Heaven, and is overthrown—after which a new dynasty reunifies the realm and the cycle begins again.

Stages

Traditional historiography describes the stages as follows: (1) a new emperor, holding the Mandate as Son of Heaven, restores order, cuts taxes, and undertakes public works; (2) the dynasty flourishes, population grows, and culture thrives; (3) later emperors become indolent, the court grows corrupt, taxes rise, land concentrates in great estates, and infrastructure decays; (4) floods, famines, epidemics, and popular rebellions signal that Heaven has withdrawn its mandate; (5) civil war ends with a new founder and a new dynasty.

Examples

The Han, Tang, Song, Ming, and Qing dynasties are each often narrated in these terms. The Ming, for example, rose from Zhu Yuanzhang's rebellion against the Yuan, flourished under the Yongle Emperor, and fell amid fiscal crisis and peasant revolt in 1644.

Criticism

Modern historians caution that the cycle is a retrospective literary device shaped by Confucian moralizing and by each dynasty's historians, who wrote the previous dynasty's history to legitimize their own rulers. Economic, climatic, and institutional factors—population pressure, silver flows, climate cooling in the 17th century—also drove dynastic rise and fall in ways the moral model obscures.
