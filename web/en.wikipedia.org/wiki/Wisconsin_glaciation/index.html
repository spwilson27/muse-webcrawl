The Wisconsin glaciation, also called the Wisconsin glacial episode, was the most recent major advance of the North American ice sheet complex, the Laurentide Ice Sheet. It is the North American equivalent of the last glacial period, broadly spanning from about 75,000 to 11,000 years ago. At its maximum, roughly 20,000 years ago, ice up to 2–3 kilometres thick covered Canada and the northern United States as far south as the Ohio and Missouri rivers, and global sea level stood about 120 metres lower than today.

Extent and chronology

The glaciation is conventionally divided into early, middle, and late Wisconsin stages. During the Last Glacial Maximum the Laurentide sheet coalesced with the Cordilleran sheet in the west, leaving an ice-free corridor whose timing is debated in discussions of the peopling of the Americas. Part of south-western Wisconsin and neighbouring states — the Driftless Area — escaped glaciation entirely.

Landforms and legacy

Retreating ice left the landscape of the Great Lakes region: the lakes themselves occupy basins scoured by ice and dammed by moraines. Meltwater formed huge proglacial lakes such as Lake Agassiz, whose catastrophic drainages may have triggered the Younger Dryas cold reversal about 12,900 years ago. The ice also deposited the rich glacial tills of the Midwest farm belt and end moraines such as the Kettle Moraine of Wisconsin, named for its many kettle lakes. Deglaciation ended around 11,000 years ago, marking the start of the Holocene epoch, and the land is still rebounding isostatically around Hudson Bay.
