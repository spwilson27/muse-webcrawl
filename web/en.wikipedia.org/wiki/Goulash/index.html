Goulash

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A steaming bowl of Hungarian goulash soup rich with paprika]

Goulash (Hungarian gulyás) is Hungary's emblematic dish of beef slow-cooked with onions and paprika — a word with two meanings that confuse foreigners. In Hungary, gulyásleves is a soup: brothy, with beef, potato, carrot and caraway. The thick, sauce-rich stew the world calls goulash is, in Hungarian terms, closer to pörkölt. Both descend from the kettle meals of Magyar herdsmen (gulyás means herdsman) on the Great Plain. The dish's defining spice is Johnny-come-lately: paprika, made from American chillies, only conquered Hungarian cooking in the 19th century.

History

Herdsmen cooked meat and onion in iron kettles over open fires for centuries; the stew travelled to Vienna with Hungarian magnates and became Rindsgulasch, then a fixture of Central European inns. Szeged and Kalocsa paprika — sun-red, graded from sweet to fiery — gave goulash its modern colour and identity, and by the late 19th century it was the national dish, served to guests and codified in cookbooks. Communism borrowed the name as slang ("goulash communism" for Hungary's softer model) — the dish is that embedded.

Family and impostors

Within Hungary: Szeged goulash layers sauerkraut through it; Székelygulyás adds sour cream; bean goulash fills winter bowls; the pörkölt family splits by protein. Abroad, adaptations drifted: German and Austrian versions stay close, while "American goulash" — ground beef, elbow macaroni and tomato sauce, called American chop suey in New England — shares nothing but the name and a faint faith in one-pot comfort.
