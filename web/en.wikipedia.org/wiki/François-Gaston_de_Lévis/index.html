François-Gaston de Lévis

François-Gaston de Lévis, duc de Lévis (20 August 1719 – 20 November 1787) was a French aristocrat and soldier who served with distinction in the War of the Austrian Succession and the Seven Years' War. In Canada he was second-in-command to the Marquis de Montcalm, and after Montcalm's death he won the last French victory of the war at the Battle of Sainte-Foy in 1760 before the final surrender of New France.

Born: 20 August 1719, Limoux, France
Died: 20 November 1787, Arras, France
Rank: Marshal of France (1783)
Battles: War of the Austrian Succession; Seven Years' War in Canada

Early career

Lévis entered the army as a cadet in the Marine infantry and served with distinction at the Siege of Prague and in Bohemia and Germany during the War of the Austrian Succession. In 1756 he was sent to New France as second-in-command of the French regular forces under Montcalm, with the rank of brigadier. He commanded the successful expedition against Fort William Henry in 1757 and played an important part in the French victory at Fort Carillon (Ticonderoga) in 1758.

The fall of New France

After the fall of Quebec and Montcalm's death in September 1759, Lévis took command of the French forces. In April 1760 he marched on Quebec and defeated the British under James Murray at the Battle of Sainte-Foy — the bloodiest battle fought on Canadian soil — but failed to retake the city once British warships arrived. Retreating to Montreal, he surrendered in September 1760 along with Governor Vaudreuil. Famous for his honour, Lévis burned his regimental colours rather than surrender them and protested the capitulation terms that denied his troops the honours of war.

Later life

Back in France, Lévis continued to serve in Germany until the war's end. He was made a Knight of the Order of the Saint-Esprit, governor of Artois, and was raised to Marshal of France in 1783 and a duke in 1784. He died at Arras in 1787, highly respected on both sides of the Atlantic; the city of Lévis, across the St. Lawrence from Quebec City, bears his name.
