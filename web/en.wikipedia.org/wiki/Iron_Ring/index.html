The Iron Ring is a ring worn by many Canadian-trained engineers as a symbol and reminder of the obligations and ethics associated with the engineering profession. The ring is presented to engineering graduates in a private ceremony known as The Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer. The concept was originated by Herbert Edward Terrence Haultain in 1922, and the rings were first conferred in 1925.

Symbolism

The ring is worn on the little finger of the working (dominant) hand. According to legend — carefully encouraged by the Corporation of the Seven Wardens, which administers the ritual — the rings are made from the steel of a beam from the Quebec Bridge, which collapsed twice during construction (in 1907 and 1916) with great loss of life, serving as a reminder of the engineer's responsibility to the public. In practice the rings are machined from stainless steel or wrought iron, and the facets cut into the band are meant to remind the wearer of the rough edges of professional duty.

The Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer

The ritual was written by the English poet Rudyard Kipling, at Haultain's request, and is administered privately by the Corporation of the Seven Wardens at "camps" across Canada. Participants take an obligation to uphold ethical practice and humility in engineering. The ritual is intentionally non-religious but solemn, and its details are kept private. A similar American ceremony, the Order of the Engineer (founded 1970), presents a stainless steel ring and was inspired by the Canadian tradition.

Wearing

The ring is traditionally worn on the little finger of the working hand so that it drags across the paper or keyboard as the engineer works, a constant tactile reminder of their obligations. Rings are returned to the Corporation on the death of the engineer or may be worn by their heirs on a chain.
