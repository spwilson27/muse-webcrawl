Penguin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Penguins are a group of aquatic, flightless birds (family Spheniscidae) of the Southern Hemisphere, superbly adapted to cold seas. There are 18 commonly recognised species, from the metre-tall emperor penguin of Antarctica to the 30-cm little penguin of Australia and New Zealand (and, contrary to the cartoon image, several species live in temperate or even equatorial waters — the Galápagos penguin).

Penguins 'fly' underwater with stiff flippers, steer with their feet, and porpoise at speed; dense waterproof feathers over blubber insulate them, and countercurrent blood flow keeps feet functional on ice. Most feed on krill, fish and squid and breed in colonies that can number in the hundreds of thousands.

Life cycle

Emperor penguins breed through the Antarctic winter: males incubate a single egg on their feet through months of darkness and storms while females feed at sea — one of the most demanding breeding cycles of any bird. Chicks gather in crèches; adults recognise their mate's and chick's calls in the colony's din.

Threats

Climate change (sea-ice loss for ice-dependent species, shifting prey), fisheries competition and oil pollution threaten many species; roughly half are considered of conservation concern. The African penguin has declined catastrophically and was uplisted to Critically Endangered in the mid-2020s.
