Donald Olding Hebb FRS (July 22, 1904 – August 20, 1985) was a Canadian psychologist who is renowned in the fields of psychology and neuroscience for his work on learning and memory. His book The Organization of Behavior (1949) introduced Hebbian theory, the idea that neural pathways strengthen when cells repeatedly participate in firing one another — often summarised as "cells that fire together wire together." Hebb is frequently called the father of neuropsychology and of neural network theory.

Hebb's postulate of learning became a cornerstone of modern neuroscience and artificial neural networks.

Life and career

Hebb was born in Chester, Nova Scotia, and studied at Dalhousie University before earning his PhD at Harvard under Karl Lashley, with whom he also worked at the Yerkes Laboratory of Primate Biology in Florida. He joined McGill University in Montreal in 1939, working with neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield at the Montreal Neurological Institute, and later served as chair of McGill's psychology department and chancellor of the university. His studies with brain-injured patients helped establish that adult brains remain plastic.

Hebbian theory

In The Organization of Behavior, Hebb proposed his famous postulate:

"When an axon of cell A is near enough to excite a cell B and repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing it, some growth process or metabolic change takes place in one or both cells such that A's efficiency, as one of the cells firing B, is increased."

He also introduced the concept of the cell assembly, a distributed network of neurons that becomes linked through experience and can represent a concept. These ideas anticipated modern research on synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation, and they underlie learning rules used in artificial neural networks.
