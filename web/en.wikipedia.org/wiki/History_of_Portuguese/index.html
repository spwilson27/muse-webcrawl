History of Portuguese

The history of Portuguese traces the language from the Vulgar Latin spoken in the Roman province of Lusitania and Gallaecia, through Galician-Portuguese in the Middle Ages, to the modern language spoken by over 250 million people across Portugal, Brazil, Africa and Asia.

Origins

Roman conquest of the Iberian Peninsula from the 2nd century BCE spread Latin, which in the northwest evolved distinctively. After the fall of Rome, Suebi and Visigoths ruled the region; the resulting Vulgar Latin developed features separating it from Castilian, including the retention of Latin initial f- and distinctive nasal vowels.

Galician-Portuguese

By the 12th century a single language, Galician-Portuguese, was spoken across the County of Portugal and Galicia. It became a celebrated literary language: the cantigas de amigo, cantigas de amor and satirical cantigas de escárnio e maldizer were composed at courts throughout Iberia, even by Castilian kings such as Alfonso X. The earliest documents in the language date from the late 12th century.

Divergence and classical Portuguese

As Portugal consolidated southward and Galicia remained under Castile, the varieties diverged from the 15th century. The Portuguese Renaissance produced the chancery standard, the grammars of Fernão de Oliveira (1536) and João de Barros, and the epic poetry of Luís de Camões, whose Os Lusíadas (1572) fixed the classical language. Maritime expansion carried Portuguese to Brazil, Africa, India, Macau and Japan, spawning creoles worldwide.

Modern Portuguese

Brazilian Portuguese developed its own phonology and norms, while European Portuguese underwent vowel reduction. Spelling was repeatedly reformed (1911, 1945), and the 1990 Orthographic Agreement aimed to unify spelling across the Lusophone world. Today Portuguese is the sixth most spoken native language and an official language on four continents.
