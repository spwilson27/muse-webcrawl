Nine-rank system

The nine-rank system (Chinese: 九品中正制; pinyin: Jiǔpǐn Zhōngzhèng Zhì), also known as the nine-grade controller system, was a civil service nomination system used in China from the Three Kingdoms period until it was superseded by the imperial examination system under the Sui dynasty. Officials known as Controllers (Zhongzheng) classified candidates for government office into nine grades according to their talent and virtue, and appointments were made on the basis of these rankings.

Origins

The system was proposed by the official Chen Qun and adopted in 220 by Cao Pi, founder of the state of Cao Wei, at the end of the Han dynasty. It replaced the Han practice of recommendation by local officials (the chaju system), which had become corrupted. Under the new system, a Controller was appointed for each commandery and province; Controllers were themselves serving officials of the second rank drawn from the candidate's home locality.

Operation

The Controller compiled dossiers on local candidates, grading them from the first (highest) to the ninth (lowest) rank and appending written comments on their ability and character. In theory the grade reflected personal merit; in practice, family background quickly became decisive, since Controllers were drawn from the very aristocratic families whose sons they graded.

Decline

By the Western Jin dynasty the system had entrenched a hereditary aristocracy: as a contemporary critic put it, "in the upper grades there are no men from humble families; in the lower grades there are no men from powerful clans." Rank came to be determined by pedigree rather than talent, and offices were monopolised by great landowning families. The rise of the imperial examination under Emperor Wen of Sui finally abolished the nine-rank system in favour of merit tested by written examination.
