Talus

The talus (plural: tali), also known historically as the astragalus, is one of the tarsal bones of the foot. It sits between the tibia and fibula of the lower leg above, and the calcaneus (heel bone) below, forming the lower part of the ankle joint. Because it transmits the entire weight of the body from the leg to the foot, the talus is a key bone in human locomotion.

Structure

The talus is the second-largest of the tarsal bones. It has no muscular attachments — a unique feature among bones — and is covered largely by articular cartilage. Anatomically it is divided into a head, a neck, and a body. The rounded head articulates with the navicular bone; the body bears a pulley-shaped upper surface, the trochlea, which fits into the mortise formed by the tibia and fibula.

Function

Through the talocrural (ankle) joint above and the subtalar joint below, the talus allows dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion of the foot. Its position makes it central to gait and balance.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the talar neck are clinically important because the bone's blood supply is retrograde and easily disrupted, risking avascular necrosis. The talus can also be dislocated in high-energy injuries. In other animals, the astragalus was historically used as a gaming die — the origin of "knucklebones."
