Market segmentation theory

The market segmentation theory is a theory of the term structure of interest rates which holds that the bond market consists of separate, largely independent segments for short-term, medium-term and long-term maturities. Under this theory, the yield on a bond of a given maturity is determined by the supply of and demand for bonds within that maturity segment, and neither investors nor borrowers readily switch between segments.

Core assumptions

The theory assumes that participants have strong, often institutionally determined maturity preferences: banks concentrate on short-term lending, pension funds and insurers on long-term bonds, and so on, often for regulatory, liquidity or liability-matching reasons. Because these clienteles will not move between segments even for modest yield differences, interest rates at each maturity are set by conditions in that segment alone, and there is no necessary relationship tying long rates to expected future short rates.

Implications

Market segmentation can explain a yield curve of any shape — upward sloping, downward sloping, flat or humped — depending on relative supply and demand pressures in each segment at a given time. It also implies that central-bank or government debt-management operations concentrated in one maturity range may affect that range's yields without proportionately affecting others.

Relation to other theories

The theory sits at one extreme among explanations of the term structure. The pure expectations hypothesis assumes perfect substitutability between maturities; the liquidity preference theory assumes substitutability but adds a term premium; and the preferred habitat theory — a modification of segmentation associated with Modigliani and Sutch — allows investors to leave their preferred segment if offered a sufficient premium. Empirical evidence generally favours preferred habitat over strict segmentation.

The term is associated particularly with the work of John M. Culbertson (1957).
