Aliquot sum

In number theory, the aliquot sum s(n) of a positive integer n is the sum of all proper divisors of n — that is, all divisors of n other than n itself. Equivalently, s(n) = σ(n) − n, where σ is the divisor function. Thus s(1) = 0, s(6) = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, and s(12) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 16.

Classification of numbers

Comparing the aliquot sum with the number itself gives the classical trichotomy known since antiquity:

  • s(n) = n: perfect numbers (6, 28, 496, 8128, …).
  • s(n) > n: abundant numbers (12, 18, 20, …).
  • s(n) < n: deficient numbers (all primes, most composites).

Two distinct numbers a and b form an amicable pair when s(a) = b and s(b) = a, the smallest example being 220 and 284.

Aliquot sequences

Iterating the aliquot sum produces an aliquot sequence. Whether every such sequence eventually reaches 1, a perfect number, or a cycle is an open problem (the Catalan–Dickson conjecture); the sequence beginning at 276 is the smallest unresolved case. Numbers that never appear as an aliquot sum of any other number are called untouchable numbers.
