Roar is a 1981 American adventure comedy-drama film written, produced and directed by Noel Marshall, starring Marshall, his wife Tippi Hedren, her daughter Melanie Griffith, and Marshall's sons. The film follows a family who find themselves besieged at an African research house by the untrained lions, tigers and other big cats kept there by the eccentric naturalist Hank (Marshall). It is notorious as one of the most dangerous productions in film history: more than 70 members of the cast and crew were injured by the animals during its eleven-year production.

Production

Marshall and Hedren conceived the film after encountering big-cat conservation issues in Africa, intending a family adventure that would promote wildlife preservation. Rather than trained film animals, they raised and kept over a hundred lions, tigers, leopards, cheetahs and other animals at their ranch in Acton, California, and in Africa. Principal photography began in 1976 and continued, with interruptions, until 1981; floods destroyed sets, financing repeatedly failed — the budget ballooned to roughly $17 million — and the human cast lived among the animals throughout.

Injuries

The production's injuries became legendary. Cinematographer Jan de Bont was scalped by a lion, requiring 220 stitches. Melanie Griffith was mauled and needed facial reconstructive surgery, narrowly avoiding the loss of an eye. Noel Marshall was bitten and mauled repeatedly, developing wounds that became gangrenous. Hedren suffered a fractured leg and claw wounds. The 2015 American re-release by Drafthouse Films marketed the film with the tagline "No animals were harmed in the making of this film. 70 cast and crew members were."

Reception and legacy

Roar was never theatrically released in the United States in 1981 and grossed little internationally, ruining Marshall. It developed a cult reputation through home video and its long-unavailable status; the 2015 re-release brought retrospective praise for its extraordinary footage of humans interacting unprotected with big cats, and renewed scrutiny of its ethics. Hedren went on to found the Shambala Preserve for big cats and became an advocate for legislation restricting private ownership of exotic animals.
