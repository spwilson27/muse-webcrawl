Vaporization (or vaporisation) is the phase transition by which a substance changes from the liquid phase to the gas (vapour) phase. It occurs in two principal forms: evaporation, a surface phenomenon that can happen at any temperature, and boiling, a bulk phenomenon that occurs at a characteristic boiling point for a given pressure.

Evaporation

Evaporation takes place at the surface of a liquid when individual molecules acquire enough kinetic energy to overcome the intermolecular forces binding them and escape into the surrounding air. Because the escaping molecules are the most energetic, evaporation has a cooling effect, exploited by sweating in animals and by evaporative coolers. The rate of evaporation increases with temperature, surface area and air movement, and decreases with humidity.

Boiling

Boiling occurs when the vapour pressure of the liquid equals the ambient pressure, allowing vapour bubbles to form throughout the liquid. The boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure is 100 °C (212 °F); it falls at high altitude, where the pressure is lower, and rises in a pressure cooker. The energy required to convert a unit mass of liquid to vapour at constant temperature is the enthalpy of vaporization (latent heat of vaporization), which is large for water—about 2,260 kJ/kg at 100 °C—because of its hydrogen bonding.

Related processes

The reverse of vaporization is condensation. Solids can also pass directly into vapour by sublimation, as with dry ice and iodine. Vaporization is central to the water cycle, distillation, refrigeration cycles and steam power generation.
