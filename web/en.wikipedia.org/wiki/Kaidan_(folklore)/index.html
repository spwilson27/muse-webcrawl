Kaidan (folklore)

Kaidan (怪談, sometimes transliterated kwaidan, literally "strange talk") is a genre of Japanese ghost stories and horror tales, traditionally told during the summer. Kaidan tales feature ghosts (yūrei), vengeful spirits, yōkai, and encounters with the supernatural, often with a moral or karmic dimension.

History

The genre flourished in the Edo period, when the parlour game hyaku monogatari kaidankai ("a gathering of one hundred supernatural tales") became popular: participants lit one hundred candles and told ghost stories one by one, extinguishing a candle after each tale until the room went dark. Classic collections and plays, such as the kabuki ghost play Tōkaidō Yotsuya Kaidan (1825), defined the genre's conventions.

In modern media

Lafcadio Hearn's Kwaidan: Stories and Studies of Strange Things (1904) introduced the tales to Western readers, and Masaki Kobayashi's film Kwaidan (1964) won acclaim at Cannes. Kaidan remains a foundation of Japanese horror cinema and literature.
