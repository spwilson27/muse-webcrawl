Slow cooking

Slow cooking is a cooking technique that uses low heat over an extended period, typically in an electric slow cooker (also known by the brand name Crock-Pot) or a covered pot in a low oven. Countertop slow cookers usually operate between about 80 °C and 150 °C on their settings, simmering food for four to twelve hours.

Method

The sealed lid traps steam, which condenses and returns moisture to the food, so little liquid is lost and tough cuts of meat become tender as collagen converts to gelatin. Because the contents are held at a gentle simmer, dishes such as stews, braises, pot roasts, pulled pork, soups, beans and chilli cook largely unattended. Foods should reach safe temperatures promptly; modern guidance recommends thawing ingredients first and avoiding leaving cooked food in the danger zone.

History

Low-and-slow cooking is ancient — hayboxes, earth ovens and Sabbath stews such as cholent all exploit retained, gentle heat. The modern electric appliance derives from Irving Naxon’s "Naxon Beanery", patented in 1940, inspired by his grandmother's cholent. Rival Manufacturing acquired the design, rebranding it the Crock-Pot in 1971; the appliance became a fixture of 1970s American kitchens, valued by working households for make-ahead meals.

Related techniques

Slow cooking overlaps with braising and stewing; contrasted with it, sous vide holds food at precisely controlled temperatures in sealed bags, and pressure cooking achieves similar tenderisation rapidly rather than slowly.
