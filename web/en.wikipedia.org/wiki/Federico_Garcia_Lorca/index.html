Federico García Lorca (5 June 1898 – 19 August 1936) was a Spanish poet, playwright, and theatre director, and one of the most celebrated figures of Spanish literature. A leading member of the Generation of '27, he achieved international recognition with the Romancero gitano (Gypsy Ballads, 1928) and with his "rural trilogy" of plays. He was executed by Nationalist forces in the opening weeks of the Spanish Civil War, and his body has never been found.

Life

Born in Fuente Vaqueros near Granada to a prosperous landowning family, Lorca studied law and literature in Granada and Madrid, where at the Residencia de Estudiantes he befriended Salvador Dalí and Luis Buñuel. A 1929–30 stay in New York produced the visionary, anguished poems of Poeta en Nueva York (Poet in New York, published posthumously in 1940). Under the Second Republic he directed the touring student theatre company La Barraca, bringing classic Spanish plays to rural audiences.

Works

Lorca's poetry fuses Andalusian folk tradition, flamenco's duende, and surrealist imagery. His great plays — Bodas de sangre (Blood Wedding, 1932), Yerma (1934), and La casa de Bernarda Alba (completed 1936) — portray passion crushed by honour, repression, and social convention in rural Spain. The posthumous Sonetos del amor oscuro (Sonnets of Dark Love) appeared only in the 1980s.

Death and legacy

Arrested in Granada in August 1936, Lorca was shot by a Nationalist firing squad near Alfacar, reportedly on 19 August; his remains, believed to lie in an unmarked mass grave, have resisted searches. Under Franco his works were suppressed in Spain, but his reputation grew worldwide: he is the most translated Spanish author after Cervantes, and a symbol of the Civil War's cultural catastrophe.
