The economy of South Korea is a highly developed mixed economy dominated by family-owned conglomerates known as chaebol. It is the fourth-largest economy in Asia and one of the ten largest in the world by nominal gross domestic product. South Korea's transformation from one of the poorest countries in the world after the Korean War into a high-income industrial power within a single generation is frequently cited as the "Miracle on the Han River."

History

Post-war poverty and industrialisation

Following the Korean War (1950–1953), South Korea was one of the poorest nations on earth, with a per-capita income comparable to that of the poorest countries of Africa and Asia. Beginning in the 1960s, under President Park Chung-hee, the government pursued export-oriented industrialisation through a series of Five-Year Plans. State-directed credit, protection of infant industries, and close cooperation between government and the emerging chaebol groups — Samsung, Hyundai, LG, Daewoo and others — drove rapid growth first in light industry (textiles, footwear) and then in heavy and chemical industries during the 1970s.

Democratisation and the Asian financial crisis

By the 1980s South Korea had become a major producer of steel, ships and automobiles. It joined the OECD in 1996. The Asian financial crisis of 1997 struck the country hard: the won collapsed, several chaebols failed (including Daewoo), and South Korea accepted a record US$58 billion rescue package from the International Monetary Fund. In return it undertook deep reforms of corporate governance, banking supervision and labour markets. The economy recovered quickly, and the IMF loans were repaid ahead of schedule in 2001.

Twenty-first century

In the 2000s and 2010s South Korea became a world leader in semiconductors, consumer electronics, displays and shipbuilding. It weathered the 2008 global financial crisis relatively well. Challenges in the 2020s include a low birth rate and rapidly ageing population, high household debt, youth unemployment, and dependence on imported energy and raw materials.

Sectors

Industry

Industry accounts for roughly a third of GDP. South Korea is the world's leading producer of memory semiconductors (through Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix), a top shipbuilder, and home to major carmakers Hyundai and Kia. POSCO is among the world's largest steelmakers. The country is also a leader in rechargeable batteries, with firms such as LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI and SK On supplying global electric-vehicle makers.

Services

Services account for around 60% of GDP, including finance, retail, telecommunications and a large cultural sector. Korean popular culture — K-pop, film and television drama — has become a significant export in its own right, boosting tourism and consumer-goods sales abroad.

Agriculture

Agriculture contributes only about 2% of GDP. Rice is the staple crop; the country imports a large share of its food and nearly all of its fossil fuels.

Trade

South Korea is one of the world's largest trading nations, with exports equivalent to roughly 40% of GDP. It has concluded free-trade agreements with the United States (KORUS FTA, in force 2012), the European Union, China and many others. China is its largest trading partner, followed by the United States.
