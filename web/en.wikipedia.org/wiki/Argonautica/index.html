The Argonautica (Greek: Ἀργοναυτικά, romanized: Argonautika) is a Greek epic poem written by Apollonius of Rhodes in the 3rd century BC. The only entirely surviving Hellenistic epic, the Argonautica tells the myth of the voyage of Jason and the Argonauts to retrieve the Golden Fleece from remote Colchis. Their heroic adventures and Jason's relationship with the Colchian princess/sorceress Medea were already well known to Hellenistic audiences, which enabled Apollonius to go beyond a simple narrative, giving it an emphasis on emotion, pathos, and a scholarly, learned texture characteristic of the age.

Plot

The poem is divided into four books. Book 1 describes the assembling of the Argonauts, including Heracles, Orpheus, Castor and Pollux, and the ship Argo, built by Argus with Athena's help. Book 2 follows the voyage through the Symplegades (Clashing Rocks) to the eastern shore of the Black Sea. Book 3, the most celebrated, depicts Medea falling in love with Jason under the influence of Hera, Athena and Eros, and her decision to aid him with her magic. Book 4 tells of the seizure of the Fleece, the murder of Medea's brother Apsyrtus, and the long return journey to Greece.

Composition and background

Apollonius, a scholar-poet associated with the Library of Alexandria, composed the epic in a Homeric dialect while self-consciously departing from Homer in scale, psychology and tone. Ancient tradition relates a quarrel with Callimachus, the leading poet-critic of the day, and a period of exile on Rhodes, whence his epithet "Rhodius". The poem's Jason is strikingly unheroic — hesitant and reliant on Medea — in contrast to the martial heroes of the Iliad and Odyssey.

Influence

The Argonautica profoundly influenced Roman poetry: Catullus' poem 64 and Virgil's portrait of Dido in the Aeneid draw on Apollonius's Medea, and Valerius Flaccus wrote a Latin Argonautica in the 1st century AD. The poem remains the fullest surviving account of the Argonaut myth.
