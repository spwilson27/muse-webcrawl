State security

State security is the protection of a state and its institutions against threats such as espionage, sabotage, subversion, terrorism, and foreign interference. The term also refers collectively to the agencies charged with this mission — intelligence services, counterintelligence organizations, and secret police — and, in the former communist states of Eastern Europe, to specific organs whose very names translate as "state security," such as East Germany's Ministry for State Security (the Stasi), Czechoslovakia's StB, Romania's Securitate, Bulgaria's Committee for State Security, and Yugoslavia's UDBA.

Meaning and functions

In general usage, state security work includes counterintelligence (detecting and neutralizing foreign intelligence services), protection of state secrets and critical infrastructure, vetting of officials, counterterrorism, and in authoritarian systems the surveillance and suppression of political dissent. Democratic states typically divide these functions among agencies operating under law — such as the United Kingdom's MI5 (Security Service), Germany's Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution (BfV), and the intelligence components of the FBI in the United States — whereas the communist-era state security services combined foreign intelligence, domestic surveillance, and political policing in a single apparatus accountable to the ruling party.

Historical examples

The Soviet lineage of state security services runs from the Cheka (1917) through the GPU, OGPU, NKVD, and MGB to the KGB (1954–1991). Nazi Germany's counterpart functions were exercised by the Gestapo and the Reich Security Main Office (RSHA). The Stasi of the German Democratic Republic became emblematic of the type, maintaining files on millions of citizens through a vast network of unofficial informants; its records are now administered by a federal archive authority (the Stasi Records Agency, BStU) and are widely used in historical research and in German public memory.

State security as a legal concept

"State security" is also a legal category in many jurisdictions: offenses such as treason, espionage, and sedition are classified as crimes against state security, and national-security exceptions qualify freedoms of speech, press, and information in international human-rights law, provided restrictions are prescribed by law and necessary in a democratic society. Critics note that the concept has often been invoked to criminalize peaceful opposition, a tension addressed by principles such as the Johannesburg Principles on national security and freedom of expression.
