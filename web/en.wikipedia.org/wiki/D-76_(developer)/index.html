D-76 (developer)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

D-76 is a photographic film developer formula introduced by Kodak in 1927. It was originally formulated for the needs of the motion picture industry, which required a fine-grain, reliable developer for processing large quantities of cinema film, and it went on to become one of the most widely used and imitated developers for black-and-white still photography of all time. D-76 is a metol–hydroquinone (MQ) developer buffered with borax.

Composition

The published formula for D-76, per litre of working solution, is approximately:

  • Metol — 2 g (developing agent)
  • Sodium sulfite (anhydrous) — 100 g (preservative)
  • Hydroquinone — 5 g (developing agent)
  • Borax (decahydrate) — 2 g (alkali/buffer)
  • Water to make 1 litre

The high sulfite content gives fine grain by acting as a mild silver solvent, while the borax buffer holds the pH at about 8.5, producing a low-contrast, compensating action. A later variant, D-76d, replaced part of the borax with a borax–boric acid buffer to resist pH drift from dissolved carbon dioxide during storage.

Use

D-76 is sold as a powder to be dissolved in warm water to make a stock solution. It can be used undiluted ("stock"), in which case it may be reused with replenishment or extended developing times, or diluted 1:1 with water as a one-shot developer — the 1:1 dilution is preferred by many photographers for increased sharpness, slightly increased grain, and more consistent results. Typical development times for common films at 20 °C range from about 6 to 12 minutes depending on film and dilution.

Equivalents and derivatives

Because the formula was published, many manufacturers offer equivalents, most famously Ilford ID-11, which is generally considered to be nearly identical in performance. Numerous published variants and clones circulate in the photographic literature, and D-76 served as a reference point against which other fine-grain developers — such as Kodak Microdol-X and D-23 — were compared.
