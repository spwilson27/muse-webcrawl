Duke

A duke is a male ruler of a duchy, or a member of royalty or nobility holding that title. Historically, dukes ranked among the highest nobility, below princes and grand dukes, and above marquesses or margraves. The female equivalent is a duchess. The title derives from the Latin dux, meaning "leader", used in the Roman Republic for military commanders without an official rank, and later for the senior military commanders of provinces in the late Roman Empire.

History

In the early Middle Ages, Frankish and other Germanic dukes were regional rulers of peoples (gentes), commanding armies and administering justice. In the Holy Roman Empire, the great stem duchies—Saxony, Franconia, Swabia, Bavaria and Lorraine—were pillars of the realm. In France, the dukes of Normandy, Burgundy, Brittany and Aquitaine were at times near-sovereign princes. Dukedoms could be sovereign states, as with many Italian and German duchies, or titles within a kingdom.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, duke is the highest rank of the peerage. The first English dukedom was created in 1337 for Edward the Black Prince (Duke of Cornwall, a title still reserved for the heir apparent). Royal dukes are dukes who are members of the royal family; there are also non-royal dukedoms, such as those of Norfolk, Somerset, Richmond, Grafton, Beaufort, St Albans and Rutland. A duke is styled "His Grace" and addressed as "Your Grace".

Other countries

Ducal titles persist as noble titles in Spain (where dukes are grandees), Italy and Belgium, and as the sovereign titles of Luxembourg's grand duke and the dukes historically ruling states such as Parma and Modena. In China, the analogous first-rank hereditary title gong is conventionally translated as duke.
