The Australian shearers' strike of 1891 was one of Australia's earliest and most significant industrial disputes. It pitted unionised shearers, organised through the Amalgamated Shearers' Union, against pastoral employers who sought to reduce wages and employ non-union ("free") labour.

Background

During the economic boom of the 1880s, shearers had won favourable piece rates, commonly £1 per 100 sheep shorn. As wool prices fell and drought approached, pastoralists' associations moved to cut rates and impose new agreements that undermined union conditions. The shearers responded by resolving that union men would not work alongside non-union labour.

The strike

The strike began in January 1891 at stations near Barcaldine in central Queensland. Thousands of shearers gathered in strike camps, the largest beneath a ghost gum at Barcaldine later known as the Tree of Knowledge. Colonial governments dispatched police and military forces; strike leaders were arrested and several were convicted of conspiracy and sentenced to imprisonment on St Helena Island. The strike collapsed in May 1891, although a second shearers' strike followed in 1894.

Legacy

The defeat convinced many unionists of the need for direct parliamentary representation. The political labour movement that grew out of the strike camps formed the world's first short-lived labour government in Queensland in 1899 under Anderson Dawson, and the Australian Labor Party traces its origins to the movement. William Lane, who addressed the Barcaldine camp, went on to found the utopian New Australia settlement in Paraguay.
