Corporatism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Corporatism is a political system of interest representation in which the constituent units are organised into a limited number of singular, compulsory, non-competitive, hierarchically ordered categories (“corporations”), recognised or licensed by the state and granted a representational monopoly in exchange for observing controls on their leadership and demands. The classic scholarly definition is Philippe Schmitter's (1974).

The doctrine has two very different families. Authoritarian (state) corporatism was practised by Fascist Italy — where Mussolini's regime created corporations of employers and workers by sector and replaced parliament with a Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (1939) — and by Salazar's Portugal, Franco's Spain and Vargas's Brazil. Democratic (societal) corporatism, or neo-corporatism, describes the institutionalised bargaining between governments, peak union confederations and employers' associations in countries such as Austria, Sweden and the Netherlands, which underpinned post-war wage moderation and welfare states.

Origins and criticism

Corporatist ideas drew on Catholic social teaching (the encyclical Quadragesimo anno, 1931), guild nostalgia, and syndicalist currents, promising a “third way” between capitalism and socialism in which class conflict would be replaced by organised collaboration. In practice, authoritarian corporatism suppressed independent unions while leaving private property largely intact, leading critics to describe it as a façade for state control; democratic neo-corporatism has been credited with economic stability and blamed for insider privilege and rigidity. Corporatist institutions declined in much of Europe from the 1980s as bargaining decentralised.
