Back-of-the-envelope calculation

A back-of-the-envelope calculation is a rough calculation, typically scrawled on a scrap of paper such as an envelope, that uses simplified assumptions and rounded numbers to estimate a quantity quickly. More precise than a wild guess but less rigorous than a full analysis, such calculations establish the order of magnitude of a result and check whether an idea is plausible at all.

Characteristics

Back-of-the-envelope calculations deliberately use numbers known to be inaccurate, substituting approximate values so that the computation can be done mentally or with minimal tools. In physics and engineering the approach is closely associated with Fermi problems, named after Enrico Fermi, who famously estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb test by dropping scraps of paper in its shock wave. Typical techniques include dimensional analysis, rounding to one significant figure, and decomposition of a problem into factors that can each be estimated.

Uses

Science and engineering educators use such calculations to build estimation skills and intuition. In business, the technique is applied to market sizing, feasibility screening and sanity-checking detailed financial models. The physicist Victor Weisskopf and the astronomer Sterl Phinney were noted advocates; Phinney popularised the phrase for quick astrophysical estimates. A well-known example is the Drake equation, which frames the search for extraterrestrial civilisations as a product of individually estimable factors.
