Aircraft catapult
PurposeAssist aircraft takeoff from a short deck
TypesSteam catapult; EMALS (electromagnetic)

An aircraft catapult is a device used to launch aircraft from ships, most famously from aircraft carriers, by accelerating them to takeoff speed over a very short distance. Catapult-assisted takeoff allows heavy aircraft — fighters, strike aircraft, airborne early-warning planes, and transports — to operate from carrier decks far too short for conventional takeoffs, forming the basis of the CATOBAR (Catapult Assisted Take-Off But Arrested Recovery) configuration used by the United States and French navies and adopted by China.

History

Early naval aviation experimented with compressed-air, hydraulic, and flywheel catapults: the first shipboard catapult launches were made by the U.S. Navy before and during the First World War, and by the 1930s hydraulic catapults were standard on major warships for launching floatplane scouts. The modern era began with the steam catapult, developed in Britain by Commander Colin Mitchell and fitted first to HMS Perseus for trials in 1950–1952. Using steam from the ship's boilers fed into two long cylinders, a piston hauled a shuttle along a slot in the flight deck, towing the aircraft by a bridle (later by a nose-gear launch bar). Steam catapults enabled the jet-age carrier: they underpin the U.S. Navy's C-7, C-11, and C-13 series fitted to supercarriers.

Electromagnetic launch

The newest generation is the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS), developed by General Atomics for the U.S. Navy's Gerald R. Ford-class carriers. A linear induction motor accelerates the shuttle, giving smoother, precisely controlled acceleration that reduces stress on airframes, can launch both very heavy and very light (including unmanned) aircraft, and removes the demand on ship's steam. China has fitted an electromagnetic system to its carrier Fujian, and the technology is under study elsewhere.

Operation

On launch, the aircraft's nose gear connects to the shuttle, a holdback bar restrains it while engines reach full power, and the "shooter" (catapult officer) fires the catapult from a deck station or an integrated catapult control station. The aircraft is accelerated along roughly 90 meters of deck, reaching flying speed at the bow in about two seconds, after which the shuttle is braked by a water brake and retracted for the next launch. Combined with angled decks and arresting gear, catapults permit launch and recovery cycles of a few minutes per aircraft.
