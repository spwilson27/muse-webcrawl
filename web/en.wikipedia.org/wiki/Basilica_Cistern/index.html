Basilica Cistern

The Basilica Cistern (Turkish: Yerebatan Sarnıcı, "sunken palace") is the largest of several hundred ancient cisterns that lie beneath the city of Istanbul, Turkey. Located southwest of the Hagia Sophia, it was built in the 6th century during the reign of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I to store water for the Great Palace of Constantinople.

History

The cistern takes its name from a large basilica that once stood above it. According to ancient texts, some 7,000 slaves worked on its construction. It provided a water filtration system for the Great Palace and other buildings on the First Hill, supplied by aqueducts including the Valens Aqueduct from forests far north of the city. After the Ottoman conquest in 1453 it supplied the Topkapı Palace, then faded from memory until the scholar Petrus Gyllius rediscovered it in the 16th century, noting that locals drew water — and even fish — through holes in their cellar floors.

Description

The underground chamber is supported by a forest of 336 marble columns arranged in 12 rows of 28, many of them reused from earlier Roman structures. Two columns in the northwest corner rest on blocks carved with the face of Medusa, one upside down and one sideways, whose origins and purpose remain debated. A raised walkway allows visitors to tour the cistern above shallow water in which carp swim.
