The Battle of Quebec was fought in October 1690 between the forces of New France, under Governor General Louis de Buade, Comte de Frontenac, and a New England expedition commanded by Sir William Phips. It was the climactic action of King William's War in Canada and ended in the successful French defence of Quebec City.

Background

In retaliation for French and Native raids on the New England frontier, Massachusetts organised a two-pronged attack on Canada in 1690. While an overland expedition against Montreal dissolved amid disease and supply failures, Phips — fresh from capturing Port Royal in Acadia — sailed up the St. Lawrence with some 34 ships and 2,300 militiamen to seize Quebec itself.

The battle

Phips arrived before Quebec on 16 October and sent an envoy demanding surrender within the hour. Frontenac famously replied that he would answer "from the mouth of my cannons and the fire of my musketry". The subsequent bombardment between the fleet and the town's batteries achieved little, and a landing force put ashore at Beauport was repeatedly repulsed by French regulars, militia and Native allies in skirmishing along the St. Charles River. With ammunition running low, smallpox spreading and winter approaching, Phips re-embarked and sailed for Boston on 24 October; several of his ships were wrecked on the return voyage.

Aftermath

The failure left Massachusetts burdened with debt — the expedition's unpaid bills prompted the colony to issue the first paper money in the English colonial world — while Frontenac's prestige soared. Quebec would remain French until Wolfe's victory on the Plains of Abraham in 1759. The battle's most famous relic, the flag of the New England expedition, was long displayed in Quebec as a trophy.
