Classical conditioning

Classical conditioning (also Pavlovian conditioning or respondent conditioning) is a type of learning in which an organism comes to associate stimuli, so that a previously neutral stimulus elicits a response originally produced by another stimulus. The response produced in this way is called a conditioned reflex. The phenomenon was first described systematically by the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov, whose experiments with dogs in the early 20th century — in which the animals learned to salivate at the sound of a bell that had repeatedly preceded feeding — became famous as "Pavlov's dogs".

Terminology and procedure

In the standard procedure, an unconditioned stimulus (US) — such as food — naturally elicits an unconditioned response (UR) — salivation. A neutral stimulus, such as a bell, is repeatedly paired with the US; it thereby becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that alone elicits a conditioned response (CR) resembling the UR. If the CS is then presented repeatedly without the US, the CR weakens and disappears, a process called extinction; spontaneous recovery may occur after a rest interval.

Pavlov's research

Pavlov originally studied the physiology of digestion, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1904. Noticing that dogs salivated at the sight of lab assistants and other cues preceding meals, he turned to studying these "psychic secretions" and established the basic laws of conditioning: acquisition, extinction, stimulus generalization, and discrimination. His work laid the foundation for behaviourism, promoted by John B. Watson, whose "Little Albert" experiment (1920) demonstrated conditioned fear in a human infant.

Later developments

Subsequent research showed that conditioning is not mere mechanical association: the Rescorla–Wagner model (1972) explained learning in terms of prediction error — a stimulus is learned about only insofar as it provides new information. Phenomena such as blocking, overshadowing, latent inhibition, and taste-aversion learning (the Garcia effect) revealed biological constraints on conditioning. Conditioned reflexes are now understood in terms of neural circuits including the amygdala (fear conditioning) and cerebellum (eyeblink conditioning), and they inform therapies such as systematic desensitization and exposure therapy for phobias and PTSD.
