Volksdeutsche

Volksdeutsche (singular Volksdeutscher) is a German term meaning "ethnic Germans" — people of German ancestry and culture living outside the borders of the German Reich. It was used in the first half of the 20th century, and acquired a specific political and legal meaning under Nazi Germany, which distinguished Volksdeutsche from Reichsdeutsche, citizens of the Reich itself.

Historical background

German-speaking communities had lived for centuries across Central and Eastern Europe: in the Sudetenland of Bohemia, in Transylvania (the Transylvanian Saxons), along the Danube (Danube Swabians), in the Baltic states, in Poland, and in Russia, where Catherine the Great had invited German settlers — the Volga Germans — in the 18th century. After the First World War, the redrawing of borders left millions of ethnic Germans as minorities in new or enlarged states.

Nazi-era usage

The Nazi regime made the Volksdeutsche central to its expansionist ideology, claiming to protect and "reunite" ethnic Germans — a pretext used for the annexation of Austria and the Sudetenland in 1938. Under the Nazi–Soviet agreements of 1939–1941, hundreds of thousands of Volksdeutsche were resettled ("Heim ins Reich" — "home into the Reich") from Eastern Europe into annexed Polish territories, often onto land from which Poles and Jews had been expelled. In occupied Poland, the Deutsche Volksliste (German People's List) classified inhabitants into categories of supposed German-ness, with privileges and coercion attached. The SS organisation VoMi (Volksdeutsche Mittelstelle) coordinated these policies.

After 1945

At the end of the Second World War, between 12 and 14 million ethnic Germans fled or were expelled from Eastern Europe. In the Federal Republic of Germany, the Basic Law and the 1953 Federal Expellees Law granted citizenship rights to ethnic German refugees and, later, to Aussiedler and Spätaussiedler — ethnic Germans from the former Soviet Union and Eastern Europe who immigrated until the programme was largely closed in the 1990s and 2000s. The term Volksdeutsche is now mainly historical.
