Telescopic sight

A telescopic sight, commonly called a scope, is a sighting device for firearms based on a refracting telescope. It magnifies the target and superimposes a reticle (crosshair or other aiming pattern) in the same optical plane, allowing precise aim at long range. Scopes also gather light, helping the shooter see in low-light conditions, and their reticles can be used to estimate range.

Construction

A scope is a sealed tube containing an objective lens, an erector system that inverts the image and (in variable-power scopes) changes magnification, a reticle, and an eyepiece. Adjustment turrets move the reticle or the erector assembly to correct windage (horizontal) and elevation (vertical), typically in clicks of 1/4 minute of angle or 0.1 milliradian. Tubes are commonly nitrogen-filled to prevent internal fogging and sealed against water.

Key specifications include magnification range (e.g., 3–9×), objective diameter (e.g., 40 mm), eye relief, field of view, and whether the reticle sits in the first or second focal plane — first-focal-plane reticles scale with magnification, keeping their subtensions accurate for range estimation at any power.

History

Experiments with telescopic sights date to the seventeenth century, but practical rifle scopes appeared in the mid-nineteenth century, notably the work of William Malcolm in the United States from the 1850s. Sharpshooters used long-tube scopes in the American Civil War; by the First World War, shorter, rugged scopes were standard sniper equipment, and the technology matured rapidly through the twentieth century.

Use

Scopes are used for hunting, target shooting, law enforcement, and military sniping. Mounting height and alignment ("zeroing") are critical: the shooter fires groups and adjusts the turrets until the point of aim coincides with the point of impact at a chosen distance.
