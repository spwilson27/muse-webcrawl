Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is a form of positive airway pressure ventilation in which a constant level of pressure is applied continuously to the airways through a mask, most commonly as the first-line treatment for obstructive sleep apnea.

A CPAP machine with hose and mask.

Mechanism

The machine raises airway pressure above atmospheric, pneumatically "splinting" the upper airway so it cannot collapse during sleep. Pressures are typically titrated between 4 and 20 cm H₂O during a sleep study.

History

CPAP was developed by Australian physician Colin Sullivan at Sydney's Royal Prince Alfred Hospital and first used clinically in 1980.

Variants

  • BiPAP (bilevel): different pressures for inhalation and exhalation.
  • APAP (auto-titrating): pressure adjusts automatically through the night.

Adherence

Long-term adherence is the main challenge; many patients use the device fewer hours than prescribed. Humidification and proper mask fitting improve tolerance.

Other uses

CPAP is also used in neonatal intensive care for respiratory distress and in pre-hospital care for acute pulmonary oedema.
