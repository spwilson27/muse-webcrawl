Old Earth creationism

Layered rock strata, cited by old Earth creationists as consistent with an ancient Earth.

Old Earth creationism (OEC) is an umbrella term for a number of types of creationism, including gap creationism and progressive creationism, which hold that the Earth and the universe are as ancient as mainstream science maintains (about 4.54 billion and 13.8 billion years respectively), while maintaining that life was created directly by God in a manner consistent with a literal or near-literal reading of the biblical Book of Genesis.

Overview

Unlike young Earth creationism, which holds that the Earth is only thousands of years old, old Earth creationism accepts the scientific consensus on the age of the Earth and the universe. Unlike theistic evolution, however, it generally rejects macroevolution as the mechanism by which the diversity of life arose, holding instead that God intervened directly at various points in natural history.

Types

Gap creationism

Gap creationism (also called ruin-restoration creationism) proposes that a vast gap of time exists between Genesis 1:1 and Genesis 1:2: God originally created the heavens and the Earth, which later "became" formless and void — possibly following the fall of Satan — before God restored or re-created life in six literal days. The view was popularized by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909).

Day-age creationism

Day-age creationism interprets each of the six "days" of Genesis 1 as representing long periods of time — millions or billions of years — rather than 24-hour periods, aligning the sequence of creation events broadly with the findings of geology and astronomy.

Progressive creationism

Progressive creationism holds that God created new forms of life in successive acts over hundreds of millions of years, consistent with the fossil record, while rejecting common descent as a full explanation. Astronomer Hugh Ross and the organization Reasons to Believe are prominent advocates.

History

Old Earth views became widespread among evangelical Christians in the 19th century as geology established the antiquity of the Earth; theologians such as Thomas Chalmers defended the gap interpretation. In the 20th century, young Earth creationism grew in influence after the publication of The Genesis Flood (1961), but old Earth creationism remained the position of many evangelical scholars and organizations.
