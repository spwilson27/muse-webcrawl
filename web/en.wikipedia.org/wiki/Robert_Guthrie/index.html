Robert Guthrie (June 28, 1916 – June 24, 1995) was an American physician and microbiologist who developed the first widely used screening test for phenylketonuria (PKU) in newborn infants — the Guthrie test — and thereby founded the modern practice of newborn screening.

The Guthrie test

Phenylketonuria is an inherited metabolic disorder in which the body cannot break down the amino acid phenylalanine; untreated, it causes severe intellectual disability, but a low-phenylalanine diet started in infancy prevents this. In 1961 Guthrie devised a simple bacterial inhibition assay: a drop of blood from a baby's heel is placed on filter paper, and the growth of Bacillus subtilis around the disc reveals elevated phenylalanine. The test was cheap, automatable and suitable for screening entire populations.

Impact

Despite initial resistance, Guthrie campaigned tirelessly for routine testing, and Massachusetts passed the first mandatory newborn PKU screening law in 1963; the practice spread worldwide. Because the filter-paper blood spot could be reused for other assays, Guthrie's card became the platform for expanding newborn screening panels that now test for dozens of congenital disorders. He spent most of his research career at the University at Buffalo.
