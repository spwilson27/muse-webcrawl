Discounted cash flow

The discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation method is used in finance to estimate the value of an investment based on its expected future cash flows. DCF analysis attempts to find the value of an investment today, based on projections of how much money it will generate in the future, adjusted for the time value of money: cash received in the future is worth less than the same amount today, so future cash flows are "discounted" at a rate reflecting their risk and the opportunity cost of capital.

Method

The analyst forecasts the free cash flows an asset will generate over an explicit projection period, then estimates a terminal value representing all subsequent cash flows, often using a perpetual growth model or an exit multiple. Each future amount is divided by (1 + r)t, where r is the discount rate and t the number of periods until it is received, to obtain its present value. The sum of the discounted cash flows and the discounted terminal value is the estimated enterprise or equity value. For valuing a whole firm, the cash flows are typically free cash flow to the firm and the discount rate is the weighted average cost of capital.

History

The mathematics of discounting is centuries old, but the method was formalised for investment analysis in the 20th century, notably in John Burr Williams' The Theory of Investment Value (1938), which argued that a stock's intrinsic value equals the present value of its future dividends. DCF became standard practice in corporate finance and investment banking with the spread of computing in the 1980s.

Limitations

DCF results are highly sensitive to assumptions: small changes in the discount rate or terminal growth rate can change the valuation greatly, and the terminal value often constitutes the majority of the total. Long-range cash flow forecasts are uncertain, leading to the criticism that the method can be a case of "garbage in, garbage out". Practitioners therefore use sensitivity and scenario analysis and cross-check DCF values against market multiples.
