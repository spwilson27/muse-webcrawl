Parent company

A parent company, sometimes called a holding company, is a company that has a controlling interest in another company, called a subsidiary. A parent company may simply own shares in subsidiaries as an investment, or it may itself carry on business operations while also controlling subsidiaries beneath it.

Control

Control of a subsidiary is usually obtained by owning a majority of its voting shares, conventionally more than 50 percent. Control can, however, be exercised with a smaller stake where the remaining shares are widely dispersed, or through agreements, board representation, or special classes of shares. Accounting standards generally require a parent to prepare consolidated financial statements combining its accounts with those of its subsidiaries.

Purposes

Corporate groups are organised under a parent company for many reasons: to limit legal liability between different lines of business, to organise operations by geography or product, to facilitate acquisitions and disposals, and for tax and regulatory planning. A "ultimate parent" sits at the top of a group; large multinational groups may have chains of intermediate holding companies.

Parent and subsidiary distinguished

Where a company owns all of the shares of another, the latter is a wholly owned subsidiary. Where the stake is large but below the level of control, the investee is instead an associate or affiliate. Regulators may "look through" corporate structures, and doctrines such as veil piercing can in limited circumstances make a parent liable for a subsidiary's obligations.
