Gossypium hirsutum

KingdomPlantae
FamilyMalvaceae
GenusGossypium
SpeciesG. hirsutum
Binomial nameGossypium hirsutum L.

Gossypium hirsutum, also known as upland cotton or Mexican cotton, is the most widely planted species of cotton in the world. Globally, about 90% of all cotton production is of cultivars derived from this species. In the United States, the world's largest exporter of cotton, it constitutes approximately 95% of all domestic cotton production.

Native to Mexico, the West Indies, northern South America, Central America and possibly tropical Florida, G. hirsutum is an allotetraploid species that arose from hybridization between an African A-genome species and an American D-genome species roughly 1–2 million years ago. Archaeological evidence from the Tehuacán Valley in Mexico indicates it was domesticated some 5,000 years ago.

Characteristics

The plant is a shrub growing to about 1–1.5 metres in cultivation, with lobed leaves and flowers that open cream-white and fade to pink. The fruit is a boll that splits open to reveal lint fibres surrounding the seeds. Fibres from this species are of medium staple length and are the workhorse of the global textile industry.

Cultivation

Upland cotton is grown across warm regions of the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia. Most commercial production uses genetically modified varieties engineered for insect resistance (Bt cotton) and herbicide tolerance.
