Cafeteria

A cafeteria, sometimes called a canteen outside the United States and Canada, is a type of food service location in which there is little or no waiting staff table service, whether in a restaurant or within an institution such as a large office building or school; a school dining location is also referred to as a dining hall or lunchroom (in American English). Cafeterias are different from coffeehouses, although the English term came from Latin American Spanish, where cafetería means "coffee shop".

History

The cafeteria format is often credited to William Childs and to the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, where a self-service restaurant demonstrated the concept; it spread as employers and institutions sought fast, cheap midday meals for large numbers. In the United States, cafeteria chains such as Childs and Horn & Hardart's Automats flourished in the early 20th century. School cafeterias became a major policy arena after the National School Lunch Act of 1946 established federally subsidised school meals.

Operation

Patrons typically take a tray and move along a serving line, selecting hot and cold items displayed behind counters or sneeze guards, then pay at a checkout (or use meal plans/ID accounts in institutional settings) before sitting at shared tables. Modern variants range from university dining halls with multiple themed stations to "grab-and-go" formats.
