Railway Mania

Railway Mania was a speculative investment bubble in the United Kingdom in the 1840s, in which share prices of railway companies soared as investors rushed to fund new lines. It followed the success of early railways such as the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, and peaked in 1846 before collapsing and ruining many investors.

Course of the bubble

Low interest rates, growing middle-class savings and promotional enthusiasm led to hundreds of proposed schemes. In the peak session of 1846, Parliament passed 263 acts authorising some 9,500 miles of new railway. Promoters such as George Hudson, the "Railway King", built empires of amalgamated companies, some paying dividends out of capital. Charles Ponzi-style accounting and outright fraud were exposed when shares fell from 1847 onward.

Consequences

Although many investors lost money, the mania left Britain with much of its core railway network: lines built with the inflated capital proved economically valuable for decades. The episode prompted tighter company law, standardised accounting for railways, and parliamentary scrutiny of private bills.

Historiography

Railway Mania is often compared with earlier bubbles such as the South Sea Bubble and with later technology booms as a classic case of speculative mania funding genuine infrastructure.
