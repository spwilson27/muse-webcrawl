Liquidation

Liquidation is the process in accounting by which a company is brought to an end. The assets and property of the business are redistributed. When a firm has been liquidated, it is sometimes referred to as wound-up or dissolved, although dissolution technically refers to the last stage of liquidation. The process of liquidation also arises when customs, an authority or agency in a country responsible for collecting and safeguarding customs duties, determines the final computation or ascertainment of the duties or drawback accruing on an entry.

Types

Compulsory liquidation

Compulsory liquidation, also called creditors' liquidation, occurs when a court orders that a company be wound up, usually on the petition of a creditor owed money by the company. In the United Kingdom, the petition is typically brought under the Insolvency Act 1986 on the ground that the company is unable to pay its debts.

Voluntary liquidation

Voluntary liquidation occurs when the members or creditors of a company resolve to wind it up. A members' voluntary liquidation is used where the company is solvent and the directors make a statutory declaration of solvency. A creditors' voluntary liquidation is used where the company is insolvent, and control of the process passes largely to the creditors.

The liquidator

Liquidation is carried out by a liquidator, usually a licensed insolvency practitioner, who takes control of the company, realises its assets, investigates the conduct of its directors, and distributes the proceeds to creditors according to a statutory order of priority. Secured creditors and preferential creditors (such as employees owed wages, up to statutory limits) rank ahead of unsecured creditors; shareholders receive any surplus only after all creditors have been paid in full.

Consequences

Once liquidation is complete, the company is dissolved and ceases to exist as a legal person. Liquidation differs from other insolvency procedures such as administration or a company voluntary arrangement, which aim to rescue the company or its business as a going concern. It also differs from bankruptcy, which in many jurisdictions applies to individuals rather than companies.
