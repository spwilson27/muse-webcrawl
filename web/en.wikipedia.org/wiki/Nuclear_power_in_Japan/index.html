Nuclear power in Japan

Nuclear power in Japan was for decades a major source of electricity in a country with few domestic fossil-fuel resources. Before 2011 a fleet of more than fifty reactors supplied about 30 percent of Japan's electricity, and official plans aimed to raise that share further. The Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster of March 2011 led to the shutdown of the entire fleet, a wholesale reform of nuclear regulation, and a long, contested process of restarts under stricter standards.

History

Japan's programme began with the Atomic Energy Basic Law of 1955, which restricted nuclear activity to peaceful purposes. The first commercial power reactor, at Tōkai, began operation in 1966, and the 1970s oil shocks accelerated construction of American-designed boiling and pressurized water reactors operated by regional utilities such as TEPCO and Kansai Electric. The Kashiwazaki-Kariwa plant became the largest nuclear station in the world by capacity.

Fukushima and its aftermath

On 11 March 2011 the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling at Fukushima Daiichi, causing three meltdowns and hydrogen explosions. All Japanese reactors were progressively shut down for checks; by May 2012 the country was briefly without nuclear generation for the first time in over forty years. The accident prompted creation of an independent Nuclear Regulation Authority and new regulatory requirements covering severe accidents, tsunami defences, and 40-year operating lifetimes (extendable once by 20 years).

Restarts

Beginning with Sendai 1 in 2015, reactors have been restarted in stages after passing the new safety reviews and obtaining local consent, though litigation and public opposition have kept the pace slow and many units are slated for decommissioning instead.
