Strange Situation
Devised byMary Ainsworth
FieldDevelopmental psychology
SubjectsInfants, 12–18 months
MeasuresAttachment style
ClassificationsSecure; insecure-avoidant; insecure-ambivalent; disorganised

The Strange Situation is a laboratory procedure devised by developmental psychologist Mary Ainsworth in the 1970s to study attachment in infants aged roughly 12 to 18 months. Building on John Bowlby's attachment theory, the procedure observes how an infant responds to a series of brief separations from, and reunions with, their caregiver in an unfamiliar room with a stranger.

Procedure

The protocol consists of eight episodes of about three minutes each. The infant and caregiver enter a room with toys; a stranger enters and interacts with the infant; the caregiver leaves the infant first with the stranger, then alone; and the caregiver returns twice. Observers score the infant's exploration, distress on separation, and — most diagnostic — behaviour on reunion: whether the child seeks contact and is soothed by it.

Attachment classifications

Ainsworth identified three patterns: secure attachment (the infant is distressed by separation but seeks and is comforted by the caregiver's return, and returns to play), insecure-avoidant (the infant shows little distress and avoids the caregiver on reunion), and insecure-ambivalent/resistant (the infant is highly distressed and cannot be easily settled). Mary Main later added a fourth category, disorganised attachment, associated with frightened or frightening caregiving and later developmental risk.

Criticism

The procedure has been criticised for cultural bias — distributions of classifications differ across countries, reflecting child-rearing norms — and for the stress it imposes on infants, though it remains one of the most influential paradigms in developmental psychology.
