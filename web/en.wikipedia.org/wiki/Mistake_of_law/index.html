Mistake of law

Mistake of law
Legal fieldCriminal law; contract law
Related maximIgnorantia juris non excusat
ContrastMistake of fact

Mistake of law is a legal defence or doctrine that arises when a person acts under a mistaken understanding of the law. It is conventionally distinguished from mistake of fact, in which a person is mistaken about the factual circumstances of their conduct.

Criminal law

The general rule in common-law systems is expressed in the maxim ignorantia juris non excusat — ignorance of the law is no excuse. A defendant cannot ordinarily escape liability by claiming not to know that the conduct was criminal. However, a mistake of law may be a defence where it negates a required mental element: some offences, such as tax offences or crimes requiring knowledge of a legal duty, demand that the defendant acted "willfully" or "knowingly," and an honest misunderstanding of the law can negate that intent, as in Cheek v. United States (1991). A related exception applies where a defendant reasonably relies on an official interpretation of the law, or where a statute is unconstitutionally vague.

United States Supreme Court cases

In Lambert v. California (1957), the Court held that due process may require notice of a registration duty where the conduct is wholly passive. In Heien v. North Carolina (2014), the Court held that a police officer's reasonable mistake of law can supply reasonable suspicion for a traffic stop.

Contract law

In contract law, a unilateral mistake of law is traditionally treated differently from a mistake of fact: historically courts were less willing to rescind a contract for a mistake of law, reasoning that the parties are presumed to know the law, though modern approaches (such as the Restatement (Second) of Contracts) largely treat mistakes of law and fact alike where they concern a basic assumption of the contract.
