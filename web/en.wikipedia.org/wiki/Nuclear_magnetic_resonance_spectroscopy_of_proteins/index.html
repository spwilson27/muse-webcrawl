Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy of proteins

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of proteins is a technique in structural biology for determining the three-dimensional structure and dynamics of proteins in solution. Along with X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, it is one of the principal methods of structural determination, and it is unique among them in reporting on proteins in near-physiological solution conditions and on their internal motions.

Principles

Because the signals of the many hydrogen atoms in even a small protein overlap heavily in one dimension, protein NMR uses multidimensional experiments that spread resonances across two, three, or four frequency dimensions. Proteins are usually produced by recombinant expression in bacteria grown on isotopically enriched media, labelling them uniformly with the NMR-active isotopes nitrogen-15 and carbon-13. The workhorse experiment is the two-dimensional 1H–15N HSQC spectrum, which gives one peak for nearly every amino-acid residue and serves as a fingerprint of the folded state.

Structure determination

Assignment proceeds through triple-resonance experiments that correlate neighbouring residues along the backbone. Structural restraints are then gathered chiefly from nuclear Overhauser effect (NOE) spectra, which reveal pairs of protons close in space, supplemented by dihedral-angle, hydrogen-bond, and residual dipolar coupling restraints. Simulated annealing software (such as CYANA, XPLOR-NIH, or ARIA) converts these restraints into an ensemble of conformers consistent with the data. Kurt Wüthrich shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing the method, first applied to proteins such as the bull seminal proteinase inhibitor in the 1980s.

Applications and limits

Solution NMR is restricted to relatively small proteins — historically under about 25–30 kDa, though TROSY techniques and methyl labelling have pushed measurements to assemblies of several hundred kilodaltons — because larger proteins tumble slowly and give broad, weak signals. Its strengths lie elsewhere: measuring dynamics across picosecond-to-second timescales, mapping binding interfaces by chemical-shift perturbation, studying intrinsically disordered proteins, and screening small-molecule ligands in drug discovery.
