Bicuspid aortic valve
SpecialtyCardiology, medical genetics
SymptomsOften none; later heart murmur, stenosis, regurgitation

A bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is a congenital heart condition in which the aortic valve has only two leaflets (cusps) instead of the normal three. It is the most common congenital cardiac abnormality, affecting roughly 0.5 to 2 percent of the population, and occurs more often in males than females.

Causes and genetics

BAV has a strong hereditary component and may run in families in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable penetrance. Mutations in genes such as NOTCH1 have been implicated, and the condition is associated with Turner syndrome and with coarctation of the aorta.

Presentation and complications

Many people with BAV have no symptoms for decades, and the valve may be discovered incidentally by echocardiography. Over time the abnormal valve tends to calcify earlier than a normal valve, producing aortic stenosis; it may also leak (aortic regurgitation). BAV is associated with dilatation and aneurysm of the ascending aorta (bicuspid aortopathy), which carries a risk of aortic dissection, and with an increased risk of infective endocarditis.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is by transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography; CT or MRI may be used to measure the aorta. Management includes regular imaging surveillance, blood-pressure control, and screening of first-degree relatives. Severe stenosis or regurgitation, or a markedly dilated aorta, may require surgical valve replacement and aortic repair, or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in selected patients.
