Rashidun Caliphate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Early Islamic armies crossing the Syrian desert, in a modern historical illustration]

The Rashidun Caliphate (632–661) was the first of the four major Islamic caliphates, ruled by the four 'rightly guided' caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — the closest companions and relatives of the Prophet Muhammad. In three decades its armies burst out of Arabia, destroyed the Sasanian Empire and stripped the Byzantine Empire of Syria, Egypt and North Africa, creating one of the largest empires yet seen and carrying Islam permanently into three continents. Its end in civil war (the first fitna) and the assassination of Ali opened the Sunni–Shia divide.

Conquests

Abu Bakr (r. 632–634) first subdued the tribes that renounced Islam after Muhammad's death (the Ridda wars), then launched invasions of Iraq and Syria. Under Umar (r. 634–644) Muslim armies won at Yarmouk (636) against Byzantium and al-Qadisiyyah (636) against Persia, took Jerusalem and Egypt, and organised the conquered lands with garrison cities, a stipend register (diwan) and the new Islamic calendar. Uthman (r. 644–656) completed the conquest of Persia and standardised the text of the Qur'an.

Fitna and end

Resentment of Uthman's nepotism led to his murder in Medina (656). His successor Ali, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, faced revolt from Mu'awiya, governor of Syria; the Battle of Siffin (657) ended in disputed arbitration, and Ali was assassinated by a Kharijite in 661. His son Hasan abdicated to Mu'awiya, who founded the Umayyad Caliphate. Those who held that leadership belonged to Ali and the Prophet's family became the Shia; the majority tradition became Sunni Islam.
