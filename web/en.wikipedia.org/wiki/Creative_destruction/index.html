Creative destruction

Creative destruction (German: schöpferische Zerstörung) is a concept in economics describing the incessant process of industrial mutation that revolutionises the economic structure from within, destroying the old one while creating a new one. The term was popularised by the Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter in his book Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy (1942), who adapted an idea he found in the work of Karl Marx.

The concept

For Schumpeter, the entrepreneur and innovation are the engines of capitalism: new products, processes, markets and organisational forms render existing firms, skills and technologies obsolete. Competition that matters, he argued, is not price competition among similar goods but competition from the new commodity, the new technology and the new source of supply, which strikes at the foundations of existing firms. Examples commonly cited include the displacement of horse-drawn transport by railways and automobiles, of film photography by digital imaging, of video rental shops by streaming, and of traditional retail by e-commerce.

Influence and criticism

The idea underlies Schumpeterian growth theory and modern evolutionary and endogenous-growth economics; models of growth through creative destruction, notably by Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt, formalised the process and helped explain the relationship between competition, innovation and growth. Critics note that the process imposes real costs — unemployment, stranded communities and lost firms — and that market power, patents and regulation can slow or distort the destruction of incumbents, while others question whether Schumpeter himself viewed the process as ultimately undermining capitalism's institutional foundations. The phrase has also entered management and popular usage as a synonym for disruptive change.
