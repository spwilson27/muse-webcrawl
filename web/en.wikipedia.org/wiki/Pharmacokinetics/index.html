Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics (from Ancient Greek pharmakon "drug" and kinetikos "moving, putting in motion") is a branch of pharmacology dedicated to describing how the body affects a specific substance after administration. The substances of interest include any chemical xenobiotic such as pharmaceutical drugs, pesticides, food additives, and cosmetics. It attempts to analyse chemical metabolism and to discover the fate of a chemical from the moment it is administered up until the point it is completely eliminated from the body.

Pharmacokinetics is often summarised by the acronym ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion. It is contrasted with pharmacodynamics, which studies what the drug does to the body.

Overview of processes

Absorption

Absorption is the process by which a drug enters the bloodstream. For oral drugs it depends on dissolution, gastrointestinal pH, transporters, and first-pass metabolism in the liver. The fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged is the drug's bioavailability.

Distribution

Once absorbed, a drug distributes through blood and tissues according to its lipophilicity, plasma protein binding, and tissue perfusion. The theoretical volume relating the amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration is the volume of distribution (Vd).

Metabolism

Metabolism, mainly in the liver, chemically modifies drugs—typically to more water-soluble metabolites. Phase I reactions (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) are often catalysed by cytochrome P450 enzymes; phase II reactions conjugate the drug with glucuronide, sulfate, or other groups.

Excretion

Excretion removes drug and metabolites from the body, principally via the kidneys into urine, and also via bile, sweat, and exhaled air.

Metrics

Commonly measured parameters include Cmax (peak plasma concentration), Tmax (time to peak), area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), clearance (Cl), elimination half-life (t½), and steady-state concentration under repeated dosing. Most drugs at therapeutic doses follow first-order kinetics, in which a constant fraction is eliminated per unit time.

Models

Pharmacokinetic modelling represents the body as one or more compartments between which drug moves at rates described by differential equations. More detailed physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models incorporate organ blood flows and tissue composition. Population pharmacokinetics studies variability between individuals, which arises from genetics, age, disease, and drug interactions.

Clinical applications

Pharmacokinetics guides dose selection and dosing intervals, therapeutic drug monitoring (e.g., for aminoglycosides, vancomycin, and antiepileptics), adjustment in renal or hepatic impairment, and prediction of drug–drug interactions, such as those mediated by CYP3A4 inhibitors and inducers.
