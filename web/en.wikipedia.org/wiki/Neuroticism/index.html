Neuroticism

Neuroticism is a personality trait characterised by a long-term tendency to experience negative emotions such as anxiety, anger, guilt, envy, and depressed mood. It is one of the Big Five personality traits, alongside openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, and agreeableness. People who score high in neuroticism are more emotionally reactive and vulnerable to stress: they interpret ordinary situations as threatening and minor frustrations as hopelessly difficult, and they tend to remain in negative moods for longer.

Measurement and facets

Neuroticism is measured by self-report inventories such as the NEO Personality Inventory and the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, in which Hans Eysenck made neuroticism (N) one of his three superfactors. In the NEO framework the trait comprises six facets: anxiety, angry hostility, depression, self-consciousness, impulsiveness, and vulnerability. Twin studies suggest that roughly half of the individual variation in neuroticism is heritable.

Health associations

High neuroticism is a robust risk factor for anxiety disorders, depression, and other internalising disorders, and it is associated with poorer physical health outcomes, greater somatic complaint reporting, and increased healthcare use. Low neuroticism, at the opposite pole, corresponds to emotional stability: calmness, even temper, and resilience under pressure. The trait is moderately stable across adulthood but tends to decline somewhat with age.

Biology

Neuroticism has been linked to heightened reactivity of the amygdala and other components of the brain's threat-detection circuitry, to serotonergic function, and to sensitivity of the behavioural inhibition system in Jeffrey Gray's reinforcement sensitivity theory. Despite the shared name, the modern trait is conceptually distinct from the older clinical category of "neurosis"; see neuropsychology for the study of brain and behaviour more broadly.
