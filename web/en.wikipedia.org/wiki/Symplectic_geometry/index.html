Symplectic geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Symplectic geometry is a branch of differential geometry and differential topology that studies symplectic manifolds; that is, differentiable manifolds equipped with a closed, nondegenerate 2-form. Symplectic geometry has its origins in the Hamiltonian formulation of classical mechanics, where the phase space of certain classical systems takes on the structure of a symplectic manifold.

Overview

A symplectic form on a vector space V is a skew-symmetric bilinear form ω that is nondegenerate: if ω(u, v) = 0 for all v, then u = 0. Such a form can exist only on even-dimensional vector spaces, and by a linear version of Darboux's theorem any symplectic vector space has a basis in which ω takes a standard block-diagonal form.

A symplectic manifold is a smooth manifold M equipped with a closed, nondegenerate differential 2-form ω, called the symplectic form. The study of symplectic manifolds is called symplectic geometry or symplectic topology. A symplectic manifold necessarily has even dimension 2n, and the form ωn is a volume form, so symplectic manifolds are orientable.

Comparison with Riemannian geometry

Unlike Riemannian manifolds, symplectic manifolds have no local invariants such as curvature: Darboux's theorem states that all symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally identical. The interesting invariants of symplectic geometry are therefore global, and symplectic topology blends ideas from geometry, analysis, and dynamics.

Important theorems

  • Darboux's theorem: every symplectic manifold is locally symplectomorphic to standard symplectic space.
  • Gromov's nonsqueezing theorem (1985): a symplectic ball cannot be embedded symplectically into a thinner cylinder, revealing a fundamental rigidity of symplectic embeddings.
  • Arnold conjecture on fixed points of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms, proved in many cases using Floer homology.

Contact with mechanics

The phase space of a classical mechanical system — the cotangent bundle of its configuration space — carries a canonical symplectic form. Hamilton's equations describe the flow of a Hamiltonian function with respect to this form, making symplectic geometry the natural mathematical language of classical mechanics.
