Indians in the United States

Indians in the United States — commonly known as Indian Americans — are citizens and residents of the United States with ancestry from India. With a population of over 4.9 million (including those reporting partial ancestry), they are among the largest Asian American groups, and the largest group of South Asian Americans. Indian Americans have, on average, among the highest educational attainment and household incomes of any ethnic group in the United States.

History

Small numbers of Indians reached the United States from the 17th century onward, with larger migration — particularly of Punjabi Sikhs to the Pacific Coast — beginning in the early 20th century, despite restrictive laws such as the Immigration Act of 1917 and the Supreme Court's ruling in United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind (1923) that Indians were ineligible for citizenship. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 transformed the community, admitting large numbers of highly educated professionals; later waves included technology workers on H-1B visas and family-sponsored migrants.

Demographics and contributions

Large communities exist in New Jersey, California, Texas, Illinois and New York. Indian Americans are prominent in medicine, engineering, academia, technology — including chief executives of major firms — and increasingly in politics, with members serving in Congress and in the highest offices of government. Major cultural observances include Diwali, now officially recognised in several states and marked at the White House.
