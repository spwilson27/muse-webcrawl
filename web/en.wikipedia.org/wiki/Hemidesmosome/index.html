Hemidesmosome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Hemidesmosome
TypeCell–matrix junction
LocationBasal surface of epithelial cells (esp. skin)
Key componentsIntegrin α6β4, plectin, BP230, BP180 (collagen XVII), laminin-332
FunctionAnchoring keratin intermediate filaments to the basement membrane

Hemidesmosomes are very small stud-like structures found in keratinocytes in the epidermis of skin that attach to the extracellular matrix. They are similar in form to desmosomes when visualized by electron microscopy; however, desmosomes attach to intermediate filaments of adjacent cells, whereas hemidesmosomes attach to the basement membrane. The name is derived from their resemblance, under the electron microscope, to half of a desmosome.

Structure

Hemidesmosomes are asymmetrical, multi-protein complexes. On the intracellular side, a dense cytoplasmic plaque contains the plakin-family proteins plectin and BP230 (BPAG1), which link the junction to the keratin intermediate-filament network of the cell. The transmembrane components are the integrin α6β4 — an unusual integrin whose β4 subunit has an exceptionally long cytoplasmic tail — and BP180, also known as collagen XVII or BPAG2. Outside the cell, these receptors bind laminin-332 in the basement membrane, which in turn connects through collagen VII anchoring fibrils to the dermis below.

Two types are described: type I hemidesmosomes, found in stratified and complex epithelia, contain plectin, BP230, BP180, and integrin α6β4; type II hemidesmosomes, found in some simple epithelia such as intestine, contain integrin α6β4 and plectin but lack the BP antigens.

Function

By riveting epithelial cells to the underlying basement membrane, hemidesmosomes give skin and other stratified epithelia their resistance to mechanical stress and shearing forces. Beyond simple adhesion, integrin α6β4 also participates in signalling pathways that influence cell survival, proliferation, and migration — important in wound healing, where hemidesmosomes are disassembled to allow keratinocytes to crawl across the wound bed.

Clinical significance

Defects in hemidesmosomal proteins cause blistering diseases of the skin. Inherited mutations produce forms of epidermolysis bullosa: mutations in the genes for integrin α6 or β4, plectin, or collagen XVII cause junctional and pyloric-atresia-associated forms in which the epidermis separates from the dermis with trivial trauma. Autoimmune attack on hemidesmosomal antigens causes bullous pemphigoid, a blistering disease of the elderly in which autoantibodies target BP180 and BP230, and mucous membrane pemphigoid, which predominantly affects mucosal surfaces.
