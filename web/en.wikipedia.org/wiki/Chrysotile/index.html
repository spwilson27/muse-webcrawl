Chrysotile, or white asbestos, is the most common form of asbestos, accounting for the great majority of the asbestos mined and used commercially worldwide. It is a soft, fibrous silicate mineral in the serpentine subgroup of phyllosilicates, with chemical formula Mg3(Si2O5)(OH)4. Unlike the amphibole asbestos minerals, chrysotile's crystal structure is a curled sheet, which rolls into cylindrical fibrils.

Properties

Chrysotile forms from the hydrothermal alteration (serpentinisation) of ultramafic rocks. Its fibres are flexible, silky and heat-resistant, with good tensile strength — properties that led to its widespread use in cement products, insulation, brake linings, gaskets, textiles and fireproofing. The mineral decomposes to forsterite and silica at temperatures around 550–750 °C.

Health effects

Chrysotile, like all asbestos forms, is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as a Group 1 carcinogen. Inhalation of its fibres can cause asbestosis, lung cancer and mesothelioma. Although chrysotile fibres are cleared from the lungs faster than amphibole fibres, major health bodies including the WHO maintain that chrysotile causes cancer and that there is no safe threshold of exposure. Its production and use are banned in more than 60 countries, though it remains in production in countries such as Russia, Kazakhstan and China.

Polytypes

Three polytypes of chrysotile are recognised: clinochrysotile (monoclinic, the most common), orthochrysotile (orthorhombic) and parachrysotile. Chrysotile is closely related to the non-fibrous serpentine minerals lizardite and antigorite, which share its chemistry but not its curled structure.
