Nanban art

Nanban art (Japanese: 南蛮美術) refers to Japanese art of the 16th and 17th centuries influenced by contact with the Nanban ("Southern barbarians"), the traders and missionaries who arrived in Japan from Europe, principally Portugal and Spain, beginning with the landing of Portuguese sailors on Tanegashima in 1543 and ending with Japan's expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639.

Nanban screens

The best-known works are the byōbu folding screens depicting the arrival of the great black ships (kurofune) at Nagasaki: Portuguese carracks crowded with sailors, merchants, African servants and Jesuit missionaries, observed by Japanese onlookers. Painted in mineral pigments and gold leaf on paper by artists of the Kanō school and by town painters, dozens of such screens survive, more than sixty of them catalogued.

Other forms

Nanban influence extended to export lacquerware decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay for European markets, including portable altars and caskets commissioned by missionaries; to Japanese renderings of Western maps and city views; and to Christian devotional objects produced for Japanese converts. Conversely, Japanese craftsmen adopted European techniques such as oil painting and copperplate engraving through Jesuit painting seminaries.

Legacy

With the expulsion edicts of the Tokugawa shogunate, Nanban art disappeared, though Nagasaki continued a limited trade with the Dutch and Chinese, giving rise to the later Nagasaki school of painting.
