Delegated legislation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Delegated legislation (also called secondary or subordinate legislation) is law made by a person or body other than the legislature under authority granted by an Act of the legislature (the “parent” or “enabling” Act). It includes regulations, orders, rules and byelaws, and in the United Kingdom is issued mainly as statutory instruments; in the United States its equivalent is agency rulemaking under the Administrative Procedure Act.

Delegation is indispensable to modern government: legislatures cannot foresee every detail of technical fields, and delegated powers allow speed and expertise. It is also constitutionally sensitive — critics since Lord Hewart's The New Despotism (1929) have warned of executive lawmaking escaping parliamentary control. Controls include laying procedures in parliament (affirmative and negative resolution), consultation and publication requirements, sunsetting, and judicial review for ultra vires (exceeding the parent Act). “Henry VIII clauses”, which let ministers amend primary legislation itself by delegated power, attract particular scrutiny.
