Friedman doctrine

Milton Friedman, who set out the doctrine in 1970.

The Friedman doctrine, also called shareholder theory or shareholder primacy, is a normative theory of business ethics advanced by the economist Milton Friedman in his 1970 New York Times Magazine essay "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits". It holds that a business's sole social responsibility is to increase its profits, within the constraints of the law and accepted ethical custom, and that managers — as agents of shareholders — must act in the shareholders' financial interest rather than pursue social objectives of their own.

The argument

Friedman argued that only individuals, not businesses, have responsibilities; that executives who spend corporate funds on social causes are effectively levying a tax on shareholders, employees or customers without democratic consent; and that the free market, through competition and the price mechanism, best serves the public interest when companies focus on profit. Social goals, he contended, are the proper business of government and private philanthropy, not of corporate management.

Influence and criticism

The doctrine became a foundational principle of Anglo-American corporate governance in the 1980s–2000s, reinforcing shareholder-value management, share buybacks and executive pay tied to stock performance. Critics argue it encourages short-termism, externalises environmental and social costs and erodes trust; the alternative stakeholder theory contends companies owe duties to employees, customers, communities and the environment. In 2019 the Business Roundtable, a group of chief executives, issued a statement committing to serve all stakeholders, a move widely read as a direct challenge to shareholder primacy, though debate continues over whether practice has materially changed.
