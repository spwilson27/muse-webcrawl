Internal labour market

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diagram of an internal labour market within a large firm

An internal labour market (ILM) is an administrative unit within a firm in which pricing and allocation of labour is governed by a set of administrative rules and procedures rather than by the external labour market. The concept was developed systematically by Peter Doeringer and Michael Piore in their 1971 book Internal Labor Markets and Manpower Analysis.

Characteristics

Jobs within an internal labour market are typically filled by promotion or transfer from within the firm, and entry from outside occurs only at a limited number of "ports of entry" at the bottom of job ladders. Wages are attached to jobs rather than to individual workers, and are often determined by custom, rules of thumb, and considerations of internal equity rather than market clearing.

Rationale

Firms maintain internal labour markets because of skill specificity (firm-specific training), on-the-job training whose returns must be protected, and the role of custom and workplace norms in stabilising expectations. Internal markets can raise productivity by encouraging long-term attachment, but they may also insulate workers and firms from external competition.

Criticism

Critics argue that internal labour markets can entrench insider–outsider divisions, discriminate against groups excluded from ports of entry, and reduce allocative efficiency. The growth of contingent work and outsourcing since the 1980s has been seen by some scholars as eroding traditional internal labour markets.
