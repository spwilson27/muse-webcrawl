Tanganyika was a colonial territory in East Africa administered by the United Kingdom from 1922 to 1961. It comprised the greater part of the former German East Africa — the provinces of Ruanda and Urundi were detached and administered separately by Belgium, and the Kionga Triangle went to Portuguese Mozambique. The territory took its name from Lake Tanganyika on its western border.

History

British forces occupied the German colony during the East African campaign of the First World War, defeating Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck's Schutztruppe only after the Armistice in November 1918. The League of Nations formally granted Britain a Class B mandate over Tanganyika in 1922. After the Second World War the mandate became a United Nations trust territory (1946), with Britain obliged to prepare it for eventual self-government.

Administration was exercised through indirect rule, with governors such as Sir Donald Cameron (1925–1931) building systems of Native Authorities. The economy depended on sisal, coffee, cotton and groundnuts; the British government's disastrous Tanganyika groundnut scheme (1946–1951) became a byword for failed colonial development planning.

Independence

In 1954 Julius Nyerere co-founded the Tanganyika African National Union (TANU), which won elections in 1958–1960 and pressed successfully for self-government. Tanganyika became independent within the Commonwealth on December 9, 1961, with Nyerere as prime minister; it became a republic a year later. In 1964 Tanganyika united with Zanzibar to form the United Republic of Tanzania.
