Border control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Border control comprises the measures a state takes to monitor and regulate the movement of people and goods across its borders: passports and visas, entry inspection by border forces, customs examination, quarantine, and the physical and technological infrastructure of boundaries (fences, sensors, maritime patrols). Its modern form dates from the First World War, when the emergency passport regime became permanent; before 1914 most of Europe permitted travel without passports at all.

Border control balances facilitation against security and migration management: the Schengen Area (in force 1995) abolished internal checks among its members while hardening the common external border (Frontex, the EU border agency, founded 2004); other systems (the US post-2001 Department of Homeland Security model) fused immigration, customs and counter-terrorism. Critics describe a “border industrial complex” and the externalisation of control to transit states; governments defend control as constitutive of sovereignty and welfare-state closure. The COVID-19 pandemic (2020–22) re-normalised wholesale border closure, and climate, conflict and inequality keep the politics of the border central to twenty-first-century states.
