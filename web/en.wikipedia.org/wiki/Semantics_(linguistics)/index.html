Semantics is the study of linguistic meaning — how words, phrases, and sentences convey meaning, and how meaning is structured and interpreted. It is a major branch of linguistics, closely related to pragmatics, which studies how context contributes to meaning.

History

The term derives from the Greek sēmantikos, "significant". Modern semantics emerged in the late 19th century through the work of Michel Bréal, and was transformed in the 20th century by logical approaches stemming from Gottlob Frege's distinction between sense and reference, and by Richard Montague's application of formal logic to natural language.

Key topics

  • Lexical semantics: the meaning of words and relations among them, such as synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, and polysemy.
  • Formal semantics: the use of logic and model theory to characterize truth conditions, quantification, and compositionality.
  • Conceptual semantics: how language encodes conceptual structures in the mind.
  • Computational semantics: automated processing of meaning, including word-sense disambiguation and semantic parsing.

Sense and reference

A foundational distinction, introduced by Frege, separates the sense of an expression (the mode of presentation of its referent) from its reference (the object it picks out). "The Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" have the same reference — the planet Venus — but different senses.
