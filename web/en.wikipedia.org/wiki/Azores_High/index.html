The Azores High, also known as the North Atlantic High, is a large subtropical semi-permanent centre of high atmospheric pressure typically found south of the Azores in the Atlantic Ocean, at the Horse latitudes. It forms one cell of the North Atlantic oscillation, and at its summer maximum it extends westward toward Bermuda, where it is known as the Bermuda High.

Formation and movement

The high is produced by the descending branch of the Hadley cell: air rising near the equator moves poleward aloft and subsides in the subtropics, warming and drying as it descends. In winter the Azores High is relatively weak and situated farther south, allowing mid-latitude storms to cross the Atlantic toward Europe. In summer it strengthens and migrates northward; the descending air tends to act as a warm cap, inhibiting atmospheric convection and producing settled, dry weather on its flanks.

Effects on weather and climate

The position and strength of the Azores High strongly influence weather in western Europe, North Africa and the eastern United States. When the high extends toward Iberia, it deflects Atlantic storm tracks northward and contributes to hot, dry summers in the Mediterranean region. Its western (Bermuda) flank pumps warm, moist air into eastern North America, contributing to summer heat and humidity, and it steers tropical cyclones: storms forming off West Africa generally travel westward along the high's southern periphery, and whether they curve northward ("recurve") often depends on weaknesses in the ridge. The high also drives the trade winds on its equatorward side and contributes to the aridity of the Sahara and the formation of the Sargasso Sea gyre.
