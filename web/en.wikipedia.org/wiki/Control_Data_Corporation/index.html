Control Data Corporation

Control Data Corporation (CDC) was a mainframe and supercomputer company that in the 1960s and 1970s was one of the nine major United States computer companies known as "IBM and the seven dwarfs" (in CDC's case, one of the "BUNCH"). Founded in 1957 in Minneapolis, Minnesota, by William Norris and engineers from Sperry Rand, CDC became famous for high-performance machines designed by Seymour Cray, including the CDC 6600 (1964), generally credited as the first successful supercomputer.

Supercomputers

The CDC 1604 (1960), designed by Cray, was an early transistorised success. The CDC 6600, with its Freon cooling, peripheral processors and scoreboarding, was roughly three times faster than its nearest rival and held the title of world's fastest computer from 1964 to 1969, when the CDC 7600 succeeded it. Cray left in 1972 to found Cray Research, after the troubled CDC STAR-100 project. CDC's later supercomputing line, via its ETA Systems subsidiary (maker of the ETA10), closed in 1989.

Other ventures

CDC sold peripherals, OEM disk drives, and services, and was an early pioneer of computer-based education with the PLATO system, a networked teaching platform with early forms of online community, message boards and multiplayer games. Under Norris, the company also pursued socially oriented business ventures in inner cities and agriculture.

Decline

Beset by the shift to minicomputers and workstations, Japanese competition, and heavy losses, CDC restructured in the late 1980s and 1990s: its hardware businesses were sold or spun off (Seagate took much of the disk drive business; the remaining services company became Ceridian). Control Data Systems was acquired by British Telecom in 1999, ending the CDC name.
