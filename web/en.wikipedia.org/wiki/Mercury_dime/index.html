The Mercury dime is a ten-cent coin struck by the United States Mint from 1916 to 1945. Designed by sculptor Adolph Alexander Weinman, the coin is officially the Winged Liberty Head dime, but it acquired its popular name because the winged cap worn by Liberty on the obverse was widely mistaken for the Roman god Mercury. The reverse depicts a fasces, symbolising unity and strength, entwined with an olive branch. The Mercury dime is widely regarded as one of the most beautiful United States coin designs.


The Winged Liberty obverse and fasces reverse of the Mercury dime.

Background and design

Under the Coinage Act of 1890, coin designs could be changed without congressional approval after 25 years in use. In 1916 the Mint replaced the Barber coinage, and Weinman, a student of Augustus Saint-Gaudens, won the commission for the dime and half dollar. His obverse shows a youthful Liberty wearing a winged Phrygian cap, intended to symbolise freedom of thought. Model Elsie Stevens, wife of the poet Wallace Stevens, is often said to have posed for the portrait. Weinman's initials appear on the obverse, and the date and mottoes complete the design.

Production and collecting

The dime was struck at the Philadelphia, Denver, and San Francisco mints. Key dates include the 1916-D, of which only about 264,000 were minted, and the 1942/1 overdates from Philadelphia and Denver. Collectors place a premium on coins with "full split bands" — fully separated horizontal bands on the fasces — indicating a sharp strike. The series was replaced in 1946 by the Roosevelt dime, issued in honour of the recently deceased President Franklin D. Roosevelt.
