Full-frame digital SLR

This page corresponds to the batch slug "Full-frame digital SLF" (SLF), the standard article being the full-frame digital single-lens reflex camera (digital SLR).

A full-frame digital SLR is a digital single-lens reflex camera whose image sensor is the same size as a frame of 35 mm film — approximately 36 × 24 mm. The term distinguishes such cameras from the majority of earlier digital SLRs, which used smaller sensors (such as APS-C) and therefore imposed a crop factor on the field of view of attached lenses.

Characteristics

A full-frame sensor captures the same angle of view that a 35 mm film camera would with a given lens, so lenses behave as they did in the film era, without a crop factor. The larger photosites typically give better low-light (high-ISO) performance and a greater ability to achieve shallow depth of field than smaller sensors. The trade-offs are larger, heavier and more expensive bodies and lenses, and higher manufacturing costs for the sensor itself.

History

Early full-frame models included the Contax N Digital (2002), Canon's EOS-1Ds (2002) and the Kodak DCS Pro 14n. Full-frame bodies long remained professional tools, but prices fell with models such as the Canon EOS 5D (2005), which brought full-frame to advanced amateurs, and Nikon's D700 (2008). In the 2010s, mirrorless cameras with full-frame sensors — beginning with the Sony α7 series in 2013 — progressively displaced the DSLR format, and by the early 2020s Canon and Nikon had ended development of new DSLR models in favour of their mirrorless mounts.
