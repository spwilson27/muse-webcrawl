Metastasis

Metastasis is the spread of a pathogenic agent from an initial or primary site to a different or secondary site within the host's body; the term is typically used when referring to metastasis by a cancerous tumor. The newly pathological sites, then, are metastases ("mets"). Cancer occurs after cells are genetically altered to proliferate rapidly and indefinitely; when such cells detach, invade surrounding tissue and spread via the blood or lymph, they can seed secondary tumors. Most cancer deaths are attributable to metastatic disease rather than to the primary tumor.

The metastatic cascade

  1. Local invasion — cancer cells invade adjacent tissue, often involving epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and loss of cell adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin.
  2. Intravasation — cells enter blood or lymphatic vessels.
  3. Circulation — tumor cells survive shear stress and immune attack, sometimes shielded by platelet aggregates; clusters of circulating tumor cells have higher metastatic potential than single cells.
  4. Extravasation — cells arrest in capillary beds and exit the circulation.
  5. Colonization — cells adapt to the new tissue microenvironment, may lie dormant for years, then proliferate into clinically detectable metastases.

Routes and patterns

Cancers spread by lymphatic spread (typical of carcinomas, to regional lymph nodes), hematogenous spread (typical of sarcomas; common sites are lung, liver, bone and brain), transcoelomic spread across body cavities (e.g., ovarian cancer seeding the peritoneum, including Krukenberg tumors), and perineural invasion. The "seed and soil" hypothesis of Stephen Paget (1889) holds that metastasis depends on compatibility between the tumor cell (seed) and the target organ microenvironment (soil).

Diagnosis and treatment

Metastatic disease is staged as stage IV in the TNM system (M1). Diagnosis relies on imaging (CT, PET, MRI), biopsy and histopathology, and tumor markers. Treatment is usually systemic — chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hormonal therapy and immunotherapy — with surgery or radiotherapy reserved for limited (oligometastatic) disease or palliation.
