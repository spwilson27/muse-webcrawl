A cherub (plural cherubim; Hebrew: כְּרוּב, keruv) is a type of supernatural being mentioned in the Hebrew Bible and in ancient Near Eastern religions. In biblical tradition, cherubim are attendants of God, guarding sacred places and bearing His throne. They are described in the Book of Ezekiel as composite creatures with multiple faces and wings, quite unlike the chubby winged infants of later Western art, a depiction that derives instead from the classical putto.

Cherubim were placed at the east of the Garden of Eden to guard the way to the Tree of Life.

In the Hebrew Bible

Cherubim appear throughout the Hebrew Bible. After the expulsion of Adam and Eve, God stationed cherubim and a flaming sword at the entrance of the Garden of Eden (Genesis 3:24). Two golden cherubim with outstretched wings overshadowed the mercy seat of the Ark of the Covenant in the Tabernacle (Exodus 25), and carved and woven cherubim decorated the curtains and walls of Solomon's Temple. In the Psalms, God is described as enthroned upon the cherubim and as riding upon a cherub in the storm (Psalm 18).

Ezekiel's vision

The prophet Ezekiel describes the cherubim in elaborate detail as "living creatures" with four faces (human, lion, ox/eagle) and four wings, moving with wheels full of eyes (Ezekiel 1 and 10). Ezekiel 28 also addresses the king of Tyre with an oracle that many interpreters read as describing a fallen cherub, a passage later linked in Christian tradition with the fall of Satan.

In later tradition

In medieval Christian angelology, systematised by Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, cherubim form the second-highest choir of angels, below the seraphim, and are associated with contemplation and wisdom. In Renaissance and Baroque art the term became conflated with the putto, producing the familiar image of a plump winged child. In Islam, the karubiyyun are angels close to God who bear His throne.

Etymology and origins

The Hebrew keruv is often connected with the Akkadian kurību, a protective intercessory spirit, and scholars compare the cherubim to the winged bulls and sphinx-like lamassu that guarded Mesopotamian and Egyptian temples and palaces.
