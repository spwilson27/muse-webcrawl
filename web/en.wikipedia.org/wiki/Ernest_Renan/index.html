Ernest Renan

Joseph Ernest Renan (27 February 1823 – 2 October 1892) was a French historian, philologist, and philosopher, author of the bestselling Life of Jesus (1863).

Joseph Ernest Renan (27 February 1823 – 2 October 1892) was a French Orientalist, historian of religion, philologist, philosopher, and biblical scholar. A leading intellectual of the Third Republic, he is best known for his Vie de Jésus (1863), the first volume of his History of the Origins of Christianity, which presented Jesus as an extraordinary human being rather than a divine figure and became one of the century's best-selling and most controversial books.

Early life

Renan was born in Tréguier, Brittany, to a family of fishermen and merchants. Destined for the priesthood, he was educated at seminaries, concluding at Saint-Sulpice in Paris, where a crisis of faith — driven by his study of Hebrew philology and German biblical criticism — led him to abandon the Church in 1845. His sister Henriette, his closest companion, supported his scholarly career. His treatise on the Semitic languages won the Prix Volney in 1847, and his doctoral work and an archaeological mission to Phoenicia (1860–61) — during which Henriette died of fever — established his scholarly reputation.

Life of Jesus and the Collège de France

Appointed to the chair of Hebrew at the Collège de France in 1862, Renan was suspended after his inaugural lecture called Jesus "an incomparable man" while denying his divinity. The published Vie de Jésus (1863) was a sensation: elegantly written, it treated the Gospels as sources to be sifted historically and painted an idyllic Galilean landscape drawn from his travels. It outraged the Church, delighted freethinkers, and was eventually placed on the Index only after long controversy; Renan was restored to his chair in 1871. He was elected to the Académie française in 1878 and became administrator of the Collège de France in 1883.

Later thought

Renan's other famous work is the 1882 Sorbonne lecture "Qu'est-ce qu'une nation?" ("What Is a Nation?"), which defined nationhood not by race, language or religion but by shared memory and a continuing consent — "a daily plebiscite". His early work on Semitic languages contained racial generalisations later criticised, though he repudiated crude racism in his later writings. The Souvenirs d'enfance et de jeunesse (1883) is his beloved memoir of Brittany and his lost faith. He died in Paris in 1892 and was buried at Montmartre; Brittany's ambivalence toward its famous son — celebrated and resented for his apostasy — persisted long after.
