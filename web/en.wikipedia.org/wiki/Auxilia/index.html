The auxilia were the non-citizen auxiliary corps of the Imperial Roman army, recruited from the peoples of the provinces (peregrini) rather than from Roman citizens. By the mid-2nd century AD the auxilia numbered as much as — and possibly more than — the citizen legions, and supplied most of the army's cavalry, archers and light troops.

Organisation

Auxiliary regiments were organised as cohortes of infantry (about 480 or 800 men), alae of cavalry (about 512 or 768), and mixed cohortes equitatae. Many units preserved the names and specialisms of the peoples from which they were first raised — Syrian archers, Batavian swordsmen, Numidian horsemen, Balearic slingers — though they increasingly recruited locally where they were stationed, mainly along the frontiers (limes).

Service and citizenship

Auxiliaries served for 25 years on somewhat lower pay than legionaries. On honourable discharge they received a bronze diploma granting Roman citizenship to themselves and (until about AD 140) their existing children, together with legalised marriage. This reward made auxilia a powerful engine of Romanisation in the frontier provinces. After Caracalla's edict of 212 granted citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire, the distinction between auxilia and legions gradually lost its original meaning.
