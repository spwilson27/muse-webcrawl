McMahon–Hussein Correspondence

The McMahon–Hussein Correspondence is a series of ten letters exchanged from July 1915 to March 1916, during the First World War, between Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, and Sir Henry McMahon, British High Commissioner in Egypt. In the correspondence, the British government promised to recognise Arab independence in a large region of the Middle East in exchange for Hussein's launching the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire.

Negotiations

Hussein's opening letter, relaying demands drafted by his son Abdullah and by Arab nationalist societies in Damascus, sought British recognition of an independent Arab state covering the Arabian Peninsula and the Levant and Mesopotamia. McMahon's letters, cleared with London, accepted most of the demands in principle but reserved several areas: the districts of Mersina and Alexandretta and portions of Syria west of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo were said not to be "purely Arab" and were excluded; Britain's existing treaties with Arab chiefs and its special position in Baghdad and Basra were also safeguarded.

The Palestine controversy

Whether Palestine was included in the promised Arab area became one of the most contested questions in modern diplomatic history. Arab interpreters held that Palestine lay within the promised territory; successive British governments argued that the exclusion of territory "west of Damascus" was intended to cover Palestine west of the Jordan. The correspondence also sits uneasily beside the secret Sykes–Picot Agreement of 1916, which partitioned the same region between Britain and France, and the Balfour Declaration of 1917 promising a Jewish national home in Palestine.

Outcome

Hussein launched the Arab Revolt in June 1916. After the war the promised unitary Arab kingdom did not materialise: Syria went to France, and Britain took mandates in Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq. The correspondence, published officially only in 1939, remains central to debates about British wartime promises and the origins of the Arab–Israeli conflict.
