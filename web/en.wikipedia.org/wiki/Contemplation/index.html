Contemplation

Contemplation is sustained, attentive consideration of an object of thought — in religious traditions, a form of prayer or meditation in which the mind rests on God, a sacred text, or ultimate reality. The word derives from the Latin contemplatio, translating the Greek theoria. In philosophy it denoted the highest human activity for Plato and Aristotle, and it occupies a central place in Christian spirituality, Eastern Orthodoxy, and (in analogous forms) in Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam and Judaism.

In Greek philosophy

For Aristotle, theoria — contemplation of eternal truths — was the activity of the highest part of the soul and the core of eudaimonia, as argued in Book X of the Nicomachean Ethics. Contemplation contrasts with praxis (action) and poiesis (making): it is pursued for its own sake and assimilates the contemplator to the divine.

In Christianity

Christian contemplation developed from the monastic practice of lectio divina (Guigo II's ladder of reading, meditation, prayer and contemplation). Medieval mystics such as Bernard of Clairvaux, Meister Eckhart, Julian of Norwich, Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross described contemplation as a loving, wordless attention to God — in John of the Cross's terms, an "infused" gift rather than an achievement of technique. The Carmelite tradition (notably The Interior Castle and The Dark Night) and, in the East, hesychasm and the Jesus Prayer, are its classic expressions. In the 20th century, Thomas Merton popularized contemplative prayer for a wide audience, and "centering prayer" adapted the medieval Cloud of Unknowing to modern practice.

In other traditions

Analogous practices include Buddhist dhyāna and vipassanā, Hindu meditation on the Self, Sufi murāqaba (watchfulness) and dhikr, and Jewish contemplative prayer. Modern psychology studies contemplative practices under the rubric of mindfulness, examining their effects on attention and mental health.
