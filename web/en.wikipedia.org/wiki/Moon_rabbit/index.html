The Moon rabbit, or Jade Rabbit, is a mythical figure in East Asian folklore based on pareidolia — the dark markings on the near side of the Moon are interpreted as a rabbit standing over a mortar and pestle. In Chinese tradition the rabbit pounds the elixir of life for the Moon goddess Chang'e; in Japanese and Korean folklore it pounds rice cakes instead. Variants of the tale also appear in Indian Buddhist tradition and in Mesoamerican mythology.

Origins

The image of a hare on the Moon is ancient. In the Indian Buddhist Śaśa Jātaka (Tale of the Hare), a self-sacrificing hare offers its own body as food to a hungry traveller — the god Śakra in disguise — who rewards it by placing its likeness on the Moon. The story travelled with Buddhism into China, where by the Han dynasty the Moon was imagined to host a jade rabbit pounding medicine beneath a cassia tree, alongside the goddess Chang'e and the woodcutter Wu Gang.

East Asian traditions

China

In Chinese folklore the Jade Rabbit (玉兔, Yù Tù) lives on the Moon with Chang'e, compounding the elixir of immortality. The rabbit is an enduring symbol of the Mid-Autumn Festival. China's first lunar rover, launched in 2013 aboard the Chang'e 3 mission, was named Yutu ("Jade Rabbit") after a public vote.

Japan and Korea

In Japan the rabbit on the Moon pounds mochi (rice cakes), a picture suggested by a wordplay between mochizuki ("full moon") and "mochi-pounding". Korean tradition likewise depicts the rabbit making rice cakes (tteok) beneath a cinnamon tree.

Mesoamerican parallels

In Aztec mythology a god — in some versions Quetzalcoatl, in others the god who became the Moon, Tecciztecatl — is said to have had a rabbit thrown at his face, dimming his brightness and leaving the rabbit's outline on the Moon. A rabbit appears with the Moon goddess in Maya art as well.
