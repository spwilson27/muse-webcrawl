Overview

The astrolabe is an ancient hand-held instrument — an analogue model of the celestial sphere flattened onto a disc. A pierced star-map plate, the rete, rotates over a plate engraved with coordinate lines for a given latitude; with it a user could tell the time from the Sun or a star, find sunrise and prayer times, measure heights, and cast horoscopes. The name is Greek for 'star-taker'.

The principles go back to the Hellenistic world of Hipparchus and Ptolemy, who described the underlying stereographic projection; the instrument was brought to perfection by Islamic astronomers and reached medieval Europe, where Chaucer wrote a treatise on it for his young son around 1391. The simpler mariner's astrolabe — a heavy ring for measuring the Sun's noon altitude at sea — served navigation until the sextant replaced it.
