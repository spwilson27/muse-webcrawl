The French Third Republic (French: Troisième République) was the system of government adopted in France from 4 September 1870, when the Second Empire collapsed during the Franco-Prussian War, until 10 July 1940, when France gave full powers to Marshal Pétain after defeat by Nazi Germany. Lasting nearly 70 years, it was the longest-lived regime in France since the Revolution.

Founding and consolidation

Proclaimed after Napoleon III's capture at Sedan, the republic survived the siege of Paris and the Paris Commune of 1871, crushed by Adolphe Thiers' government. The constitutional laws of 1875 created a bicameral parliament with a president elected by both chambers. Monarchist hopes faded after the crisis of 16 May 1877, and republicans led by Léon Gambetta and Jules Ferry consolidated the regime, establishing free, compulsory, secular primary education (1881–82) and expanding the French colonial empire.

Crises and the Belle Époque

The republic weathered the Boulanger affair, the Panama scandals, and above all the Dreyfus affair (1894–1906), which divided France over the wrongful conviction of a Jewish officer and culminated in the 1905 law separating church and state under Émile Combes and Aristide Briand. Despite chronic ministerial instability, the regime endured and presided over the cultural flowering of the Belle Époque.

War and interwar years

The Third Republic mobilised France through the First World War under the union sacrée, winning victory in 1918 under Georges Clemenceau at the cost of 1.4 million dead. The interwar decades brought financial crises, the Popular Front government of Léon Blum (1936–37) with its labour reforms, and deepening political polarisation as Nazi Germany rearmed.

Fall

In May–June 1940 German forces shattered the French army; on 10 July 1940 the National Assembly voted full powers to Pétain, effectively ending the republic and inaugurating Vichy France.
