Astronomical Diaries

A cuneiform astronomical diary tablet from Babylon

The Astronomical Diaries are a large collection of Babylonian cuneiform texts recording regular nightly observations of the Moon and planets, maintained by astronomer-scribes in Babylon from at least the 7th century BC (reign of Nabonassar, from 747 BC) into the 1st century BC.

Contents

Each diary covers six months and notes lunar and planetary positions and phenomena, eclipses, solstices and equinoxes, weather, river (Euphrates) levels, commodity prices, and significant historical events — making them a unique combined astronomical and historical archive.

Significance

The diaries underpin the Babylonian lunar theory used by later Greek astronomers such as Ptolemy, and their dated observations allow modern researchers to measure changes in the Earth's rotation (ΔT) over more than two millennia. The surviving tablets are largely in the British Museum and were edited in the 20th century by Abraham Sachs and Hermann Hunger.
