Parliamentary inquiry

A parliamentary inquiry (also rendered parliamentary enquiry in British English) is an official investigation conducted by a parliament or one of its committees into a matter of public concern, such as government policy, maladministration, scandal, or disaster. Distinct from a parliamentary inquiry in the procedural sense (a motion asking the chair for information), the investigation is a core mechanism of legislative oversight of the executive.

Forms and powers

Inquiries are usually carried out by a select committee or a specially appointed committee of inquiry. Depending on the jurisdiction, such committees may be empowered to summon witnesses, compel the production of documents, take evidence under oath, and hold public hearings. Reports are published and debated, and inquiries typically conclude with findings and recommendations, though they usually cannot impose penalties directly.

Examples by country

  • Germany — The Bundestag's Untersuchungsausschuss (committee of inquiry) is a constitutionally guaranteed minority right: one quarter of members can compel its establishment. Famous inquiries have addressed party donations, the NSA surveillance affair, and the Wirecard scandal.
  • United Kingdom — Parliamentary inquiries are conducted by select committees; major public disasters are more often examined by statutory public inquiries chaired by judges, such as the Leveson Inquiry into press ethics.
  • France — The National Assembly and Senate may set up commissions d'enquête with powers to compel testimony.
  • Netherlands — The parlementaire enquête is the strongest investigative instrument of the States General, with witnesses heard under oath; historically significant examples include the inquiry into the 1992 Bijlmermeer air disaster.
  • United States — Analogous investigations are conducted by congressional committees, sometimes as select committees, e.g., the Senate Watergate Committee.

Purpose and criticism

Parliamentary inquiries serve accountability, fact-finding, and reform: they can expose wrongdoing, restore public confidence, and generate legislative change. Criticisms include politicization — majority parties may shield the government, while opposition parties may use inquiries for partisan theatre — and duplication of, or interference with, judicial proceedings.
