Coat of arms of Italy

The coat of arms of Italy (Italian: stemma della Repubblica Italiana) is the national emblem of the Italian Republic, officially adopted on 5 May 1948. Strictly speaking it is an emblem rather than a heraldic coat of arms, since it was designed without regard for the traditional rules of heraldry. It replaced the arms of the House of Savoy, which had served the Kingdom of Italy until the abolition of the monarchy in 1946.

Design

The emblem consists of three main elements:

  • The Stella d'Italia (Star of Italy), a white five-pointed star outlined in red — the oldest national symbol of Italy, associated with the personification of Italia since ancient times.
  • A steel cogwheel behind the star, symbolising work and industry; it refers to Article 1 of the Italian Constitution, which declares that "Italy is a democratic Republic founded on labour."
  • Two branches bound together by a red ribbon inscribed REPVBBLICA ITALIANA: an oak branch on the right, representing strength and dignity, and an olive branch on the left, representing peace and harmony.

History

After the 1946 referendum abolished the monarchy, the new republic needed an emblem to replace the Savoy arms. A public competition was held in 1946–47, with the requirement that the design exclude party symbols and include the Stella d'Italia. The winning entry was by the Turinese artist Paolo Paschetto, a professor at the Institute of Fine Arts in Rome, chosen from hundreds of submissions. The emblem was approved by the Constituent Assembly on 31 January 1948 and promulgated by President Enrico De Nicola on 5 May 1948.

Use

The emblem appears on Italian passports, government buildings, official documents, and the presidential standard's canton is distinct — the President uses a separate standard based on the flag of the Napoleonic Italian Republic. Unlike many national arms, the emblem is not displayed on Italian euro coins, which instead carry designs such as the Marcus Aurelius equestrian statue and Leonardo's Vitruvian Man.
