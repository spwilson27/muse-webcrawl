Semtex

Semtex is a general-purpose plastic explosive developed in Czechoslovakia in the late 1950s by the chemist Stanislav Brebera at the VCHZ Synthesia company. It takes its name from Semtín, the suburb of Pardubice where the factory was located, and from Explosia, the manufacturer. Semtex consists mainly of the high explosives RDX and PETN bound with plasticizers, giving it a malleable, putty-like consistency, typically orange-red in colour.

Properties

Semtex is valued for being odourless, highly malleable over a wide temperature range, waterproof, and effective in small quantities — a few hundred grams can destroy an aircraft or a vehicle. These same properties made older, untagged Semtex notoriously difficult for security screening to detect.

Use by terrorists and countermeasures

During the Cold War, Czechoslovakia exported large quantities of Semtex to allied states, including Libya and North Vietnam. Libyan supplies reached the Provisional IRA and Palestinian groups, and Semtex was the explosive used in the 1988 bombing of Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland. After the Cold War, international pressure led Czechoslovakia (and its successors) to restrict exports, and modern Semtex is manufactured with detection taggants and volatile marking agents, in line with the 1991 Montreal Convention on the marking of plastic explosives, and is given a limited shelf life.

Legitimate uses

Semtex remains in production for commercial blasting and military demolition applications under strict controls.
