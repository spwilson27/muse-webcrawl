Open Sound Control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Open Sound Control (OSC) is a protocol for networking sound synthesisers, computers, and other multimedia devices for purposes such as musical performance or show control. It was originally developed at the Center for New Music and Audio Technologies (CNMAT) at the University of California, Berkeley, by Matt Wright and Adrian Freed, and first described in 1997. OSC was designed as a modern, network-based successor to MIDI for real-time control of musical and media systems.

Features

OSC messages are transmitted over networks, most commonly using UDP (and sometimes TCP). A message consists of an address pattern — a hierarchical, URL-like string such as /synth/osc1/frequency — followed by a type tag string and typed arguments (integers, floats, strings, blobs, and 64-bit time tags). Receivers can use pattern matching to dispatch incoming messages to methods, and multiple messages can be grouped into bundles that execute atomically at a specified time. Compared with MIDI, OSC offers much higher resolution (floating-point parameters rather than 7-bit values), symbolic naming of parameters, network transparency, and precise time-stamping.

Applications

OSC is widely used in electronic music performance, interactive installations, live coding, robotics, and lighting and show control. Software supporting OSC includes Max/MSP, Pure Data, SuperCollider, Ableton Live (via extensions), TouchOSC controller apps, and many digital audio workstations and media servers. Hardware controllers and synthesisers increasingly include OSC alongside MIDI.

Specification

The OSC 1.0 specification was published in 2002 and the 1.1 revision in 2009. The protocol is transport-independent, defines a standard binary encoding with 4-byte alignment, and leaves application-specific address spaces to implementers.
