Egyptian Islamic Jihad

The group emerged in Egypt in the late 1970s

Egyptian Islamic Jihad (EIJ), formally al-Jihad, is an Egyptian Islamist militant organization that sought to overthrow the Egyptian government and replace it with an Islamic state. It is best known for its role, alongside a faction of al-Gama'a al-Islamiyya, in the 1981 assassination of President Anwar Sadat, and for its later merger in all but name with al-Qaeda.

History

The organization coalesced in the late 1970s around Muhammad abd-al-Salam Faraj, whose pamphlet The Neglected Obligation argued that jihad against the "near enemy" — apostate Muslim rulers — was a religious duty. On 6 October 1981, an EIJ cell led by Lt. Khalid Islambouli assassinated Sadat during a military parade. Faraj and Islambouli were executed, and the group was driven underground and abroad by mass arrests.

Exile and al-Qaeda

Under Ayman al-Zawahiri, exiled members regrouped in Afghanistan and Sudan, where EIJ became the core partner of Osama bin Laden in the 1998 "World Islamic Front" declaration. EIJ figures were implicated in the 1998 U.S. embassy bombings in East Africa and the 1995 attack on the Egyptian embassy in Islamabad. In 2001 the organization formally merged into al-Qaeda, and al-Zawahiri became al-Qaeda's deputy and, after 2011, its leader. Much of the Egyptian domestic remnant renounced violence under the ceasefire initiatives of the early 2000s.
