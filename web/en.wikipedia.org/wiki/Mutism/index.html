Mutism

Mutism is the inability or unwillingness to speak, resulting in the absence or marked reduction of verbal output. It is a symptom rather than a single disorder and can arise from psychological, neurological or developmental causes. Mutism is distinct from aphasia, in which language function itself is impaired by brain injury, although akinetic mutism and some other forms do result from neurological damage.

Types

  • Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder, most often seen in children, in which a person who is capable of speech fails to speak in specific social situations (such as school) while speaking normally in others (such as at home). It is classified in the DSM-5 among the anxiety disorders.
  • Elective mutism was an older term, implying a voluntary refusal to speak; the renaming to "selective" reflects recognition that the silence is not a deliberate choice.
  • Total mutism involves speaking in no situations at all.
  • Akinetic mutism is a rare neurological state, usually from bilateral frontal lobe or related damage, in which a person lies awake and aware but does not move or speak.
  • Traumatic mutism may follow psychological trauma, particularly in children.
  • Mutism also occurs as a feature of catatonia, some psychotic disorders, and severe depression.

Causes

Causes are grouped as psychogenic (anxiety, trauma, conversion), neurological (stroke, tumour, infection or injury affecting frontal lobes, Broca's area or motor pathways), and developmental (speech and language disorders, autism spectrum conditions). Diagnosis involves ruling out hearing impairment and primary speech disorders, and assessing the contexts in which speech does and does not occur.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause. Selective mutism is usually treated with behavioural approaches such as stimulus fading, shaping and graded exposure, sometimes combined with medication for anxiety. Mutism within catatonia may respond to benzodiazepines or electroconvulsive therapy. Speech-language therapy supports patients recovering from neurological causes. Early intervention in childhood selective mutism improves outcomes.
