Master of Business Administration

MBA students during a case-method class.

A Master of Business Administration (MBA; also Master's in Business Administration) is a postgraduate degree focused on business administration. The core courses in an MBA program cover various areas of business administration such as accounting, applied statistics, human resources, business communication, business law, marketing, finance, managerial economics, management, entrepreneurship, operations management, and strategic management in a manner most relevant to management analysis and strategy.

History

The degree originated in the United States in the early 20th century, when the country industrialized and companies sought scientific approaches to management. The first business school, Wharton at the University of Pennsylvania, was founded in 1881; Harvard Business School, founded in 1908, offered the first MBA programme. The degree spread internationally in the postwar period; INSEAD (1957) pioneered the one-year European MBA.

Programme types

  • Full-time MBA — typically two years in North America, one year in much of Europe.
  • Part-time and online MBAs — taken alongside employment.
  • Executive MBA (EMBA) — designed for experienced managers, with modular or weekend classes.
  • Distance learning/online MBA — delivered wholly or predominantly over the internet.

Admissions and curriculum

Admission commonly requires an undergraduate degree, several years of work experience, letters of recommendation, essays, and a standardized test score (GMAT or GRE). Accreditation is provided by bodies such as AACSB, AMBA, and EFMD (EQUIS); programmes holding all three are called "triple crown" accredited. Rankings by publications such as the Financial Times, The Economist, and U.S. News & World Report are influential in the market for the degree.

Criticism

Critics, including management scholars Henry Mintzberg and Jeffrey Pfeffer, have questioned whether MBA programmes teach practical managerial skills, encourage short-term or unethical thinking, or deliver a return on their high tuition costs. Defenders point to salary premiums, career-switching opportunities, and alumni networks as the degree's principal value.
