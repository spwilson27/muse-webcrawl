Psychology of religion

The psychology of religion is the application of psychological methods and interpretive frameworks to the diverse contents of religious traditions, as well as to both religious and irreligious individuals. It studies religious experiences, beliefs, practices, and institutions from a scientific perspective, without presupposing the truth or falsity of religious claims.

History

The field emerged in the late 19th century. William James's The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902) — the Gifford Lectures — remains a foundational text, focusing on individual experience and conversion. G. Stanley Hall founded the American Journal of Religious Psychology in 1904. Sigmund Freud treated religion as illusion and collective neurosis, while Carl Jung took a more sympathetic view, regarding religious symbols as expressions of the collective unconscious.

Major topics

Research topics include religious development across the lifespan, conversion and deconversion, prayer and meditation, mystical experience, the relationship between religiosity and mental and physical health, religious coping with suffering, fundamentalism and authoritarianism, and the cognitive science of religion, which examines how ordinary mental processes — agency detection, theory of mind, memory — make religious ideas natural and transmissible.

Methods

Psychologists of religion use surveys, experiments, interviews, and case studies. Challenges include defining "religion" operationally and avoiding the reduction of faith to psychology alone; the field generally distinguishes explaining religious behavior from explaining it away.
