Contact tracing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In public health, contact tracing is the process of identifying persons ("contacts") who may have been exposed to an infected individual, and of subsequently following up with those contacts to test, treat, isolate, or monitor them, with the aim of interrupting chains of transmission of an infectious disease. It is a core tool in controlling outbreaks of diseases such as tuberculosis, measles, Ebola, and sexually transmitted infections, and is often paired with case investigation and supported isolation or quarantine.

Process

Forward contact tracing begins when a confirmed or probable case is identified: trained case investigators interview the patient to reconstruct their movements and interactions during the infectious period, then notify contacts of their exposure — usually without naming the source patient — and advise testing, symptom monitoring, and isolation if infected. Contacts are typically followed for the duration of the incubation period. Backward (or source) tracing instead seeks where the case was infected, which can identify superspreading events and clusters. The effectiveness of tracing depends on speed, the proportion of contacts reached, the fraction of transmission occurring before symptoms, and public trust and cooperation.

COVID-19 and digital tracing

During the COVID-19 pandemic, contact tracing was deployed at unprecedented scale, with large workforces recruited in many countries; its impact varied with test availability and turnaround time. Digital proximity tracing using Bluetooth signals on smartphones — notably the decentralised Apple–Google Exposure Notification framework and apps built on it — supplemented manual efforts while raising debates about privacy, uptake thresholds, and effectiveness. Contact tracing remains standard practice for diseases such as tuberculosis and HIV, where partner notification services are a long-established variant.
