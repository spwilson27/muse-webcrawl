History of Bolivia

The history of Bolivia spans from the ancient cultures of the Altiplano, through Inca and Spanish rule, to the independence movement, the loss of territory in wars with its neighbours, and the social revolutions of the 20th and 21st centuries. The territory of modern Bolivia was inhabited for millennia before the Spanish conquest, most notably by the Tiwanaku civilization centred near Lake Titicaca.

Pre-Columbian era

The Tiwanaku (or Tiahuanaco) culture, which arose around 400 BCE near the southern shore of Lake Titicaca, built one of the most important civilizations of the Andes, with monumental stone architecture at its capital and a sphere of influence extending across the Altiplano. It declined around 1000 CE. The region was later dominated by Aymara kingdoms, and in the 15th century it was incorporated into the Inca Empire as the province of Collasuyu.

Spanish conquest and colonial period

Spanish conquistadors under Francisco Pizarro and Diego de Almagro conquered the region in the 1530s. Upper Peru, as Bolivia was known, was administered through the Real Audiencia of Charcas, based at Sucre. The discovery of immense silver deposits at Potosí in 1545 made the region one of the richest parts of the Spanish Empire; the Cerro Rico mine was worked with forced Indigenous labour under the mita system, adapted from Inca labour obligations, at terrible human cost.

Independence

The independence movement began with uprisings in Chuquisaca (Sucre) and La Paz in 1809. Royalist forces held Upper Peru for most of the wars of independence, and liberation came from outside, with the armies of Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre. After the Battle of Ayacucho (1824) and the liberation of Upper Peru, the republic of Bolivia was proclaimed on 6 August 1825, named in honour of Bolívar, with Sucre as its first president.

19th century: instability and lost territories

The early republic was marked by caudillo rule and chronic instability. The Peru–Bolivian Confederation of Andrés de Santa Cruz (1836–1839) was dissolved after defeat by Chile. In the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), Bolivia lost its Litoral department — its entire coastline — to Chile, leaving it landlocked, a grievance that remains central to Bolivian politics. In the Chaco War with Paraguay (1932–1935), Bolivia lost most of the disputed Gran Chaco. The Acre territory was ceded to Brazil in 1903.

20th century: tin, revolution, and dictatorship

Silver gave way to tin as the dominant export, controlled by mining magnates such as Simón Patiño. The defeat in the Chaco War discredited the old order and led to the Bolivian National Revolution of 1952, led by the Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario (MNR) of Víctor Paz Estenssoro. The revolution nationalized the mines, enacted universal suffrage, and implemented agrarian reform.

Military coups followed from 1964, including the dictatorship of Hugo Banzer (1971–1978). In 1967, Ernesto "Che" Guevara's guerrilla campaign in Bolivia was defeated and Guevara captured and executed. Democracy was restored in 1982, and in 1985 Paz Estenssoro's government imposed neoliberal shock therapy to end hyperinflation. Water and gas wars in 2000–2005 toppled governments amid mass protests.

21st century

In 2005, Evo Morales of the Movement for Socialism (MAS) became the first Indigenous president of Bolivia. His government nationalized hydrocarbons, wrote a new plurinational constitution (2009), and oversaw economic growth and poverty reduction fuelled by gas revenues. Morales resigned in November 2019 amid disputed elections and protests, in what supporters called a coup and opponents called a popular uprising; after an interim government, MAS returned to power with Luis Arce in 2020.
