Stipulative definition

A stipulative definition is a type of definition in which a new or currently existing term is given a new specific meaning for the purposes of argument or discussion in a given context. When the term already exists in general use, a stipulative definition may modify or narrow its ordinary meaning; when the term is newly coined, the stipulative definition creates its meaning outright.

Contrast with lexical definition

Unlike a lexical (dictionary) definition, which reports how a word is actually used and can therefore be true or false, a stipulative definition cannot be incorrect as a report — because it is not a report at all. The definer simply assigns the meaning. A stipulative definition may nevertheless be criticised as unhelpful, confusing, unnecessarily divergent from established usage, or question-begging if it smuggles a contested claim into the guise of a definition.

Uses

Stipulative definitions are indispensable in mathematics, science, law, and technical writing, where precision matters more than ordinary usage. A mathematician may stipulate, "Let a 'snark' be a connected graph of girth five in which every vertex has degree three," and thereafter use the term with that fixed sense. Statutes routinely stipulate that a word such as "person" includes corporations for the purposes of the act. Philosophers also employ operational and stipulative definitions to clarify the key terms of an argument before disputing about the things those terms denote.

Persuasive definitions

A special and controversial case is the persuasive definition, identified by the philosopher Charles Stevenson: a definition that retains the emotive charge of a word while altering its descriptive content, so as to redirect attitudes without appearing to argue. Calling true courage "what I mean by courage is standing firm for our party's programme" is a familiar rhetorical example. Awareness of persuasive definition is a standard topic in critical thinking courses.

Evaluating stipulative definitions

Although not true or false, stipulative definitions can be assessed: they should not conflict with the work the term must do, should not surreptitiously change mid-argument (the fallacy of equivocation), and should be signalled clearly to the audience — often by phrases such as "for present purposes", "in this paper", or "I shall use the term … to mean …".
