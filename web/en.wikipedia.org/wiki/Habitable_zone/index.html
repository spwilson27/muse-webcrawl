Habitable zone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Habitable zone
Also calledCircumstellar habitable zone (CHZ); "Goldilocks zone"
DefinitionRange of orbits where a planet can maintain liquid water on its surface, given sufficient atmospheric pressure
For the Sun~0.95–1.67 AU (conservative-to-optimistic estimates)
Related conceptsGalactic habitable zone; habitable zone for life in general

In astronomy and astrobiology, the habitable zone (HZ), or more precisely the circumstellar habitable zone (CHZ), is the range of orbits around a star within which a planetary surface can support liquid water given sufficient atmospheric pressure. Liquid water is widely considered essential for life as we know it, so the habitable zone is central to the search for exoplanets that might harbour life. Liquid water may also exist outside the zone — for example in subsurface oceans of icy moons heated by tides — so the zone describes surface habitability, not the only possible abode of life.

History

Ideas resembling the habitable zone appear in the work of Isaac Newton and William Whewell, who wrote of a "temperate zone" around the Sun in 1853. The modern concept developed in the 1950s and 1960s through the work of Hubertus Strughold and Harlow Shapley, and was given quantitative form by Stephen Dole (1964) and Michael Hart (1978–79). James Kasting and colleagues (1993) produced the widely used climate-model-based boundaries that later studies by Kopparapu and others (2013) updated with revised absorption coefficients.

Boundaries

The inner edge is set by the runaway or moist greenhouse effect, in which a planet's oceans evaporate, water vapour becomes a major atmospheric constituent, and hydrogen escapes to space. The outer edge is set by the maximum greenhouse effect of carbon dioxide: beyond it, adding more CO2 cannot keep the surface above freezing, and CO2 begins to condense. For the Solar System, conservative estimates place the zone at about 0.95 to 1.37 AU, while optimistic estimates, based on the past histories of Venus and Mars, extend it from about 0.75 to 1.77 AU.

Stellar dependence

The habitable zone's distance scales with the square root of stellar luminosity. Around cool red dwarfs it lies very close to the star, with consequences including tidal locking and exposure to flares; around hot, massive stars the zone is distant but the stars' short lifetimes may not allow life time to develop. The zone also migrates outward as a star brightens over its main-sequence life — the "continuously habitable zone" is the region that remains habitable for a long period.

Exoplanets in the habitable zone

Since the discovery of Kepler-186f (2014) and Kepler-452b (2015), dozens of planets have been catalogued in their stars' habitable zones, including Proxima Centauri b, several planets of TRAPPIST-1, LHS 1140 b, and TOI 700 d and e. Occurrence-rate studies based on the Kepler mission suggest that a significant percentage of Sun-like and red dwarf stars host Earth-sized habitable-zone planets, though most known examples orbit red dwarfs.

Beyond the classical zone

The discovery of possible subsurface oceans on Europa, Enceladus, Ganymede and other icy bodies shows that liquid water — and perhaps life — can exist far outside the classical zone, maintained by tidal heating and radiogenic heat. Concepts such as the galactic habitable zone extend the idea to galactic scales, weighing supernova rates, metallicity and radiation.
