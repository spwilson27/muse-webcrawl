Grus is a genus of large birds in the crane family. The genus has a worldwide distribution, with species breeding across northern parts of Europe, Asia, and North America. The name derives from the Latin grus, "crane".

Taxonomy

The genus Grus was erected by the French zoologist Mathurin Jacques Brisson in 1760. The taxonomy of the group has been debated: various authorities have placed several species in the genera Antigone, Leucogeranus, and Bugeranus on the basis of molecular phylogenetic studies. Species typically included in Grus include the common crane (G. grus), sandhill crane (G. canadensis), whooping crane (G. americana), black-necked crane (G. nigricollis), red-crowned crane (G. japonensis), white-naped crane (G. vipio), hooded crane (G. monacha), and demoiselle crane (sometimes placed in Anthropoides).

Description and behaviour

Grus cranes are large, long-legged, long-necked birds. They are opportunistic feeders that change their diets according to season and energy requirements, consuming plant material, rodents, fish, and amphibians. They are monogamous breeders, performing elaborate unison calls and dancing displays. Most northern populations are migratory.

Conservation

Several species are threatened: the whooping crane is endangered, having recovered from a low of roughly 21 birds in 1941, and the Siberian crane (often placed in Leucogeranus) is critically endangered. Habitat loss in wetland breeding and wintering areas is the main threat to the genus.
