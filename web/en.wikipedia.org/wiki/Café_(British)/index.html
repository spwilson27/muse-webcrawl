Café (British)

This article is about the traditional British café, sometimes called a “caff” or “greasy spoon”. For the coffeehouse generally, see Coffeehouse.

A café in British usage — colloquially a caff and often grouped with the “greasy spoon” tradition — is a small, inexpensive eating establishment serving cooked breakfasts, hot drinks and simple meals. Unlike the continental European café, which centres on coffee and pastries, the archetypal British café is known above all for the “full English breakfast”: fried eggs, bacon, sausages, baked beans, grilled tomatoes, mushrooms, fried bread or toast, and black pudding, typically accompanied by mugs of strong tea or instant coffee.

Character

British cafés are traditionally independent, family-run businesses, often with Formica-topped tables, a counter at which customers order and pay, laminated or hand-written menus, and condiments such as brown sauce and ketchup on every table. Many are located near markets, industrial estates, taxi ranks and transport interchanges, and historically catered to working-class customers, lorry drivers, market traders and manual workers. Cafés on major roads serving long-distance drivers are often referred to as transport cafés.

The heyday of the British café is often placed in the post-war decades, when Italian, Greek Cypriot and other immigrant families ran many such establishments, particularly in London. Though their numbers have declined under pressure from coffee-shop chains and changing eating habits, the traditional café remains a recognised institution of British food culture, celebrated for its affordability, generous portions and lack of pretension.

Typical menu

Alongside breakfasts served throughout the day, typical offerings include sandwiches, jacket potatoes, pies, omelettes, fish and chips, tea and coffee, and set meals with names advertising their contents. Customers may order individual items or a numbered “set” breakfast.
