Death certificate

A standard death certificate form, recording the fact, time and cause of a death.

A death certificate is an official document issued by a government authority declaring the date, location and cause of a person's death. It is normally completed by a medical practitioner, coroner or medical examiner who certifies the cause of death, and it is registered with a civil registry office as part of a jurisdiction's vital statistics system.

Purpose

The death certificate serves several functions. Legally, it provides official proof of death, which is required to settle the deceased's estate, claim life insurance and survivor benefits, arrange burial or cremation, and close accounts. From a public-health perspective, the causes of death recorded on certificates are aggregated to produce mortality statistics, which guide medical research, health policy and resource allocation. Internationally, causes are coded using the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases (ICD).

Contents

Although the format varies between countries, a death certificate typically records the deceased's full name, date and place of birth, date, time and place of death, occupation and residence, the immediate cause of death, contributing conditions, and the manner of death (natural, accident, suicide, homicide or undetermined). Certifiers are instructed to record the sequence of conditions leading to death and to avoid listing mechanisms of death, such as cardiac arrest, as the underlying cause.

Certification and registration

In most jurisdictions a physician who attended the deceased may certify a death from natural causes, while sudden, unexplained, violent or suspicious deaths must be referred to a coroner or medical examiner, who may order an autopsy. In England and Wales deaths must be registered within five days; the system was reformed following the Shipman Inquiry, and from 2024 all deaths not investigated by a coroner are reviewed by an independent medical examiner. Certified copies issued by registrars are the documents most commonly requested by families, solicitors and insurers.
