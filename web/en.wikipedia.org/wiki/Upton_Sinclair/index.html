Upton Sinclair, author of The Jungle

Upton Beall Sinclair Jr. (September 20, 1878 – November 25, 1968) was an American author, journalist, and political activist who wrote nearly 100 books across multiple genres. A leading figure of the muckraking movement, he achieved fame with his 1906 novel The Jungle, which exposed labour abuses and unsanitary conditions in the American meatpacking industry and helped spur the passage of the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act that same year.

Life and work

Sinclair was born in Baltimore and raised in circumstances that alternated between poverty with his parents and comfort with his wealthy grandparents — an experience he credited with awakening his socialist convictions. He began writing dime novels as a teenager, graduated from the City College of New York, and attended Columbia University. After several unsuccessful novels, The Jungle (1906), based on seven weeks spent undercover in Chicago's stockyards, made him internationally famous. Sinclair famously remarked: "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach."

A lifelong socialist, he founded the Intercollegiate Socialist Society (later the League for Industrial Democracy), ran unsuccessfully for office several times, and wrote exposés of journalism (The Brass Check, 1919), education, and religion. In 1934 he ran for Governor of California as a Democrat on the "End Poverty in California" (EPIC) platform, winning the Democratic primary but losing the general election amid a pioneering smear campaign by Hollywood studios and newspapers. His novel Dragon's Teeth (1942), part of the eleven-volume Lanny Budd series chronicling world events between the wars, won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction. Oil! (1927) was loosely adapted into the 2007 film There Will Be Blood.

Legacy

Sinclair is remembered as one of the most influential American writers of social protest. The Jungle remains a staple of school curricula, and his career — combining literature, investigative reporting, and electoral politics — exemplified the muckraking era's faith that exposure could drive reform.
