Treatment-resistant depression

Specialty: Psychiatry

Definition: Major depressive disorder failing to respond adequately to at least two antidepressant trials

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) is a term used in clinical psychiatry to describe a form of major depressive disorder (MDD) which does not respond adequately to a course of appropriate antidepressant medication. The most common definition requires failure of at least two antidepressants of adequate dose and duration, though definitions in the literature and in regulatory practice vary. It has been estimated that between 20 and 30 per cent of people with MDD meet criteria for TRD.

Risk factors and assessment

Factors associated with treatment resistance include longer duration of illness, greater severity, comorbid anxiety and substance use disorders, personality disorders, chronic medical illness and early age of onset. Before concluding that depression is treatment-resistant, clinicians should confirm the diagnosis, check adherence, and exclude causes of "pseudo-resistance" such as inadequate dosing, misdiagnosed bipolar disorder, hypothyroidism, or ongoing psychosocial stressors.

Treatment

Strategies include switching antidepressant class, combining antidepressants, and augmenting with atypical antipsychotics (such as aripiprazole or quetiapine), lithium or thyroid hormone. Other options include psychotherapy, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) — still the most effective acute treatment for severe resistant depression — repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), and, since 2019, intranasal esketamine approved specifically for TRD. The landmark STAR*D trial showed that cumulative remission rates decline with each successive treatment step, with about a third of patients failing to remit after multiple steps.
