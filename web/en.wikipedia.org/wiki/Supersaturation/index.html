Supersaturation

Adding a seed crystal to a supersaturated sodium acetate solution triggers rapid crystallisation.

In physical chemistry, supersaturation occurs when a solution contains more of a dissolved material than could be dissolved by the solvent under equilibrium conditions. The term is also applied to vapour mixtures: air is supersaturated with respect to water when its relative humidity exceeds 100%, a state that in the atmosphere normally requires condensation nuclei for droplets to form. Supersaturated states are metastable — they persist until a disturbance, a seed crystal, or a nucleation site triggers crystallisation or condensation.

Preparation and behaviour

A supersaturated solution is commonly prepared by dissolving a solute in hot solvent and cooling the solution slowly and undisturbed. Because solubility usually falls with temperature, the cooled solution holds more solute than equilibrium allows. Introducing a tiny seed crystal, scratching the vessel wall, or agitating the liquid provides nucleation sites, and excess solute crystallises out, often rapidly and with release of the latent heat of crystallisation — the principle behind reusable sodium acetate hand warmers.

Applications and occurrences

  • Crystal growing and purification by recrystallisation
  • Cloud formation: water vapour in rising air becomes supersaturated before condensing on aerosol particles; cloud chambers exploit supersaturated alcohol vapour to reveal particle tracks
  • Carbonated drinks, which are supersaturated solutions of carbon dioxide released on opening
  • Recompression sickness ("the bends"), in which body tissues become supersaturated with nitrogen during ascent from a dive
  • Precipitation of scale and kidney stones in supersaturated bodily fluids
