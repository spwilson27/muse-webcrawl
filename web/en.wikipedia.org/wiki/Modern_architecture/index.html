Modern architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Bauhaus building in Dessau (1926): white cubic volumes, a glazed curtain-wall workshop wing and horizontal balcony slabs

Modern architecture, also called modernist architecture, was the dominant architectural movement of much of the 20th century, based on new construction technologies — steel, reinforced concrete and plate glass — and on the functionalist principle that form should follow function, with ornament rejected as unnecessary.

Its roots lie in the work of figures such as Louis Sullivan and Frank Lloyd Wright in America and the Bauhaus school (founded 1919 by Walter Gropius) in Germany. Leading modernists include Le Corbusier, whose 'five points' and Villa Savoye defined the International Style, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe ('less is more'), and later the exponents of Brutalism. From the 1970s, postmodern architecture reacted against modernist austerity.
