Acclimatization

Mountaineers acclimatise by ascending gradually and resting at intermediate camps.

Acclimatization (also spelled acclimatisation) is the process by which an individual organism adjusts to a change in its environment — such as a change in altitude, temperature, humidity or photoperiod — allowing it to maintain performance across a range of environmental conditions. The adjustments are reversible and occur within the lifetime of the organism, distinguishing acclimatization from evolutionary adaptation.

High altitude

At high altitude, lower atmospheric pressure reduces the oxygen available in each breath. Over days to weeks the body compensates by increasing breathing rate, producing more red blood cells under the influence of erythropoietin, and increasing haemoglobin concentration. Failure to acclimatise can lead to altitude sickness, including acute mountain sickness and the potentially fatal high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral oedemas.

Heat and cold

Heat acclimatization develops over roughly one to two weeks of repeated heat exposure: sweating begins earlier and becomes more copious, plasma volume expands, and heart rate for a given workload falls. Cold acclimatization is generally less pronounced in humans, involving changes in insulation, peripheral blood flow and metabolic response.

Plants and animals

Many species acclimatise seasonally — for example, fish adjusting enzyme systems to water temperature, or mammals growing thicker winter coats. In laboratory practice, animals are acclimatised to new housing before experiments to reduce stress-related variation.
