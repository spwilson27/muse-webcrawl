Fall of the Western Roman Empire

The fall of the Western Roman Empire (also called the fall of Rome) was the loss of central political control in the Roman Empire's western provinces — a process in which the Empire failed to enforce its rule, and its vast territory was divided into several successor polities. By 476 AD, when the Germanic king Odoacer deposed the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, no Roman emperor exercised effective rule in the West, though the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire endured for nearly another thousand years.

Course of decline

Historians point to the crisis of the third century, chronic political instability, civil wars, splitting of the Empire after Diocletian and Theodosius I (395), economic troubles, over-reliance on Germanic foederati, and the pressure of migrations and invasions: the crossing of the Rhine in 406, the Visigothic sack of Rome in 410 under Alaric, the Vandal sack in 455, and the loss of North Africa's tax base to the Vandals. Effective power passed to barbarian generals such as Stilicho, Aëtius and Ricimer behind a succession of weak emperors.

Interpretations

Edward Gibbon's Decline and Fall (1776–1789) blamed, among other causes, the loss of civic virtue and the rise of Christianity. Modern scholarship divides between "catastrophe" views (e.g. Bryan Ward-Perkins, stressing abrupt material collapse), transformationist views (the "Late Antiquity" school associated with Peter Brown, stressing gradual cultural change), and debates over climate, disease and fiscal-military sustainability. Hundreds of specific causes have been proposed; many scholars reject any single explanation and describe a convergence of stresses on a system whose western half, unlike the wealthier East, could not adapt.

Legacy

The successor kingdoms — Ostrogothic Italy, Visigothic Hispania, Frankish Gaul, Vandal Africa — preserved much Roman law, administration and culture, while Roman institutions such as the Senate lingered in Italy into the 6th–7th centuries. The event became a foundational metaphor for civilisational decline.
