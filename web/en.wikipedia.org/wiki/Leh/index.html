Leh (/leɪ/) is a city in Indian-administered Ladakh in the disputed Kashmir region. It is the largest city and the joint capital of Ladakh, and the administrative headquarters of Leh district. Situated in the upper Indus valley at an altitude of about 3,500 metres, Leh was for centuries a key entrepôt on the trade routes between Tibet, Kashmir, Central Asia and the plains of India, and the capital of the Himalayan Kingdom of Ladakh until its annexation by Dogra forces in 1842.

History

The town grew around the trade in pashmina wool, salt, grain, silk and charas carried by caravans over passes such as the Khardung La and through the Karakoram Pass to Yarkand and Kashgar. The nine-storey Leh Palace, modelled in part on the Potala Palace in Lhasa, was built by King Sengge Namgyal in the 17th century, below the earlier Namgyal Tsemo fort and gompa. In 1834 Zorawar Singh's Dogra army conquered Ladakh, and in 1842 a treaty confirmed its incorporation into the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir. Moravian missionaries arrived in the 1880s, establishing a church, school and hospital. The historic caravan trade ended with the closure of the borders after 1949 and the Sino-Indian War of 1962.

Geography and climate

Leh lies north of the Indus River on a gently sloping desert plain beneath barren mountains. It has a cold desert climate (Köppen BWk): winters are long and severe, with temperatures well below freezing and occasional snowfall, while summers are short, sunny and warm by day. Precipitation is minimal (about 100 mm annually), and the town depends on glacial meltwater. Visitors arriving by air at Kushok Bakula Rimpochee Airport — one of the world's highest commercial airports — are advised to acclimatise against altitude sickness. Road access is by the Srinagar–Leh highway (NH 1) and the Leh–Manali Highway, both open only in the summer months.

Sights and culture

Principal landmarks include Leh Palace, the Shanti Stupa (built by Japanese Buddhists, inaugurated 1991), the Jama Masjid in the main bazaar, the Namgyal Tsemo gompa, Sankar Monastery and the Central Asian Museum. Leh is the base for visiting the great monasteries of the Indus valley — Thiksey, Hemis, Spituk, Shey and Alchi — and for treks and journeys to the Nubra Valley and the high lakes Pangong Tso and Tso Moriri. The annual Ladakh Festival and the Hemis festival draw large numbers of visitors; tourism, the Indian Army's large presence, and government administration dominate the modern economy.
