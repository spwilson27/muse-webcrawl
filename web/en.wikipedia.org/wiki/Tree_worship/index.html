Tree worship

Tree worship refers to the tendency of many societies throughout history to worship or otherwise hold in high esteem trees or other plants — found often in manifestations of animism, and famously as part of Celtic and Germanic paganism. Trees have been regarded as sacred because of their size, longevity, usefulness, and their position linking earth and sky; particular species and individual trees, as well as sacred groves, have served as shrines, meeting places and abodes of spirits or gods in traditions worldwide.

In world traditions

In Norse mythology the world tree Yggdrasil joined the realms of gods and humans, and Germanic peoples worshipped in sacred groves and at holy trees such as Donar's Oak and the Saxon Irminsul. The Druids of Celtic Gaul and Britain are associated with oak groves. In the ancient Near East, the goddess Asherah was represented by a sacred pole or tree, and the Hebrew Bible repeatedly mentions worship at "high places" and under green trees. In India, the Bodhi tree under which the Buddha attained enlightenment is venerated at Bodh Gaya, and the banyan, peepal and tulsi are sacred in Hinduism. In Japan, sacred trees (shinboku) at Shinto shrines are marked by rice-straw ropes (shimenawa). The "tree of life" motif recurs in Mesoamerican, Mesopotamian and many other mythologies.

Sacred trees today

Veneration continues in folk practices — wish trees hung with rags or coins (clootie wells in Scotland and Ireland), the planting of memorial and liberty trees, Christmas trees as a domesticated survival of evergreen symbolism, and environmental movements that frame ancient trees as deserving protection in their own right.
