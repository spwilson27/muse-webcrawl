Mathematical billiards

Mathematical billiards is a branch of dynamical systems theory that studies the motion of a point particle moving in a straight line inside a bounded planar region (the "billiard table") and reflecting elastically from the boundary, so that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. The motion is free between collisions, and the resulting system is a simple model exhibiting a wide range of behaviour, from complete integrability to full chaos.

Types of tables

Behaviour depends strongly on the shape of the boundary:

  • Rectangular and elliptical tables are integrable: the motion decomposes into simple regular patterns, and in an ellipse a trajectory always stays tangent to a confocal ellipse or hyperbola.
  • Dispersing billiards, such as the Sinai billiard (a square with a circular obstacle), are strongly chaotic and ergodic.
  • The stadium billiard (a rectangle capped by semicircles), introduced by Leonid Bunimovich, is also chaotic despite having no concave obstacles; its defocusing mechanism produces ergodicity.
  • Polygonal billiards have zero entropy but rich structure; rational polygons unfold into flows on translation surfaces.

Significance

Billiards serve as models in statistical mechanics (a gas of hard balls reduces, in a limiting sense, to a Sinai billiard), optics, and quantum chaos, where the spectra of billiard-shaped resonators are studied. Open problems remain, such as whether every triangular billiard has a periodic orbit.
