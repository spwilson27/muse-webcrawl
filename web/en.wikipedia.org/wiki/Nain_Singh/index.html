Nain Singh Rawat (21 October 1830 – 1 February 1882) was one of the first Indian explorers employed by the British to explore the Himalayas and Central Asia. He came from the village of Milam in Uttarakhand, in the Johar Valley near the Tibet frontier. His exploration work earned him fame as one of the so-called "pundits" of the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India.

Explorations

In the 1860s, Tibet and the cities of Central Asia were closed to Europeans. Captain Thomas George Montgomerie of the Survey of India trained native agents — the pundits — to travel disguised as pilgrims or traders, carrying surveying instruments concealed in prayer wheels and staffs, and measuring distance by precisely spaced paces counted on a modified rosary of 100 beads.

Nain Singh made several journeys. On his first great expedition (1865–66), he entered Tibet from Nepal, reached Lhasa — forbidden to Europeans — in January 1866, fixed its position astronomically, and returned via Lake Manasarovar and the upper Indus valley. He also visited the gold fields of Thok Jalung and explored the trade routes of western Tibet. On his last expedition (1873–75), organized under the patronage of Commissioner Henry Ramsay, he travelled from Leh to Lhasa by a northerly route and returned through Assam, mapping vast areas never before surveyed.

Accuracy and legacy

His route surveys covered some 2,000 miles of unknown country, and his determinations of altitude and position proved remarkably accurate when later checked. In 1877 he received the Royal Geographical Society's Founder's Medal (Victoria Medal), and the Society of Geographers of Paris also honoured him. A mountain in Tibet and a lake were named after him (Pundit Nain Singh Range and Nain Singh's "Mipham" lake associations in expedition reports); in 2004 the Government of India issued a commemorative postage stamp on the 150th anniversary of the Survey's pundit programme, and he is commemorated in his home region of Uttarakhand.
