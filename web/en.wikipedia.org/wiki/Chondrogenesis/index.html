Chondrogenesis

Chondrogenesis is the biological process by which cartilage is formed from condensed mesenchyme tissue, which differentiates into chondroblasts and begins secreting the molecules that make up the extracellular matrix of cartilage, including type II collagen and aggrecan. It is the earliest stage in the development of most of the skeleton, because the cartilage models formed by chondrogenesis are later replaced by bone in endochondral ossification.

Stages

Chondrogenesis begins with the aggregation (condensation) of mesenchymal stem cells at sites of future cartilage. Cell–cell signalling through molecules such as fibronectin and N-CAM increases, and the transcription factor SOX9 — together with SOX5 and SOX6 — activates cartilage-specific genes. The differentiating chondroblasts secrete matrix and become embedded in it as chondrocytes. In developing long bones the chondrocytes of the growth plate proliferate, mature and hypertrophy in organised zones before the matrix calcifies and is invaded by blood vessels and osteoblasts.

Molecular regulation

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) promote early chondrogenesis, while Wnt signalling tends to inhibit it. The Indian hedgehog (IHH) – parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) feedback loop regulates the pace of chondrocyte hypertrophy in the growth plate. Mutations in SOX9 cause campomelic dysplasia, and defects in cartilage matrix genes underlie many skeletal dysplasias.

Clinical significance

Understanding chondrogenesis is central to regenerative medicine: mesenchymal stem cells can be induced to undergo chondrogenesis in vitro (for example in pellet culture with TGF-β) to generate cartilage for repairing joint defects, though producing durable hyaline cartilage remains challenging.
