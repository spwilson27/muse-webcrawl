Endogamy

Endogamy is the cultural practice of marrying within a specific social group, religious denomination, caste, or ethnic group, rejecting unions with those outside the group as unsuitable. It is the opposite of exogamy, marriage outside one's group. Many cultures and religions impose endogamous rules, enforced by custom, law, or social pressure.

Forms

Endogamy may be religious (for example, rules favouring marriage within the same faith), ethnic, or caste-based: in the Indian caste system, marriage traditionally occurs within one's jati. Royal and aristocratic dynasties practised endogamy to preserve wealth and power, sometimes including close-kin unions. Small or isolated communities — island populations, religious minorities such as the Amish, Parsis, and Samaritans — often show high rates of endogamy out of demographic necessity as much as rule.

Social functions

Anthropologists note that endogamy strengthens group solidarity, keeps property within the group, preserves cultural and religious identity, and maintains social stratification. Sociologist Pierre Bourdieu described endogamous marriage strategies as a means of reproducing social capital.

Genetic consequences

Long-term endogamy in small populations reduces genetic diversity and increases the frequency of recessive genetic disorders — a phenomenon studied in population genetics under the founder effect. Well-documented examples include elevated rates of certain disorders among Ashkenazi Jews, the Finnish disease heritage, and hereditary conditions in some royal lines, such as the Habsburgs.
