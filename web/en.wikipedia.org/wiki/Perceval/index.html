Perceval (also spelled Percival, and in German Parsifal) is a figure in Arthurian legend, best known as the original hero of the quest for the Holy Grail before his role was partly taken over by Galahad. He first appears in Perceval, the Story of the Grail (Perceval ou le Conte du Graal), the unfinished final romance of Chrétien de Troyes, written in the late 12th century. His Welsh counterpart is Peredur, hero of Peredur son of Efrawg, one of the tales associated with the Mabinogion.

In Chrétien de Troyes

Chrétien's Perceval is a naive Welsh youth raised in the forest by his widowed mother, deliberately kept ignorant of knighthood because his father and brothers died as knights. On first seeing knights he mistakes them for angels, then leaves home — his mother dies of grief — and makes his way to King Arthur's court, where a maiden prophesies his greatness. After early blunders caused by taking his mother's advice too literally, he is trained by the nobleman Gornemant of Gohort and becomes a formidable knight.

At the castle of the Fisher King, Perceval witnesses a mysterious procession including a bleeding lance and a graal (a serving dish, later identified with the Holy Grail) but fails to ask about their meaning, having been taught not to talk too much. He later learns that asking the question would have healed the maimed king and restored his lands. The unfinished romance thereafter divides its attention between Perceval's quest to return to the Grail castle and the adventures of Gawain.

Later tradition

Numerous continuations of Chrétien's poem were written, and the Grail quest was retold in the Vulgate Cycle, in Robert de Boron's works, and in Wolfram von Eschenbach's Middle High German epic Parzival, where the hero (Parzival) eventually becomes Grail King. In the later prose traditions and in Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Percival — son of King Pellinore — remains one of the three knights (with Galahad and Bors) who achieve the Grail, though Galahad takes the leading role. Percival dies a year after the quest, having become a hermit.

In modern culture

Richard Wagner's opera Parsifal (1882) is based principally on Wolfram's version and presents Parsifal as the "pure fool" who redeems the knights of the Grail. Perceval and his variants appear in numerous later novels, films, and works inspired by the Arthurian legends.
