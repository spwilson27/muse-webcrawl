Jennifer Anne Doudna (born February 19, 1964) is an American biochemist who has made pioneering contributions to the study of RNA and genome editing. Together with Emmanuelle Charpentier, she shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of the CRISPR-Cas9 method for genome editing.

Education and early work

Doudna grew up in Hilo, Hawaii, and studied chemistry at Pomona College, graduating in 1985. She earned her Ph.D. in biological chemistry and molecular pharmacology from Harvard Medical School in 1989, working with Jack Szostak on ribozymes—catalytic RNA molecules. Her postdoctoral work with Thomas Cech at the University of Colorado Boulder produced the first three-dimensional crystal structure of a ribozyme, establishing her reputation in RNA structural biology.

CRISPR-Cas9

In 2012, Doudna and Charpentier, together with collaborators including Martin Jinek, published the landmark demonstration that the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 immune system could be repurposed as a programmable tool for cutting DNA at chosen sites, using a single guide RNA. The simplicity and versatility of the system transformed molecular biology, enabling precise genome editing in a wide range of organisms and sparking applications in medicine, agriculture, and basic research, as well as ethical debates over germline editing.

Career

Doudna is a professor of biochemistry and molecular biology at the University of California, Berkeley, and an investigator of the Howard Hughes Medical Institute. She founded the Innovative Genomics Institute and has co-founded several biotechnology companies involved in CRISPR technology, including Editas Medicine, Caribou Biosciences, and Intellia Therapeutics. She has also been a prominent voice calling for responsible governance of genome editing.
