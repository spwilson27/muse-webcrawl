Kuratowski's theorem

Kuratowski's theorem is a mathematical forbidden-graph characterisation of planar graphs, proven by Kazimierz Kuratowski in 1930. It states that a finite graph is planar — that is, it can be drawn in the plane with no edge crossings — if and only if it does not contain a subgraph that is a subdivision of K5 (the complete graph on five vertices) or of K3,3 (the complete bipartite graph on six vertices, three of each type, also known as the utility graph).


K5 and K3,3: any nonplanar graph contains a subdivision of one of them.

Subdivisions and minors

A subdivision of a graph is obtained by inserting vertices of degree two along its edges. Kuratowski's criterion is equivalent to Wagner's theorem, which states the same characterisation using graph minors instead of subdivisions: a graph is planar if and only if it has neither K5 nor K3,3 as a minor. The two conditions differ in general, but for these two graphs they coincide.

Significance

The theorem is a founding result of graph theory and the prototype of a forbidden-subgraph (or excluded-minor) characterisation. The idea that a graph property can be captured by finitely many obstructions was vastly generalised by the Robertson–Seymour graph minor theorem, which shows that every minor-closed family of graphs has a finite set of forbidden minors. Kuratowski's theorem also underlies planarity-testing algorithms, although practical algorithms (such as the linear-time Boyer–Myrvold test) do not search for the forbidden subgraphs directly.
