Format war

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A format war is a competition between mutually incompatible proprietary formats that compete for the same market, such as competing videotape systems or rival standards for electrical plugs. Format wars arise in markets with strong network effects, where the value of a format grows with the number of its users, so that early momentum — content availability, manufacturer support and installed base — can matter more than technical merit.

Notable examples

The classic case is the videotape format war of the late 1970s and 1980s between Sony's Betamax and JVC's VHS, won by VHS despite Betamax's reputation for better picture quality, largely through longer recording times and wider licensing. Other celebrated wars include Blu-ray versus HD DVD in the 2000s, resolved in Blu-ray's favour in 2008; the war of the currents between Edison's direct current and the Tesla–Westinghouse alternating current in the 1880s–90s; competing railway gauges; audiocassette versus 8-track; and numerous battles among video game consoles, web browsers and streaming codecs.

Economics

Economists study format wars under the heading of standards competition and path dependence. Firms may license liberally to build an installed base, secure exclusive content, or bundle formats with complementary products. Losing formats leave consumers with stranded hardware and media libraries, though multi-format devices and adapters can soften the outcome. Industry consortia and standards bodies exist partly to avert such wars by agreeing a single standard in advance.
