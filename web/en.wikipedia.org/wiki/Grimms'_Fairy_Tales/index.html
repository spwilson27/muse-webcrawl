Grimms' Fairy Tales, originally known as the Kinder- und Hausmärchen ("Children's and Household Tales"), is a German collection of fairy tales published by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, the Brothers Grimm, beginning in 1812. One of the most famous collections of folklore in the world, it popularised stories such as "Cinderella", "Snow White", "Hansel and Gretel", "Rapunzel", "Rumpelstiltskin", "Little Red Riding Hood" and "Sleeping Beauty".

Composition

The first edition (1812–1815) contained 156 stories, many collected from oral sources in Hesse — notably the storyteller Dorothea Viehmann and the Hassenpflug family — though a number derived from literary sources and from French tales transmitted through Huguenot families. Through seven editions up to 1857, Wilhelm, the principal editor, revised the tales: softening some violence against children, removing sexual elements, adding Christian moral colouring, and homogenising the style, which made the collection progressively more suitable for the nursery while retaining episodes of striking brutality, especially in punishments meted out to villains.

The scholarly project

The collection formed part of the Grimms' wider programme of German philology and Romantic nationalism: the brothers believed that folk tales preserved an ancient Germanic mythology (Naturpoesie), and they worked in parallel on a German grammar and the great Deutsches Wörterbuch. Their tale numbering ("KHM") and the system of tale-type classification developed partly in response to their work (the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index) remain standard in folktale studies.

Influence

Translated into more than 160 languages, the tales have been continuously adapted for stage, film and animation — the Disney features Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) and Tangled (2010) among them. UNESCO inscribed the annotated first editions in the Memory of the World register in 2005.
