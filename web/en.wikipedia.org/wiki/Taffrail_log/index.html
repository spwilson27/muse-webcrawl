A taffrail log, also called a patent log, is a mechanical instrument for measuring a ship's speed through the water and the distance it has travelled. It consists of a register mounted on the taffrail (the rail at the stern of the ship), connected by a braided log line to a towed rotator—a small brass torpedo-shaped body with spiral fins. As the ship moves, the rotator spins in proportion to the speed, and the line transmits the rotation to gears in the register, whose dials show speed in knots and total distance in nautical miles.

History

The taffrail log superseded the older chip log, in which a knotted line was heaved over the stern and counted against a sandglass to estimate speed. The first successful patent logs appeared in the early 19th century; Edward Massey's log (patented 1802) used a rotator whose revolutions were counted by a separate mechanism hauled aboard periodically. Thomas Ferdinand Walker's firm of Birmingham perfected the type from the 1860s onward: Walker's "Harpoon", "Cherub" and "Trident" logs made the register a permanent fixture of the taffrail, readable at any time. For more than a century the spinning rotator trailing astern was a familiar sight on merchant ships and yachts.

Use and limitations

The navigator read the log at each change of watch to compute the distance run for dead reckoning. Accuracy was affected by seaweed fouling, following seas, and the rotator being attacked by sharks or lost. Taffrail logs measure speed through the water, not over the ground, so tidal streams and currents still had to be estimated separately. From the late 20th century, electronic logs (electromagnetic and ultrasonic), Doppler logs and satellite navigation displaced the mechanical taffrail log, which survives mainly on traditional vessels and small yachts.
