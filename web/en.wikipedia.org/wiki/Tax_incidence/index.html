Tax incidence

[Image: A tax creates a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive]

Tax incidence is the analysis of the effect of a particular tax on the distribution of economic welfare. It measures who ultimately bears the burden of a tax, which need not be the person legally required to pay it. Economists distinguish the statutory incidence — the party that remits the tax to the government — from the economic incidence, determined by how prices adjust in markets.

Elasticity and burden sharing

In the standard supply-and-demand model, a per-unit tax drives a wedge between the price paid by consumers and the price received by producers. The burden falls more heavily on the side of the market that is less elastic: if demand is highly inelastic relative to supply, consumers bear most of the tax through higher prices; if supply is less elastic, producers absorb most of it. In the extreme cases, a perfectly inelastic demand means consumers bear the entire tax, while perfectly elastic demand shifts it entirely to producers.

Applications

Incidence analysis underlies debates over payroll taxes (statutorily split between employers and employees but largely borne by labour, according to most studies), corporate income taxes (shared in contested proportions between shareholders, workers and consumers), and excise duties on goods such as fuel and tobacco. The tax also creates a deadweight loss by reducing the quantity traded below the efficient level, except where it corrects an externality (a Pigouvian tax).
