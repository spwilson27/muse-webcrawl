War on Poverty

The War on Poverty is the unofficial name for legislation first introduced by United States President Lyndon B. Johnson during his State of the Union address on 8 January 1964, in response to a national poverty rate of around 19 percent. The speech led the United States Congress to pass the Economic Opportunity Act, which established the Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO) to direct a range of community-based anti-poverty programs.

Background

Michael Harrington's The Other America (1962) had drawn attention to persistent poverty amid affluence, and President John F. Kennedy had begun planning anti-poverty measures before his assassination. Johnson, who had taught poor Mexican-American children in Texas, made the program the centrepiece of his Great Society domestic agenda.

Programs

The Economic Opportunity Act created Job Corps, VISTA (a domestic Peace Corps), the Community Action Program, Head Start for preschool children, and work-study programs. Related Great Society legislation created Medicare and Medicaid (1965), expanded food stamps, and increased Social Security benefits. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act channelled federal aid to schools serving poor children.

Effects and debate

The official poverty rate fell from 19 percent in 1964 to about 11 percent by 1973. Critics, including later President Ronald Reagan — who quipped that "we waged a war on poverty, and poverty won" — argued the programs created dependency and bureaucracy; the OEO was dismantled under Richard Nixon in the early 1970s, though many programs survived in other departments. Supporters point to long-term studies showing gains in health, education and earnings for participants in programs such as Head Start and Medicaid. The debate over causes and cures of poverty in America continues to be framed by the War on Poverty's legacy.
