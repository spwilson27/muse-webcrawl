Mars rover

A Mars rover is a remote-controlled motor vehicle designed to travel across the surface of Mars after landing. Rovers have several advantages over stationary landers: they explore more territory, can be directed to interesting features, can position themselves to survive winter, and can advance knowledge of remote robotic control. To date, six rovers have successfully operated on Mars, five American and one Chinese.

Earlier Soviet rovers — the Prop-M rovers carried by Mars 2 and Mars 3 in 1971 — reached Mars but never operated, as both landers failed shortly after touchdown.

Design

Mars rovers must survive launch, cruise, entry, descent and landing, then operate in an environment of thin atmosphere, extreme cold, dust and intense radiation. Most use a rocker-bogie suspension that lets wheels climb obstacles larger than a wheel diameter while keeping the body level. Power has come from solar panels (Sojourner, Spirit, Opportunity, Zhurong) or radioisotope thermoelectric generators (Curiosity, Perseverance). Communication is relayed via orbiters or direct to the Deep Space Network; because of the light-time delay of up to about 22 minutes one-way, rovers drive semi-autonomously with onboard hazard avoidance.

Science goals

Rovers carry cameras, spectrometers, drills and chemical laboratories to characterise Martian geology, search for evidence of past water, and assess past habitable conditions. Perseverance additionally caches samples for possible return to Earth and carried the Ingenuity helicopter, the first aircraft to fly on another planet.

Future rovers

Planned missions include the European Space Agency's Rosalind Franklin rover (ExoMars), whose launch has been delayed following the suspension of cooperation with Roscosmos, and sample-retrieval concepts associated with the Mars Sample Return program.
