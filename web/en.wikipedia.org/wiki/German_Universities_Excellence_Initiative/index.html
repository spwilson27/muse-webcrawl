German Universities Excellence Initiative

The Excellence Initiative (German: Exzellenzinitiative) of the German federal and state governments was a competition-based funding programme intended to promote top-level research and to strengthen Germany's position as a location for science and research. Launched in 2005/2006 by agreement between the federal government and the Länder, it marked a significant shift in German higher-education policy, which had traditionally treated universities as broadly equal in status.

Funding lines

The initiative distributed funds along three lines:

  • Graduate schools (Graduiertenschulen) to promote young researchers through structured doctoral training;
  • Clusters of excellence (Exzellenzcluster) to strengthen internationally visible, often interdisciplinary, research priorities at a university;
  • Institutional strategies (Zukunftskonzepte) to strengthen a university as a whole, an award that conferred the informal status of an "elite university" (Eliteuniversität).

Rounds

The first two funding rounds ran from 2006/2007 to 2017, disbursing roughly €4.6 billion in total, with the federal government providing 75 per cent and the host state 25 per cent. Administration was handled by the German Research Foundation (DFG) for graduate schools and clusters, and by the German Council of Science and Humanities (Wissenschaftsrat) for institutional strategies. In the second round announced in 2012, eleven universities held institutional-strategy awards, including LMU Munich, TU Munich, Heidelberg, the Free University of Berlin, Humboldt University of Berlin, and RWTH Aachen.

Excellence Strategy

The initiative was succeeded in 2019 by the permanent Excellence Strategy (Exzellenzstrategie), jointly funded by the federal and state governments at about €533 million per year. It retains two lines — Clusters of Excellence and Universities of Excellence — with periodic re-competition every seven years. Ten universities and one university alliance (in Berlin) held the University of Excellence title in the first period.

Criticism

Critics have argued that the competition concentrates resources on a small number of already strong institutions, widening the gap between "elite" and ordinary universities, and that the short funding cycles encourage strategic behaviour and bureaucracy. Supporters counter that the programme raised the international visibility of German research and stimulated structural reform across the sector.
