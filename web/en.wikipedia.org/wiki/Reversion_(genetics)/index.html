Reversion (genetics)

In genetics, a reversion is a mutation that restores the original sequence and hence the original phenotype of a gene that had previously been altered by a mutation. An organism that has undergone such a mutation is called a revertant. Reversions are classically identified because a mutant strain regains the wild-type trait — for example, bacteria that had lost the ability to synthesize an amino acid regain the ability to grow without it.

Mechanisms

True reversion (back mutation) occurs when a second mutation at the same site precisely reverses the original change, restoring the original DNA sequence and amino acid. Second-site reversion, or suppression, occurs when a compensating mutation elsewhere in the same gene (intragenic suppression) or in another gene (extragenic suppression) restores function — for example, a frameshift mutation offset by another insertion or deletion nearby, or a suppressor tRNA that reads through a nonsense codon.

Significance

Reversion assays, such as the Ames test for mutagenicity, measure the rate at which mutant strains revert to wild type in the presence of test compounds. Reversion is also clinically important: drug-resistant viruses and tumour cells can revert toward sensitivity when drug pressure is removed, and the reversion of attenuating mutations in live vaccines (as occasionally occurs with oral poliovirus vaccine) can restore virulence.
