The economy of Vietnam is a socialist-oriented market economy and one of the fastest-growing economies in Southeast Asia. Since the launch of the Đổi Mới (renovation) reforms in 1986, Vietnam has moved from a centrally planned system toward a mixed economy combining state direction with private enterprise and deep integration into global trade. The country has transformed itself from one of the world's poorest nations into a lower middle-income manufacturing hub, particularly for electronics, garments, and footwear.

History

After reunification in 1976, Vietnam attempted to extend the centrally planned model of the north to the whole country, with disappointing results. Hyperinflation and food shortages in the mid-1980s prompted the Sixth Party Congress in December 1986 to adopt Đổi Mới, which legalised private business, decollectivised agriculture, and opened the country to foreign investment. Normalisation of relations with the United States in 1995, accession to the World Trade Organization in 2007, and a series of free-trade agreements — including the CPTPP (2018) and the EU–Vietnam Free Trade Agreement (2020) — progressively anchored Vietnam in the world economy.

Sectors

Services account for roughly 40 per cent of GDP, industry and construction about 37 per cent, and agriculture around 12 per cent, though agriculture still employs a large share of the workforce. Vietnam is a leading exporter of rice, coffee (of which it is the world's largest robusta producer), cashew nuts, black pepper, and seafood. Manufacturing exports are dominated by mobile phones and electronics — Samsung alone accounts for a substantial share of total exports — as well as textiles, garments, and footwear for brands such as Nike and Adidas.

Trade and investment

The United States, China, the European Union, South Korea, and Japan are Vietnam's principal trading partners. Foreign direct investment has been a key driver, attracted by competitive labour costs, political stability, and membership in regional supply chains; the country's position as an alternative manufacturing base to China has drawn further investment since the late 2010s.

Challenges

Economists cite state-owned enterprise reform, infrastructure bottlenecks, skills shortages, corruption, and vulnerability to climate change in the Mekong Delta among the main challenges, alongside dependence on imported components and a rapidly ageing population later in the century.
