Albinism

Albinism affects skin, hair and eye pigmentation
SpecialtyDermatology, ophthalmology, genetics
SymptomsVery light skin, hair and eyes; vision problems; sun sensitivity
CauseGenetic mutations affecting melanin production
InheritanceUsually autosomal recessive
FrequencyAbout 1 in 17,000–20,000 people (varies by type and region)

Albinism is a group of inherited genetic conditions characterised by the absence or significant reduction of the pigment melanin in the skin, hair and eyes. It results from mutations in genes involved in melanin production or distribution, most often inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. The term derives from the Latin albus, meaning "white".

Types and causes

The most familiar form, oculocutaneous albinism (OCA), affects the eyes, skin and hair and comprises several subtypes (OCA1–OCA7) caused by mutations in different genes, including TYR, which encodes the enzyme tyrosinase essential for melanin synthesis. In ocular albinism, pigmentation loss is largely confined to the eyes. Albinism can also occur as part of syndromes such as Hermansky–Pudlak syndrome and Chédiak–Higashi syndrome, which involve additional medical complications.

Signs and symptoms

Melanin is critical to the normal development of the visual system, so virtually all people with albinism have eye abnormalities: reduced visual acuity, nystagmus, strabismus, photophobia, and misrouting of optic nerve fibres at the chiasm, which impairs binocular vision and depth perception. Lack of skin pigment also confers high sensitivity to sunlight and a greatly increased risk of skin cancer, particularly in sunny climates.

Management

There is no cure; management consists of lifelong sun protection, regular skin surveillance, and optical aids and educational support for low vision. Sunglasses and tinted lenses can reduce photophobia.

Society and culture

Prevalence varies widely around the world — roughly 1 in 17,000 to 20,000 people in many populations, and considerably higher in parts of sub-Saharan Africa. People with albinism in some regions face severe discrimination and even violence driven by superstition, a situation the United Nations has addressed through dedicated human-rights resolutions and the observance of International Albinism Awareness Day on 13 June. The condition also occurs throughout the animal kingdom, where it is often conspicuous in otherwise camouflaged species.
