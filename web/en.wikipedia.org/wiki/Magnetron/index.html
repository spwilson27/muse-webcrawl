Magnetron

The magnetron is a high-powered vacuum tube that generates microwaves using the interaction of a stream of electrons with a magnetic field while moving past a series of cavity resonators. Electrons are produced from a heated cathode and are influenced by a static magnetic field applied perpendicular to the electric field between the cathode and a surrounding anode block. The most influential design, the cavity magnetron, was developed in Britain in 1940 and transformed radar technology during the Second World War. Today magnetrons are best known as the microwave source in microwave ovens.


A cavity magnetron with its anode block and cooling fins.

Operation

In a cavity magnetron, a cylindrical cathode sits at the centre of a circular anode block into which an even number of resonant cavities are machined. A magnet applies a field along the axis. Electrons emitted by the cathode would travel radially to the anode under the electric field alone, but the magnetic field bends their paths into curved trajectories. At a critical combination of voltage and field strength, electrons sweep past the cavity openings in rotating "spokes," transferring energy to the resonant cavities and sustaining oscillations at microwave frequencies. An antenna probe or loop extracts the microwave power from one cavity to a waveguide or coaxial output.

History

Early magnetrons were developed in the 1920s and 1930s by researchers including Albert W. Hull in the United States, but their output power was modest. In February 1940, John Randall and Harry Boot at the University of Birmingham produced the resonant cavity magnetron, which generated around 400 watts at a wavelength of about 10 cm — orders of magnitude more powerful than existing devices. The invention was shared with the United States through the Tizard Mission and became the basis of centimetric radar sets that were decisive in the Battle of the Atlantic and in night bombing operations. After the war the magnetron was adapted to heating, and engineer Percy Spencer's observations at Raytheon led to the microwave oven.

Applications

  • Radar: pulsed magnetrons remain common in marine, weather-avoidance, and surveillance radars.
  • Microwave ovens: a magnetron operating at 2.45 GHz typically delivers 600–1,200 watts of microwave power.
  • Industrial heating: used for drying, curing, and plasma generation.
  • Lighting: magnetrons excite electrodeless sulphur lamps.

Health and safety

Because magnetrons produce intense microwave fields, equipment using them must be properly shielded; leakage standards for ovens are strictly regulated. The cathode of many magnetrons contains thoriated materials or is operated at hazardous voltages, and servicing requires appropriate precautions.
