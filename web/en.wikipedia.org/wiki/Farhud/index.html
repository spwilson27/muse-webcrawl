Farhud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Farhud (Arabic: الفرهود) was a pogrom carried out against the Jewish population of Baghdad, Iraq, on June 1–2, 1941, immediately following the British victory in the Anglo-Iraqi War. The riots took place during the Jewish holiday of Shavuot, in the power vacuum between the collapse of Rashid Ali al-Gaylani's pro-Nazi government and the entry of British and Transjordanian forces into the city. The word Farhud is commonly translated as "violent dispossession."

Background

Iraq's Jewish community, one of the oldest in the world, numbered around 90,000 in Baghdad in 1941 — roughly a quarter of the city's population. During the 1930s, Nazi propaganda spread in Iraq through German envoy Fritz Grobba and nationalist groups, and anti-Jewish incitement grew. Rashid Ali seized power in a coup in April 1941 and aligned Iraq with the Axis, but his government fell after a month of fighting with British forces. Retreating soldiers, demobilized troops, and crowds inflamed by propaganda turned on the Jewish population as the old regime collapsed.

The pogrom

Violence began on June 1, 1941, when a Jewish delegation returning from a meeting with the regent was attacked. Over two days, mobs murdered Jewish residents, raped women, looted and burned homes and businesses, and desecrated synagogues and a Torah school. Estimates of the dead vary widely, from an official Iraqi figure of about 175 Jews (alongside 120 Muslims killed when order was restored) to higher figures of 600–780 given by other sources. Order was restored on June 2 by troops loyal to the regent, often firing on the rioters.

Aftermath

An Iraqi government inquiry blamed Nazi propaganda and supporters of the fallen regime; several perpetrators were executed. The Farhud is widely seen as a turning point for Iraqi Jews, undermining confidence in their future in the country. A decade later, in 1950–1951, the great majority of Iraq's roughly 135,000 Jews left for Israel in Operations Ezra and Nehemiah after the Iraqi government permitted emigration while stripping emigrants of citizenship and freezing their assets. In 2015, the United Nations formally recognized June 1 as International Farhud Day.
