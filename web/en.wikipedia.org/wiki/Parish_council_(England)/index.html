Parish council (England)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A parish council is a civil local authority found in England, which is the lowest tier of local government. They are elected corporate bodies, with variable tax-raising powers, and carry out beneficial services in their parish area. There are about 9,000 parish and town councils in England, covering roughly 16 million people; however, much of urban England, including most of London, is unparished.

Parish councils were created by the Local Government Act 1894, which transferred the secular functions of church parish vestries to new civil bodies. In areas where the council has declared itself a town council, the parish council takes the title "town council" and its chairman is styled "town mayor", though the powers are identical.

Powers and duties

Parish councils have few duties but many discretionary powers. Typical responsibilities include allotments, bus shelters, community centres and village halls, play areas, litter bins, war memorials, cemeteries and closed churchyards, public clocks, and some footway lighting. Councils are statutory consultees on planning applications in their area and may produce neighbourhood plans under the Localism Act 2011, which also introduced a conditional "general power of competence" for eligible councils.

Funding and elections

Parish councils are funded principally by a precept, a charge added to the council tax bills of residents. Councillors are elected every four years; many seats are uncontested, and vacancies are often filled by co-option. Every civil parish has a parish meeting, and small parishes may have a parish meeting instead of a separate council.

Structure

A council consists of a chairman (or chair) and a number of councillors fixed by the principal authority. It employs a clerk, who is its proper officer and usually also its responsible financial officer. Councils must hold an annual meeting in May and at least three other meetings a year, open to the public.
