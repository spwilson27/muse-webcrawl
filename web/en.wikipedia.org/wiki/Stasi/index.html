The Ministry for State Security (German: Ministerium für Staatssicherheit, MfS), commonly known as the Stasi, was the official state security service of the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) from 1950 to 1990. Described by its long-serving chief Erich Mielke as the "shield and sword of the Party" (the ruling Socialist Unity Party), it operated one of the most extensive surveillance systems in history, monitoring the GDR's own population through a vast network of informants as well as running foreign espionage through its Main Directorate for Reconnaissance (HVA).

Surveillance state

By 1989 the Stasi employed roughly 91,000 full-time staff and relied on perhaps 174,000 unofficial collaborators (Inoffizielle Mitarbeiter), giving it one informer for every few dozen citizens. It maintained files on millions of people — the surviving archives run to over 100 kilometres of shelving — and practised Zersetzung ("decomposition"), psychological harassment designed to disrupt dissidents' lives and careers without open arrest. Its foreign arm, the HVA under Markus Wolf, placed high-level spies in West Germany, most famously Günter Guillaume, whose exposure brought down Chancellor Willy Brandt in 1974.

Fall and legacy

As the East German regime collapsed in late 1989, citizens occupied Stasi offices — culminating in the storming of the Berlin headquarters on 15 January 1990 — to prevent the destruction of files. The Stasi Records Act of 1991 opened the archive to citizens, journalists, and researchers through what became the Stasi Records Agency (BStU), a model of transitional justice widely studied elsewhere. The Normannenstraße headquarters is now a museum and research site.
