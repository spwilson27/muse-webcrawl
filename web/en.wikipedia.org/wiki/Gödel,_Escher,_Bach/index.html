Gödel, Escher, Bach

Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid, also known as GEB, is a 1979 book by Douglas Hofstadter. By exploring common themes in the lives and works of logician Kurt Gödel, artist M. C. Escher, and composer Johann Sebastian Bach, the book expounds concepts fundamental to mathematics, symmetry, and intelligence. It won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction in 1980.

Through its dialogues between Achilles and the Tortoise (borrowed from Lewis Carroll and Zeno), Hofstadter explores themes of self-reference, formal systems, recursion, consciousness, and artificial intelligence. Through analogy, it shows how self-reference and formal rules arise in Gödel's incompleteness theorems, Escher's art, and Bach's music.

Content

The book weaves chapters with dialogues. It introduces formal systems, the concept of "strange loops" — hierarchical structures that loop back on themselves — and uses the analogy of an ant colony to discuss mind and emergence. It explains Gödel's incompleteness theorems, which show that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains true statements that cannot be proven within the system.

Hofstadter also discusses the nature of meaning and symbols, using examples from mathematics, music (canon and fugue), and art (Escher's hands drawing themselves, his impossible staircases).

Reception

GEB became a bestseller and a cult classic, particularly among computer scientists and AI researchers, and is credited with popularising the idea of emergence and self-reference in artificial intelligence. A 20th anniversary edition with a new preface by Hofstadter was published in 1999.
