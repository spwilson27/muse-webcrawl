Medina Azahara

Medina Azahara (Arabic: مدينة الزهراء, Madīnat az-Zahrāʾ, "the radiant city") is the ruins of a vast, fortified palace-city built in the middle of the 10th century by Abd ar-Rahman III, the first caliph of Córdoba, and his successors. It is located on the western outskirts of Córdoba in present-day Spain.

Built as the de facto capital of al-Andalus and as a symbol of the caliph's power, the city was effectively abandoned and destroyed during the civil wars that ended the Caliphate of Córdoba in the early 11th century. Its ruins were rediscovered in 1911, and only about 10 percent of the site has been excavated. On 1 July 2018, the site was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Construction began around 936 or 940 under Abd ar-Rahman III, who had proclaimed himself caliph in 929. Popular legend attributes the city's founding to the caliph's favorite, az-Zahrāʾ, though historians generally ascribe it to political and ideological motives: a new palace-city to embody the dignity of the newly declared caliphate and rival the Fatimid and Abbasid capitals.

The city included ceremonial reception halls, mosques, administrative offices, gardens, workshops, barracks, and residences, laid out on three terraces descending the slope of the Sierra Morena foothills, with the caliph's palace on the highest level. The celebrated reception hall of Abd ar-Rahman III (Salón Rico) is noted for its lavish carved decoration.

During the fitna (civil war) of al-Andalus, the city was sacked and destroyed between 1010 and 1013. Over the following centuries its remains were quarried for building stone.

Archaeology and museum

Excavations have proceeded intermittently since 1911. A museum and visitor center designed by Nieto Sobejano architects, opened in 2009, stands near the site. The excavated area includes the Alcázar's upper terrace, the House of Ya'far, the Salón Rico, and the congregational mosque.
