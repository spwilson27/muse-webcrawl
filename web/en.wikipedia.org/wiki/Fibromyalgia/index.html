Fibromyalgia is a medical condition characterised by chronic widespread pain and a heightened pain response to pressure (allodynia and hyperalgesia), accompanied by fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difficulties ("fibro fog"), and often mood symptoms. It is considered a disorder of central pain processing — "central sensitisation" — rather than a disease of the muscles or joints themselves, and no specific laboratory test or imaging finding confirms it.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical. The 1990 American College of Rheumatology criteria required widespread pain plus tenderness at 11 of 18 specified tender points; the 2010/2016 revisions replaced the tender-point examination with the Widespread Pain Index and Symptom Severity Scale and require that symptoms have been present for at least three months and are not better explained by another disorder. Conditions to exclude or recognise as co-morbid include hypothyroidism, inflammatory rheumatic diseases, sleep apnoea, depression, and chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), with which fibromyalgia frequently overlaps.

Causes and mechanism

The cause is unknown. Proposed mechanisms include abnormal amplification of pain signals in the central nervous system, altered levels of neurotransmitters such as substance P and serotonin, dysregulation of the stress (HPA) axis, small-fibre neuropathy in a subset of patients, and genetic predisposition; onset often follows physical trauma, infection, surgery, or psychological stress. Fibromyalgia affects roughly 2–4% of the population, and is diagnosed considerably more often in women than in men, though criteria changes have narrowed the apparent gap.

Treatment

Evidence most strongly supports graded aerobic exercise, good sleep hygiene, and cognitive behavioural therapy. Medications approved for fibromyalgia in the United States are the serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors duloxetine and milnacipran and the anticonvulsant pregabalin; amitriptyline is widely used off-label. Opioids are generally discouraged as ineffective for this type of pain and potentially worsening. Fibromyalgia was historically controversial — the term dates to the 1970s, replacing "fibrositis" — but it is now recognised by bodies including the WHO (ICD-11 chronic primary pain) and the ACR.
