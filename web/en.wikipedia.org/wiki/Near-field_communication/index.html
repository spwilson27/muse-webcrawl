Near-field communication

Frequency: 13.56 MHz (HF band)
Range: typically under 4 cm
Data rates: 106–848 kbit/s
Standards: ISO/IEC 14443, ISO/IEC 18092, FeliCa
Promoter: NFC Forum (founded 2004)

Near-field communication (NFC) is a set of communication protocols that enables two electronic devices, one often a smartphone, to exchange data when brought within about 4 cm (1.6 in) of each other. NFC evolved from radio-frequency identification (RFID) technology and operates at 13.56 MHz; unlike longer-range RFID, its very short range is a security feature, making it suitable for contactless payment and access control.

Technology

NFC devices operate in three modes: reader/writer mode, in which a powered device reads passive tags; card emulation mode, in which the device behaves like a contactless smart card; and peer-to-peer mode for exchanging small data payloads between two active devices. Passive NFC tags draw power from the reader's radio field, as in ISO/IEC 14443 proximity cards (MIFARE) and Sony's FeliCa system, long used for transit fares in Japan. Data rates range from 106 to 848 kbit/s.

Applications

NFC underlies mobile payment systems such as Apple Pay, Google Pay, and Samsung Pay, which emulate payment cards with tokenised credentials; it is also used in transit cards, e-passports, electronic ticketing, access badges, pairing of Bluetooth and Wi-Fi devices by tapping, and smart posters with embedded tags. The NFC Forum, founded in 2004 by Nokia, Philips, and Sony, promotes interoperability and certification.

Security

The short range limits casual eavesdropping but does not eliminate it; relay attacks and tag tampering have been demonstrated. Payment implementations therefore rely on secure elements or host card emulation with tokenisation and transaction cryptograms rather than on the radio link itself.
