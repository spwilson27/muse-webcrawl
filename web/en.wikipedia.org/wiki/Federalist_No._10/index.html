Federalist No. 10

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Federalist No. 10 is an essay written by James Madison as the tenth of The Federalist Papers, a series of eighty-five essays arguing for the ratification of the United States Constitution. It was first published in the Daily Advertiser (New York) on 23 November 1787 under the pseudonym Publius, and is generally regarded as among the most important of the papers and of American political thought.

Madison defines a faction as a number of citizens united by a common impulse of passion or interest adverse to the rights of others or to the community's permanent and aggregate interests. Since the causes of faction are sown in human nature and liberty itself, they cannot be removed without destroying freedom; their effects must be controlled. A pure democracy cannot do so, but a republic — representation over an extended territory — can: a large republic multiplies interests and sects, refines public views through chosen representatives, and makes majority faction harder to concert. The essay's “extended republic” argument inverted the classical (Montesquieu) teaching that republics must be small, and it remains the founding text for pluralist theories of democracy and for constitutional defences of large, diverse polities.
