Solar analog

Solar-type stars, solar analogs, and solar twins are categories of stars classified by their similarity to the Sun, in increasing order of likeness. Studying them helps astronomers understand the Sun's past and future, calibrate stellar models, and identify promising targets in the search for Earth-like planets and extraterrestrial life.

Definitions

  • Solar-type stars are main-sequence stars with masses roughly 0.8–1.04 solar masses and spectral types late F through early K — thousands are known.
  • Solar analogs are a narrower set, closely matching the Sun's temperature (within a few hundred kelvin), metallicity, and luminosity, and not having a close stellar companion. Examples include 18 Scorpii, HD 98618, and stars in the Hyades cluster.
  • Solar twins are nearly indistinguishable from the Sun in temperature (within ~50 K), metallicity, mass, and age. HIP 56948 is frequently cited as one of the closest known solar twins; only a few dozen are confirmed.

Importance

Solar twins allow astronomers to measure the Sun's properties against external reference points — for example, to test whether the Sun is unusually inactive, to study how rotation and magnetic activity evolve with age, and to examine fine chemical differences, such as lithium depletion, that may reflect planet formation. Because analogs are prime SETI and exoplanet targets, surveys such as Kepler and Gaia have greatly expanded their catalogues. Candidates are vetted against criteria on temperature, gravity, metallicity, activity, and binarity.
