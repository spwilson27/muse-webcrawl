Carolingian art
Periodc. 780 – c. 900 AD
RegionFrankish Empire (Western and Central Europe)
Key centresAachen, Tours, Reims, Metz, St Gall
Known forIlluminated manuscripts, ivory carving, metalwork, fresco

Carolingian art is the art of the Frankish Empire under the Carolingian dynasty, roughly from the reign of Charlemagne (c. 780) to the end of the 9th century. Part of the Carolingian Renaissance, it deliberately revived Roman and early Christian models, producing the first broadly "classical" revival in medieval Western art.

Manuscripts

The supreme achievement of Carolingian art is the illuminated manuscript. Court and monastic scriptoria — at Aachen, Tours, Reims, Corbie and St Gall — produced lavish Gospel books such as the Godescalc Evangelistary, the Ebbo Gospels with their agitated, expressive evangelist portraits, and the Utrecht Psalter, whose lively pen drawings profoundly influenced later medieval art. The period also standardised the Caroline minuscule script, a legible book hand that became the ancestor of modern lower-case type.

Other media

Carolingian craftsmen excelled in ivory carving for book covers and liturgical objects, goldsmith work such as the altar of Sant'Ambrogio in Milan, and gem-studded treasure bindings. Monumental painting survives only in fragments, as in the frescoes at Müstair, but architecture — the Palatine Chapel at Aachen above all — expressed the same imperial Roman revival.

Legacy

Carolingian art laid the foundations for Romanesque art and preserved classical texts and images that would otherwise have been lost, transmitting classical iconography to the later Middle Ages.
