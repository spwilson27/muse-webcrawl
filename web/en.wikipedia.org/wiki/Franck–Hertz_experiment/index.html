Franck–Hertz experiment

The Franck–Hertz experiment was the first electrical measurement to clearly show the quantum nature of atoms and provided direct support for the Bohr model of the atom, in which electrons occupy discrete energy states. It was presented on April 24, 1914, by James Franck and Gustav Hertz to the German Physical Society, and both scientists were awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1925 for this work.

Description

In the experiment, electrons are accelerated through a tube containing mercury vapour. As the accelerating voltage increases, the current rises, but it drops sharply each time the voltage passes a multiple of about 4.9 volts. These drops occur because electrons lose their kinetic energy in inelastic collisions that excite mercury atoms to the first excited state, 4.9 electron-volts above the ground state. The experiment thus measured the energy separation between atomic energy levels directly, and the excited mercury atoms were observed to emit ultraviolet light of the corresponding wavelength.

Other gases

Versions of the experiment with other gases, such as neon, produce visibly glowing discharge bands and characteristic excitation potentials, making the Franck–Hertz experiment a standard undergraduate physics laboratory demonstration of quantized atomic energy levels.
