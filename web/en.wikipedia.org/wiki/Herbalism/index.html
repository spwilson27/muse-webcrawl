Herbalism is the study of pharmacognosy in using medicinal plants, which are a basis of traditional medicine. It is sometimes defined more broadly to include fungi and bee products, as well as minerals, shells, and certain animal parts. Scientific evidence for the effectiveness of many herbal treatments is limited, although some plant-derived compounds are the basis of established pharmaceuticals.

Herbalism has a long tradition of use outside conventional medicine. It is becoming more mainstream as improvements in analysis and quality control, along with advances in clinical research, show the value of herbal medicine in treating and preventing disease. The World Health Organization estimates that a large share of the world's population relies primarily on traditional, plant-based medicine for primary health care, and it supports the integration of proven traditional practices into health systems.

History

The use of plants as medicines predates written history: archaeological evidence, including residues in Neanderthal burials and the 60,000-year-old Shanidar site, suggests very early use of medicinal plants. Written traditions include the Chinese Shennong Ben Cao Jing (compiled around the 1st–2nd centuries CE from much older lore), the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE), Indian Ayurvedic texts, and the Greek De materia medica of Dioscorides (1st century CE), which described some 600 plants and remained the standard pharmacopoeia of Europe for more than 1,500 years.

In medieval and early modern Europe, monasteries preserved and extended herbal knowledge, and printed herbals — such as those of Leonhart Fuchs (1542), John Gerard (1597), and Nicholas Culpeper, whose Complete Herbal (1653) made herbal medicine available in English to lay readers — spread it widely. In the 19th century, chemists began isolating pure active compounds from plants: morphine from opium poppy, quinine from cinchona bark, salicin (leading to aspirin) from willow and meadowsweet, and later digoxin from foxglove — marking the birth of modern pharmacology and the gradual eclipse of whole-plant prescribing in Western medicine.

Modern practice

Contemporary herbalism includes Western herbal medicine, traditional Chinese medicine (which prescribes complex multi-herb formulas), Ayurveda, and various indigenous traditions. Herbal preparations take the form of teas (infusions and decoctions), tinctures, capsules of dried plant material, standardized extracts, and topical preparations. Some herbal medicines have good evidence of efficacy for specific conditions — for example, certain extracts of St John's wort for mild to moderate depression (though with significant drug interactions), and senna as a laxative — while many others have not been rigorously tested.

Safety and criticism

Critics note that "natural" does not mean safe: herbal products can be toxic (e.g., comfrey, ephedra, aconite), can interact dangerously with prescription drugs (St John's wort induces drug-metabolising enzymes and can reduce the effectiveness of immunosuppressants, contraceptives, and anticoagulants), and are often poorly standardized in dose and purity; cases of adulteration and heavy-metal contamination are documented. Regulation varies widely, from licensing of practitioners and medicines in some countries to largely unregulated supplement markets in others. Pharmacovigilance and clinical trials of herbal products remain active areas of research, and a substantial fraction of modern prescription drugs are derived from, or inspired by, plant compounds.
