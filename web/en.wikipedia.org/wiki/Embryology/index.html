Embryology

Embryology (from Greek ἔμβρυον, embryon, "the unborn, embryo"; and -λογία, -logia) is the branch of biology that studies the prenatal development of gametes (sex cells), fertilization, and development of embryos and fetuses. Additionally, embryology encompasses the study of congenital disorders that occur before birth, known as teratology.

History

Ideas about embryonic development date to Aristotle, who distinguished between preformation and epigenesis. The debate between preformationists, who held that the organism was already formed in miniature in the egg or sperm, and epigenesists continued into the 18th century. Karl Ernst von Baer, considered a founder of modern embryology, discovered the mammalian ovum in 1827 and formulated von Baer's laws describing general features of development.

Development

After fertilization, the zygote undergoes cleavage into a blastula, followed by gastrulation, which establishes the germ layers, and organogenesis. Modern embryology combines classical descriptive work with genetics, cell biology, and molecular techniques, and forms a core of developmental biology.
