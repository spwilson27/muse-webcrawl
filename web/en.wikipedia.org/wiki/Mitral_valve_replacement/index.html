Mitral valve replacement

Mitral valve replacement is a cardiac surgical procedure in which a patient's diseased mitral valve is replaced with either a mechanical prosthetic valve or a biological (tissue) valve. It is performed when the valve is too damaged to be repaired, most commonly because of severe mitral stenosis or mitral regurgitation.

Indications

Replacement is considered when valve disease causes significant symptoms or cardiac dysfunction and when mitral valve repair is not feasible — for example in heavily calcified rheumatic valves or extensive leaflet destruction from endocarditis.

Types of prosthesis

  • Mechanical valves are durable and typically last a lifetime, but require lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin because of the risk of blood clots.
  • Bioprosthetic valves, made from porcine or bovine tissue, generally do not require long-term anticoagulation but degenerate over time and may need re-replacement after 10–20 years.

Procedure

The operation is traditionally performed through a median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass; minimally invasive and robotic approaches are also used. Transcatheter mitral valve replacement is an emerging catheter-based alternative for selected high-risk patients.
