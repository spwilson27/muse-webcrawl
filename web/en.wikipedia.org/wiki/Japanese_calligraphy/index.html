Japanese calligraphy

Japanese calligraphy (書道, shodō, "the way of writing") is a traditional art of writing the Japanese language with brush and ink, developed from Chinese calligraphy and encompassing kanji, hiragana and katakana scripts.

History

Chinese characters reached Japan by the 5th century CE. Court and temple scribes developed distinctive Japanese styles; the monk Kūkai is traditionally credited with foundational calligraphy, and the kana syllabaries allowed a uniquely Japanese cursive aesthetic in works such as The Tale of Genji manuscripts.

Styles and tools

Principal styles include kaisho (regular, block style), gyōsho (semi-cursive) and sōsho (cursive "grass script"). The calligrapher's "Four Treasures" are the brush (fude), ink stick (sumi), ink stone (suzuri) and paper (washi).

Zen calligraphy

Under Zen Buddhism, calligraphy became a meditative discipline; the ensō circle and vigorous bokuseki inscriptions of Zen masters express enlightenment in a single gesture. Calligraphy remains a required art in Japanese schools and a popular hobby.
