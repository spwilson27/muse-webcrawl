Acacia, commonly known as the wattles, is a genus of shrubs and trees in the legume family Fabaceae. Following a long nomenclatural dispute, the name Acacia was conserved for the predominantly Australian group of roughly 1,000 species formerly placed in subgenus Phyllodineae, while most African and American species were moved to the genera Vachellia and Senegalia.

Description

Australian acacias usually bear phyllodes — flattened leaf stalks that function as leaves — rather than true bipinnate leaves, an adaptation to dry climates. Flowers are small, yellow and arranged in globular heads or cylindrical spikes. Many species are fire-adapted, with hard seeds that germinate after burning.

Distribution and ecology

Acacias dominate much of arid and semi-arid Australia and occur across Africa, Asia and the Americas in the broader sense of the group. As legumes, they fix nitrogen and are ecologically important as pioneer species and food plants for herbivores; many African species are defended by thorns and by mutualistic ants.

Uses

Acacias yield timber, tannin, edible seeds (bush tucker "wattleseed"), and gum arabic (from Senegalia senegal). The golden wattle (Acacia pycnantha) is Australia's floral emblem.
