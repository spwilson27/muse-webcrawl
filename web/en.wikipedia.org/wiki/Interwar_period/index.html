Interwar period

In the history of the 20th century, the interwar period was the period between the end of the First World War (11 November 1918) and the beginning of the Second World War (1 September 1939) — in total, 20 years, 9 months and 21 days. The period was marked by dramatic social, political and economic upheaval and ended with the collapse of the post-war order.

Overview

The peace settlements of 1919–1920 — above all the Treaty of Versailles — redrew the map of Europe, dissolved empires (German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian), and created new states. The League of Nations was founded to preserve the peace, but lacked enforcement powers and the membership of the United States.

The 1920s saw reconstruction, cultural efflorescence (the "Roaring Twenties"), and fragile stabilisation, but also hyperinflation in Germany, political violence, and the rise of radical movements. The Wall Street Crash of 1929 triggered the Great Depression, which devastated economies worldwide, weakened democracies and fuelled extremism.

Rise of totalitarianism and breakdown

Fascist Italy under Mussolini (from 1922) and Nazi Germany under Hitler (from 1933) pursued revisionist and expansionist policies, alongside the consolidation of Stalin's dictatorship in the Soviet Union and militarism in Japan, which invaded Manchuria in 1931. Rearmament, the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the Spanish Civil War (1936–39), the Anschluss and the Munich Agreement (1938) progressively destroyed the Versailles system, culminating in Germany's invasion of Poland in September 1939.
