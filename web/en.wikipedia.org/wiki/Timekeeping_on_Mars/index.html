Though no standardised timekeeping or time zones have been established for the planet Mars, various schemes have been proposed and used by space agencies. Because the Martian solar day, called a sol, is only about 2.75% longer than Earth's day, mission teams operating landers and rovers have often lived and worked on "Mars time," shifting their schedules by roughly 40 minutes each Earth day.

The sol and Martian clock

A sol is 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds. For mission purposes it is divided into 24 "hours" of 60 "minutes" of 60 "seconds," each unit stretched by 2.75% relative to Earth's. NASA's Mars lander teams, beginning with Mars Pathfinder and including the Mars Exploration Rovers and the Phoenix, Curiosity, and Perseverance missions, used custom wristwatches and clocks calibrated to sol units, and named each mission day by sol number starting from landing day.

Time of day and longitude

Mars Coordinated Time (MTC) is the analogue of Universal Time, defined as mean solar time at Mars's prime meridian, which passes through the small crater Airy-0 in Sinus Meridiani. Local mean solar time at a lander's longitude is expressed as an offset from MTC. The Mars Sol Date (MSD) is a running count of sols since an epoch in 1873, analogous to the Julian date, allowing precise conversion between Earth and Mars dates.

Calendar proposals

The Martian year of 668.6 sols divides unevenly into months, prompting numerous calendar proposals. The most widely discussed is the Darian calendar, devised by Thomas Gangale in 1985, which has 24 months of 27 or 28 sols across six-month quarters, with leap-year rules to track the Martian seasons. Martian seasons are more unequal than Earth's because of Mars's greater orbital eccentricity, and northern and southern hemisphere seasons differ markedly in length.
