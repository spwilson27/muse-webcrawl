Replica trick

In condensed matter physics and statistical mechanics, the replica trick is a mathematical technique used to compute averages of the logarithm of the partition function in disordered systems such as spin glasses. Such averages over quenched disorder are difficult because the logarithm must be averaged, rather than the partition function itself.

The identity

The trick rests on the identity

ln Z = limn→0 (Zn − 1) / n.

For integer n, Zn is the partition function of n identical, non-interacting copies ("replicas") of the system sharing the same disorder realisation. One computes the disorder average of Zn for integer n — a tractable annealed average — and then analytically continues the result to n → 0 to obtain the quenched free energy.

Applications

The replica method underlies the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick solution of the mean-field spin glass, developed by Edwards, Anderson, Sherrington and Kirkpatrick in the 1970s. Giorgio Parisi's discovery of replica symmetry breaking — in which the symmetric saddle point is unstable and must be replaced by a hierarchical structure of replica overlaps — gave the correct mean-field theory and anticipated ultrametric organisation of spin-glass states. The method has also been applied to neural networks, combinatorial optimisation, polymers, random matrices, and, more recently, to computations of entanglement entropy in quantum field theory and gravity, where the "replica trick" computes Rényi entropies by replicating the path integral.

Criticism

The analytic continuation from integer n to n → 0 is not mathematically rigorous, and its non-uniqueness long troubled researchers. Nevertheless, replica results have been confirmed in many cases by other methods and by rigorous work, notably the proofs by Michel Talagrand of the Parisi formula for the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model.
