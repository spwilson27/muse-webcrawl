Witold Lutosławski

Witold Roman Lutosławski (25 January 1913 – 7 February 1994) was a Polish composer and conductor. Among the major European composers of the 20th century, he is often considered the greatest Polish composer since Chopin. His compositions include four symphonies, works for orchestra such as Musique funèbre and the Concerto for Orchestra, concertos for cello, piano and other instruments, and a number of chamber works.

Life

Lutosławski was born in Warsaw into a musical family. He studied piano and composition at the Warsaw Conservatory, where his teachers included Jerzy Lefeld and Witold Maliszewski. During the Second World War he played piano duets with his friend Andrzej Panufnik in Warsaw cafés. After the war, his First Symphony was condemned as "formalist" by Poland's Stalinist authorities, but following the thaw of 1956 he was able to develop his mature style freely.

Style and technique

Lutosławski's mature music is characterised by a distinctive use of twelve-note harmonies, dense orchestral textures, and what he called "limited aleatorism" — passages in which performers repeat short phrases at their own speed within a controlled framework, creating shimmering, cloud-like sonorities. His major works include the Venetian Games (1961), the String Quartet (1964), Paroles tissées (1965), the Cello Concerto (1970, written for Mstislav Rostropovich), and the Third (1983) and Fourth (1992) Symphonies.

Recognition

He received numerous international honours, including the Order of the White Eagle, honorary doctorates from many universities, and the Royal Philharmonic Society Gold Medal. He continued composing and conducting his own works around the world until his death in Warsaw in 1994.
