Force majeure

Force majeure (French for "superior force") is a common clause in contracts which essentially frees both parties from liability or obligation when an extraordinary event or circumstance beyond the control of the parties — such as war, strike, riot, crime, epidemic or an act of God (such as flooding or earthquake) — prevents one or both parties from fulfilling their obligations under the contract.

Origin

The concept derives from French civil law (the Napoleonic Code) and is recognised in many civil-law jurisdictions. In common-law systems such as English law, there is no general doctrine of force majeure; instead, parties rely on express contractual clauses, supplemented by the narrower doctrine of frustration of contract.

Scope and requirements

Because force majeure clauses differ widely, their effect depends on drafting: which events qualify, whether the event must make performance impossible (rather than merely more expensive), notice requirements, and the duty to mitigate. Typically the invoking party must show that the event was beyond its reasonable control, could not reasonably have been avoided or overcome, and was the cause of non-performance. International instruments such as the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (Article 79) and the UNIDROIT Principles provide analogous exemptions for impediments beyond a party's control.

Modern relevance

Force majeure gained renewed prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic, when widespread cancellations and supply failures generated extensive litigation over whether epidemics and government restrictions fell within contractual clauses.
