Feminist literature

Feminist literature is literature — fiction, poetry, drama and nonfiction — that supports the aims of feminism: defining, establishing and defending equal political, economic and social rights for women. The term also covers the scholarly project of recovering neglected women writers and reading canonical texts through feminist criticism.

History

Early touchstones include Christine de Pizan's The Book of the City of Ladies (1405), Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), and the 19th-century novels and poems of writers such as Jane Austen, the Brontës, George Eliot and Emily Dickinson, many of whom navigated social constraints on women's writing — Charlotte Brontë and her sisters, like Eliot, first published under male pseudonyms.

First-wave feminist literature accompanied the suffrage movement; second-wave feminism of the 1960s–70s produced landmark works including Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949, an earlier precursor widely adopted by the movement), Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963), Kate Millett's Sexual Politics (1970), and fiction by Doris Lessing, Margaret Atwood, Alice Walker, Toni Morrison and Angela Carter. Virginia Woolf's A Room of One's Own (1929) — with its call for women to have money and a room of their own — became foundational, as did Elaine Showalter's concept of "gynocriticism", the study of women's writing as its own tradition.

Themes and later developments

Feminist literature explores the construction of gender, the body, domestic life, work, sexuality and power, often experimenting with form. Third-wave and intersectional writing — influenced by bell hooks, Audre Lorde and Kimberlé Crenshaw — foregrounded differences of race, class and sexuality; feminist speculative fiction by authors such as Ursula K. Le Guin, Octavia Butler and Joanna Russ imagined alternative gender orders.
