Single malt Scotch whisky

Single malt Scotch whisky is a malt whisky from Scotland produced at a single distillery. Under the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, it must be made exclusively from malted barley (though caramel colouring may be added), distilled in pot stills at one distillery, matured in oak casks in Scotland for at least three years, and bottled in Scotland at no less than 40% ABV. The oldest and most prestigious category of Scotch whisky, single malts are associated with distinctive regional characters shaped by water, peat, still shape, and cask type.

Production

Barley is malted — steeped, germinated, and kiln-dried, sometimes over peat smoke, which imparts smoky phenolic flavours — then milled and mashed with hot water to produce wort. Yeast ferments the wort into a beer-like "wash" of about 8% ABV. The wash is distilled twice (occasionally three times) in copper pot stills; only the "heart" of the second distillation is kept. The new-make spirit is then matured, usually in ex-bourbon or ex-sherry oak casks, losing a fraction each year to evaporation known as the "angels' share".

Regions

Scotland's whisky regions traditionally comprise the Highlands (including the islands), the Lowlands, Speyside — home to more than half of Scotland's distilleries, such as Glenfiddich, The Macallan, and Glenlivet — Islay, famed for heavily peated malts like Laphroaig, Ardbeg, and Lagavulin, and Campbeltown. Flavour generalisations by region are traditional rather than legal, and individual distillery character varies widely.

History

Distilling in Scotland is documented from 1494, when the Exchequer Rolls recorded malt supplied to Friar John Cor to make aqua vitae. For most of its history, malt whisky was consumed locally or blended; single malts as a premium bottled category rose to global prominence only from the 1960s onward, when Glenfiddich pioneered marketing them outside Scotland. Today single malts are exported worldwide and collectible bottles fetch record prices at auction.
