Carpenter Gothic (also called Carpenter's Gothic or Rural Gothic) is a distinctly North American architectural style that applies the forms of the stone-built Gothic Revival to wooden construction. Flourishing from the 1840s to the 1880s, it translated pointed arches, steep gables, pinnacles, and lacy ornament into board-and-batten siding and scroll-sawn "gingerbread" trim, making picturesque Gothic design available to village carpenters and rural congregations.

Characteristics

The style was popularized by the pattern books of Alexander Jackson Davis (Rural Residences, 1837) and Andrew Jackson Downing (Cottage Residences, 1842; The Architecture of Country Houses, 1850). Typical features include:

  • Steeply pitched roofs and cross gables, often with decorative vergeboards (bargeboards).
  • Pointed-arch (lancet) windows, sometimes with simple wooden tracery imitating stone.
  • Vertical board-and-batten siding emphasizing height.
  • Porches, finials, and crockets cut with the newly available steam-powered scroll saw—hence "gingerbread."

Because ecclesiastical Gothic was built in wood, small rural churches are among the most common surviving examples; architect Richard Upjohn, a leading Gothic Revivalist, published designs for wooden churches and himself designed St. James the Less in Philadelphia and promoted modest board-and-batten chapels for frontier parishes.

In culture

Carpenter Gothic houses have become icons of American life—most famously the Dibble House in Eldon, Iowa, which Grant Wood used as the backdrop for his painting American Gothic (1930). The style also colors the architecture of countless small-town churches, railway stations, and farmhouses across the United States and Canada, and survives today in preservation districts and in the "Steamboat Gothic" houses of the Mississippi valley described by Mark Twain.
