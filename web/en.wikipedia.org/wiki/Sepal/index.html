Sepal

A sepal is a part of the flower of angiosperms (flowering plants). Usually green, sepals typically function as protection for the flower in bud, and often as support for the petals when in bloom. Together, all of the sepals of a flower are called the calyx. The term "sepal" was coined by Noël Martin Joseph de Necker in 1790, derived from the Greek skepē, "covering".

Morphology

Sepals are modified leaves and form the outermost whorl of the flower, below the petals. They are usually green and leaf-like, containing chlorophyll, but in some plants they are brightly coloured and resemble petals; when sepals and petals are indistinguishable, they are collectively called tepals. After flowering, most sepals serve no obvious function and often wither or become reflexed, though in some plants they persist and enlarge to protect the developing fruit — as in the cape gooseberry's inflated calyx — or form the leafy crown on top of fruits such as the persimmon and rose hip.

Variation

The number of sepals in a flower, and whether they are free (aposepalous) or fused into a tube (synsepalous), is an important diagnostic character in plant taxonomy. The calyx may be regular, with equal sepals, or irregular. In the aster family, the calyx is highly modified into a pappus of scales, bristles, or hairs that aids seed dispersal by wind.

Function

Beyond protecting the bud from desiccation and herbivores, sepals can photosynthesize, secrete nectar, deter predators with spines or toxins, and assist in pollination when coloured. Their development is controlled by the A-class genes in the ABC model of flower development.
