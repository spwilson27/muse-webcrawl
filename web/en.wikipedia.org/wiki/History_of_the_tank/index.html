History of the tank

The history of the tank begins in the First World War, when Britain developed the first armoured, tracked fighting vehicle to break the stalemate of trench warfare. The name "tank" was a security code word for the secret weapon. Tanks debuted at the Battle of Flers–Courcelette (15 September 1916) and proved their value en masse at Cambrai (1917). Between the wars, theorists such as J. F. C. Fuller, B. H. Liddell Hart, Heinz Guderian and Charles de Gaulle debated armoured doctrine; the Second World War saw tanks become the decisive arm of land warfare. Cold War and modern main battle tanks combined firepower, protection and mobility into a single design philosophy.

A British Mark IV tank, 1917.

Origins and the First World War

The deadlock of the Western Front — barbed wire, machine guns and artillery — demanded a vehicle that could cross trenches under fire. The British Landships Committee (1915), championed by Winston Churchill and designed around William Tritton and Walter Wilson's work, produced "Little Willie" and then the rhomboidal Mark I. France independently developed the Schneider CA1 and Saint-Chamond, and the light Renault FT (1917), whose turret-over-hull layout set the pattern for nearly all later tanks. Germany fielded only about 20 A7Vs but used captured British tanks.

Interwar development

Treaty restrictions pushed German development abroad (the Kama tank school in the USSR). Britain experimented with the Experimental Mechanized Force (1927) and tankettes; the Soviet Union built vast fleets and "deep battle" doctrine; the United States disbanded its Tank Corps in 1920 and starved armour of funds. Tanks fought in the Spanish Civil War (1936–39), where Soviet T-26s and German Panzer Is previewed future conflicts and the vulnerability of thin armour to anti-tank guns.

The Second World War

Germany's panzer divisions, combining tanks, motorised infantry and air support, executed Blitzkrieg conquests of Poland (1939) and France (1940). The Soviet T-34 (1940), with sloped armour, a powerful 76.2 mm (later 85 mm) gun and diesel engine, shocked German forces in 1941 and is often rated the war's best tank. The war's largest tank battle, Kursk (1943), and the Anglo-American Sherman fleets ground down Germany's heavier Panthers and Tigers. Production totals dwarfed all else: some 84,000 T-34s and 49,000 Shermans were built.

Cold War and the main battle tank

Postwar design converged on the main battle tank (MBT): the British Centurion, Soviet T-54/55 and T-62/T-72 series, American Pattons and M1 Abrams, and German Leopard 1 and 2. Composite (Chobham) armour, smoothbore guns, thermal sights and gas-turbine engines transformed capability. Tanks fought in Korea, the Indo-Pakistani wars, the Arab–Israeli wars (notably 1967 and 1973, where anti-tank missiles shook confidence in armour), the Iran–Iraq War and the 1991 Gulf War. In the twenty-first century, cheap drones and top-attack munitions — visible in the Russo-Ukrainian War — have renewed debate about the tank's future, yet no army has dispensed with it.
