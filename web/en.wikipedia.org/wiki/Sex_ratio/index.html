Sex ratio

The sex ratio is the ratio of males to females in a population. In humans it is usually expressed as the number of males per 100 females. Biologists distinguish the primary sex ratio (at conception), the secondary sex ratio (at birth), and the tertiary sex ratio (in the adult or breeding population).

Humans

In human populations the sex ratio at birth is typically around 105 boys per 100 girls, a slight male excess that appears broadly consistent across populations in the absence of intervention. Because male mortality is higher at most ages, the ratio declines through life: in most countries women outnumber men at older ages, and in many developed countries the overall population ratio is slightly female-biased.

Some populations show markedly skewed birth ratios — historically above 110 in parts of China, India, the Caucasus, and Southeast Asia — attributed principally to sex-selective abortion following the spread of prenatal sex determination, combined with son preference; China and India together have been estimated to have tens of millions of "missing women", economist Amartya Sen's famous phrase. Such imbalances are a matter of policy concern for their social consequences, including marriage-market squeezes. Birth ratios in the most affected countries have tended to normalise as policies, urbanisation, and norms change.

In biology

Sex ratios vary widely across species. Ronald Fisher's principle (1930) explains why many species evolve toward an approximately even sex ratio: in a population skewed toward one sex, parents producing the rarer sex gain a reproductive advantage, pushing the ratio back toward equilibrium. Systematic departures are explained by mechanisms such as local mate competition, environmental sex determination (for example, temperature-dependent sex determination in reptiles), meiotic drive, and sex-determining parasites such as Wolbachia bacteria.

Measurement

Demographers compute sex ratios from censuses and birth registrations; the ratio is a basic indicator in population pyramids and is used in planning, epidemiology, and actuarial work.
