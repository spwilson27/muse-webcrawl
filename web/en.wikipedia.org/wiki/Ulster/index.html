Ulster (Irish: Ulaidh [ˈʊləi]; Ulster Scots: Ulstèr) is one of the four traditional provinces of Ireland, situated in the north of the island. It comprises nine counties: six of them — Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Derry/Londonderry, and Tyrone — form Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom; the remaining three — Cavan, Donegal, and Monaghan — are in the Republic of Ireland. "Ulster" is often used as a synonym for Northern Ireland, especially by unionists, though the two are not coterminous.

History

In early Irish history Ulster (Ulaid) was one of the five kingdoms or "fifths" of Ireland, celebrated in the Ulster Cycle of mythology, whose hero is Cú Chulainn. After the Norman invasion the province was progressively brought under English control, and following the Flight of the Earls in 1607 the Plantation of Ulster settled large numbers of English and Scottish Protestants on confiscated lands, reshaping the province's demography. The partition of Ireland under the Government of Ireland Act 1920 created the six-county Northern Ireland and left the three Ulster counties of Donegal, Cavan, and Monaghan in the Irish Free State.

Geography and culture

Ulster's landscape includes Lough Neagh, the largest lake in the British Isles, the Mourne Mountains, the Sperrins, the Antrim coast with the Giant's Causeway, and Ireland's most northerly point at Malin Head in County Donegal. Belfast and Derry are its principal cities. The province has a distinctive mix of Irish, Ulster Scots, and English traditions, and its Gaelic games, music, and dialect have strong regional identities.
