Dative case

The dative case (abbreviated DAT) is a grammatical case used in some languages to indicate the recipient or beneficiary of an action. It corresponds most closely to the English prepositions to and for, and typically marks the indirect object of a verb: in "I gave the book to Maria", the recipient would stand in the dative in a language with case marking. The word derives from Latin casus dativus, "case for giving", itself a translation of the Greek term.

In German, the definite article changes form in the dative case: dem, der, dem, den.

Functions

  • Marking the indirect object (recipient or beneficiary).
  • Expressing possession in some languages, such as the Latin dativus possessivus.
  • Governing certain prepositions, as in German where prepositions such as mit, zu and von always take the dative.
  • Indicating the experiencer of states in constructions like German mir ist kalt ("I am cold", literally "to me is cold").

Distribution

The dative is found in many Indo-European languages, including Latin, Ancient Greek, German, Russian and other Slavic languages, and Sanskrit. English has lost case marking on nouns, retaining only vestiges in pronouns such as him and whom. In some languages, such as Hungarian, a dedicated dative suffix (-nak/-nek) exists; in others the dative has merged with other cases over time, as happened with the locative in Latin.
