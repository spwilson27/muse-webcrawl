Open innovation

Term coined byHenry Chesbrough (2003)
FieldInnovation management
Opposite modelClosed innovation
RelatedCrowdsourcing, open source, co-creation

Open innovation is a model of innovation management in which firms use external as well as internal ideas, and external as well as internal paths to market, as they develop technology and products. The term was popularised by Henry Chesbrough, then a professor at Harvard Business School, in his 2003 book Open Innovation: The New Imperative for Creating and Profiting from Technology. It contrasts with the closed innovation model, in which a company generates, develops, and commercialises its own ideas entirely in-house.

Definition

In Chesbrough's later formulation, open innovation is "the use of purposive inflows and outflows of knowledge to accelerate internal innovation, and expand the markets for external use of innovation, respectively." Ideas may flow inside-in (licensing in, acquiring start-ups, crowdsourcing problems to solvers) or outside-out (licensing unused patents, spinning out ventures), so the boundary between the firm and its environment becomes permeable.

History

Chesbrough argued that the closed model — exemplified by great corporate laboratories such as Xerox PARC and Bell Labs — eroded in the late 20th century as skilled workers became more mobile, venture capital funded start-ups that commercialised shelved corporate research, and suppliers and universities grew more capable. Xerox's failure to commercialise many PARC inventions (the graphical user interface, Ethernet, laser printing) while others profited from them is the model's canonical cautionary tale. Companies such as Procter & Gamble (with its "Connect + Develop" programme), IBM, and LEGO became prominent practitioners.

Mechanisms

  • Inbound: technology scouting, licensing, corporate venture capital, innovation contests and intermediaries (e.g. InnoCentive), university partnerships, user innovation and crowdsourcing.
  • Outbound: out-licensing of unused intellectual property, spin-offs, patent sales.
  • Coupled: alliances, joint ventures, and innovation ecosystems in which partners co-create.

Criticism

Critics note that openness has costs — coordination, IP leakage, dependence on partners — and that empirical studies show an inverted-U relationship: beyond a point, more external searching reduces innovation performance. Others observe that "open innovation" often rebrands long-standing practices, and that successful internal R&D remains essential to absorb external knowledge ("absorptive capacity").
