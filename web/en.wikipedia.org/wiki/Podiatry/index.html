Podiatry

Podiatry is a branch of medicine devoted to the study, diagnosis, medical and surgical treatment of disorders of the foot, ankle and lower extremity. A doctor of podiatric medicine (DPM), known in the United Kingdom and some Commonwealth countries as a podiatrist or chiropodist, specialises in conditions ranging from ingrown toenails, bunions and heel pain to diabetic foot ulcers and sports injuries.

Podiatrists diagnose and treat conditions of the foot, ankle and related structures of the leg.

Scope of practice

Podiatrists treat bone, joint, ligament, muscle and tendon disorders of the foot and ankle; skin and nail conditions such as fungal infections, corns and calluses; and complications of systemic disease, notably the diabetic foot, where screening and ulcer management help prevent amputation. Practice may include biomechanical assessment, prescription of orthotics, injections, minor surgery under local anaesthetic, and — for podiatric surgeons — reconstructive foot and ankle surgery.

Training and regulation

In the United States, podiatrists complete a four-year Doctor of Podiatric Medicine degree at one of the accredited colleges of podiatric medicine, followed by a three-year hospital-based residency in medicine and surgery of the foot and ankle. In the United Kingdom, podiatrists qualify with a BSc in podiatry and are regulated by the Health and Care Professions Council; the titles "podiatrist" and "chiropodist" are legally protected. Podiatry is also an established profession in Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa.

History

Foot care was practised from antiquity; professional chiropody developed in the nineteenth century, with practitioners such as Lewis Durlacher, chiropodist to Queen Victoria. The first school of chiropody in the United States opened in New York in 1912, and the profession gradually acquired surgical and prescribing rights through the twentieth century.
