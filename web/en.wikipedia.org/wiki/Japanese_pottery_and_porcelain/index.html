Japanese pottery and porcelain

Japanese ceramics: a Raku tea bowl and Arita porcelain

OriginsJōmon period, among the world's earliest pottery
Famous kilnsBizen, Shigaraki, Seto, Arita, Kutani, Mino
TraditionsTea ceremony wares, Imari/Kakiemon export porcelain

Japanese pottery and porcelain (陶磁器, tōjiki) is one of the oldest Japanese craft traditions, stretching back to the Jōmon period, whose flame-decorated earthenware ranks among the earliest pottery in the world. Japanese ceramics are celebrated for their interplay of rustic stoneware — prized in the tea ceremony — and refined porcelain exported around the globe.

History

After Jōmon and Yayoi earthenware, continental techniques arrived via Korea: Sue ware stoneware in the Kofun–Nara periods, and ash-glazed wares at kilns such as Sanage. In the medieval period the "Six Ancient Kilns" — including Bizen, Shigaraki, Echizen, Seto, Tokoname and Tamba — produced durable unglazed or naturally ash-glazed stonewares. The rise of the tea ceremony under Sen no Rikyū in the 16th century transformed taste toward rough, asymmetrical wares: Shino, Oribe and the hand-formed Raku bowls of the potter Chōjirō.

Porcelain production in Japan began around 1616 at Arita, using techniques brought by Korean potters relocated during Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea, which discovered kaolin deposits at Izumiyama. Underglaze-blue Imari ware and enamelled Kakiemon ware were exported in vast quantities by the Dutch East India Company, filling the gap left by the disruption of Chinese porcelain exports, and profoundly influenced European manufacturers such as Meissen. Kutani, Nabeshima and Hirado wares served domestic and shogunal markets.

Modern period

After the Meiji Restoration, Japanese ceramics won acclaim at world expositions, and studio pottery flourished in the 20th century through the Mingei (folk craft) movement of Yanagi Sōetsu and potters such as Hamada Shōji and Bernard Leach. Today regions like Mashiko, Arita/Imari and Kyoto remain active centres, and master potters are designated Living National Treasures.
