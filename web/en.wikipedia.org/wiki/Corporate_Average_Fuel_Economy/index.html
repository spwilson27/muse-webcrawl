Corporate average fuel economy

Corporate average fuel economy (CAFE) standards are regulations in the United States, first enacted by the United States Congress in 1975, to improve the average fuel economy of cars and light trucks (including trucks, vans and sport utility vehicles) produced for sale in the United States. The standards were enacted in the wake of the 1973–74 Arab oil embargo and are administered by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA).

Overview

CAFE standards specify a sales-weighted harmonic mean fuel economy, in miles per gallon, that a manufacturer's fleet of a given model year must achieve. Since reforms enacted in 2007 (EISA), standards have been footprint-based: vehicles are assigned efficiency targets according to their size, so larger vehicles face less stringent absolute targets. Passenger car standards rose from 18 mpg in 1978 to 27.5 mpg in 1985 and remained there for many years before rising substantially in the 2010s and 2020s.

Debate

Supporters credit CAFE with large petroleum savings and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. Critics argue the standards distorted the market toward light trucks and SUVs (which were long subject to weaker standards), raised vehicle prices, and may have encouraged downsizing that affected safety — claims that remain contested in the literature.
