Russian folklore

Russian folklore comprises the folk traditions of the Russian people: fairy tales (skazki), heroic epics (byliny), songs, proverbs, rituals, and a rich cast of spirits and magical beings rooted in Slavic paganism and reshaped over centuries of Orthodox Christianity.

Fairy tales

The great collection is Alexander Afanasyev's Russian Fairy Tales (1855–1863), modelled on the Grimms' collection. Recurring figures include the hero Ivan Tsarevich; the Firebird; the witch Baba Yaga, who lives in a hut on chicken legs and flies in a mortar; and her sometime adversary Koschei the Deathless, who hides his death in a needle inside an egg. Tales such as The Frog Princess, Vasilisa the Beautiful, and Marya Morevna follow the functions analysed by Vladimir Propp in his Morphology of the Folktale (1928).

Byliny and spirits

The byliny sung in the Russian North celebrate bogatyrs of Kievan Rus: Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich, and Alyosha Popovich defending the land against steppe enemies. Everyday belief peopled the world with household and nature spirits: the domovoi (house spirit), the leshy (forest spirit), the vodyanoy (water spirit), and rusalki, the drowned maidens of rivers and lakes.

Traditions and legacy

Calendar rituals — Maslenitsa before Lent, Ivan Kupala at midsummer, Svyatki divination at Christmas — blended pagan and Christian practice in the dual faith (dvoeverie) of the peasantry. Russian folklore profoundly influenced literature, opera, and ballet: Rimsky-Korsakov, Stravinsky's The Firebird and Petrushka, and the paintings of Viktor Vasnetsov and Ivan Bilibin all drew on it.
