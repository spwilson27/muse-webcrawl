Hoarding (castles)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hoarding or hoard (French: hourd) was a temporary wooden gallery built on the outside of a medieval castle's stone walls during a siege. Supported on cantilevered beams inserted into putlog holes in the masonry, the roofed gallery projected outward from the parapet and was floored with removable planks or hinged trapdoors.

Purpose

The hoarding's overhang let defenders shoot arrows, drop stones, and pour water (against attempts to set fires) directly onto attackers at the foot of the wall — the "dead ground" that the flat wall face otherwise protected. Its roof sheltered defenders from arrows and missiles lobbed from siege engines. Because it was wooden, hoarding could be erected only when a siege threatened and could be dismantled afterwards; fires set by besiegers were its chief weakness.

Stone successors

From the late 12th century, permanent stone machicolations — parapets with floor openings between corbels — replaced wooden hoardings in many castles, performing the same function without vulnerability to fire. Surviving putlog holes and corbels on many castle walls mark where hoardings once stood, and reconstructions exist at sites such as the Château de Castelnaud in France and several Welsh castles. Retractable wooden hoardings are also preserved in place on a few towers, notably at Provins.
