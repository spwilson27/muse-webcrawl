Richard Doddridge Blackmore (7 June 1825 – 20 January 1900), usually known as R. D. Blackmore, was an English novelist best remembered for Lorna Doone (1869), his romantic tale of Exmoor in the seventeenth century. He was one of the most popular novelists of his day, and his vivid descriptions of the southwestern English countryside shaped the region's literary identity.

Life

Blackmore was born at Longworth in Berkshire, the son of a clergyman, and was educated at Blundell's School and Exeter College, Oxford. Called to the bar in 1852, he practised briefly before ill health turned him to teaching, translating and writing. He later settled at Teddington, where he ran a successful market garden and fruit-growing business alongside his literary career — a practical knowledge of horticulture that colours his writing with unusually exact observation of plants and seasons.

Works

Blackmore published poetry and translations of Virgil before turning to fiction. Clara Vaughan (1864) was his first novel. Lorna Doone: A Romance of Exmoor (1869), centred on the outlaw Doone family and narrated by the farmer John Ridd, was initially a modest success but grew steadily in fame and has never been out of print; it helped popularise Exmoor as a tourist destination. Other novels include The Maid of Sker (1872), Springhaven (1887), set during the Napoleonic Wars, and Dariel (1897).

Legacy

Blackmore's regional romances place him among the predecessors of Thomas Hardy, and the Blackmore country of Devon and Somerset remains a fixture of English literary geography, with memorials at Oare church and in Exeter Cathedral.
