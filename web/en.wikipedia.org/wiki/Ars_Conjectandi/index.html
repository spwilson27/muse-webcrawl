Ars Conjectandi (Latin for "The Art of Conjecturing") is a foundational work on probability theory by the Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli, published posthumously in Basel in 1713 by his nephew Nicolaus Bernoulli, eight years after Jacob's death. It is regarded as the first systematic treatise on probability and a landmark of mathematical history.

Contents

The work is divided into four parts. The first reproduces and annotates Christiaan Huygens's De ratiociniis in ludo aleae (1657), the first printed work on probability. The second develops the theory of permutations and combinations, including the numbers now called Bernoulli numbers. The third applies combinatorics to games of chance. The fourth, unfinished, part turns to applications in civil, moral and economic affairs, and contains Bernoulli's masterpiece: the first version of the law of large numbers (his "golden theorem"), proving that observed frequencies converge to the true probability as trials increase.

Significance

Ars Conjectandi transformed probability from a calculus of games into a general art of reasoning under uncertainty, introducing ideas — such as probability as a degree of certainty — that shaped statistics, demography and actuarial science for centuries.
