Phong shading

Phong shading is an interpolation technique for surface shading in 3D computer graphics. Also called Phong interpolation or normal-vector interpolation shading, it interpolates surface normals across a polygon and computes the lighting at each pixel, producing smoother results and more accurate specular highlights than flat shading or Gouraud shading. It is normally used together with the Phong reflection model.

Method

Each vertex of a polygon carries a surface normal, typically averaged from the faces meeting at that vertex. Across the polygon's surface the normals are interpolated and normalized per pixel, and the illumination equation — ambient plus diffuse plus specular terms, with the specular term depending on the reflected light direction and viewer position — is evaluated for every pixel. This lets highlights appear in the interior of large polygons, which per-vertex Gouraud shading can miss.

History

The technique was published by Vietnamese computer scientist Bui Tuong Phong in his 1973 PhD thesis and a 1975 paper, alongside his empirical Phong reflection model. A modified half-vector formulation by Jim Blinn (Blinn–Phong) became the standard in fixed-function graphics hardware and in APIs such as OpenGL and Direct3D, and per-pixel Phong-style shading remains the conceptual basis of modern programmable shaders.

Limitations

Phong shading cannot reproduce effects such as shadows, reflections of other objects, or accurate silhouettes, and its empirical specular term is not physically based; modern physically based rendering (PBR) models generalize its ideas with energy-conserving microfacet BRDFs.
