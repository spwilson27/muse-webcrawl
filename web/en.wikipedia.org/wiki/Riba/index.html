Riba

Arabicالربا (literally "increase, excess")
DomainIslamic law (fiqh), Islamic finance
Principal sourcesQur'an (e.g. 2:275–280, 3:130, 4:161, 30:39); hadith of the six commodities
StatusProhibited (haram)

Riba (Arabic: الربا, romanised ar-ribā) is an Arabic word meaning "excess" or "increase." In Islamic law (Sharia), it refers to unjust, exploitative gains made in trade or business, and is most commonly understood as interest charged on loans. Riba is prohibited in Islam, and the prohibition is the foundation of Islamic banking and finance, which structures transactions to avoid predetermined interest.

Scriptural basis

Several verses of the Qur'an condemn riba in escalating terms. The final and most emphatic passage (Qur'an 2:275–279) declares that God has permitted trade and forbidden riba, describes those who consume it as waging "war against God and His Messenger," and instructs creditors to take only their principal. Verse 3:130 forbids consuming riba "doubled and multiplied." The hadith of the six commodities (gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates and salt, reported in Sahih Muslim) extends the prohibition to unequal or deferred exchanges of specified fungible goods.

Classical jurisprudence

Jurists distinguished two principal categories:

  • Riba al-nasī'ah — an increase stipulated in exchange for delay in repayment of a debt; this includes conventional loan interest and is prohibited by consensus.
  • Riba al-faḍl — an unequal spot exchange of the same ribawi commodity (e.g. gold for gold in different amounts), prohibited according to most schools on the basis of the six-commodities hadith.

The underlying rationale debated by scholars includes the exploitation of need, the requirement that profit be linked to real economic risk and effort, and the prevention of wealth concentration.

Modern debates

Whether all modern bank interest constitutes the riba banned by the Qur'an has been contested since the late nineteenth century. Reformists such as Fazlur Rahman argued that the Qur'anic prohibition targeted the doubling, usurious charges of pre-Islamic Arabia; the mainstream of contemporary scholarship and the organised Islamic finance industry holds that any stipulated excess over principal is riba. Islamic banks accordingly use contracts such as murabaha (cost-plus sale), ijara (leasing), mudaraba and musharaka (profit-and-loss sharing) and sukuk (asset-backed certificates). Critics argue some instruments replicate interest economics under different forms; defenders stress legal form, asset-backing and risk-sharing as meaningful differences.
