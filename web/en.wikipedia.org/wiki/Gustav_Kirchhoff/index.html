Gustav Robert Kirchhoff (12 March 1824 – 17 October 1887) was a German physicist who contributed fundamentally to the understanding of electrical circuits, spectroscopy and black-body radiation. He is remembered for Kirchhoff's circuit laws, for founding with Robert Bunsen the science of chemical analysis by spectrum, and for coining the term "black body".

Circuit laws

In 1845, while still a student at the University of Königsberg, Kirchhoff generalised Ohm's law to networks of conductors in two rules: the current law (the algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero — a statement of conservation of charge) and the voltage law (the algebraic sum of voltage changes around any closed loop is zero — conservation of energy). These remain the starting point of circuit analysis. He later (1857) showed that electrical signals travel in a lossless wire at the speed of light.

Spectroscopy

At Heidelberg, Kirchhoff collaborated with the chemist Robert Bunsen. Together they built an improved spectroscope and, in 1859–60, showed that each chemical element emits and absorbs light at characteristic wavelengths, founding spectrum analysis; with it they discovered the new elements caesium (1860) and rubidium (1861). Kirchhoff explained the dark Fraunhofer lines in the solar spectrum as absorption by elements in the Sun's atmosphere — making it possible to determine the composition of stars — and formulated three laws of spectroscopy describing the spectra of hot solids, hot gases and gases in front of hotter sources.

Black-body radiation

Kirchhoff proved that for a body in thermal equilibrium the ratio of emissive to absorptive power depends only on temperature and wavelength, and defined the perfect absorber-emitter, the black body, whose radiation spectrum is a universal function of temperature alone. The problem of finding that function, which he posed, was solved only in 1900 by Max Planck, in the discovery that launched quantum theory.
