Impulse (physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical mechanics, impulse (symbolized by J or Imp) is the change in linear momentum of an object. It is the integral of the force acting on the object over the time interval during which the force acts:

J = ∫ F dt = Δp = m Δv

where p is momentum, m is mass, and v is velocity. Impulse has the same units and dimensions as momentum: the newton-second (N⋅s) in the SI system, or kg⋅m/s.

Mathematical derivation

Impulse follows directly from Newton's second law. Since force equals the rate of change of momentum, F = dp/dt, integrating both sides over the interval from t1 to t2 gives J = p2 − p1. This relationship is known as the impulse–momentum theorem. When the mass is constant, it simplifies to J = m(v2 − v1). For a constant force, impulse is simply the product of the force and the duration of its application.

Applications

The concept is central to understanding collisions. In a collision, large forces act over short times, and the impulse determines the resulting change in motion. Engineers exploit this idea in safety design: crumple zones in automobiles, airbags, and padded surfaces all work by extending the time over which a given impulse is delivered, thereby reducing the peak force experienced by occupants. In sports, following through on a swing increases contact time and thus the impulse imparted to a ball. Impulse is also used in rocket science, where the specific impulse measures the efficiency of a propellant.
