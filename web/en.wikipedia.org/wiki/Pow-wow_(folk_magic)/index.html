Pow-wow (folk magic)

Pow-wow flourished among the Pennsylvania Dutch of rural Pennsylvania.

Pow-wow, sometimes spelled powwow and in Pennsylvania Dutch called Brauche or Braucherei, is a tradition of folk magic and faith healing practised among the Pennsylvania Dutch (German-American) communities of rural Pennsylvania and neighbouring states. Despite its name — borrowed in the colonial era from a Native American word for a shaman or gathering — the tradition is European in origin, drawing on German grimoires, charms and folk remedies brought to America in the eighteenth century, and it is practised within a broadly Christian framework of prayer, scripture and ritual gesture.

Practice

Practitioners, called pow-wowers or Brauchers, treat ailments of people and livestock — warts, burns, fevers, sprains, nosebleeds and "wasting" conditions — using spoken charms, Bible verses, passes of the hands, and simple remedies. Healing was traditionally done in the name of the Trinity and often cost nothing, payment being considered to invalidate the cure. The art was commonly passed within families, by tradition from a person of one sex to a person of the other.

Texts

The best-known pow-wow manual is The Long Lost Friend (Der lange verborgene Freund), published in 1820 by Johann Georg Hohman, a German immigrant to Berks County, Pennsylvania. It collects spells, remedies and protective charms, many invoking saints and biblical figures. Other influential texts include the Egyptian Secrets attributed to Albertus Magnus and the apocryphal Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses.

History and controversy

Pow-wow was a routine part of rural life in the Pennsylvania Dutch country into the twentieth century, coexisting with orthodox Lutheran and Reformed church membership, though some clergy condemned it as superstition or witchcraft. It attracted sensational attention through the 1928 "hex murder" of Nelson Rehmeyer in York County, whose killers believed he had bewitched them — an event that prompted local campaigns against the practice. The tradition has declined with modern medicine and cultural assimilation but survives among some practitioners, and aspects of it are studied by folklorists and incorporated into modern Heathen and occult movements.
