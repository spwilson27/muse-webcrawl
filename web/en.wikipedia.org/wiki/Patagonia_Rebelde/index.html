Patagonia Rebelde

Patagonia Rebelde (also known as Patagonia Trágica, "Tragic Patagonia") was the name given to the violent suppression of a rural workers' strike in the Argentine territory of Santa Cruz in Patagonia between 1920 and 1922. The events are regarded as among the most notorious episodes of state violence against labour in Argentine history.

Background

Sheep-ranching dominated the economy of Santa Cruz, where largely immigrant rural labourers (peones) worked in harsh conditions for estancia owners, many of them British. Influenced by anarchist and syndicalist ideas, workers organised through the Sociedad Obrera de Río Gallegos, led by figures such as Antonio Soto.

The strikes

A first strike in late 1920 won concessions, but employers failed to honour the agreement and arrested union leaders. A general strike began in 1921, with workers occupying estancias and taking hostages as pressure tactics. President Hipólito Yrigoyen's government sent the army under Lieutenant Colonel Héctor Benigno Varela to restore order.

Repression

Varela's campaign, particularly in late 1921, ended with mass executions of surrendered strikers. Estimates of the dead commonly range from about 300 to 1,500 workers, executed by firing squads at estancias and railway stations. Varela was assassinated in 1923 by the anarchist Kurt Wilckens in revenge.

Legacy

The events were largely silenced for decades before historian Osvaldo Bayer's research in the late 1960s and 1970s brought them to national attention; his work inspired the 1974 film La Patagonia rebelde, which was censored under the subsequent military dictatorship. Memorials and official commemorations in Santa Cruz now honour the executed workers.
