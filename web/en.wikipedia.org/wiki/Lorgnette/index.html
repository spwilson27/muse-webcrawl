Lorgnette

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lorgnette is a pair of spectacles with a handle, used to hold them in place, rather than fitting over the ears or nose. The word is derived from the French lorgner, "to squint" or "to ogle". Lorgnettes were popular during the 19th century, particularly among fashionable women, for whom they were as much an item of jewellery as a visual aid.

History

The lorgnette is generally credited to the English optician George Adams the elder in the mid-18th century, although similar handled reading glasses existed earlier. By the Regency and Victorian eras, lorgnettes were produced in gold, silver, tortoiseshell, mother-of-pearl and enamel, and folding versions were made that collapsed into their handle, which could double as a decorative case. They were especially associated with the opera and the theatre, where a lorgnette allowed the user to view both the stage and fellow audience members.

Types

Two main forms existed: the simple lorgnette, with a single pair of lenses on a handle, and the spring-loaded folding lorgnette, in which the lenses unfolded from the handle at the press of a small lever. Related optical devices include the pince-nez, the monocle and opera glasses, which unlike the lorgnette usually incorporated magnification for distance viewing in a binocular form.

In culture

The lorgnette became a stage shorthand for aristocratic haughtiness: the gesture of raising a lorgnette to inspect someone signified disdainful scrutiny, and it appears frequently in the novels and illustrations of the period.
