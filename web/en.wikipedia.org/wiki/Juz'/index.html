Juz'

A juz' (Arabic: جُزْء, plural ajzā', أَجْزَاء) is one of thirty parts of roughly equal length into which the Qur'an is divided. The division is primarily a practical one, intended to facilitate recitation of the entire text over the course of a month — one juz' per day — especially during the nights of Ramadan in tarawih prayers. The divisions do not correspond to breaks in subject matter and sometimes fall in the middle of a surah (chapter).

Structure

Each juz' is further subdivided into two ḥizb (halves), and each ḥizb into four rubʿ (quarters), so that the whole Qur'an contains 60 aḥzāb and 240 quarters. These subdivisions are marked in printed editions with special symbols in the margin.

The thirty ajzā' are often known by the opening words of their first verse. The first juz' is called Alif Lām Mīm, after the mysterious letters opening Surah Al-Baqarah; the last, Juz' 30, is called 'Amma after its opening word, and contains the short, frequently memorised final surahs. Juz' 30 is commonly printed separately for children learning to read and memorise the Qur'an.

Use

Printed muṣḥafs (copies of the Qur'an), particularly the standard Madinah edition of 604 pages, place a decorative marker at the beginning of each juz'. The juz' division is conventionally attributed to the era of al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf (late 7th–early 8th century CE), when the text was divided for teaching and recitation purposes.
