Eco-terrorism

Eco-terrorism (also spelled ecoterrorism) is a term used to describe acts of violence or sabotage committed in the name of environmental or animal rights causes, typically directed against property — such as logging equipment, research laboratories, fur farms, or developments — rather than against people. The term is controversial: activists and civil-liberties advocates argue it misapplies the label "terrorism" to property crime, while law-enforcement agencies use it for crimes committed by groups such as the Earth Liberation Front (ELF) and the Animal Liberation Front (ALF).

Definition and usage

The FBI has defined eco-terrorism as "the use or threatened use of violence of a criminal nature against innocent victims or property ... by an environmentally oriented, subnational group for environmental-political reasons". In the United States, the Animal Enterprise Terrorism Act of 2006 expanded federal offences relating to animal enterprises. Critics, including the ACLU, contend that such laws can chill lawful protest.

History

Radical environmental direct action emerged in the 1970s and 1980s with groups such as Earth First!, which popularised tree-sitting and "monkeywrenching" (sabotage of machinery). The ELF, founded in Britain in 1992, claimed responsibility for a series of arson attacks in the United States in the late 1990s and early 2000s, including the 1998 fire at the Vail ski resort in Colorado. US federal investigations, notably Operation Backfire, led to convictions of several ELF and ALF members.

Debate

Scholars distinguish eco-terrorism from the much larger environmental movement, which is overwhelmingly non-violent. Debate continues over whether property destruction without intent to harm people should be labelled terrorism at all, and over the proportionality of prosecutions and sentences.
