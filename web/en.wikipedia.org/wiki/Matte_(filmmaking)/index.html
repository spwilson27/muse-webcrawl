Matte (filmmaking)

In filmmaking, a matte is a technique for combining two or more image elements into a single final image. Traditionally, part of the scene was blocked out — "matted out" — so that another element, often a painting, could be inserted in its place. The related matte painting, executed in oils or acrylics on glass or board, allowed filmmakers to create landscapes, cityscapes, and architecture that would have been impossible or prohibitively expensive to build.

History

The technique dates to the earliest years of cinema. Norman Dawn pioneered the glass shot in the 1900s and 1910s, painting scenery on glass placed between the camera and the live action. Through the studio era, departments at MGM, Warner Bros., and other studios produced celebrated mattes; artists such as Albert Whitlock, who painted backgrounds for Alfred Hitchcock and for films including The Hindenburg (1975), became renowned specialists. Matte paintings supplied the vistas of Gone with the Wind, the castle in The Wizard of Oz, and large parts of the original Star Wars trilogy, where Industrial Light & Magic artists painted environments later composited with live action and miniatures.

Methods

A static matte blocks a fixed region of the frame, while a travelling matte changes from frame to frame to follow a moving subject; travelling mattes were long produced by rotoscoping or by processes such as the sodium-vapour and blue-screen techniques. In the digital era, mattes are extracted by keying (chroma key or luma key) and compositing is performed in software, but the underlying concept — separating foreground and background with a mask, the matte — remains the foundation of visual-effects compositing. A matte also survives as the alpha channel of a digital image.
