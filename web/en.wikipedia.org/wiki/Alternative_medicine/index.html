Alternative medicine

Alternative medicine
Herbal remedies and acupuncture are among the best-known alternative therapies
ExamplesHomeopathy, acupuncture, herbal medicine, chiropractic, Ayurveda, traditional Chinese medicine
Related termsComplementary medicine; integrative medicine; fringe medicine

Alternative medicine is any practice that aims to achieve the healing effects of medicine, but which lacks biological plausibility, testability, repeatability, or supporting evidence from clinical trials. The term is used variously to describe practices used instead of conventional medicine ("alternative"), alongside it ("complementary medicine"), or integrated with it ("integrative medicine").

Alternative therapies share a number of common features: they are generally not taught in medical schools, not used in hospitals as standard care, and not covered by medical insurance in many jurisdictions. Because of the absence of competent, reliable scientific evidence, alternative medical practices are often criticized as ineffective, and their use is a matter of ongoing public and professional debate. Some practices, such as acupuncture for certain types of pain, have accumulated partial evidence, while others, such as homeopathy, are considered implausible by the scientific community.

Use and regulation

Surveys in the United States and Europe have repeatedly found that a substantial share of adults — often a third or more — report using some form of complementary or alternative therapy in a given year, most commonly for musculoskeletal pain, anxiety, or general wellness. Regulation varies widely by country and by therapy, from state licensure of chiropractors and acupuncturists to largely unregulated markets for dietary supplements under laws such as the U.S. Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994.

Criticism

Critics argue that alternative medicine can harm patients directly, through toxic or contaminated products, and indirectly, when patients delay or forgo effective conventional treatment for serious illness. Supporters respond that some practices offer comfort, a sense of control, and attention to the whole person that conventional medicine may neglect. Medical bodies generally advise that alternative therapies should be evaluated by the same evidentiary standards as any other treatment.
