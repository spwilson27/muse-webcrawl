Budget deficit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Chart of government revenues and expenditures showing a deficit gap]
A deficit occurs when government spending exceeds revenue

A budget deficit occurs when a government's expenditures exceed its revenues over a given period, usually a fiscal year. The opposite of a deficit is a budget surplus, and when revenues equal expenditures the budget is said to be balanced. A government's overall budget balance is commonly expressed as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP), which allows comparisons across countries and over time.

Definitions

The primary deficit is the budget deficit excluding interest payments on existing debt; it measures the current fiscal stance apart from the legacy of past borrowing. The structural deficit estimates what the deficit would be if the economy were operating at potential output, removing the effect of the business cycle, while the cyclical deficit is the portion attributable to economic fluctuations — recessions automatically raise deficits by reducing tax revenue and increasing spending on unemployment benefits and other stabilisers.

Causes

Deficits can result from deliberate policy, such as tax cuts or increased public spending, or from economic conditions. Large deficits commonly accompany wars, financial crises, and recessions. Persistent structural deficits arise when a government's ongoing spending commitments — on pensions, healthcare, defence, and public services — exceed its ongoing tax base.

Financing

A government finances a deficit mainly by issuing debt, such as treasury bills and bonds, sold to private investors, pension funds, foreign governments, and central banks. Over time, accumulated deficits form the stock of government debt. In some cases governments have resorted to monetary financing — effectively borrowing from the central bank — which can risk high inflation if used extensively. Deficits can also be reduced by raising taxes, cutting spending, or through economic growth that raises revenue.

Economic views

Economists disagree about deficits. Keynesian economics holds that deficits are appropriate during downturns to support aggregate demand, and that governments should aim for balance over the cycle rather than every year. Critics emphasise the risks of high debt: rising interest costs, crowding out of private investment, reduced fiscal flexibility in future crises, and in extreme cases sovereign debt crises. Some frameworks, such as Ricardian equivalence, argue that deficit financing merely shifts taxes into the future and may not stimulate spending at all.

Examples

Many advanced economies ran very large deficits during the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, when deficits in some countries exceeded 10 percent of GDP. Rules such as the European Union's Stability and Growth Pact (with its 3 percent of GDP deficit reference value) and various national balanced-budget provisions attempt to constrain deficit spending.
