Cyanoacrylate
TypeFast-acting adhesive ("superglue")

Cyanoacrylates are a family of strong, fast-acting adhesives with industrial, medical, and household uses, widely known by trade names such as Super Glue and Krazy Glue. They are acrylic resins that rapidly polymerise in the presence of water — even the trace moisture on most surfaces — forming long, strong chains that join the bonded surfaces together.

History

Cyanoacrylates were first synthesized in 1942 by Harry Coover and Fred Joyner at Eastman Kodak while searching for materials for clear plastic gun sights; the substance stuck to everything and was initially discarded. Coover rediscovered it while working on heat-resistant jet-canopy polymers, recognised its potential, and it was marketed in 1958 as Eastman 910. Coover later demonstrated the adhesive's strength on television by lifting a man with a single drop.

Properties and uses

Common household superglue is mainly ethyl cyanoacrylate. It bonds skin almost instantly and sets within seconds on most materials, achieving full strength in hours. Longer-chain medical grades — n-butyl and 2-octyl cyanoacrylate — are less toxic and are used to close wounds and surgical incisions. Cyanoacrylate fumes are used in forensics to develop latent fingerprints on non-porous surfaces. Shelf life is limited, typically about a year unopened, and refrigeration slows degradation.

Safety

Cyanoacrylates can bond skin and eyelids, irritate eyes and airways, and react exothermically with cotton and wool. Bonds to skin generally wear off within days; solvents such as acetone soften the cured adhesive.
