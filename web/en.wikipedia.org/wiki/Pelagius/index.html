Pelagius

Born: c. 354
Died: c. 418
Known for: Pelagianism

Pelagius (c. 354 – c. 418) was a British monk and theologian whose teachings on free will and original sin provoked one of the great doctrinal controversies of the early Church. He taught that human beings can take the first steps toward salvation by their own efforts, and denied that Adam's sin was transmitted to his descendants as inherited guilt.

Life and controversy

Pelagius lived as an ascetic teacher in Rome until the Gothic sack of 410, after which he travelled to Carthage and Palestine. His ideas were attacked by Augustine of Hippo, whose doctrine of original sin and predestination developed in large part through this controversy. Pelagianism was condemned at the Councils of Carthage (418) and Ephesus (431), and Pelagius was excommunicated by Pope Innocent I. Little is known of his final years.

Legacy

Though condemned as heresy, the questions Pelagius raised about grace, freedom, and responsibility recurred throughout Christian history, including in later debates over semi-Pelagianism and in Reformation-era controversies.
