Acute coronary syndrome

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a syndrome due to decreased blood flow in the coronary arteries such that part of the heart muscle is unable to function properly or dies. The most common symptom is centrally located chest pain, often radiating to the left shoulder or angle of the jaw, crushing, central and associated with nausea and sweating. Many people with acute coronary syndromes present with symptoms other than chest pain, particularly women, older people and people with diabetes mellitus.

Classification

Acute coronary syndrome is subdivided into three categories based on the electrocardiogram (ECG) and cardiac biomarkers: ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina. STEMI results from complete occlusion of a coronary artery and requires immediate reperfusion, usually by percutaneous coronary intervention. NSTEMI involves myocardial necrosis (raised troponin) without ST elevation, while unstable angina presents with ischaemic symptoms at rest or on minimal exertion without biomarker evidence of necrosis.

Pathophysiology

The usual underlying mechanism is rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque in a coronary artery, exposing thrombogenic material and triggering platelet aggregation and thrombus formation. Less common causes include coronary artery spasm, dissection, embolism and severe anaemia or hypoxia causing supply–demand mismatch (type 2 myocardial infarction).

Treatment

Initial management includes antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor), anticoagulation, nitrates and beta-blockers where appropriate, high-intensity statins, and risk stratification (e.g. the GRACE score). Patients with STEMI are taken for emergency primary angioplasty; those with high-risk NSTEMI undergo early invasive angiography. Long-term secondary prevention involves lifestyle modification, antiplatelet therapy, statins and treatment of hypertension and diabetes.
