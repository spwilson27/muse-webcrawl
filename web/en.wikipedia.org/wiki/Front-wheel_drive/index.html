Front-wheel drive

Front-wheel drive (FWD) is a form of engine and transmission layout used in motor vehicles, in which the engine drives the front wheels only. In the most common configuration the engine is mounted transversely across the front of the vehicle, with the gearbox alongside or beneath it, forming a compact transaxle. Front-wheel drive is the dominant layout for ordinary passenger cars worldwide.

History

Experiments with front-wheel drive date to the earliest automobiles, including the steam-powered Cugnot of 1769. Practical early examples include the Cord L-29 (1929) and the Citroën Traction Avant (1934), a landmark design. The layout became widespread after the success of the original Mini of 1959, whose transverse engine and space-saving packaging demonstrated the advantages for small cars. During the 1970s and 1980s most manufacturers switched their volume models from rear-wheel drive to front-wheel drive, a transition led by cars such as the Volkswagen Golf and accelerated in the United States by the oil crises and downsizing.

Advantages and disadvantages

Front-wheel drive eliminates the propshaft and rear differential, reducing cost, weight and interior intrusion and allowing a flat floor and more cabin and luggage space. Because the engine's weight sits over the driven wheels, traction on slippery surfaces is generally good, and the layout tends toward stable understeer. Disadvantages include unequal-length driveshafts producing torque steer under hard acceleration, a front-heavy weight distribution, more crowded engine bays, and limits on engine size and power; for these reasons high-performance, luxury and heavy-duty vehicles more often use rear-wheel or all-wheel drive.
