Physiocracy

[Image: an 18th-century French farm landscape of wheat fields, the Physiocrats' source of wealth]

Physiocracy is an 18th-century French school of economic thought that held that the wealth of nations derives solely from land and agriculture, and that economies are governed by a natural order best left unimpeded — the origin of Laissez-faire. Its name, coined by Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours (1767), means 'government of nature'.

Doctrine

The school's founder, François Quesnay (physician to Louis XV), modelled the economy in his Tableau Économique (1758) — the first schematic of economic circulation, an ancestor of input–output analysis and of macroeconomic accounting — dividing society into the productive class (farmers), the proprietary class (landowners) and the sterile class (manufacturers and merchants, who transform but do not add wealth). Physiocrats advocated a single tax on land rent (the impôt unique), free trade in grain, and dismantling guilds and mercantilist regulation. Turgot, as controller-general (1774–76), attempted reforms in this spirit. Adam Smith absorbed much from the Physiocrats while rejecting the unproductiveness of manufactures; their land-tax single idea echoes in Henry George and modern land-value taxation.
