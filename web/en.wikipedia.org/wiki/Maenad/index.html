Maenad

In Greek mythology, maenads (Greek: μαινάδες, "raving ones") were the female followers of Dionysus, the god of wine and ecstasy. Together with satyrs and sileni they formed the god's retinue, the thiasos, and wandered the mountains in a state of ecstatic frenzy induced by dance, music and wine.

Mythology

Maenads are depicted in art wearing fawn skins and ivy wreaths, carrying the thyrsus — a fennel staff tipped with a pine cone — and handling snakes. In myth their frenzy could turn violent: in Euripides' tragedy The Bacchae, the maenads of Thebes, led by Agave, tear apart King Pentheus, who had spurned the god. Maenads also feature in the story of Orpheus, whom they dismembered.

Cult

The maenads of myth reflect real ritual practice: historical Greek women participated in Dionysiac rites, and every other year bands of worshippers known as Thyiades made pilgrimages to Mount Parnassus to celebrate the god in winter. Roman counterparts were called Bacchantes, from Bacchus, the Roman name of Dionysus.
