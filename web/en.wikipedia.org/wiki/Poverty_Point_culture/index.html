Poverty Point culture

The Poverty Point culture is the archaeological culture of a pre-Columbian Native American society that inhabited the lower Mississippi Valley and surrounding Gulf Coast from about 1730 to 1350 BC, during the Late Archaic period. It is named for the Poverty Point site near present-day Epps in northeastern Louisiana, one of the largest early earthwork complexes in North America.

The Poverty Point site

The site consists of six concentric earthen ridges arranged in a C-shape around a central plaza, along with large mounds including Mound A, about 22 metres (72 ft) high and shaped, some argue, like a bird. The complex was built by hunter-gatherers rather than an agricultural society, an unusually large undertaking for a non-farming people. In 2014 the site was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Monumental Earthworks of Poverty Point.

Material culture and trade

Poverty Point peoples imported stone, copper, and minerals from hundreds of kilometres away — from the Great Lakes, the Ouachita and Ozark mountains, and the Appalachians — indicating wide trade networks. Distinctive artefacts include baked clay cooking balls ("Poverty Point objects") used for stone-boiling-style cooking in earth ovens, microliths, plummets, and small carved jasper owl figurines.
