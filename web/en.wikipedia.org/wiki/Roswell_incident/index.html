Roswell incident

The Roswell incident was the July 1947 recovery of metallic and rubber debris from a ranch near Roswell, New Mexico, by United States Army Air Forces personnel. On July 8, 1947, the Roswell Army Air Field issued a press release stating that it had recovered a "flying disc", but the statement was retracted within a day and the Army said the material was from a crashed weather balloon. The event faded from public attention for three decades until, beginning in the late 1970s, ufologists promoted the theory that the debris was from an extraterrestrial spacecraft and that the incident was the subject of a government cover-up. In the 1990s the U.S. Air Force published reports concluding that the debris came from a Project Mogul balloon train — a top-secret programme using high-altitude balloons to detect Soviet atomic bomb tests — and that later stories of alien bodies were probably distorted memories of anthropomorphic test dummies and real incidents. Roswell remains the most famous case in UFO lore, and the town has become a centre of UFO-themed tourism.

Events of 1947

In early July 1947, foreman W. W. "Mac" Brazel found a field of debris — sticks, tinfoil-like material, rubber strips and tape bearing floral patterns — on the Foster ranch about 75 miles (121 km) northwest of Roswell. Having heard reports of "flying discs" (the Kenneth Arnold sighting had occurred on June 24), Brazel reported the find to the Chaves County sheriff, who contacted Roswell Army Air Field, home of the 509th Bomb Group. Intelligence officer Major Jesse Marcel and counter-intelligence agent Sheridan Cavitt collected material from the site.

On July 8, public information officer Walter Haut issued a press release, authorised by base commander Colonel William Blanchard, announcing recovery of a "flying disc". The Roswell Daily Record ran the headline "RAAF Captures Flying Saucer on Ranch in Roswell Region". Within hours, Brigadier General Roger Ramey at Fort Worth Army Air Field held a press conference identifying the material, displayed with weather officer Irving Newton, as the remains of a weather balloon with a radar target (a Rawin reflector). The story briefly made national headlines before dying down.

Revival and conspiracy theories

Interest revived in 1978 when physicist and ufologist Stanton Friedman interviewed Jesse Marcel, who maintained that the debris he handled was unusual and not from a weather balloon. Charles Berlitz and William Moore's 1980 book The Roswell Incident introduced claims of a second crash site and recovered alien bodies, drawn from accounts such as that of civil engineer Grady "Barney" Barnett (relayed secondhand). Through the 1980s and 1990s, numerous books, documentaries and a 1994 television film elaborated the story, including claims of alien autopsies and threats against witnesses; a 1995 "alien autopsy" film promoted by Ray Santilli was later admitted to be a fabrication.

Air Force reports

Prompted by a congressional inquiry from New Mexico Representative Steven Schiff, the General Accounting Office investigated, and the Air Force issued two reports. The 1994 report The Roswell Report: Fact Versus Fiction in the New Mexico Desert concluded the debris was a Project Mogul balloon train of the type launched from Alamogordo Army Air Field on June 4, 1947 (flight No. 4), whose neoprene balloons, radar targets with patterned tape, and balsa struts matched the described wreckage. A 1997 follow-up, The Roswell Report: Case Closed, attributed reports of bodies to misremembered encounters with anthropomorphic dummies dropped in high-altitude tests in the 1950s (compressed in memory into 1947) and to two genuine accidents involving injured or killed airmen.

Legacy

Sceptics broadly accept the Mogul explanation, noting that no physical evidence of an extraterrestrial craft has ever been produced, while believers continue to dispute it. Roswell has embraced the legend: the International UFO Museum and Research Center opened in 1991, and the annual Roswell UFO Festival draws thousands of visitors, making the incident a permanent fixture of popular culture.
