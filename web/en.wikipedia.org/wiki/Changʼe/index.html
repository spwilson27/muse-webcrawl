Chang'e (Chinese: 嫦娥; pinyin: Cháng'é), originally known as Heng'e (姮娥), is the goddess of the Moon in Chinese mythology. She is the wife of the legendary archer Hou Yi. In the best-known version of her story, Chang'e consumed an elixir of immortality — in some versions stealing it from her husband — and flew to the Moon, where she has lived ever since, accompanied by the Jade Rabbit. Her myth is the origin of the Mid-Autumn Festival, at which she is honoured with offerings of mooncakes.


Chang'e flying to the Moon, a common subject in Chinese art.

Chang'e is one of the most beloved figures in Chinese folklore. The image of her silhouette within the Moon, sometimes discerned in its markings, remains a familiar poetic trope. China's lunar exploration programme, the Chang'e programme, is named after her.

The myth

In ancient times, according to the myth, ten suns appeared in the sky at once, scorching the earth and causing great hardship. The archer Hou Yi shot down nine of them, leaving only one, and was rewarded for his deed. In many versions, the Queen Mother of the West (Xiwangmu) gave Hou Yi an elixir of immortality, enough for one person, or in some tellings two half-doses. Hou Yi, unwilling to part from his wife Chang'e, entrusted the elixir to her keeping.

Accounts of what followed differ. In the version most often retold, Hou Yi's apprentice — named Feng Meng or Pang Meng in later retellings — coveted the elixir and tried to seize it while Hou Yi was away hunting. To keep it from him, Chang'e swallowed the whole elixir herself. Overcome by its power, she floated upward, past the heavens, and came to rest on the Moon, the nearest of the celestial bodies. There she remained, in the company of a rabbit that pounds the elixir of immortality with mortar and pestle — the Jade Rabbit.

When Hou Yi returned and learned what had happened, he was stricken with grief. Looking up at the full Moon, he saw the likeness of his wife within it. He set out her favourite fruits and cakes as offerings, a practice said to be the origin of the moon-worship customs of the Mid-Autumn Festival.

Versions of the story

The story of Chang'e developed over many centuries, and early texts preserve a darker tale. The Huainanzi (compiled around 139 BCE) records that Chang'e stole the elixir of immortality from her husband and fled to the Moon. Some early commentaries describe her as transforming into a toad after reaching the Moon, an element that persisted in art and folklore: the outline of a toad, like that of the rabbit, was discerned in the lunar markings. A passage in the Guicang, an early divination text recovered from a tomb, and a silk painting from the Han dynasty Mawangdui tombs, which depicts a figure with a toad beneath the crescent moon, are among the earliest attestations.

In other versions, Chang'e is a sympathetic or wronged figure: she takes the elixir to prevent it from falling into the hands of a villain, or Hou Yi himself becomes a tyrant after his triumph over the suns, and Chang'e drinks the elixir so that he cannot become an immortal despot. In still another tradition, Chang'e and Hou Yi were originally celestial beings banished to the mortal world, and the elixir was their means of return. The name Heng'e was changed to Chang'e because heng (恆) became taboo as part of the personal name of Emperor Wen of Han (Liu Heng), in accordance with the Chinese naming taboo custom.

Worship and the Mid-Autumn Festival

Chang'e is the central deity of the Mid-Autumn Festival (中秋節), held on the fifteenth day of the eighth month of the Chinese calendar, when the Moon is at its fullest and brightest. Families gather outdoors, set up altars with mooncakes, fruit and other offerings, and pray to Chang'e for luck and happiness. The festival is celebrated throughout China and in Chinese communities worldwide, and analogous moon festivals exist in Vietnam (Tết Trung Thu) and Korea (Chuseok). In Taoist tradition, Chang'e is venerated as a moon goddess, sometimes under titles such as the Moon Lady (月姑) or as an aspect of the goddess Taiyin Xingjun.

In popular culture

Chang'e has been depicted for two millennia in poetry, painting, opera and sculpture; the Tang dynasty poet Li Shangyin wrote a famous poem simply titled "Chang'e", expressing the loneliness of her eternal vigil. In modern times she appears in novels, films and video games, including as a playable character in the game Smite. The 2020 animated film Over the Moon retells her legend for a global audience. China's series of lunar probes — Chang'e 1 (2007) through Chang'e 6 (2024), the latter returning the first samples from the far side of the Moon — bear her name, and the rover deployed by Chang'e 3 was named Yutu, "Jade Rabbit", after her companion.
