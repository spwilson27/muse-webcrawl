Otter

Otters
Subfamily: Lutrinae
Family: Mustelidae
Genera: 7 living genera, 13 species

Otters are carnivorous mammals in the subfamily Lutrinae. The 13 extant otter species are all semiaquatic, aquatic or (in the case of the sea otter) marine, feeding mainly on fish and invertebrates. Lutrinae is a branch of the Mustelidae family, which also includes weasels, badgers, mink and wolverines.

Description

Otters have long, slim bodies and relatively short limbs, powerful webbed feet for swimming, and dense, insulating fur — the sea otter's coat, with up to a million hairs per square inch in places, is the densest of any animal. They can close their ears and nostrils underwater and hold their breath for several minutes. Sizes range from the Asian small-clawed otter, about 0.8 m long and weighing a few kilograms, to the giant otter of Amazonia, which may reach 1.8 m and 30 kg.

Distribution and habitat

Otters are found on every continent except Australia and Antarctica. River otters inhabit freshwater rivers, lakes and wetlands; the sea otter (Enhydra lutris) inhabits the kelp forests of the North Pacific; the marine otter lives on the Pacific coast of South America. A resting place is called a holt or couch; river otters mark territory with droppings called spraint.

Behaviour and diet

Otters are playful animals, sliding down mud banks, juggling stones and wrestling — behaviour that also trains the young in hunting skills. The diet consists mainly of fish, supplemented by frogs, crayfish, crabs, shellfish and small mammals. Sea otters use stones as tools to crack open shellfish and are a classic keystone species: by preying on sea urchins they protect kelp forests. Social structure varies: Eurasian otters are largely solitary, giant otters live in cooperative family groups, and male sea otters form rafts of resting individuals.

Conservation

Many otter species were hunted extensively for their fur — the sea otter was driven close to extinction by the maritime fur trade of the 18th and 19th centuries. Habitat loss, pollution and conflict with fisheries remain threats; most of the 13 species are listed by the IUCN as Vulnerable, Endangered or Near Threatened, though some populations, such as the Eurasian otter in western Europe, have recovered with cleaner rivers and legal protection.
