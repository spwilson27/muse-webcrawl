In logic, the law of noncontradiction (LNC; also called the principle of noncontradiction or principle of contradiction) states that contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time: formally, ¬(P ∧ ¬P) — "not (P and not-P)". It is one of the traditional three laws of thought, alongside the law of identity and the law of excluded middle.

History

Aristotle gave the classic defence of the principle in Book Gamma of the Metaphysics, calling it "the most certain of all principles". He held that it cannot be demonstrated directly, since all demonstration presupposes it, but that it can be defended elenctically: an opponent who says anything significant must already rely on words meaning one thing rather than its opposite. Avicenna, Aquinas and later scholastics preserved the principle as a foundation of metaphysics and reasoning. Leibniz and Kant treated versions of it as fundamental to analytic judgement.

Interpretations

The law can be read in three registers: ontological (the same attribute cannot belong and not belong to the same subject at the same time and in the same respect), doxastic (no one can rationally believe a contradiction), and semantic (a statement and its negation cannot both be true). In classical logic, accepting a contradiction leads to explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet): from P and ¬P, anything at all can be derived, making the system trivial.

Challenges

Some logicians and philosophers have questioned or restricted the law. Paraconsistent logics are formal systems in which contradictions can be locally tolerated without explosion, developed partly to handle paradoxes and inconsistent information. Dialetheists such as Graham Priest argue that a few contradictions — notably the liar paradox ("this sentence is false") — may actually be true. Heraclitus and Hegel are sometimes read as embracing a unity of opposites, though interpreters disagree on whether they denied the law itself. In mainstream logic and mathematics, however, the law of noncontradiction remains axiomatic.
