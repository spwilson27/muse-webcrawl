Catalan nationalism (Catalan: nacionalisme català) is a political movement asserting the national identity of Catalonia, grounded in its language, culture and medieval institutions. It ranges from demands for greater autonomy within Spain to outright independence, and it has shaped Catalan and Spanish politics since the late 19th century.

Origins

Modern Catalanism grew out of the 19th-century Renaixença, a revival of Catalan language and culture, and the federal republican ideas of Pi i Margall. Figures such as Valentí Almirall and Enric Prat de la Riba — whose La nacionalitat catalana (1906) became a foundational text — turned cultural revival into political organisation, culminating in the Mancomunitat (1914), a first modern Catalan self-governing body. The Republic of 1931 granted a Statute of Autonomy and restored the Generalitat under Francesc Macià and Lluís Companys.

Franco era and transition

After the Spanish Civil War, Franco's regime abolished Catalan institutions and suppressed the language; nationalism survived in exile and through cultural resistance. Following the transition to democracy, the 1979 Statute restored the Generalitat, and Jordi Pujol's Convergència i Unió governed Catalonia for over two decades on an autonomist programme.

Independence movement

Disputes over the 2006 Statute (cut back by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2010) and the fiscal crisis fuelled mass pro-independence mobilisation from 2012, led by parties such as ERC and by civic groups (ANC, Òmnium Cultural). An unofficial referendum was held in 2014; an independence referendum on 1 October 2017, declared illegal by Spanish courts and disrupted by police action, was followed by a unilateral declaration of independence, the imposition of direct rule, and the trial and imprisonment (later pardons) of Catalan leaders. The movement remains a central, unresolved question in Spanish politics.
