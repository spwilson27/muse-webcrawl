Leonin

Léonin (also Leoninus, Latinised as Magister Leoninus; fl. c. 1150s – c. 1201) was a French composer of polyphonic music associated with the cathedral of Notre Dame in Paris. He is credited — principally on the testimony of the later English theorist known as Anonymous IV — with compiling the Magnus liber organi ("Great Book of Organum"), the foundational repertory of the Notre Dame school of polyphony.

Life

Almost nothing is known of Léonin's life beyond Anonymous IV's treatise, written more than a century later, which names "Master Leonin" as the best composer of organum (optimus organista) and says he made the great book of organum used in the choir of Notre Dame. He may have been a canon of the cathedral or of the collegiate church of Saint-Victor. Modern scholars generally place his activity in the second half of the 12th century.

Music

The Magnus liber contained two-voice organa — settings of the solo portions of liturgical chant (responsorial chants for Mass and Office) for the feasts of the church year. In Léonin-style organum, the chant (tenor) is sung in very long notes while the upper voice (duplum) weaves elaborate melismas above it (organum purum); in contrasting sections called discant, both voices move in measured rhythmic patterns (the rhythmic modes). The repertory survives principally in two later manuscripts, known by their sigla W1 (Wolfenbüttel) and F (Florence), and attributions of individual pieces remain debated.

Anonymous IV also relates that Pérotin — "the best composer of discant" — revised and expanded Léonin's book, adding three- and four-voice organa and many clausulae. Léonin thus stands at the head of the first well-documented tradition of composed polyphony in Western music.
