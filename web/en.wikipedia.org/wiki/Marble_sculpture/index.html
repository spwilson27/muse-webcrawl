Marble sculpture

Marble sculpture is the art of creating three-dimensional forms from marble, a metamorphosed limestone prized for its whiteness, softness relative to other stones, slight translucency and ability to take fine detail. Marble has been the prestige material of Western sculpture since classical antiquity, when Greek and Roman sculptors exploited the fine marbles of Paros, Pentelicon and later Carrara.

Properties of the material

Marble is comparatively soft when freshly quarried and hardens on exposure, making it workable with chisels, rasps and drills while holding crisp edges. Its slight translucency at shallow depths resembles human skin, a quality sculptors have exploited for flesh. Against these virtues, marble weathers outdoors, is heavy and brittle in thin projections, and flaws or veins in a block can ruin a work late in carving.

Technique

The sculptor typically works from a full-size model in clay or plaster, transferring measurements to the block by pointing. Carving proceeds from roughing out with a point chisel, through toothed and flat chisels and rasps, to abrasion with pumice and polishing. Michelangelo, who favoured Carrara marble, famously described liberating the figure he saw within the block. Renaissance and Neoclassical workshops, such as that of Antonio Canova, delegated much carving to assistants, the master finishing the surface.

History

Marble sculpture flourished in archaic and classical Greece (the Parthenon sculptures), in Rome — whose copies preserve many lost Greek bronzes — and again from the Italian Renaissance (Donatello, Michelangelo's David and Pietà) through Bernini's Baroque virtuosity and the Neoclassicism of Canova and Thorvaldsen. In South Asia, white marble was used for Jain temples and Mughal inlay work such as the Taj Mahal. Modern sculptors from Rodin to Henry Moore continued to carve marble alongside newer media.
