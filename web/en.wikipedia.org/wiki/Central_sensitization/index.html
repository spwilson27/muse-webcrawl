Central sensitization

Central sensitization is an enhancement of the responsiveness of neurons within the central nervous system — particularly in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord — so that normal or sub-threshold sensory inputs generate exaggerated pain responses. Neuroscientist Clifford Woolf, who formalised the concept in the 1980s, described it as a form of activity-dependent synaptic plasticity in the pain system.

Mechanisms

Intense or repeated nociceptive input releases glutamate, substance P and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the dorsal horn. Activation of NMDA receptors removes their magnesium block, increasing excitability; microglia and astrocytes contribute through cytokine signalling. The results include expanded receptive fields, allodynia (pain from innocuous stimuli) and secondary hyperalgesia in uninjured tissue.

Clinical significance

Central sensitization is thought to underlie the persistence of pain in conditions such as fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, temporomandibular disorders, chronic low back pain and post-surgical pain. It helps explain why pain can persist after tissues have healed and why conventional anti-inflammatory drugs may fail.

Treatment approaches

Management may include gabapentinoids, serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, exercise, sleep restoration and cognitive behavioural therapy aimed at reducing central amplification rather than peripheral input alone.
