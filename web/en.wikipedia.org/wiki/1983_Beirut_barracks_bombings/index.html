The 1983 Beirut barracks bombings were twin suicide truck bombings on October 23, 1983, against the barracks of the Multinational Force in Lebanon. A truck laden with explosives equivalent to some 12,000 pounds of TNT detonated at the headquarters of the 1st Battalion, 8th Marines at Beirut International Airport, killing 241 American servicemen—the deadliest single day for the U.S. Marine Corps since Iwo Jima. Minutes later, a second truck destroyed the French paratroopers' barracks at the Drakkar building, killing 58 French soldiers.

Background

The Multinational Force—American, French, Italian, and British troops—had deployed to Beirut in 1982 during the Lebanese Civil War, initially to oversee the PLO evacuation and then as a peacekeeping presence. By late 1983 the Americans were increasingly perceived as combatants supporting the Lebanese government, and U.S. naval gunfire had struck Druze and Syrian positions.

The attacks

At 6:22 a.m., a yellow Mercedes-Benz truck circled the airport parking lot, drove through barriers and concertina wire, and detonated in the lobby of the Battalion Landing Team headquarters, collapsing the four-story building. Rules of engagement left the guards' weapons unloaded, preventing effective fire on the truck. The French barracks was destroyed by a second bomber moments later. Responsibility was claimed by the Islamic Jihad Organization; U.S. courts later found Hezbollah, backed by Iran, responsible.

Aftermath

The bombings led to the withdrawal of the Multinational Force from Lebanon by early 1984 and a lasting debate in U.S. policy—the "Powell Doctrine" emphasis on overwhelming force and clear objectives was shaped in part by the experience. Investigations criticized the chain of command for concentrating troops in a single vulnerable building with inadequate perimeter security.
