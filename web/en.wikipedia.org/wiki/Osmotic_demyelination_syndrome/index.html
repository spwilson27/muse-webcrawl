Osmotic demyelination syndrome

Osmotic demyelination syndrome

Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) is a neurological disorder caused by severe damage to the myelin sheaths of nerve cells in the brain, most often as a complication of overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia (low blood sodium). When the pons is affected it is called central pontine myelinolysis; lesions outside the pons, in regions such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebellum, are termed extrapontine myelinolysis. The two frequently occur together.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically appear several days after the sodium has been corrected, giving a characteristic biphasic course: the patient first improves as the hyponatremia is treated, then deteriorates. Manifestations depend on the structures involved and may include dysarthria and dysphagia from pseudobulbar palsy, spastic quadriparesis, horizontal gaze palsies, behavioural change, movement disorders resembling parkinsonism, and in severe cases a locked-in syndrome or coma.

Cause and pathophysiology

In chronic hyponatremia the brain adapts by extruding organic osmolytes from its cells. If the serum sodium is then raised too quickly, water leaves brain cells faster than the osmolytes can be reaccumulated, injuring oligodendrocytes and stripping myelin from axons while relatively sparing the axons themselves. Alcoholism, malnutrition, liver transplantation, and low potassium all increase susceptibility. To reduce risk, guidelines generally recommend that chronic hyponatremia be corrected by no more than about 8–10 mmol/L in any 24-hour period, and less in high-risk patients.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the clinical history of rapid sodium shifts together with magnetic resonance imaging, which shows symmetric T2 hyperintensity in the central pons — sometimes described as a trident or bat-wing shape — with sparing of the periphery. Imaging changes may lag behind the clinical picture by days to weeks.

Treatment and prognosis

There is no established cure; management is supportive. If recognised very early, deliberately re-lowering the sodium with hypotonic fluids and desmopressin may limit injury. Historically the condition was regarded as usually fatal or devastating, but modern series show that many patients recover substantially, sometimes almost completely, with rehabilitation.

History

Central pontine myelinolysis was first described by Raymond Adams and colleagues in 1959 in malnourished and alcoholic patients; its link to rapid correction of hyponatremia was recognised in the 1970s and 1980s, after which preventive correction limits entered routine practice.
