Red River Resistance

The Red River Resistance (also known as the Red River Rebellion, the Red River Uprising, or the First Riel Rebellion) was the sequence of events that led up to the 1869 establishment of a provisional government by Métis leader Louis Riel and his followers at the Red River Colony, in the early stages of the creation of the Canadian province of Manitoba. Earlier, it had been a territory called Rupert's Land under the control of the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) and was administered by the HBC's Council of Assiniboia.

The Canadian government had arranged the transfer of Rupert's Land from the HBC in 1869 without consulting the colony's largely Métis population. When surveyors arrived and the appointed lieutenant-governor, William McDougall, attempted to enter, Riel's National Committee of the Métis barred his way. The Métis seized Upper Fort Garry, drew up a List of Rights, and in December 1869 proclaimed a provisional government to negotiate terms of union directly with Ottawa.

Course of events

Attempting to rally support among English-speaking settlers and to counter a small armed "Canadian party" centred on John Christian Schultz, the provisional government imprisoned several opponents. One of them, Thomas Scott, an Orangeman from Ontario, was tried by court martial for insubordination and executed by firing squad on 4 March 1870 — an act that inflamed Protestant Ontario and made Riel a wanted man. Negotiations nevertheless continued: Métis delegates reached Ottawa, and the result was the Manitoba Act, passed 12 May 1870, creating the small "postage-stamp province" of Manitoba with protections for French language, Catholic schools and 1.4 million acres reserved for Métis children.

Red River Expedition and aftermath

Ottawa dispatched a military expedition under Colonel Garnet Wolseley, ostensibly to assert authority against possible Fenian or American threats but widely understood as an instrument of reprisal. When the troops arrived in August 1870, Riel and his advisers fled across the border to the United States. Promised amnesty never fully materialised; Riel was twice elected to Parliament in exile, banished for five years in 1875, and eventually returned to lead the North-West Resistance of 1885, after which he was hanged for treason.

Terminology and legacy

The Manitoba Métis Federation and many historians prefer "Resistance" to "Rebellion", arguing that Riel's government resisted an imposed transfer rather than rebelling against a lawful authority, and that it kept order and negotiated Manitoba into Confederation. The events are foundational to Métis identity and to Manitoba's history; in 2019 Manitoba recognised Riel as the province's first premier in educational materials, and Louis Riel Day is a February provincial holiday.
