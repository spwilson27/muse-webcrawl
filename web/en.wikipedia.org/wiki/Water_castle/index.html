Water castle

A water castle (German: Wasserschloss or Wasserburg) is a castle or stately home whose site is entirely or largely surrounded by water, whether a natural body such as a lake or river or an artificial moat. Water castles are especially characteristic of the low-lying plains of northern Europe, where they are most common in Germany, the Low Countries, northern France and Scandinavia.

Characteristics

Unlike hilltop castles, water castles occupy lowland sites and rely on water defences instead of elevation. The surrounding water hindered attackers, undermined siege mining and protected timber foundations from rot. Many began as fortified medieval noble residences; from the Renaissance onward, numerous were rebuilt as comfortable, largely undefended palaces and manor houses while retaining their moats and island settings for prestige and beauty.

Examples

Notable water castles include Schloss Schwerin and Moritzburg in Germany, the moated manor houses of England such as Bodiam Castle and Ightham Mote, Château de Chenonceau spanning the River Cher in France, and Egeskov Castle in Denmark. In German usage a distinction is drawn between a Wasserschloss, an aristocratic residence, and a Wasserburg, a more strictly fortified site.
