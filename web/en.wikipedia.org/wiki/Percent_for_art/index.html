The percent-for-art principle is a public funding mechanism for public art under which a fixed percentage — typically one percent — of the construction cost of a public building or infrastructure project is reserved for the commissioning of artworks integrated into or placed at the site.

History

Ideas of earmarking a share of building budgets for art circulated in Europe from the early twentieth century, and Finland adopted recommendations in the 1930s. The first formal municipal ordinance is generally credited to Philadelphia, which in 1959 required one percent of the cost of city construction projects to be spent on art. The United States federal General Services Administration followed with its Art in Architecture programme (1963), reserving a half-percent of federal construction estimates.

Adoption

Percent-for-art schemes have been adopted by hundreds of cities and states in the United States and by governments in Europe and elsewhere, including Germany (Kunst am Bau), France (1% artistique, in place since 1951 for schools and later extended), Ireland, Australia and the United Kingdom (usually as planning policy rather than statute). Some jurisdictions apply the requirement to large private developments through zoning incentives or conditions.

Debate

Supporters argue the programmes democratise access to art, enliven public spaces and provide steady commissions for artists. Critics object that they can produce formulaic "plop art" — abstract sculpture dropped into plazas with little relation to its setting — and that earmarking is an inflexible use of public funds. Later programmes have responded by involving artists early in design teams and by funding temporary, functional or community-based works.
