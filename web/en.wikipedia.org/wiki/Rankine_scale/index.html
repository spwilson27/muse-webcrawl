Rankine scale

The Rankine scale (symbol °R) is an absolute scale of thermodynamic temperature named after the Scottish engineer and physicist William John Macquorn Rankine, who proposed it in 1859. Like the Kelvin scale, its zero point is absolute zero, but its degree size equals the Fahrenheit degree rather than the Celsius degree. It is thus the absolute counterpart of Fahrenheit, just as Kelvin is the absolute counterpart of Celsius.

Comparison of temperature scales

Definition and conversion

Temperatures on the Rankine scale are related to Fahrenheit by a simple offset: °R = °F + 459.67. Absolute zero is 0 °R (equal to −459.67 °F and 0 K); the freezing point of water is 491.67 °R; and the boiling point of water is 671.67 °R. Conversion to Kelvin is K = °R × 5/9. Intervals on the Rankine scale are identical in size to Fahrenheit intervals, so a temperature difference of 1 °R equals 1 °F.

Usage

The Rankine scale has been used in some fields of engineering in the United States, particularly in thermodynamics calculations involving steam tables, aerospace and older petroleum engineering, where Fahrenheit-based data were traditional. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology discourages its use in favour of Kelvin, and it is now uncommon outside legacy applications. William Rankine developed the scale as part of his pioneering work on thermodynamics and the theory of steam engines.
