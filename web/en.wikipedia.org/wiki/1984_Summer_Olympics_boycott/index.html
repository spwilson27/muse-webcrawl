1984 Summer Olympics boycott

The boycott of the 1984 Summer Olympics in Los Angeles involved fourteen Eastern Bloc countries and allies, led by the Soviet Union, which announced its non-participation on 8 May 1984. The boycott was widely seen as retaliation for the United States-led boycott of the 1980 Summer Olympics in Moscow over the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, although the Soviet authorities officially cited security concerns and alleged anti-Soviet sentiment in the United States.

The Los Angeles Games proceeded without the Soviet Union and most of its allies.

Background

In 1980, the United States under President Jimmy Carter led a boycott of the Moscow Olympics joined by more than sixty countries, protesting the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. When Los Angeles hosted the Games of 1984, relations between the superpowers remained strained, worsened by the Soviet downing of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 in September 1983 and the deployment of Pershing II missiles in Western Europe.

The boycott

The Soviet National Olympic Committee announced that Soviet athletes would not attend, citing concerns for their safety and accusing the US government of violating the Olympic Charter. Fourteen nations joined the boycott, including East Germany, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland, Cuba, Vietnam, Mongolia, Laos, Afghanistan, South Yemen, North Korea, Ethiopia and Angola. Notable exceptions among socialist states were Romania, which attended and finished second in the medal table, and China, which competed at a Summer Olympics for the first time since 1952. Yugoslavia also attended.

Friendship Games

The boycotting countries organised an alternative multi-sport event, the Friendship Games, held in July and August 1984 across nine countries, including athletics in Moscow and events in several Eastern Bloc capitals. In many sports the standards at the Friendship Games exceeded those in Los Angeles, and numerous world records were set.

Impact

Despite the boycott, the Los Angeles Games attracted a record 140 nations and were a commercial success, generating a substantial surplus. The absence of the Soviet and East German teams nonetheless depleted the fields in many events, particularly in women's athletics, weightlifting and gymnastics. The cycle of boycotts ended in 1988, when both superpowers competed at the Seoul Olympics.
