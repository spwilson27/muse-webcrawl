Plasmolysis

Plasmolysis is the process in which plant cells lose water when placed in a hypertonic solution, so that the protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall. It is the reverse of cytolysis, which can occur in animal cells when water rushes in. Because plant cells possess rigid walls, plasmolysis provides a classic demonstration of osmosis and of the role of the selectively permeable cell membrane.

Mechanism

When a plant cell is surrounded by a solution with a higher solute concentration (and therefore a lower water potential) than the cell sap, water leaves the vacuole and cytoplasm by osmosis. The vacuole shrinks and the plasma membrane pulls away from the wall, often remaining connected to it at points called Hechtian strands, where plasmodesmata pass through the wall. The cell becomes flaccid; in a tissue, widespread plasmolysis causes wilting.

Terminology

Incipient plasmolysis is the point at which about half the cells in a tissue have just begun to show separation of membrane from wall; the external solution at this point is approximately isotonic with the cell sap, which lets physiologists estimate a cell's osmotic potential. If the solution is replaced by pure water or a hypotonic solution before damage occurs, water re-enters and the protoplast re-expands against the wall — a reversal called deplasmolysis. Prolonged or severe plasmolysis kills the cell, since the membrane may tear or its structure be disrupted.

Significance

Plasmolysis is routinely studied in school and university laboratories using onion epidermis or Elodea leaves in salt or sugar solutions. In agriculture and food science the same osmotic principles underlie wilting, the preservation of food by salting and sugaring, and the effects of drought and soil salinity on crops.
