San rock art

San rock art is the rock paintings and engravings produced by the San peoples (Bushmen) and related hunter-gatherer groups of southern Africa, over a period spanning several thousand years. Found at tens of thousands of sites in caves, rock shelters, and on open boulders — from the Drakensberg and Maloti mountains to the Brandberg in Namibia and the Tsodilo Hills in Botswana — the paintings are famous for their fine-line technique and shaded polychrome depictions of animals, especially eland, and of human figures.

Technique and distribution

Paintings were made with mineral pigments — red and yellow ochres, white clay or gypsum, black charcoal or manganese — bound with blood, egg, or plant juices and applied with brushes of hair, feather, or reed. Engravings (petroglyphs), pecked or incised into rock surfaces, predominate in the drier interior. The oldest dated painted slabs, from Apollo 11 Cave in Namibia, are around 27,000 years old, and the tradition continued into the 19th century, when some San artists depicted horses, wagons, and colonial soldiers.

Interpretation

Once dismissed as simple hunting records or "art for art's sake", the paintings are now understood largely through San shamanism. Drawing on ethnographic records of San religion — including the 19th-century /Xam testimony collected in the Bleek and Lloyd archive — researchers such as David Lewis-Williams interpret much of the art as depicting the experiences of ritual specialists ("shamans") in trance dances: the eland, believed to possess supernatural potency, the sensation of transformation into animals, and entoptic (geometric) imagery associated with altered states. Trance-related motifs include figures bleeding from the nose, arms raised behind the back, and therianthropes (part-human, part-animal beings).
