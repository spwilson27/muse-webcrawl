The Ego and Its Own (German: Der Einzige und sein Eigentum, literally "The Unique and Its Property"; also rendered "The Individual and His Property") is an 1844 work of philosophy by Max Stirner, the pen name of Johann Kaspar Schmidt. A central text of individualist anarchism, it mounts a radical critique of every authority that claims to stand above the individual — God, the state, morality, humanity, law, and even liberal and socialist ideals.

Content

Stirner argues that human beings are ruled by "spooks" (also translated "fixed ideas" or "phantoms") — abstractions such as the State, the People, Morality, and Man himself — which individuals serve as if sacred. Against these he sets the Unique One (der Einzige), the ego that owns itself, treats all things as its property to use or discard, and recognizes no calling higher than its own self-enjoyment. Rather than the egoism of greed, Stirner advocates "ownness": a self liberated from fixed ideas, relating to the world in conscious egoism. Parts of the book survey human life as an allegory of world history (the ancients, the moderns, "the free"), and attack contemporaries including Feuerbach, Bruno Bauer, Proudhon, and the German communists.

Reception and influence

Published in Leipzig in late 1844, the book was promptly banned in several German states yet quickly acquired a scandalous fame. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels devoted the vast bulk of The German Ideology to refuting it ("Saint Max"). After decades of obscurity, it was revived in the 1890s, influencing individualist anarchists and, through later readings, existentialism and post-structuralist thought. Where Kropotkin's The Conquest of Bread built anarchism on mutual aid, Stirner's path to anarchism runs through the sovereign ego — the two are often read as the twin poles of the tradition.
