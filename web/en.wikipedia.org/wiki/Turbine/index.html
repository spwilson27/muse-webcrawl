Turbine

A turbine (from the Greek τύρβη, tyrbē, "turbulence") is a rotary mechanical device that extracts energy from a moving fluid and converts it into useful rotational work. The fluid — which may be water, steam, combustion gases or air — pushes against a set of blades mounted on a rotor, spinning it. Turbines are at the heart of most electricity generation worldwide and of jet propulsion.

Types

  • Steam turbines — driven by high-pressure steam, used in thermal, nuclear and geothermal power stations; pioneered commercially by Charles Parsons from 1884.
  • Gas turbines — driven by hot combustion gases; used in jet engines and gas-fired power plants, often in combined cycle with a steam turbine.
  • Water turbines — hydroelectric machines such as the Pelton, Francis and Kaplan designs, matched to different heads and flow rates.
  • Wind turbines — convert the kinetic energy of wind into electricity with large aerodynamic rotors.

Principles

Turbines are classified as impulse machines, in which the fluid's pressure drop occurs entirely in fixed nozzles and the jet strikes the rotor blades, or reaction machines, in which pressure drops across the moving blades as well, so the rotor is driven partly by reaction forces; most large machines combine both principles across stages. Efficiency depends on blade aerodynamics, stage count, sealing against leakage, and materials capable of withstanding extreme temperatures and centrifugal stresses — gas-turbine blades operate in gas hotter than the melting point of their alloys, protected by ceramic coatings and internal cooling passages.
