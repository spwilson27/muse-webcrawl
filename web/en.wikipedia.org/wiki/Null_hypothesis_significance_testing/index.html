Null hypothesis significance testing

Null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) is a method of statistical inference by which an observation is tested against a hypothesis of no effect or no difference (the null hypothesis) and, if the observation would be sufficiently unlikely under that hypothesis (as judged by a p-value falling below a pre-set significance level, usually 0.05), the null hypothesis is rejected in favour of an alternative hypothesis.


The sampling distribution under the null hypothesis, with the rejection region shaded.

Origins

Modern NHST is a hybrid of two historically distinct approaches: Ronald Fisher's significance testing, in which the p-value measures the strength of evidence against the null hypothesis, and Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson's hypothesis testing, in which a fixed significance level α controls the long-run Type I error rate and tests are chosen for their statistical power. The hybrid that became standard practice in psychology, medicine, and other sciences combines elements of both, a mixture neither Fisher nor Neyman and Pearson fully endorsed.

Procedure

The researcher states a null and an alternative hypothesis, chooses a test statistic and significance level, collects data, and computes the p-value — the probability, under the null hypothesis, of results at least as extreme as those observed. A result "significant at the 0.05 level" leads to rejection of the null; otherwise the null is not rejected (which is not the same as accepting it as true).

Criticism

NHST has been persistently criticised: the p-value is widely misinterpreted as the probability that the null hypothesis is true; the 0.05 threshold is arbitrary yet treated as a bright line; significance says nothing about effect size or practical importance; and the method encourages publication bias and "p-hacking." The American Statistical Association issued a formal statement on p-values in 2016, and some journals have banned significance testing outright, promoting instead confidence intervals, effect sizes, Bayesian methods, and replication.
