Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (BGB), the German Civil Code, is the principal code of private law in Germany, in force since 1 January 1900. It is the archetype of the Pandectist style of codification: five books — General Part (legal persons, acts, limitation), Obligations, Property, Family Law and Succession — built on abstract concepts and general clauses applied across fields, and it influenced codes from Japan and China (Republican era) to Brazil, Greece and Portugal.

The BGB was the product of two commissions and prolonged debate between Romanist and Germanist jurists after German unification (1871); critics from the start charged it with abstraction remote from social realities (Otto von Gierke) and with bourgeois individualism (Anton Menger's Civil Law and the Propertyless Classes, 1890). It survived Empire, Republic, Nazism (which planned but never enacted a replacement), division and reunification, with major amendments including the 2002 modernisation of the law of obligations implementing European consumer law. Its general clauses — good faith (Treu und Glauben, §242), public policy (§138), delict (§823) — gave courts the tools to adapt it across a century of change.
