Ergative–absolutive language

An ergative–absolutive language, or simply ergative language, is one that treats the subject of an intransitive verb and the object of a transitive verb in the same way grammatically (the absolutive case), while marking the subject of a transitive verb distinctly (the ergative case). This contrasts with nominative–accusative languages such as English, which group the two kinds of subject together.

Examples

Ergative alignment is found in languages including Basque, many Caucasian languages (such as Georgian, with split ergativity), Kurdish varieties, Inuit languages, Mayan languages, many Australian Aboriginal languages (such as Dyirbal), and Sumerian. Hindi and other Indo-Aryan languages show split ergativity, using ergative marking only in perfective aspect.

Features

Ergative languages often lack a passive voice, instead using an antipassive construction that demotes the object. Morphological ergativity (in case marking) does not always coincide with syntactic ergativity (in coordination and subordination); in Dyirbal, famously, syntax works on an ergative basis as well, while most morphologically ergative languages are syntactically accusative.
