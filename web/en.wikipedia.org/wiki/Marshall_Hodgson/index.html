Marshall Goodwin Simms Hodgson (April 11, 1922 – June 10, 1968) was an American historian of Islam and world history, a professor at the University of Chicago. His posthumously published three-volume work The Venture of Islam: Conscience and History in a World Civilization (1974) is considered a landmark of Islamic historiography, and he coined the influential term "Islamicate" to refer to cultural phenomena associated with Islam but not strictly religious in character.

Life

Hodgson was raised a Quaker and was a conscientious objector during the Second World War, performing civilian public service. He studied at the University of Chicago, joined its faculty in 1949, and chaired the Committee on Social Thought. His early scholarship included The Order of Assassins (1955), a study of the Nizari Ismailis. He died suddenly in 1968, aged 46, leaving his magnum opus in manuscript.

The Venture of Islam

Edited by Reuben Smith and published by the University of Chicago Press in 1974, The Venture of Islam reinterpreted Islamic history within a world-historical frame, rejecting what Hodgson saw as Western-centric "impact-and-response" narratives. He proposed a rich new vocabulary: "Islamicate" for the culture of Islamdom beyond strictly religious matters, "Islamdom" for the society in which Islam predominates, and "Persianate" for the Persian-influenced cultural zone. His essays on world history, collected in Rethinking World History (1993), anticipated many themes of later global history.
