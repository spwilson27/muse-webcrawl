Sound card

A sound card (also known as an audio card) is an internal expansion card that provides input and output of audio signals to and from a computer under the control of computer programs. The term sound card is also applied to external audio interfaces used for professional audio applications.

Sound functionality can also be integrated onto the motherboard, using components similar to those found on plug-in cards. The integrated sound system is often still referred to as a sound card. Sound processing devices (audio DSPs and DACs) are also found on modern graphics cards with HDMI output, to allow audio output along with video.

History

Early personal computers such as the IBM PC produced sound only through a simple internal speaker capable of square-wave beeps. Through the late 1980s, add-on cards such as the AdLib Music Synthesizer Card (1987) and Creative Labs' Sound Blaster (1989) brought FM synthesis and digital sampled audio to home computers. The Sound Blaster line became a de facto standard for DOS gaming, and competing products from Gravis, Media Vision, and others followed.

During the 1990s, standards such as Microsoft's AC'97 (1997) encouraged manufacturers to integrate audio codecs directly onto motherboards. The Intel High Definition Audio (HD Audio) specification of 2004 improved on this with higher sample rates, more channels, and better jack sensing. As integrated audio improved, the market for discrete sound cards contracted to enthusiasts, audiophiles, and professional users, though USB audio interfaces became popular for music production.

Characteristics

Channels and connectors

Most sound cards support stereo output, while higher-end models provide 5.1 or 7.1 surround sound. Connections are typically made with 3.5 mm TRS jacks, colour-coded according to the PC System Design Guide: green for line out, pink for microphone input, and blue for line in. Digital connections may include S/PDIF optical or coaxial outputs.

Signal processing

A sound card contains a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) that turns digital audio data into an analog signal for speakers or headphones, and an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) for recording. Key quality measures include signal-to-noise ratio and total harmonic distortion. Some cards include a digital signal processor (DSP) that can apply effects such as reverberation, chorus, and environmental audio extensions like Creative's EAX.

Synthesis

Older cards generated music using FM synthesis, in which operators modulate simple waveforms to create complex timbres, or wavetable synthesis, which plays back recorded samples of real instruments. Modern systems generally perform synthesis in software.

Integrated sound

Nearly all modern desktop and laptop motherboards include integrated audio, most commonly based on codecs from Realtek. Integrated solutions share system resources and rely on the CPU for much processing, but for typical listening and communication they are considered adequate by most users. Discrete cards remain relevant where low noise floors, high output power for demanding headphones, or specialised inputs are required.
