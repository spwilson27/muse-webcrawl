Low German is a West Germanic language variety, or group of varieties, spoken mainly in Northern Germany, the eastern Netherlands and historically in parts of Poland and the Baltic region. It descends from Old Saxon and its Middle Low German form was the dominant written language of the Hanseatic League and the lingua franca of trade in northern Europe for several centuries.

Classification and features

Low German did not take part in the High German consonant shift that distinguishes High German; hence Low German maken corresponds to German machen ("to make"), Dorp to Dorf ("village"), and Water to Wasser ("water"). Its closest relative is Low Franconian (including Dutch). Major dialect groups include Westphalian, Eastphalian, Northern Low Saxon, East Low German (including Mecklenburgish and Brandenburgish), and the Low Saxon varieties of the eastern Netherlands such as Gronings, Drents, Twents and Achterhoeks.

History and status

From the 16th century, the rise of Standard (High) German as the language of print, administration and education progressively displaced Low German, which today survives chiefly as a spoken regional language, maintained in everyday life by a shrinking number of speakers — commonly estimated in the low millions in Germany, with fewer than a few hundred thousand fully fluent. It is recognised in Germany and the Netherlands as a regional language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and there is an active literature, theatre and media scene working to preserve it.
