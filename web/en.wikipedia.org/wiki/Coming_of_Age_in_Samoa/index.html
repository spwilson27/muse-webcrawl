Coming of Age in Samoa is a book by American anthropologist Margaret Mead published in 1928 by William Morrow and Company. The book is based on her research and study of youth — adolescent girls — on the island of Ta'ū in the Samoan Islands. The book details the sexual life of teenagers in Samoan society in the early 20th century, and theorizes that culture has a leading influence on psychosexual development.

Background

Mead, a student of Franz Boas at Columbia University, undertook the fieldwork in 1925–1926 at the age of 23. Boas had asked her to investigate whether adolescence was necessarily a time of emotional and psychological stress, as the theories of G. Stanley Hall suggested, or whether such turmoil was culturally produced. Mead reported that Samoan adolescents passed smoothly into adulthood, without the anxiety attributed to adolescents in the United States, and attributed the difference to cultural patterns rather than biology.

Controversy

In 1983, New Zealand anthropologist Derek Freeman published Margaret Mead and Samoa: The Making and Unmaking of an Anthropological Myth, arguing that Mead had been misled by her informants and that Samoan society was in fact competitive, violent, and sexually restrictive. The ensuing "Mead–Freeman controversy" became one of the most famous disputes in anthropology; later scholars, including Paul Shankman, have defended much of Mead's account while noting errors on both sides.
