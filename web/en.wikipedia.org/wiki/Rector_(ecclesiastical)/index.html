Rector (ecclesiastical)

This article is about the ecclesiastical office. For the academic office, see Rector (academia).

A rector is, in certain Christian denominations, a cleric who functions as an administrative leader in some type of parish. The term originates from Latin regere, "to rule". Its meaning varies between churches and historical periods.

Church of England

Historically in the Church of England, a rector was the incumbent of a parish who was entitled to receive all the tithes of the parish, in contrast to a vicar, who received only the "small tithes" while the "great tithes" went to an impropriator (often a monastery or lay rector) who in return supported the vicar. A rector also bore responsibility for the repair of the chancel of the parish church. With the abolition of tithes and the reorganisation of benefices, the distinction between rectors and vicars largely lost financial significance, and in modern team ministries a "team rector" leads a group of parishes served by team vicars.

Roman Catholic Church

In the Roman Catholic Church, a rector is the priest in charge of a church that is not a parish church — for example a cathedral (where the rector serves under the bishop), a basilica, a shrine or a seminary church. Canon law also speaks of the rector of a seminary or a Catholic university. A "rector major" is the head of certain religious orders and pontifical institutions, such as the Salesians.

Episcopal Church (United States)

In the Episcopal Church, the rector is the priest elected by the vestry, with the consent of the bishop, to lead a self-supporting parish, in contrast to a vicar, who leads a mission congregation supported by the diocese. This usage derives from the Church of England but carries a distinct canonical meaning.

Other uses

In some Lutheran and other traditions "rector" has been used for the head of a church-run school or institution. In Scotland, the parish minister of certain historic charges also bore the title historically.
