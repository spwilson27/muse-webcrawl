Historiography is the study of the methods of historians in developing history as an academic discipline, and by extension the body of historical work on a particular subject. The historiography of a specific topic covers how historians have studied that topic using particular sources, techniques, and theoretical approaches.

Definitions

The term can refer to (1) how history has been produced — the narrative, interpretations, worldview, use of evidence and presentation of a body of work — and (2) the study of that production itself. Furay and Salevouris define historiography as "the study of the way history has been and is written — the history of historical writing".

Ancient historiography

In the Western tradition, Herodotus (c. 484–425 BC) is called the "father of history" for his Histories of the Greco-Persian Wars, while his contemporary Thucydides wrote a more analytical, source-critical account of the Peloponnesian War. In China, Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BC) set the model for dynastic history with the Records of the Grand Historian. In the Islamic world, Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah (1377) proposed a critical, causal science of history.

Modern approaches

From the 19th century, Leopold von Ranke and the German seminar tradition professionalised history around archival evidence and the ideal of showing the past "as it actually was" (wie es eigentlich gewesen). The 20th century broadened the field: the French Annales school emphasised long-term structures and social and economic history; Marxist historiography stressed class and material forces; later movements added women's history, oral history, microhistory, postcolonial and global history, and quantitative cliometrics.
