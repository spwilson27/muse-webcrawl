Small Solar System body

A small Solar System body (SSSB) is an object in the Solar System that is neither a planet, nor a dwarf planet, nor a natural satellite. The term was first defined in 2006 by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) in the resolution that also reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet:

All other objects, except satellites, orbiting the Sun shall be referred to collectively as "Small Solar System Bodies".

Scope of the definition

The category includes most Solar System asteroids, most trans-Neptunian objects, all comets, and many meteoroids. In current usage it generally covers the minor planets that are not dwarf planets, centaurs, Kuiper belt objects that fall short of dwarf planet status, trojans, near-Earth objects, and the nuclei of comets. The largest small Solar System bodies include objects such as the asteroid Pallas and Vesta, and the trans-Neptunian object Orcus.

Distinction from dwarf planets

The boundary between a dwarf planet and a small Solar System body is whether the object has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape. Most small bodies are irregular rubble piles or fractured fragments far below this threshold.

Orbits and composition

Small Solar System bodies occupy nearly every region of the Solar System: the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, the Jupiter trojans, the Kuiper belt, the scattered disc, and the spherical Oort cloud. They range from rocky, metal-rich material in the inner system to ice-rich bodies in the outer system, providing samples of the primordial material from which the planets formed.
