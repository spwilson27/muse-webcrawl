Adalimumab, sold under the brand name Humira among others, is a monoclonal antibody medication used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, plaque psoriasis, hidradenitis suppurativa, uveitis, and juvenile idiopathic arthritis. It works by binding and neutralising tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), a key inflammatory cytokine. For many years it was the world's best-selling drug, with annual sales exceeding US$20 billion at its peak.

Medical uses

Adalimumab is given by subcutaneous injection, typically every other week, often self-administered with an auto-injector pen. In rheumatoid arthritis it slows joint damage, usually in combination with methotrexate; in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis it can induce and maintain remission; in plaque psoriasis it clears skin lesions in a large proportion of patients.

Adverse effects

By suppressing TNF, adalimumab increases the risk of serious infections, including reactivation of latent tuberculosis and invasive fungal infections, and it carries a boxed warning for these and for lymphoma and other malignancies observed in treated children and adolescents. Other common side effects include injection-site reactions, headache, and rash. Live vaccines should be avoided during treatment.

History and market

Adalimumab was developed through a collaboration involving BASF's Knoll Pharmaceuticals and Cambridge Antibody Technology, using phage display to produce a fully human antibody; it was approved in the United States in December 2002 and acquired by Abbott Laboratories, whose pharmaceutical division became AbbVie in 2013. Humira became the highest-grossing pharmaceutical product in the world. European biosimilars launched in 2018 and United States biosimilars in 2023, ending its patent-protected exclusivity after years of litigation over AbbVie's large patent estate.
