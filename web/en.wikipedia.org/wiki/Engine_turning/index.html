Engine turning

Engine turning is a form of geometric ornamentation in which fine, precise patterns are cut into a material with a lathe equipped with special attachments. The resulting decorative finish — often called guilloché when applied to watch cases, jewellery and enamel work — consists of overlapping, mathematically regular lines that shimmer as the surface catches the light.

History

The technique flourished in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Ornamental turning lathes such as the rose engine, developed from earlier German and French machines, allowed craftsmen to cut complex rosettes and waves into wood, ivory and metal. Watchmakers — most famously Abraham-Louis Breguet — used engine-turned dials protected by translucent enamel, and the craft spread to snuff boxes, cigarette cases, fountain pens and banknotes.

Technique

A rose engine lathe rotates the workpiece against a cutting frame while shaped cams ("rosettes") displace the headstock in controlled patterns, producing sinusoidal or lobed cuts. Straight-line machines cut repeating parallel or basket-weave patterns, a finish familiar from the facia panels of vintage cars and aircraft, where large swirls produced by abrasive tools are also commonly called engine turning.

Uses

Beyond decoration, fine guilloché patterns serve a security function: their complexity and precision make them difficult to counterfeit, which is why engine-turned designs long appeared on paper currency, passports and certificates. Today a small number of artisans continue the craft on restored antique machinery, and the patterns are also reproduced by computer-controlled equipment.
