A hygrometer is an instrument used to measure the amount of water vapour in air, soil, or confined spaces. Humidity measurement instruments usually rely on measurements of some other quantity, such as temperature, pressure, mass, or a mechanical or electrical change in a substance as moisture is absorbed. By calibration and calculation, these measured quantities can lead to a measurement of humidity.

History

Simple hygrometers were described in the 15th century; Leonardo da Vinci sketched a design using a balance and absorbent material. In the 18th century, Horace Bénédict de Saussure built a hygrometer using a human hair under tension (1783), and Johann Heinrich Lambert coined the term "hygrometer." The wet-and-dry-bulb psychrometer was developed in the 19th century and remains a standard reference instrument.

Types

Mechanical hygrometers

Hair tension hygrometers exploit the fact that human or animal hair lengthens as humidity increases. Metal-paper coil hygrometers, common in inexpensive household instruments, use a paper strip coated with salt bonded to a metal coil that changes shape with humidity.

Psychrometers

A psychrometer comprises two thermometers, one with a dry bulb and one with a bulb kept moist by a wick (the wet bulb). Evaporation cools the wet bulb; the temperature difference, with ambient temperature, determines the relative humidity using psychrometric tables. In a sling psychrometer the thermometers are whirled through the air.

Electronic hygrometers

Modern electronic sensors measure changes in electrical capacitance or resistance of a polymer or metal oxide layer as it absorbs water vapour. Chilled-mirror dew point hygrometers detect condensation on a cooled mirror and are used as precision standards in meteorology and industry.

Applications

Hygrometers are used in weather stations, greenhouses, museums and archives (to protect artefacts), HVAC systems, industrial drying, and in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals and electronics, where humidity must be controlled.
