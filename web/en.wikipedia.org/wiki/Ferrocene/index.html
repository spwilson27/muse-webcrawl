Ferrocene

Ferrocene is an organometallic compound with the formula Fe(C5H5)2. It consists of an iron atom sandwiched between two cyclopentadienyl (Cp) rings — the prototypical metallocene and sandwich compound. It is an orange solid with a camphor-like odor that sublimes readily above 100 °C.

Discovery and significance

Ferrocene was discovered accidentally in 1951 at Duquesne University (and independently in Britain). Its unusual structure, proposed by Wilkinson, Fischer, and others, transformed organometallic chemistry: the bonding between the iron center and the aromatic Cp rings illustrated the 18-electron rule and opened the field of modern transition-metal organometallic chemistry, earning Fischer and Wilkinson the 1973 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Chemistry and uses

Ferrocene undergoes electrophilic substitution much like an aromatic compound and is readily oxidized to the blue ferrocenium ion, making the ferrocene/ferrocenium couple a standard electrochemical reference. Derivatives are used in catalysis, materials, and medicinal chemistry (e.g., ferroquine).
