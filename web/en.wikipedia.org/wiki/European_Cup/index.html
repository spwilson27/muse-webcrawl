The European Cup, formally the European Champion Clubs' Cup, was an annual association football competition organised by UEFA from 1955 to 1992, contested by the league champions of Europe's top national associations in a straight knockout format. In 1992 the competition was rebranded and reformatted as the UEFA Champions League, which retains the same trophy.

History

The tournament was championed by Gabriel Hanot, editor of the French sports daily L'Équipe, and inaugurated in the 1955–56 season with 16 clubs. Real Madrid, led by Alfredo Di Stéfano, won the first five editions (1956–1960), culminating in the famous 7–3 victory over Eintracht Frankfurt at Hampden Park in 1960, often called the greatest final of the era.

Subsequent decades brought dynasties and iconic winners: Benfica and Inter Milan in the 1960s; Ajax and Bayern Munich, each winning three in a row in the 1970s; Liverpool, Nottingham Forest, and Aston Villa during a run of English dominance (1977–1984); and AC Milan's back-to-back titles in 1989–1990. The era was also marked by tragedy at the 1985 final at Heysel Stadium in Brussels, where 39 spectators died, leading to a five-year ban on English clubs. Barcelona's "Dream Team" won the final European Cup under that name in 1992, beating Sampdoria at Wembley.

Format

Under the classic format, entrants were national league champions (plus the holders), playing two-legged knockout ties, with a single-match final at a neutral venue. The absence of group stages and seeding protections made early giant-killings a hallmark of the competition.
