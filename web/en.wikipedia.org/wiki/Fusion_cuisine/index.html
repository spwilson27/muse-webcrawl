Fusion cuisine

Fusion cuisine
OriginTerm popularised in the 1970s–1980s (France, United States)
Key figuresNorman Van Aken, Wolfgang Puck, Roy Yamaguchi, Peter Gordon

Fusion cuisine is a cuisine that combines elements of different culinary traditions that originate from different countries, regions, or cultures. Cuisines of this type are not categorised according to any one particular cuisine style and have played a part in innovations of many contemporary restaurant cuisines since the 1970s.

History and types

Although cooks have combined culinary traditions for centuries — through trade, colonisation and migration, as in Peruvian-Chinese chifa, Nikkei cuisine, Tex-Mex, and the creole cuisines of colonial port cities — "fusion" as a self-conscious movement began in the 1970s, when French chefs combined nouvelle cuisine techniques with Asian ingredients. It flourished in the United States in the 1980s and 1990s: Wolfgang Puck's Chinois on Main (opened 1983) became emblematic of East–West fusion, Norman Van Aken coined "New World cuisine" for his Florida fusions, and Roy Yamaguchi developed Hawaiian fusion cuisine.

Fusion can operate at several levels: combining ingredients or techniques from two traditions in one dish (sub-cuisine fusion), drawing an entire menu from several traditions, or creating a regional fusion cuisine over generations. The term "confusion cuisine" is sometimes used pejoratively for combinations critics consider ill-conceived. In recent decades, naturally evolved regional fusions and diaspora cooking have often been distinguished from chef-driven fusion, and chefs such as Peter Gordon have been credited with a more ingredient-led modern approach.

Examples

  • Asian fusion / Pacific Rim — combining East and Southeast Asian techniques with Western presentation.
  • Tex-Mex — Texas and Mexican border cooking.
  • Nikkei — Japanese-Peruvian cuisine.
  • Modern Australian — European techniques with Asian and native Australian ingredients.
