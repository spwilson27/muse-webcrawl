Angolan Civil War

The Angolan Civil War (Portuguese: Guerra Civil Angolana) was a major civil conflict in the Southern African country of Angola, beginning in 1975 and continuing, with interludes, until 2002. The war began immediately after Angola became independent from Portugal in November 1975, and was in part a power struggle between two former anti-colonial guerrilla movements, the communist People's Movement for the Liberation of Angola (MPLA) and the anti-communist National Union for the Total Independence of Angola (UNITA).

Background

Angola's independence followed the Carnation Revolution in Portugal and the end of the Portuguese Colonial War. Three liberation movements—the MPLA, UNITA, and the FNLA—had competed for power, and the Alvor Agreement's transitional government collapsed into fighting in 1975.

Foreign intervention

The war became a major Cold War proxy conflict. The MPLA government was supported by Cuba, which deployed tens of thousands of troops, and by the Soviet Union; UNITA was backed by the United States and by apartheid-era South Africa, whose forces intervened directly from Namibia. The FNLA, supported by Zaire and the United States, was defeated early in the war. Major engagements included the Battle of Cuito Cuanavale (1987–88), among the largest battles in Africa since the Second World War.

Peace attempts and end

The 1988 New York Accords led to Cuban withdrawal and Namibian independence, but UNITA rejected the results of the 1992 elections and fighting resumed, including the siege known as the "War of the Cities". The 1994 Lusaka Protocol also failed. The war finally ended in 2002 after UNITA leader Jonas Savimbi was killed in combat; a ceasefire followed and UNITA demobilised, becoming a political party.

Impact

Estimates suggest up to 800,000 people were killed and millions displaced. Landmines and destroyed infrastructure hampered Angola's recovery for decades, even as its oil wealth drove rapid economic growth after the war.
