Debye–Hückel theory

The Debye–Hückel theory is a theoretical model of electrolyte solutions proposed by Peter Debye and Erich Hückel in 1923. It explains why the activity coefficients of ions in dilute solution fall below the values expected for ideal behaviour, and yields a limiting law that matches experimental measurements of activity coefficients at low concentrations.

The model

The theory treats each ion as surrounded by an "ionic atmosphere" of opposite net charge: by electrostatic attraction, counter-ions cluster preferentially near a given ion, screening its charge. The characteristic screening distance is the Debye length. The solvent is treated as a continuous dielectric, the ions as point charges, and the charge distribution is described by combining the Poisson equation with the Boltzmann distribution.

The limiting law

The theory gives the mean ionic activity coefficient γ± as log10 γ± = −A |z+z−| √I, where I is the ionic strength of the solution and A is a constant depending on the solvent and temperature (about 0.51 for water at 25 °C). The extended Debye–Hückel equation adds an ion-size parameter to improve agreement at moderate concentrations. Because the approximations break down in concentrated solutions, later treatments such as the Pitzer equations are used there.
