New Red Sandstone

The New Red Sandstone is a chiefly British geological term for the beds of red sandstone and associated sedimentary rocks laid down in the Permian and Triassic periods. The name distinguishes these relatively young red beds from the older Old Red Sandstone of Devonian age. The rocks are typically brick-red in colour because their sand grains are coated with haematite (iron oxide), and they were deposited largely in arid, desert environments within the supercontinent of Pangaea.

Distribution and lithology

Outcrops stretch from southwest England (Devon and Somerset) through the English Midlands — including the Sherwood Sandstone, a major aquifer and building stone used in many cathedrals and civic buildings — to Cheshire, Cumbria, and parts of Scotland such as Dumfries and the Moray Firth coast. The succession includes dune-bedded sandstones such as the Penrith Sandstone, whose large-scale cross-bedding records wind-blown desert dunes; fluvial sandstones and conglomerates; and the mudstones and evaporites of the Mercia Mudstone Group, which include the rock salt deposits mined in Cheshire. Fossils are sparse compared with the Old Red Sandstone, but include reptile footprints (such as Chirotherium) and, in the Midlands, early dinosaurs and dicynodont remains.

History of the term

The term dates to the early days of stratigraphy in the nineteenth century, when William Smith's successors grouped the red beds above the Coal Measures as the "New Red Sandstone". Although superseded in formal lithostratigraphy by named groups and formations, it remains in wide use as a convenient collective term, and internationally the corresponding desert red-bed successions are a major reservoir rock for oil, gas, and groundwater.
