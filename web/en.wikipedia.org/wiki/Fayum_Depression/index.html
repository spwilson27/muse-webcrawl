Fayum Depression

The Fayum Depression (also spelled Faiyum) is a depression and oasis region located west of the Nile in Egypt, in the Faiyum Governorate, roughly 62 kilometres southwest of Cairo. Unlike most Egyptian oases, which are fed by groundwater, the Fayum is watered by Nile water brought in through a network of canals, principally the Bahr Yussef channel, which enters the depression through a gap in the desert ridge.

Geography

The depression covers an area of about 1,700 to 2,000 square kilometres and descends below sea level, reaching some 45 metres below sea level at Lake Qarun (ancient Lake Moeris), a saline lake of about 200 square kilometres that occupies the lowest part of the basin. The lake is fed by drainage water from the irrigated lands of the oasis and has no outlet.

History

The Fayum was extensively developed in the Middle Kingdom, when kings of the Twelfth Dynasty undertook major land-reclamation works that turned the region into one of Egypt's most productive agricultural areas. Under the Ptolemies the reclaimed land was settled with veterans and new towns, and the region is famous for the Fayum mummy portraits of the Roman period — naturalistic painted portraits attached to mummies. The depression is also a celebrated fossil site: the nearby Wadi El Hitan ("Valley of the Whales"), a UNESCO World Heritage Site, preserves abundant remains of early whales such as Basilosaurus, and the Fayum fossil beds have yielded important evidence for the evolution of primates and proboscideans.

Today

The Fayum is a densely populated agricultural region producing cotton, cereals, figs and grapes, and is a popular destination for its lake, waterfalls at Wadi El Rayan, and archaeological sites including the ancient city of Karanis.
