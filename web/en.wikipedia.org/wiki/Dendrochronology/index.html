Dendrochronology

Dendrochronology (or tree-ring dating) is the scientific method of dating tree rings (also called growth rings) to the exact year they were formed. As well as dating them, this can give data for dendroclimatology, the study of climate and atmospheric conditions during different periods in history from wood. Dendrochronology derives from the Ancient Greek dendron (δένδρον), meaning "tree", and chronos (χρόνος), meaning "time".

Growth rings in a cross-section of wood; each ring typically represents one year of growth.

Principles

Trees in temperate regions grow one ring per year, with ring width varying according to growing conditions — wide rings in favourable years, narrow rings in droughts or cold years. By matching (cross-dating) the pattern of ring widths in a sample of unknown age against a master chronology built from overlapping samples of known age, each ring can be assigned an exact calendar year.

History

The method was formalised in the early 20th century by astronomer A. E. Douglass at the University of Arizona, who founded the Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research in 1937. Douglass used tree rings to date archaeological timbers in the American Southwest and to study sunspot cycles.

Applications

  • Archaeology: dating timbers in buildings, ships, and artefacts.
  • Climatology: reconstructing past temperature and precipitation over thousands of years; bristlecone pine chronologies extend back more than 8,000 years.
  • Calibration: tree-ring dates are used to calibrate radiocarbon dating.
  • Ecology and forensics: dating fires, avalanches, and authenticating works of art and wooden instruments.
