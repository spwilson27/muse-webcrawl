Peristalsis

Peristalsis is the radially symmetrical contraction and relaxation of smooth muscle that propagates in a wave down a tubular organ, propelling its contents in one direction. In the human body it is best known as the mechanism that moves food through the oesophagus, stomach and intestines, but peristaltic movement also occurs in the ureters, fallopian tubes and other ducts.

Mechanism in the gut

In the gastrointestinal tract, a peristaltic wave consists of contraction of circular muscle behind a bolus and relaxation of the muscle ahead of it, coordinated by the enteric nervous system — the classic "law of the intestine" described by Bayliss and Starling. Distension of the gut wall triggers ascending excitation (via acetylcholine and substance P) and descending inhibition (via nitric oxide and VIP), so contents move from mouth to anus. In the small intestine, slow segmentation movements for mixing alternate with peristaltic rushes; between meals the migrating motor complex sweeps residual material through the tract.

Elsewhere in biology

Peristalsis moves urine from kidney to bladder along the ureters, propels ova in the fallopian tubes, and drives locomotion in earthworms, whose bodies advance by waves of contraction through hydrostatic segments. In engineering, the same principle is used in peristaltic pumps, which compress a flexible tube with rollers to move fluids without contaminating them.

Disorders

Impaired peristalsis underlies conditions such as achalasia of the oesophagus, gastroparesis, intestinal pseudo-obstruction and ileus, while exaggerated or retrograde movement contributes to vomiting and some forms of reflux. Loss of enteric neurons, as in Hirschsprung's disease, abolishes the reflex in the affected segment.
