Egrets are herons in the family Ardeidae, generally long-legged wading birds, that have white or buff plumage, developing fine plumes (usually milky white) during the breeding season. Egrets are not a biologically distinct group from herons and have the same build. The word "egret" comes from the French word aigrette that means both "silver heron" and "brush", referring to the long, filamentous feathers that seem to cascade down an egret's back during the breeding season (also called "aigrettes").

Biology

Like other herons, egrets are long-necked, long-legged waders of wetlands and shallow waters. They feed mainly on fish, frogs, crustaceans and insects, which they spear or snatch with a sharp, dagger-like bill, often standing motionless or stalking slowly before striking. Many species breed colonially in trees or reedbeds near water, building platform nests of sticks.

Species

Well-known species include the great egret (Ardea alba), a large, cosmopolitan species; the snowy egret (Egretta thula) of the Americas; the little egret (Egretta garzetta) of the Old World; and the cattle egret (Bubulcus ibis), which follows grazing animals and has spread naturally across much of the world. The reddish egret (Egretta rufescens) occurs in white and dark colour morphs.

Plume trade and conservation

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, egret breeding plumes were highly prized in the millinery trade, and the slaughter of birds at their nests brought species such as the snowy egret close to extinction in North America. Public outrage over the plume trade helped found early conservation organisations, including the National Audubon Society, and led to protective legislation such as the United States' Lacey Act (1900) and Migratory Bird Treaty Act (1918). Most egret populations subsequently recovered, although wetland loss remains a threat.
