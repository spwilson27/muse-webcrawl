House of Dunkeld

The House of Dunkeld (in Scottish Gaelic, Dùn Chailleann) is a historiographical and genealogical construct used to describe the succession of Scottish kings from Duncan I to Alexander III, ruling from 1034 to 1286. The dynasty takes its name from Dunkeld in Perthshire, an early ecclesiastical centre with which the family of Crínán, the lay abbot of Dunkeld, was associated.

Origins

The dynasty's founder in the female line was Bethóc, daughter of Malcolm II, who married Crínán of Dunkeld. Their son became Duncan I, who succeeded his grandfather Malcolm II in 1034 and was killed in 1040 by Macbeth, the subject of Shakespeare's tragedy. Duncan's sons Malcolm III ("Canmore") and Donald III restored the line after Macbeth's fall.

The Canmore kings

Under Malcolm III and his sons — Edgar, Alexander I and especially David I — Scotland underwent profound change. David I (reigned 1124–1153) promoted feudal institutions, founded burghs and monasteries, and extended royal authority, a transformation sometimes called the "Davidian Revolution". The later kings William the Lion, Alexander II and Alexander III consolidated the kingdom, which reached a high point of prosperity and stability under Alexander III.

End of the dynasty

The direct line ended with the death of Alexander III in 1286 and of his granddaughter and heir, Margaret, Maid of Norway, in 1290. The resulting succession crisis led to the Competitors' claims, Edward I of England's intervention, and ultimately the Wars of Scottish Independence, from which the House of Balliol, the House of Bruce and later the House of Stewart emerged.
