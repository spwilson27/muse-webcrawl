Saints Cyril and Methodius

Cyril (c. 827–869) and Methodius (c. 815–885) were Byzantine Greek brothers from Thessalonica who became missionaries to the Slavs. Known as the "Apostles to the Slavs", they created the Glagolitic alphabet, the first alphabet used for Slavic languages, and translated the Bible and liturgy into Old Church Slavonic.

Feast dayFebruary 14 (Roman Catholic), May 11 (Orthodox)
Known forGlagolitic alphabet, mission to Great Moravia
PatronageEurope, Slavic peoples

Mission to Moravia

In 862 Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia asked Emperor Michael III for missionaries who could teach in Slavonic. The brothers arrived in 863 with a written Slavonic liturgy, educating local clergy. Their work provoked conflict with Frankish clergy who insisted on Latin.

Rome and legacy

Summoned to Rome, they won Pope Adrian II's approval of the Slavonic liturgy. Cyril died in Rome in 869, taking the monastic name Cyril shortly before death (he was born Constantine). Methodius became archbishop of Moravia and Pannonia, endured imprisonment by Frankish bishops, and died in 885; his disciples, including Clement of Ohrid, spread Slavonic literacy to Bulgaria, where the Cyrillic script — named in Cyril's honour — was developed from Greek uncial with Glagolitic elements.

Veneration

Pope John Paul II named them co-patron saints of Europe in 1980. Their feast is a public holiday in several Slavic countries, and May 24 is celebrated as Slavonic Literature and Culture Day.
