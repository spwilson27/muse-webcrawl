Nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve

Nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve
Latinnervus laryngeus non recurrens
PrevalenceApproximately 0.3–1.6% (right side)

Overview

A nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve is an anatomical variant of the recurrent laryngeal nerve, a branch of the vagus nerve that supplies the muscles of the larynx. In the usual anatomy, the nerve loops under the subclavian artery on the right and the aortic arch on the left before ascending to the larynx; in the nonrecurrent variant, the nerve branches directly from the vagus in the neck and travels straight to the larynx.

Anatomy and embryology

The variant is almost always found on the right side and is associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery arising directly from the aortic arch (arteria lusoria). During embryonic development, the nerve normally is pulled down into the chest by the descending vessels; when the right subclavian artery develops abnormally, this descent does not occur and the nerve remains in the neck.

A true left-sided nonrecurrent nerve is extremely rare and is associated with situs inversus or a right-sided aortic arch.

Clinical significance

The nonrecurrent laryngeal nerve is of considerable importance in thyroid and parathyroid surgery, because its unexpected course places it at increased risk of injury during neck operations. Damage to the nerve can cause vocal cord paralysis and hoarseness. Surgeons may suspect the variant when preoperative imaging shows an aberrant right subclavian artery.
