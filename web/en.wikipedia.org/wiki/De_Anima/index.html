De Anima

De Anima (On the Soul)
Author: Aristotle
Language: Ancient Greek
Subject: Philosophy of mind, psychology
Date: c. 350 BC

De Anima (Greek: Περὶ Ψυχῆς, Perì Psychês; Latin: De Anima, "On the Soul") is a major treatise by Aristotle, written around 350 BC. In three books it develops his theory of the soul (psyche) as the form of a living body, and discusses nutrition, perception and intellect. It is among the most influential works in the history of the philosophy of mind and was a central text for medieval Islamic, Jewish and Christian philosophy.

Contents

Book I reviews the views of Aristotle's predecessors and raises difficulties. Book II gives the famous definition: the soul is "the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially" — the form, not a separate substance, of a living organism. Aristotle distinguishes grades of soul: the nutritive soul of plants, the sensitive soul of animals, and the rational soul of humans. He then treats the five senses individually. Book III discusses imagination (phantasia), thought, and the notoriously difficult doctrine of the "active intellect" (nous poietikos), which later commentators variously interpreted as a faculty of the individual mind, a separate divine intellect, or God.

Influence

The work was commented on by Alexander of Aphrodisias, Themistius, Averroes and Aquinas, among many others. Debates over the active intellect and the unity of the intellect shaped centuries of philosophy. Modern scholars continue to dispute whether Aristotle's account is best read as a kind of materialism, dualism, or a distinctive hylomorphic alternative to both.
