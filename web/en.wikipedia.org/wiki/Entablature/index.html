Entablature

An entablature is the horizontal structure supported by the columns of a classical building and supporting in turn the roof or pediment. In the architecture of ancient Greece and Rome, and in the classical traditions derived from them, the entablature is divided into three parts, from bottom to top: the architrave, the frieze and the cornice.

The three parts

  • The architrave is the lowest division, the beam that rests directly on the column capitals and spans between them. In the Doric order it is plain, capped by a band and small pegs (guttae) beneath the triglyphs above.
  • The frieze is the middle, often decorated band. In the Doric order it alternates triglyphs (grooved blocks said to imitate the ends of wooden beams) with metopes, which could be sculpted. In the Ionic order the frieze is a continuous band, often carrying relief sculpture, while in the Corinthian order it is similarly continuous.
  • The cornice is the projecting upper member, which throws rainwater clear of the building and crowns the composition; above it may sit the triangular pediment of the roof.

Proportions and use

The proportions of the entablature relative to the column height are a defining feature of each classical order: a Doric entablature is relatively heavy, about a quarter of the column's height, while Ionic and Corinthian entablatures are progressively lighter. Renaissance theorists such as Palladio codified these proportions, and the entablature became a standard element of classical and neoclassical buildings worldwide, from temples and basilicas to banks and railway stations. In simplified modern usage the word may refer to any beam-like horizontal band above columns or across a façade.
