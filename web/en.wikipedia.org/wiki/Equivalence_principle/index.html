Equivalence principle

Equivalence principle
StatementGravitational and inertial mass are equivalent; locally, gravity is indistinguishable from acceleration
Key figuresGalileo, Newton, Eötvös, Einstein
Test precisionParts in 1015 (MICROSCOPE satellite)

The equivalence principle is the hypothesis that the gravitational "force" experienced locally while standing on a massive body such as the Earth is the same as the pseudo-force experienced by an observer in a non-inertial (accelerated) frame of reference. It is the cornerstone of Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity.

Forms

The weak form states that the trajectory of a freely falling test body depends only on its initial position and velocity, not its composition — inertial and gravitational mass are equal. The Einstein form adds that local non-gravitational experiments in a freely falling frame give results independent of velocity and location. The strong form extends this to gravitational and self-gravitating experiments.

Tests

Galileo's (legendary) drop experiments, Newton's pendulums, Eötvös's torsion balance, lunar laser ranging and the MICROSCOPE satellite have verified the weak principle to extraordinary precision, finding no difference between materials such as titanium and platinum in free fall.
