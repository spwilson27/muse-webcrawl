Naming Commission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Naming Commission (Slovenian: Komisija za preimenovanja; officially the Commission for the Naming of Settlements and the Renaming of Streets, Squares and Buildings) was a body established in Slovenia after the Second World War by the new communist authorities, tasked with systematically reviewing and changing place-names and street-names across the republic.

Background

After 1945, the authorities of the People's Republic of Slovenia sought to remove names associated with the monarchy, the church, and the former regimes of Yugoslavia, Austria-Hungary, and Fascist Italy. In 1948, a law on the naming of settlements and the marking of streets, squares, and buildings provided the legal basis for a coordinated renaming campaign, supervised by a central commission working with local committees.

Work

The commission's most distinctive task was the removal of religious elements from Slovenian toponyms. Settlements whose names contained Sveti or Sveta ("Saint") were given new names — for example, Sveti Lovrenc became Lovrenc na Pohorju, Šentjur's earlier forms were adjusted, and Sveta Trojica v Slovenskih goricah lost its religious prefix in this period (the name was restored in the 1990s). In towns and cities, streets named after saints, kings, and Habsburg figures were renamed for partisans, national heroes, work, and the ideals of the new state, such as Titova cesta (Tito Street) in Ljubljana and Maribor.

The commission's decisions were implemented in several waves between 1948 and 1955, affecting hundreds of settlements and thousands of street names; official gazeteers and postal directories were revised accordingly.

Legacy

After Slovenia's independence in 1991, many pre-communist names were restored, including a number of "Saint" settlement names and historic street names in Ljubljana and other cities, though many partisan-era names remain in use. The work of the Naming Commission remains a frequently cited example of politically motivated toponymy — the use of place-naming as an instrument of ideology and collective memory.
