Sleep twitch

A sleep twitch is a small, brief, involuntary contraction of a muscle or group of muscles that occurs during sleep, especially during active (REM-like) sleep in infants. Twitches affect the face, limbs, fingers, and toes, and arise from activity in the sensorimotor system. In developmental neuroscience, sleep twitches — clinically classed as a form of myoclonus — are of special interest because they are abundant in early life and appear to help the developing nervous system learn about the body.

Developmental function

Research associated with Mark Blumberg and colleagues at the University of Iowa has shown that when an infant's limb twitches during sleep, the movement triggers bursts of activity ("reafference") in the corresponding somatosensory cortex and hippocampus. These feedback signals are thought to help calibrate spinal and cortical circuits and build body maps before the infant can move voluntarily with precision. Twitching declines with age as voluntary movement takes over.

Sleep twitches in adults

Adults also twitch occasionally in sleep. Distinct phenomena include hypnic jerks — the whole-body jolt sometimes felt while falling asleep — and periodic limb movements. Ordinary sleep twitches are benign; unusually frequent or violent movements can indicate disorders such as REM sleep behaviour disorder, in which the normal paralysis of dreaming sleep is lost.
