Anaphora (linguistics)

In linguistics, anaphora is the use of an expression whose interpretation depends on another expression that appears earlier (or, in some analyses, later) in the discourse. The dependent expression is called an anaphor — most commonly a pronoun — and the expression it depends on is its antecedent. For example, in "Susan dropped the plate and it shattered," the pronoun it is an anaphor whose antecedent is the plate.

Types

Several kinds of anaphoric expressions are distinguished. Pronominal anaphora involves pronouns ("he," "she," "they"). Reflexive anaphors such as "herself" are, in many languages, restricted to local antecedents. Noun-phrase anaphora includes expressions like "the former" or repeated descriptions, and verb-phrase anaphora covers forms like "do so." When the dependent expression precedes its antecedent, as in "When he arrived, John sat down," the relation is often called cataphora.

Binding theory

Within generative grammar, binding theory — developed by Noam Chomsky — states the structural conditions under which anaphors, pronouns, and referring expressions may or must be co-indexed with antecedents. Principle A requires a reflexive anaphor to be bound within its local domain, while Principle B requires ordinary pronouns to be free in that same domain, explaining contrasts such as "John saw himself" versus "*John saw him" (on the reading where him = John).

Anaphora resolution

In computational linguistics and natural language processing, anaphora resolution — a core part of coreference resolution — is the task of determining what an anaphor refers to. It remains a challenging problem because it draws on syntax, semantics, and world knowledge simultaneously.
