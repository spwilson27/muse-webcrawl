Segregated cycle facilities

Placeholder image: a physically separated cycle track in a city.

Segregated cycle facilities are cycling infrastructure, such as cycle tracks and protected bike lanes, that separate cyclists physically from motor vehicle traffic (and usually from pedestrians). Unlike painted bicycle lanes, which mark out space within the carriageway, segregated facilities use kerbs, bollards, medians, parked cars, or other barriers to keep motor vehicles out.

Rationale and debate

Physical separation is widely credited for the high cycling rates and low casualty rates in the Netherlands and Denmark, where extensive networks of separated tracks make cycling feel safe for riders of all ages. Advocates argue that perceived safety is the main barrier to cycling uptake and that segregation is essential on busy, fast roads. Some advocates of "vehicular cycling" have historically opposed segregation, arguing that cyclists are safer and better respected when integrated with traffic; the modern consensus in transport planning favours separation on high-traffic routes combined with junction designs that protect cyclists at intersections, where most conflicts occur.

Types

  • Cycle tracks at kerb height or intermediate level, parallel to a road.
  • Protected lanes at road level separated by bollards, wands, or parked vehicles.
  • Off-road paths and greenways entirely separate from the road network.
