The Johnson–Corey–Chaykovsky reaction (also called the Corey–Chaykovsky reaction) is a chemical reaction used in organic synthesis for the synthesis of epoxides, aziridines and cyclopropanes from carbonyl compounds, imines and enones respectively, using a sulfur ylide.

History

A. William Johnson reported in 1961 that sulfonium ylides react with carbonyl compounds to give epoxides. E. J. Corey and Michael Chaykovsky independently developed practical procedures in the early 1960s using dimethyloxosulfonium methylide and dimethylsulfonium methylide, generated from trimethylsulfoxonium iodide or trimethylsulfonium iodide with a strong base such as sodium hydride or butyllithium.

Mechanism and scope

The ylide carbon adds nucleophilically to the carbonyl group, forming a betaine intermediate; intramolecular displacement of dimethyl sulfide (or dimethyl sulfoxide) then closes the three-membered ring. With saturated aldehydes and ketones the product is an epoxide — effectively a one-carbon homologation of the carbonyl to a terminal epoxide, complementary to the Wittig reaction, which would give an alkene. With α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds, conjugate addition predominates and cyclopropanes result. Imines give aziridines by the analogous pathway.

Variants

The two classical reagents differ in reactivity: dimethylsulfonium methylide reacts faster and irreversibly, while the more stable dimethyloxosulfonium methylide adds reversibly, favouring cyclopropanation of enones. Catalytic and asymmetric versions employ chiral sulfides, and the reaction has been applied in numerous total syntheses of natural products.
