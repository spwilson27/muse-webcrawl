The Cotton Belt is a region of the Southern United States in which cotton was historically the predominant cash crop from the late 18th through the 20th century. In its classic extent it stretched in a broad crescent from Virginia and the Carolinas across Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Tennessee into Louisiana, Arkansas, and Texas, roughly bounded by the latitude beyond which the growing season was too short and, to the west, by the rainfall line beyond which irrigation was required.

History

The belt took shape after Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made short-staple upland cotton profitable to process, triggering a rapid expansion of cotton cultivation — "King Cotton" — across the Deep South. The industry rested on the labor of enslaved African Americans: the enslaved population and cotton output both soared through the domestic slave trade and the forced removal of Native American nations from the Southeast. By 1860 the United States produced about two-thirds of the world's cotton, and cotton accounted for more than half of U.S. export value.

After the Civil War, production recovered under sharecropping and tenant farming, which kept many Black families in debt-bound poverty. In the 20th century the belt was transformed by the boll weevil infestation (from the 1890s), New Deal acreage reductions, mechanization — the mechanical cotton picker spread after the 1940s — and the Great Migration of Black Southerners to northern and western cities. Much former cotton land shifted to soybeans, corn, peanuts, pine plantations, and pasture.

Legacy

The Cotton Belt closely overlaps the Black Belt, named for its dark, rich prairie soils (and later for its African American population), whose demographic and political character still reflects the plantation economy. Today the United States remains among the world's largest cotton exporters, but production is concentrated in Texas, the Mississippi Delta, and irrigated areas of the Southwest rather than the old belt as a whole.
