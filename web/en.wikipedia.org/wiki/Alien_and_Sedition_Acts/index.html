Alien and Sedition Acts

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Enacted1798, by the 5th United States Congress
Signed byPresident John Adams
ActsNaturalization Act; Alien Friends Act; Alien Enemies Act; Sedition Act

The Alien and Sedition Acts were a series of four laws passed by the U.S. Congress in 1798 amid widespread fear of war with France during the Quasi-War. Signed by President John Adams, they restricted immigration and speech critical of the government, and became one of the most controversial episodes of the early republic.

The four acts

Naturalization Act

The Naturalization Act of 1798 increased the residency requirement for American citizenship from five to fourteen years. It was aimed in part at French and Irish immigrants, who tended to support the Democratic-Republican opposition.

Alien Friends Act and Alien Enemies Act

The Alien Friends Act authorised the president to deport any non-citizen deemed dangerous in peacetime; the Alien Enemies Act, still part of U.S. law today, permitted the apprehension and deportation of resident aliens of a hostile nation during wartime. Neither was directed by Adams against individuals during his term, though the Enemies Act was later used in the War of 1812 and both World Wars — most infamously enabling the internment of Japanese Americans in the 1940s.

Sedition Act

The Sedition Act made it a crime to publish "false, scandalous and malicious writing" against the government or its officials. Under it, Democratic-Republican editors and even a congressman, Matthew Lyon, were prosecuted and jailed.

Reaction and legacy

Thomas Jefferson and James Madison answered with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798–99), which argued that the states could judge the constitutionality of federal laws — a landmark in the theory of states' rights. The Acts helped sweep the Federalists from power in the election of 1800. The Sedition Act expired in 1801, and Jefferson pardoned those convicted under it. Although the Supreme Court never ruled on the Sedition Act, later jurisprudence (notably New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 1964) treated it as condemned by history and inconsistent with the First Amendment.
