History of western fashion

The history of western fashion is the history of clothing styles in Europe and the European-influenced world from antiquity to the present. Fashion as a system of rapidly changing styles emerged in the late Middle Ages at the courts of Burgundy and France. Thereafter, silhouettes cycled between volume and slimness, ornament and restraint, driven by textile technology, court culture, industrialisation, mass media and, from the twentieth century, subcultures and globalised fast fashion.

Crinoline dresses of the 1860s exemplify fashion's cyclical exaggeration of silhouette.

Antiquity and the Middle Ages

Greco-Roman dress was draped (chiton, himation, toga), changing little over centuries. In the fourteenth century, fitted tailoring, buttons and the short doublet for men launched recognisably "fashionable" change; sumptuary laws attempted to reserve fabrics and furs by rank. Burgundian court dress — houppelandes, hennin headdresses — set fifteenth-century taste.

Renaissance to the eighteenth century

Renaissance courts favoured broad silhouettes, slashing and rich brocades; Spanish fashion imposed black, farthingales and ruffs. Baroque dress loosened into lace, ribbons and (for men) the cravat and justaucorps, precursors of the three-piece suit codified under Charles II of England. Eighteenth-century France under Louis XV and XVI epitomised luxury: sacque gowns, paniers, powdered wigs and, in reaction, Anglomania and simpler muslin chemise dresses associated with Marie Antoinette.

Nineteenth century: industrialisation

The sewing machine, synthetic dyes (mauveine, 1856) and department stores democratised fashion. Charles Frederick Worth founded haute couture in Paris (1858). Women's dress moved from crinolines to bustles to the S-bend corset; men's dress settled into the dark lounge suit. Reform movements and sport (bicycling) promoted rational dress and bloomers.

Twentieth century

Paul Poiret freed women from corsets; wartime utility and Chanel's jersey suits modernised dress. Dior's "New Look" (1947) restored hourglass luxury; youth culture then seized the initiative — miniskirts (Mary Quant), jeans, punk, hip-hop streetwear. Designers such as Yves Saint Laurent (Le Smoking, 1966) blurred gender lines. Ready-to-wear displaced couture; by the 1990s global brands and supermodels dominated.

Twenty-first century

Fast fashion (Zara, H&M, and later ultra-fast online retailers) accelerated trend cycles to weeks, raising sustainability concerns; social media influencers, resale and vintage platforms, and athleisure reshaped consumption, while fashion weeks in Paris, Milan, London and New York retain symbolic primacy.
