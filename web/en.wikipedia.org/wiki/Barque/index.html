Barque

Barque
TypeSailing vessel
RigThree or more masts: square-rigged except the aftermost (mizzen), which is fore-and-aft rigged

A barque or bark is a sailing vessel with three or more masts of which all except the aftermost (the mizzen) are square-rigged; the mizzen carries fore-and-aft sails. The rig combined the windward efficiency of square sails with a smaller crew requirement than a full-rigged ship, and became the workhorse rig of late nineteenth-century ocean commerce.

Development and use

Barques ranged from small coastal traders to steel-hulled giants of over 5,000 tons. The four-masted barque proved especially economical for long bulk voyages — grain from Australia, nitrates from Chile, timber from the Pacific Northwest — and remained competitive with steam on such routes into the early twentieth century through the era of the great "windjammers". Famous survivors include the Star of India (1863) and the training ships of the Åland shipping magnate Gustaf Erikson.

Distinctions

A vessel square-rigged on all masts is a full-rigged ship; a barquentine is square-rigged only on the foremast. In older usage "bark" described various small vessels, but the rig definition above was standard by the nineteenth century.
