West African monsoon


Summer monsoon convection over West Africa.

The West African monsoon is a seasonal weather system that brings rainfall to West Africa, especially the Sahel and the Guinean coast, during the northern hemisphere summer. It results from the interaction between warm, moist air flowing inland from the Gulf of Guinea in the tropical Atlantic and the hot, dry air of the Sahara, as the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) migrates northward over the continent.

Seasonal cycle

Rains typically begin along the coast in spring, advance northward with an abrupt "monsoon jump" in late June, and reach their northernmost extent in August before retreating southward in September and October. Much of the rainfall is delivered by large organised systems called mesoscale convective systems, often triggered by African easterly waves travelling westward across the region; these waves can also seed tropical cyclones in the Atlantic.

Variability and impacts

Monsoon rainfall varies greatly from year to year and decade to decade, influenced by sea surface temperatures in the Atlantic and Indian oceans and globally. The severe Sahel droughts of the 1970s and 1980s were linked to a weakened monsoon, with devastating consequences for agriculture and livelihoods. Because farming across the region is overwhelmingly rain-fed, predicting the monsoon's onset, intensity, and breaks is a major focus of climate research, including the AMMA (African Monsoon Multidisciplinary Analysis) programme.
