Otello is an opera in four acts by Giuseppe Verdi to an Italian libretto by Arrigo Boito, based on William Shakespeare's tragedy Othello. Premiered at La Scala, Milan, on 5 February 1887, it was Verdi's penultimate opera and is regarded, with Falstaff, as the summit of his late style and one of the greatest operas ever written.

Composition

After Aida (1871), Verdi had effectively retired from opera. His publisher Giulio Ricordi, keen to lure him back, arranged a collaboration with the composer-poet Arrigo Boito, whose respect for Shakespeare matched Verdi's own. Boito's libretto compressed the play, dropping Shakespeare's Venetian first act entirely, and invented Iago's "Credo in un Dio crudel", a monologue of nihilistic evil with no direct source in the play. Composition occupied Verdi through much of 1884–1886.

Synopsis

Otello, the Moorish governor of Cyprus in Venetian service, returns victorious from battle with the Turks. His ensign Iago, passed over for promotion, engineers the disgrace of the lieutenant Cassio and plants Desdemona's handkerchief to convince Otello that his wife has betrayed him with Cassio. Tormented by jealousy, Otello publicly humiliates Desdemona and then smothers her in their chamber. When Iago's plot is exposed by his wife Emilia — whom Iago kills — Otello, realising his error, stabs himself and dies beside Desdemona.

Music

The opera is through-composed, with the orchestra carrying a continuous symphonic argument against which set pieces — the opening storm chorus, the love duet closing Act 1, Desdemona's "Willow Song" and "Ave Maria" — emerge organically. The storm that opens the opera begins without overture, plunging the audience directly into the drama. Francesco Tamagno, the first Otello, defined the role's heroic tenor demands; Victor Maurel created Iago and Romilda Pantaleoni Desdemona.

Performance history

Otello was an immediate triumph and entered the international repertory at once. Twentieth-century interpreters such as Jon Vickers, Plácido Domingo, and Renata Tebaldi sustained its standing. Franco Zeffirelli directed a film version in 1986. The casting of the title role — Shakespeare's Moor — has in recent decades prompted opera houses to abandon the older practice of darkening the singer's skin.
