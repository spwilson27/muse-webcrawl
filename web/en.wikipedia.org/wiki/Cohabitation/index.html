Cohabitation

Cohabitation is an arrangement where people who are not legally married live together in a long-term, marriage-like relationship that includes cohabitation and often an emotional and/or sexually intimate relationship. Cohabiting couples may live together before marriage, in place of marriage, or after the end of a previous marriage; many have children together.

Trends

Cohabitation has increased dramatically in Western countries since the 1960s and 1970s, driven by changing social norms, secularisation, contraception, and delayed marriage. In Scandinavia, living together outside marriage — sambo in Swedish — has long been a mainstream, often permanent arrangement. In the United States, the number of cohabiting couples rose from under half a million in 1960 to many millions by the 2000s, and a majority of marriages are now preceded by cohabitation. Similar growth has occurred across Europe, Latin America (where consensual unions have a long history), and parts of Asia.

Legal status

The legal position of cohabitants varies widely. Some jurisdictions grant extensive rights through registered partnerships, domestic partnership laws, or recognition of common-law marriage; others treat cohabitants as legal strangers, especially in inheritance, property division on separation, and parental rights. France's PACS, Australia's de facto relationship law, and Scotland's statutory rights for cohabitants illustrate intermediate approaches. A widespread public misconception in England and Wales is that "common law marriage" confers rights; it does not.

Research findings

Demographers study how cohabitation relates to union stability, fertility, and child outcomes. Early research found that premarital cohabitation was associated with higher divorce risk (the "cohabitation effect"), though later studies attribute much of this to selection — people who cohabit differ in background and attitudes — and to the age at which couples begin living together. In societies where cohabitation is nearly universal, outcomes for children in long-term cohabiting families resemble those of married families more closely.

Religious and political views

Many religious traditions disapprove of cohabitation outside marriage, while others accept it as a stage of courtship. The spread of cohabitation has been a recurring topic in debates about family policy, marriage promotion, and the extension of legal rights to unmarried partners.
