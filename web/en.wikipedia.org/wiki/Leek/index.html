Leek

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bundle of leeks with white shanks and broad green flags]

The leek (Allium ampeloprasum var. porrum) is a vegetable of the onion family grown for its thick white-to-pale-green shank of tightly wrapped leaf bases — milder and sweeter than the onion, indispensable to the cooking of northern Europe. It is the national emblem of Wales: legend says Saint David told Welsh soldiers to wear leeks into battle against the Saxons so they would know friend from foe, and the leek is worn still on Saint David's Day (and brandished, in Shakespeare's Henry V, by the magnificently leek-proud Fluellen). Roman epicures prized it; Nero ate leek soup daily in the belief it improved his singing voice, earning himself the nickname Porrophagus, "leek-eater".

1. Kitchen

Kitchen

Leeks demand washing — soil lodges between the layers — and reward patience: sweated in butter they melt into soups (Scotland's cock-a-leekie, with prunes by tradition; France's leek-and-potato, which Louis Diat chilled into vichyssoise at New York's Ritz-Carlton in 1917 and named for his native Vichy), braised as a side (poireaux vinaigrette, the bistro classic), or baked in tarts and gratins. The French call it the asparagus of the poor, which in leek season is no insult at all. Gardeners blanch the shanks by earthing them up, and leek shows — giant leeks raised competitively in the north of England — are their own sporting world.
