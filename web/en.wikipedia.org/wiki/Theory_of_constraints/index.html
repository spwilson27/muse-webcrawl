Theory of constraints

Theory of constraints (TOC)
Originator: Eliyahu M. Goldratt
Introduced in: The Goal (1984)
Field: operations management
Core idea: every system has at least one constraint limiting its output; improving the constraint improves the whole system
Key measures: throughput, inventory, operating expense

The theory of constraints (TOC) is a management philosophy introduced by Eliyahu M. Goldratt in his 1984 business novel The Goal and developed in later works such as Theory of Constraints (1990) and Critical Chain (1997). It holds that any manageable system is limited in achieving more of its goal by a very small number of constraints, and that the performance of the whole system can be improved by identifying and systematically strengthening the weakest link.

Core concept

TOC uses the analogy of a chain: just as a chain's strength is that of its weakest link, a factory, project, or organisation achieves output only at the rate permitted by its single most limiting factor — the constraint, commonly a bottleneck resource. Improvements made anywhere other than the constraint do not increase the system's overall output (its "throughput") and may even be counterproductive, for example by piling up inventory in front of the bottleneck.

The five focusing steps

  1. Identify the system's constraint.
  2. Exploit the constraint — squeeze the maximum performance out of it without major investment (e.g., never let it sit idle or process defective work).
  3. Subordinate everything else to the decision above: align all other processes to support the constraint, even at the cost of their local efficiency.
  4. Elevate the constraint — if output is still insufficient, invest to increase its capacity.
  5. If the constraint is broken, return to step one, and do not allow inertia to become the new constraint.

Measures and applications

Goldratt proposed evaluating decisions by their effect on three global measures: throughput (the rate at which the system generates money through sales), inventory (money tied up in things the system intends to sell), and operating expense (money spent turning inventory into throughput). Associated techniques include drum-buffer-rope scheduling for production, buffer management, throughput accounting, and the "thinking processes" — logical tools such as the current reality tree for diagnosing problems in complex organisations. Critical Chain applied TOC to project management, challenging the critical path method's handling of uncertainty and multitasking.

Criticism

Critics have argued that TOC oversimplifies systems with multiple interacting constraints, that its accounting conflicts with established cost accounting practice, and that parts of it restate ideas from earlier operations research. Supporters reply that its simplicity is precisely its practical strength, and it has been widely adopted in manufacturing, healthcare, and services.
