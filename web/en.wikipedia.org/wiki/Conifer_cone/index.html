Conifer cone

[Image placeholder: pine cone with open scales]

A conifer cone (in formal botanical usage: a strobilus, plural strobili) is a seed-bearing organ of conifer plants. It consists of a central axis bearing spirally arranged modified leaves (scales), and is the reproductive structure from which the division Pinophyta takes its common character. Cones are of two kinds on most conifers: smaller male (pollen) cones and larger female (seed or ovulate) cones, usually borne on the same tree (monoecy).

Female cones

The familiar woody "pine cone" is the mature female cone of pines and relatives. Each scale complex bears two ovules on its upper surface; after wind pollination and a long maturation — in many pines up to two or three seasons — fertilized ovules develop into winged seeds. In many species the scales open when dry and close when wet (hygroscopic movement), releasing seeds in favourable weather. Some pines, such as lodgepole pine, have serotinous cones sealed with resin that open only after the heat of a forest fire.

Male cones

Male cones are smaller, soft and short-lived, clustered at branch tips in spring. They produce vast quantities of wind-borne pollen from microsporangia on their scales, then wither and fall.

Variation

Not all conifer "cones" are woody: junipers bear berry-like cones with fused fleshy scales, and yews bear a fleshy red aril rather than a true cone. Cones are also the state flower and appear widely in art and heraldry; the largest are the cones of the sugar pine, reaching 50 cm or more.
