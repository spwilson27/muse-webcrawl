Erntedankfest

The Erntedankfest (German for "harvest thanksgiving festival") is a Christian harvest festival celebrated in Germany and Austria, as well as in German-speaking communities elsewhere. It is traditionally held on the first Sunday of October — near the feast of Michaelmas (29 September) — and combines church services of thanksgiving for the harvest with processions, markets and village festivities.

Traditions

Churches are decorated with produce, sheaves of grain and a woven Erntekrone (harvest crown). Worshippers bring gifts of fruit, vegetables and bread, which are often afterwards donated to charitable causes. In rural areas the festival may include processions of decorated wagons, traditional costume (Tracht) groups, brass bands and harvest queens.

History

Harvest thanksgiving rites are ancient, with roots in pre-Christian and Roman customs as well as the biblical Feast of Ingathering. The church festival took a more fixed form in the German lands in the 18th and 19th centuries; the Prussian church fixed the date as the Sunday after Michaelmas in 1773. During the Nazi era the regime attempted to appropriate the festival for propaganda, staging a massive annual Reichserntedankfest rally on the Bückeberg near Hamelin from 1933 to 1937. After the Second World War the festival returned to its church and community character.

Modern observance

Today Erntedankfest is observed by both Protestant and Catholic parishes across Germany, Austria and Switzerland, and is increasingly linked with themes of fair trade, food waste and care for creation. It is a church festival rather than a public holiday.
