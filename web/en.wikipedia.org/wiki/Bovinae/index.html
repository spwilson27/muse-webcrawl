The Bovinae are a subfamily of the family Bovidae (the family that also includes antelopes, gazelles, sheep, and goats). They are large, hoofed, even-toed ungulates that include domestic cattle, bison, African buffalo, water buffalo, the yak, and the four-horned and spiral-horned antelopes.

The subfamily comprises 10 genera and 30 species, most of which are found in Asia and Africa. The closest extant relatives of the bovines among the antelopes are the nilgai, the four-horned antelope, and the spiral-horned antelopes such as kudus and elands. Bovinae originated in Asia during the early Miocene, roughly 18–20 million years ago, and spread widely through the Old World, later reaching North America across the Bering land bridge.

Characteristics

Bovines are generally robust, heavily built animals. Most species possess horns in both sexes (or in males only, depending on tribe), which — unlike antlers — are permanent, unbranched keratin sheaths over a bony core. Many are grazers or mixed feeders adapted to open grasslands, though the spiral-horned antelopes (Tragelaphini) inhabit woodlands and thickets. Body size ranges from the relatively small nilgai and four-horned antelope to the massive gaur, the largest wild bovid, which can weigh over 1,500 kg.

Classification

The subfamily is divided into three tribes:

  • Boselaphini — the nilgai (Boselaphus tragocamelus) and the four-horned antelope (Tetracerus quadricornis).
  • Bovini — cattle (genus Bos), bison (genus Bison), buffalo (genera Bubalus and Syncerus), the kouprey, and the saola.
  • Tragelaphini — the spiral-horned antelopes: kudus, elands, nyalas, bushbuck, sitatunga, and bongo (genera Tragelaphus and Taurotragus).

Evolution

The earliest known bovines, such as Eotragus, appeared in the early Miocene of Asia. The group diversified during the middle and late Miocene as grasslands expanded, and members of the tribe Bovini eventually became among the largest and most successful terrestrial herbivores. During the Pleistocene, bovines ranged across Eurasia, Africa, and North America; several large forms, such as the aurochs (the wild ancestor of domestic cattle) and the long-horned Pelorovis, later became extinct.

Relationship with humans

Bovines have been of immense economic importance. Cattle were domesticated from the aurochs roughly 10,000 years ago in the Near East and the Indus Valley, and the water buffalo was domesticated in Asia; both provide milk, meat, leather, and draught power. The yak is central to pastoral life on the Tibetan Plateau. Several wild bovines, including the kouprey and the saola, are critically endangered.
