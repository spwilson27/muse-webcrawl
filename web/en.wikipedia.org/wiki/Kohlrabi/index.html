Kohlrabi (from German Kohl, "cabbage", and Rübe, "turnip"; scientific name Brassica oleracea Gongylodes Group), also called the German turnip or turnip cabbage, is a biennial vegetable, a low, stout cultivar of wild cabbage. It is another cultivar of the same species as cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts, collard greens, Savoy cabbage, and gai lan.

Description

Kohlrabi is grown for its swollen, spherical stem, which forms just above the ground from a thickened, fused stem base. The bulb-like stem is surrounded by two or three sets of leaves; the flesh is pale and crisp, with a flavour often described as a milder, sweeter mixture of cabbage and turnip, or like broccoli stem and cucumber with radish-like pepperiness. Varieties come in pale green, white and purple skins; the flesh of all is creamy white. The young leaves are also edible. Kohlrabi is harvested young, at about 5–8 cm diameter, before the stem becomes woody and tough.

History

The plant was described by European botanists in the 16th century — it appears in the herbal of Pietro Andrea Mattioli (1554) and was known in Italy as cavolo rapa. It spread through northern Europe, where it became a staple in German-speaking lands, and later to Asia; it is a common vegetable in northern Indian cooking, especially in Kashmir (where it is called monj), and in Vietnamese and other cuisines.

Nutrition

Raw kohlrabi is rich in vitamin C and a good source of dietary fibre, potassium, and vitamin B6, with about 27 kilocalories per 100 grams. Like other brassicas it contains glucosinolates, the compounds responsible for its mild pungency.

Culinary use

Kohlrabi may be eaten raw — sliced into salads and slaws, or cut into sticks for dipping — or cooked by steaming, roasting, stir-frying, or adding to soups and stews. In Germany it is often served in a cream sauce; in Kashmir the whole plant including the leaves (haakh monj) is cooked in spiced preparations; in Cyprus the purple form is traditionally grilled with salt and lemon.
