The gravitational constant is an empirical physical constant involved in the calculation of gravitational effects in Sir Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation and in Albert Einstein's theory of general relativity. It is also known as the universal gravitational constant, the Newtonian constant of gravitation, or the Cavendish gravitational constant, denoted by the capital letter G.

In Newton's law, it is the proportionality constant connecting the gravitational force between two bodies with the product of their masses and the inverse square of the distance between them: F = G m1 m2 / r2. In the Einstein field equations, it quantifies the relation between the geometry of spacetime and the energy–momentum tensor.

Value and uncertainty

The 2018 CODATA-recommended value is G = 6.67430(15) × 10−11 m3·kg−1·s−2, with a relative standard uncertainty of 2.2 × 10−5. G is notoriously difficult to measure accurately because gravity is so weak compared with the other fundamental forces; measurements by different laboratories with torsion balances have persistently disagreed by more than their stated uncertainties, making G one of the least precisely known fundamental constants.

History of measurement

The constant first appeared explicitly in Newton's Principia (1687) only as a proportionality; the first direct measurement was the Cavendish experiment of 1797–98, in which Henry Cavendish used a torsion balance devised by John Michell to measure the force between lead spheres — an experiment Cavendish framed as "weighing the Earth". Modern determinations use time-of-swing and angular-acceleration torsion balances, atom interferometry, and beam balances.

Role in physics

Together with Planck's constant ħ and the speed of light c, G defines the Planck units of length, mass, and time. In cosmology, G enters the Friedmann equations governing the expansion of the universe. Despite speculation (notably by Dirac's large numbers hypothesis) that G might vary over cosmic time, observations constrain any such variation extremely tightly.
