Fail-safe

Railway signalling is a classic fail-safe system

In engineering, a fail-safe is a design feature or practice that, in the event of a specific type of failure, inherently responds in a way that causes minimal or no harm to other equipment, the environment or people. Unlike fault-tolerant systems, which continue operating after a failure, a fail-safe system may shut down, provided it does so safely.

Examples

  • Railway signalling: signals are designed so that a power failure or broken wire causes the signal to show its most restrictive aspect (red/stop), and track circuits fail to "occupied".
  • Air brakes: on trains and trucks, brakes are applied by stored energy and released by air pressure, so a loss of pressure applies the brakes rather than releasing them.
  • Dead man's switch: machinery stops unless the operator continuously holds a control.
  • Electrical fuses and breakers: fail open, disconnecting the circuit on overload.
  • Nuclear reactor control rods held up by electromagnets drop into the core under gravity if power is lost.
  • Elevator brakes engage automatically if the hoisting cable fails.

Fail-safe versus fail-secure

A distinction is drawn between fail-safe behaviour, which protects people (for example, a fire door that unlocks on power loss so occupants can escape), and fail-secure behaviour, which protects property or security (a door that stays locked). Good design chooses the appropriate failure mode for each function and considers how failures propagate through the wider system.
