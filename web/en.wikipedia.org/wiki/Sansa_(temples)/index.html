Sansa (temples)

Sansa (Korean: 산사, 山寺) are Buddhist mountain monasteries in South Korea. Located in remote mountain settings, most sansa were founded during the Three Kingdoms and Goryeo periods and preserved Korean Buddhism through the Joseon dynasty, when Buddhism was officially suppressed in favour of Confucianism and its institutions were pushed out of the cities into the mountains. In 2018, seven sansa were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as "Sansa, Buddhist Mountain Monasteries in Korea".

The seven World Heritage monasteries

  • Tongdosa in Yangsan, South Gyeongsang — one of the Three Jewel Temples, representing the Buddha; founded in 646 and notable for having no outdoor Buddha statue.
  • Buseoksa in Yeongju, North Gyeongsang — founded in 676 by the monk Uisang.
  • Bongjeongsa in Andong — preserving some of Korea's oldest wooden architecture.
  • Beopjusa in Boeun — home to a huge standing Maitreya statue and a five-storey wooden pagoda.
  • Magoksa in Gongju.
  • Seonamsa in Suncheon.
  • Daeheungsa in Haenam.

Characteristics

Sansa share a common layout adapted to mountain slopes: entrance gates, a hall of the Four Heavenly Kings, and the main Buddha hall arranged along an axis in courtyards, typically preserving the madang (open courtyard) layout characteristic of Korean monasteries from the seventh century onward. They remain living religious communities as well as pilgrimage and cultural destinations, and many offer temple-stay programmes for visitors.

History

After Buddhism's introduction to Korea in the fourth century, mountain temples flourished under Goryeo patronage. Joseon-era persecution drove monks into the mountains, where the sansa served as refuges preserving doctrine, art, and printing — including woodblocks and temple records. Monks from these temples also famously fought as militia during the Japanese invasions of the 1590s.
