Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is a medical condition caused by compression of the median nerve as it passes through the carpal tunnel in the wrist. It is the most common entrapment neuropathy, affecting roughly 1–5% of adults, and occurs more often in women than in men.

Signs and symptoms

The main symptoms are pain, numbness and tingling in the thumb, index finger, middle finger and the radial half of the ring finger — the territory of the median nerve. Symptoms typically come on gradually and are often worse at night, waking the sufferer, who may shake the hand for relief (the "flick sign"). In advanced cases there may be clumsiness, dropping objects, and wasting of the thenar muscles at the base of the thumb.

Causes and diagnosis

Anything that reduces the space in the carpal tunnel or swells its contents can raise pressure on the nerve: fluid retention in pregnancy, diabetes, hypothyroidism, obesity, inflammatory arthritis and wrist fractures are established associations, while the role of computer keyboard use is disputed. Diagnosis rests on the history and physical signs (such as Tinel's sign and Phalen's manoeuvre), confirmed where needed by nerve conduction studies.

Treatment

Mild cases are managed with night splints holding the wrist neutral and modification of activities. Corticosteroid injections give temporary relief. For persistent or severe cases, carpal tunnel release surgery — dividing the transverse carpal ligament, by open or endoscopic technique — is highly effective.
