Rātana is a Māori Christian church and pan-tribal movement originating in New Zealand. It was founded by Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana (1873–1939), a Māori farmer from the Whangaehu valley near Whanganui, following a vision he experienced on 8 November 1918.

History

During the influenza pandemic of 1918, Rātana reported a divine vision calling him to preach the gospel to the Māori people and to heal the sick through faith. His reputation as a faith healer (tohunga having renounced traditional practices that he held to conflict with Christianity) spread rapidly, and thousands of Māori — the mōrehu, or survivors/remnant — flocked to his settlement at Rātana Pā, south of Whanganui, where a distinctive church with twin bell towers was opened in 1925.

The movement developed two arms: the spiritual church, Te Haahi Rātana, registered in 1925, and a political movement. Rātana aimed to have the Treaty of Waitangi recognised and enshrined in law, presenting petitions to Parliament and to King George V. From 1928 the movement contested the Māori seats in Parliament; its candidates ("the Bodyguard") won all four Māori seats by 1943, and in 1936 Rātana entered a lasting alliance with the Labour Party.

Beliefs and practice

Rātana teaching is Christian, centred on Ihoa (Jehovah), the Trinity — Te Matua, Te Tama, Te Wairua Tapu — together with Te Anahera Pono (the faithful angel) and Te Mangai ("the Mouthpiece", Rātana himself). Worship takes place in distinctive temples marked by the movement's symbols, including the whetū mārama (star and crescent moon) emblem. The church remains one of the largest Māori denominations in New Zealand, and the annual celebrations at Rātana Pā each January attract thousands, including political leaders.
