Young Ottomans

Active: c. 1865–1878
Key figures: Namık Kemal, İbrahim Şinasi, Ziya Pasha, Ali Suavi
Ideology: Constitutionalism, Ottoman patriotism, Islamic modernism
Legacy: Ottoman constitution of 1876

The Young Ottomans (Turkish: Yeni Osmanlılar) were a secret society of Ottoman intellectuals, bureaucrats, and journalists formed in 1865 who sought to reform the empire through a constitutional government that would reconcile European political ideas with Islamic principles. Dissatisfied with the authoritarian style of the Tanzimat reformers Âli and Fuad Pashas, they pioneered modern Turkish political journalism and coined much of the vocabulary of Ottoman liberalism.

Ideas

Through newspapers such as Şinasi's Tasvir-i Efkâr and, in exile, Hürriyet ("Liberty"), the Young Ottomans popularised concepts like vatan (fatherland), freedom, and popular sovereignty. Namık Kemal, the movement's leading publicist, argued that consultation (meşveret), an elected assembly, and the rule of law were not foreign imports but authentically Islamic institutions. Their patriotism was Ottoman and supra-ethnic, aiming to bind the empire's religious communities into one citizenry.

Exile and the constitution

Suppressed at home, leading members fled in 1867 to Paris and London, where the wealthy Egyptian prince Mustafa Fazıl financed their press. Returning after 1870, they allied with the grand vizier Midhat Pasha, whose circle deposed Sultan Abdülaziz in 1876 and proclaimed the first Ottoman constitution, the Kanun-i Esasi, with Midhat as grand vizier. The new sultan, Abdülhamid II, soon exiled Midhat, prorogued parliament in 1878, and ruled autocratically for three decades — yet the Young Ottomans' ideas seeded the later Young Turk movement and the constitutional revolution of 1908.
