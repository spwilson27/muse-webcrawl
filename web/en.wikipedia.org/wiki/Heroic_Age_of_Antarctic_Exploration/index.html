The Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration describes the period from the end of the 19th century to the early 1920s during which seventeen major expeditions, mounted by nine countries, explored the last unknown continent. These voyages combined geographical discovery with pioneering science, carried out under extreme conditions with technology barely changed from the age of sail — wooden ships, dog teams, man-hauled sledges and no radio contact with the outside world. The era's popular image of heroism rests on feats of endurance and on the deaths of several leaders, above all Robert Falcon Scott and his polar party in 1912.

Expeditions

The age is conventionally opened by the Belgian Belgica expedition (1897–99), the first to winter in the Antarctic ice, and by Carsten Borchgrevink's Southern Cross expedition (1898–1900), the first to winter on the continent itself. Scott's Discovery expedition (1901–04), with Ernest Shackleton and Edward Wilson, set a new farthest south. Shackleton's Nimrod expedition (1907–09) reached within 97 miles of the Pole and made the first ascent of Mount Erebus.

The race for the Pole culminated in 1911–12: Roald Amundsen's Norwegian team, using dogs, reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911, while Scott's five-man party arrived on 17–18 January 1912 and perished on the return march. Douglas Mawson's Australasian expedition (1911–14) included the lone survival trek of the Far Eastern Party. The age closed with Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic (Endurance) expedition (1914–17) — a failed crossing attempt that became a legendary survival story — and his death aboard the Quest at South Georgia in January 1922.

Character and legacy

The expeditions suffered nineteen deaths in total and relied on techniques — man-hauling versus dogs, diet, clothing — that were debated ever afterwards. Their scientific results in geology, magnetism, meteorology and marine biology laid foundations for modern Antarctic science, and their literature, from Scott's journals to Shackleton's South and Apsley Cherry-Garrard's The Worst Journey in the World, remains central to the literature of exploration.
