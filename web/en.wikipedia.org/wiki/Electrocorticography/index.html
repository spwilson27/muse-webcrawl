Electrocorticography

Electrocorticography (ECoG)
Type: invasive electrophysiology
Signal: local field potentials from cortical surface
Uses: epilepsy surgery, brain–computer interfaces

Electrocorticography (ECoG) is a technique for recording electrical activity directly from the exposed surface of the cerebral cortex using electrode grids or strips placed beneath the skull. Because the electrodes sit on the brain rather than the scalp, ECoG signals have much higher spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio than electroencephalography (EEG).

Clinical use

ECoG is most often used in the surgical treatment of epilepsy. Grids implanted for days to weeks allow clinicians to localise seizure onset zones and to map eloquent cortex — areas controlling speech and movement — by direct stimulation before resections are performed.

Research and interfaces

ECoG captures high-gamma activity (roughly 70–200 Hz) that correlates closely with neuronal firing, making it valuable for neuroscience research and for brain–computer interfaces that decode intended movement or speech in paralysed patients.

Comparison with other methods

ECoG is more invasive than EEG or magnetoencephalography but less invasive than penetrating microelectrode arrays. It offers millisecond temporal resolution and millimetre-to-centimetre spatial resolution.
