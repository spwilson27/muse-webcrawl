Perturbation theory (quantum mechanics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of successive corrections of increasing order to an energy level diagram]
Perturbation theory refines approximate solutions in successive orders.

In quantum mechanics, perturbation theory is a set of approximation schemes directly related to mathematical perturbation for describing a complex quantum system in terms of a simpler one. The idea is to start with a simple system for which a mathematical solution is known, and add a 'perturbing' Hamiltonian representing a weak disturbance; if the disturbance is not too large, the quantities of interest can be expressed as a power series of corrections to the simple system's values.

In Quantum field theory the same idea organises calculations as a series in the coupling constant — for Quantum electrodynamics, in α/2π — whose terms correspond to Feynman diagrams of increasing complexity. Perturbation theory in its time-independent form was developed in the 1920s (Schrödinger) and its time-dependent form (Fermi's golden rule) remains the standard tool for transition rates. Although famously powerful in QED (where α is small), the series are typically asymptotic — they diverge if pushed to very high order — and perturbation theory fails entirely for strongly coupled systems such as low-energy Quantum chromodynamics, where lattice methods take over.

Applications

Perturbation theory explains the fine and hyperfine structure of hydrogen, the Zeeman and Stark effects, scattering cross-sections, and the multi-loop corrections to the electron's anomalous magnetic moment. Its non-perturbative complements — variational methods, WKB, exact diagonalisation and lattice simulation — handle the many problems where no small parameter exists.
