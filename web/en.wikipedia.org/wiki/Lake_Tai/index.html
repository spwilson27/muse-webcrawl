Lake Tai

Lake Tai or Taihu (Chinese: 太湖, pinyin: Tàihú, literally "Great Lake") is a large freshwater lake in the Yangtze Delta plain, on the border of Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces in eastern China. With an area of about 2,250 square kilometres and an average depth of only about 2 metres, it is the third-largest freshwater lake in China, after Poyang Lake and Dongting Lake.

Geography

The lake lies in the flat, densely populated Yangtze Delta, about 100 kilometres west of Shanghai. It is fed by numerous rivers and canals from the west and south, including the waterways of the Grand Canal, which passes along its eastern shore, and drains eastward through the Suzhou Creek and other channels toward the Yangtze River and the East China Sea. Some ninety islands dot the lake, the largest being Xishan Island (Dongting Xishan), a popular scenic area now belonging to Suzhou. The lake's major shore cities are Wuxi and Suzhou in Jiangsu and Huzhou in Zhejiang.

Formation

Lake Tai is generally believed to have originated as a coastal lagoon of the East China Sea that was gradually sealed off by the growth of the Yangtze Delta's sediment deposits over the last several thousand years, converting it into a freshwater lake. Its basin is extraordinarily shallow and flat, and the water level is regulated in part by the Taipu River sluice works built in the 1990s.

Economy and culture

The lake region is one of China's most productive agricultural and aquacultural areas, famous for the "three whites" of Taihu — white fish, white shrimp, and whitebait — and for hairy crabs and Biluochun tea grown on the Dongting hills of Xishan. The distinctive perforated limestone rocks eroded from the lake shore, known as Taihu stones, have been prized ornaments in Chinese scholar's gardens for over a thousand years. Carved ferry and tour boats serve scenic areas such as Yuantouzhu (Turtle Head Isle) in Wuxi, famed for cherry blossom viewing in spring.

Environment

Rapid industrialisation and dense settlement around the lake have made Taihu a byword for eutrophication in China. In May 2007 a massive bloom of blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) contaminated the drinking water supply of Wuxi, leaving millions of residents without clean tap water for days and prompting a major national pollution-control campaign, including factory closures, aquaculture restrictions, and large investments in wastewater treatment in the basin.
