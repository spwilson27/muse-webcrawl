Any planet is an extremely faint light source compared to its parent star. For example, a star like the Sun is about a billion times as bright as the reflected light from any orbiting Earth-like planet, so nearly all known exoplanets have been discovered through indirect methods that detect the planet's effect on its star rather than the planet's own light.

Transit photometry

The transit method measures the slight dimming of a star when a planet passes in front of it. It yields the planet's radius (relative to the star) and, with radial-velocity follow-up, its density. NASA's Kepler space telescope (2009–2018) used this method to discover more than 2,600 planets, the majority of all confirmed exoplanets; TESS continues the survey over the whole sky.

Radial velocity

A star and planet orbit their common centre of mass, so the star wobbles; Doppler spectroscopy detects this via periodic shifts in the star's spectral lines. This method produced the first exoplanet around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (1995), and gives the planet's minimum mass. Modern spectrographs such as ESPRESSO reach precision below 1 m/s.

Direct imaging

Using coronagraphs to block starlight, a handful of large, young, self-luminous planets far from their stars have been photographed directly — beginning with 2M1207b (2004) and the HR 8799 system (2008). Direct imaging allows spectroscopy of planetary atmospheres.

Other methods

Gravitational microlensing detects planets through brief brightening of a background star; astrometry measures the star's positional wobble on the sky; pulsar timing discovered the first exoplanets in 1992 around PSR B1257+12; transit timing variations reveal additional planets in multi-planet systems.
