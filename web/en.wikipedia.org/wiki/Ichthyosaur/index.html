Ichthyosaurs (from Greek ἰχθύς ichthys, "fish", and σαῦρος sauros, "lizard") are an order of large extinct marine reptiles belonging to the group Ichthyosauria. They thrived during much of the Mesozoic era, first appearing about 250 million years ago, in the Early Triassic, and disappearing about 90 million years ago, in the Late Cretaceous. During the Jurassic they reached their highest diversity and evolved streamlined, dolphin-like body plans.

Description

Ichthyosaurs ranged from about 1 metre to more than 20 metres in length in the largest forms such as Shastasaurus. Most had fish-shaped bodies with large eyes — the largest eyes of any vertebrate, adapted for deep diving — vertical tail fins, dorsal fins, and paddle-like limbs for steering. Their diet included fish, cephalopods (ammonites and belemnites), and other marine reptiles. Fossil evidence, including embryos preserved inside mothers, shows they gave birth to live young at sea.

Discovery and research

The first complete ichthyosaur fossils were found in the early 19th century at Lyme Regis, England, by Mary Anning and her brother Joseph, helping to establish palaeontology as a science. Spectacularly complete skeletons also come from Holzmaden in Germany, where soft-tissue outlines were preserved.

Evolution and extinction

Ichthyosaurs evolved from land reptiles that returned to the sea shortly after the end-Permian mass extinction. They radiated rapidly and were top predators through the Triassic and Jurassic, but diversity declined in the Cretaceous and they disappeared about 28 million years before the end-Cretaceous extinction, possibly through climate change and competition.
