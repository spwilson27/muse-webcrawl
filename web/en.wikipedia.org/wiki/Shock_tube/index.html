Shock tube

A shock tube is an instrument used to replicate and study shock waves and high-temperature gas flows in the laboratory. It consists of a long tube divided by a diaphragm into a high-pressure "driver" section and a low-pressure "driven" section. When the diaphragm bursts, a shock wave propagates into the driven gas, heating and compressing it almost instantaneously to conditions that persist for a few milliseconds — long enough for measurement.

Operation

The driver gas (often helium or hydrogen at high pressure) expands suddenly on diaphragm rupture, sending a normal shock into the driven section while a rarefaction wave travels back into the driver. The region behind the shock reaches thousands of kelvin and many atmospheres, depending on the initial pressures and gases. In a shock tunnel, a reservoir and nozzle are added to produce brief hypersonic flows.

Applications

Shock tubes are a workhorse of chemical kinetics: rate constants for combustion and atmospheric reactions are measured behind reflected shocks using spectroscopic diagnostics. They are also used to calibrate pressure sensors, study blast-wave effects, test aerodynamic heating on models, and investigate the physics of plasmas and equations of state.

History

The device was pioneered in the 1890s by French chemist Paul Vieille and developed extensively during and after the Second World War for ballistics and aerodynamics research.
