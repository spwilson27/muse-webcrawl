Anterior cruciate ligament

ToPosterior part of the medial surface of the lateral femoral condyle

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the four major ligaments of the human knee and one of the two cruciate ligaments (with the posterior cruciate ligament) that cross within the joint. It connects the tibia to the femur, runs upward, backward, and laterally, and primarily resists anterior displacement of the tibia relative to the femur as well as rotational loads.

The ACL sits in the centre of the knee, crossing the PCL.

Structure

The ACL consists largely of type I collagen arranged in fascicles, functionally divided into anteromedial and posterolateral bundles that tighten at different flexion angles. It averages about 3–4 cm in length and receives its blood supply mainly from the middle genicular artery; mechanoreceptors within it contribute to proprioception.

Injury

ACL tears are common sports injuries — on the order of 100,000–200,000 annually in the United States — occurring most often in non-contact pivoting, deceleration, or awkward landing, with a notably higher incidence in female athletes. Diagnosis rests on history (often a "pop" and rapid swelling), the Lachman and pivot-shift tests, and MRI. Treatment may be conservative for lower-demand patients, but athletes commonly undergo arthroscopic ACL reconstruction using autografts (patellar tendon, hamstring, or quadriceps tendon) or allografts, followed by 6–12 months of rehabilitation. ACL injury increases the long-term risk of knee osteoarthritis.
