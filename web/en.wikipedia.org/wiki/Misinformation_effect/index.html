Misinformation effect

The misinformation effect occurs when a person's recall of an event becomes less accurate because of information received after the event. It is a classic finding in the psychology of memory, associated especially with the research of Elizabeth Loftus, and has been extensively studied because of its implications for eyewitness testimony.

Paradigm

In the standard experimental procedure, participants view a film or slides of an event, then receive a narrative or questionnaire containing misleading details (for example, referring to a "stop sign" when a yield sign was shown, or asking how fast cars were going when they "smashed" rather than "hit" each other). At test, misled participants are more likely to report the suggested details as if they had seen them.

Explanations

Theories of the effect include alteration of the original memory trace, source misattribution (confusing the post-event suggestion with the witnessed event), and simple response bias. The effect demonstrates that memory is reconstructive rather than a literal recording, and it underlies concerns about suggestive questioning of witnesses, especially children.
