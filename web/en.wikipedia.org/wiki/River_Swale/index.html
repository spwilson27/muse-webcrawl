River Swale

River Swale
Location: North Yorkshire, England
Length: 117.8 km (73.2 mi)
Mouth: River Ure, Myton-on-Swale

The River Swale is a river in North Yorkshire, England. It is a major tributary of the River Ure, which becomes the River Ouse, and hence the Humber Estuary. The Swale is reputed to be the fastest-flowing river in England, and it rises on the slopes of Great Shunner Fell and Nine Standards Rigg in the Pennines, near the Cumbria border, before flowing east through the valley that bears its name — Swaledale — one of the northernmost dales of the Yorkshire Dales National Park.

Course

The river is formed by the confluence of Birkdale Beck and Great Sleddale Beck near the hamlet of Keld. From there it flows east past waterfalls including Kisdon Force, Catrake Force and Wain Wath Force, through the villages of Muker, Gunnerside and Reeth, and the market town of Richmond, where it passes beneath Richmond Castle. Leaving the dales, it flows across the Vale of Mowbray past Catterick and Morton-on-Swale before joining the River Ure at Myton-on-Swale near Boroughbridge.

History and economy

Swaledale was historically a centre of lead mining, whose remains — hushes, smelt mills and mine workings — survive in the landscape, and the valley is famous for its drystone walls, hay meadows and Swaledale sheep, a hardy breed named after the valley. The river's name derives from Old English and Old Norse elements thought to mean a rushing, swirling or turbulent stream, consistent with its fast-flowing character and tendency to rise rapidly after rain on the fells — a spate river.

Ecology and recreation

The Swale supports populations of brown trout and grayling and is fished along much of its length. The Coast to Coast Walk, devised by Alfred Wainwright, follows the river through Swaledale. The river and its valley are designated within the Yorkshire Dales National Park, and parts are Sites of Special Scientific Interest for their hay meadows and riverine habitats.
