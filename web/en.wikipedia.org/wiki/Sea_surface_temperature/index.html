Sea surface temperature

Sea surface temperature (SST) is the temperature of ocean water close to the surface, typically measured within the uppermost metre or so (the exact depth depends on the method). SST is a fundamental variable in meteorology, oceanography and climate science: it governs heat and moisture exchange between ocean and atmosphere, drives weather patterns, and is a key indicator of climate change.

Measurement

Historically SST was measured by ships using buckets and later engine-intake thermometers, and by drifting and moored buoys. Since the 1980s, satellites with infrared and microwave radiometers have provided global, near-daily coverage, though infrared sensors are blocked by cloud. Blended products combine satellite, buoy and ship data into gridded analyses.

Patterns and significance

SST ranges from near −2 °C at the freezing point of seawater in polar regions to about 30–32 °C in the tropical "warm pool". Variations in tropical Pacific SST define El Niño and La Niña, with worldwide weather consequences. Hurricanes generally require SSTs above about 26.5 °C. Long-term records show a clear warming trend in global mean SST over the 20th and 21st centuries, and marine heatwaves have become more frequent.

Climate records

Because methods changed over time (canvas buckets, insulated buckets, engine intakes, buoys), constructing a homogeneous historical SST record requires careful bias adjustments — a topic of continuing research in climate science.
