The Main Market Square (Polish: Rynek Główny) in Kraków, Poland, is the largest medieval town square in Europe, measuring roughly 200 by 200 metres (about 3.79 hectares). It is the principal urban space of Kraków's Old Town, laid out in 1257 when the city received its charter under Magdeburg law, and forms the centrepiece of the Old Town district inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 1978. The square is surrounded by historic townhouses, palaces, and churches, and is divided by the Cloth Hall.


The Main Market Square with St Mary's Basilica and the Cloth Hall.

History

The square was designed in 1257 as the commercial heart of the rebuilt city, following the destruction of Kraków during the Mongol invasion of 1241. Merchants traded goods from across Europe and the East here, and the square also served ceremonial purposes: homage ceremonies, royal entries, and public celebrations took place on it. In 1794 Tadeusz Kościuszko took his famous oath in the square at the start of the Kościuszko Uprising. The square's layout of streets at right angles, unusual for its period, reflected the planned character of the chartered city.

Principal buildings

  • Cloth Hall (Sukiennice) — the Renaissance market hall at the centre of the square, originally built for the cloth trade; its upper floor houses a gallery of nineteenth-century Polish art, and an underground museum presents the square's medieval archaeology.
  • St Mary's Basilica — the Gothic brick church on the eastern side, famous for its uneven towers, the hourly hejnał bugle call, and the carved altarpiece of Veit Stoss.
  • Town Hall Tower — the surviving tower of the demolished medieval town hall.
  • Church of St Adalbert — a small Romanesque church predating the square itself.
  • Adam Mickiewicz Monument — the 1898 monument to Poland's national poet, a traditional meeting place.

The square today

The Rynek is largely pedestrianised and is a focus of Kraków's cultural life: it hosts the Christmas and Easter markets, the Lajkonik parade, open-air concerts, and the famous flower stalls. Cafés and restaurants occupy the surrounding townhouses, many with cellars dating to the Middle Ages, and horse-drawn carriages carry visitors around the square. An archaeological reserve beneath the square, opened as the Rynek Underground museum in 2010, displays the remains of medieval market structures.
