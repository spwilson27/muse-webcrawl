Grote Reber (December 22, 1911 – December 20, 2002) was an American pioneer of radio astronomy, which combined his interests in amateur radio and astronomy. He was instrumental in investigating and extending Karl Jansky's pioneering work and conducted the first sky survey in the radio frequencies.

His 1937 radio antenna was the second ever built, after Jansky's, and the first parabolic reflecting telescope used as a radio telescope. For nearly a decade he was the world's only radio astronomer.

Early life and education

Reber was born in Wheaton, Illinois, a suburb of Chicago, and graduated from the Armour Institute of Technology (now the Illinois Institute of Technology) in 1933 with a degree in electrical engineering. He was an amateur radio operator (call sign W9GFZ) and worked for various radio manufacturers in Chicago from 1933 to 1947.

First radio telescope

When he learned of Karl Jansky's 1932 discovery of radio waves from the Milky Way, Reber decided to build his own receiver. After being turned down for a job at Bell Labs, where Jansky worked, he built a parabolic dish antenna in his backyard in Wheaton in 1937. The reflector was 9.6 metres (31.5 feet) in diameter, made of sheet metal mounted on a wooden frame. The antenna could be tilted but not rotated, relying on the Earth's rotation to scan the sky.

After early attempts at higher frequencies failed, Reber succeeded at 160 MHz in 1938, confirming Jansky's results. In 1940 he published his findings in the Astrophysical Journal. Turning his attention to longer wavelengths, he produced the first radio maps of the sky, published in 1944, which revealed bright sources in Cygnus and Cassiopeia, later identified with the supernova remnant Cassiopeia A and the radio galaxy Cygnus A.

Later work

Reber later worked for the National Bureau of Standards and, in the 1950s, moved to Tasmania, Australia, where he built a large array of dipoles to observe very low frequency cosmic radio waves through gaps in the ionosphere. His Wheaton dish was reassembled at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory in Green Bank, West Virginia, where it stands as a historical monument near a replica of Jansky's antenna.

Honours

Reber received the Bruce Medal of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific in 1962 and the Jackson-Gwilt Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1983. The asteroid 6886 Grote is named in his honour. He died in Tasmania two days before his 91st birthday, and a portion of his ashes was distributed among major radio observatories around the world.
