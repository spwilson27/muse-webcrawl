Union blockade

The Union blockade in the American Civil War was a naval strategy by the United States (the Union) to prevent the Confederacy from trading. Proclaimed by President Abraham Lincoln on 19 April 1861 and extended on 27 April, the blockade required the closure of 3,500 miles (5,600 km) of coastline and 180 ports, harbours, and other navigable places along the Confederate states.

Union Navy vessels enforcing the blockade off the Confederate coast.

The Anaconda Plan

The blockade was a central element of General Winfield Scott's "Anaconda Plan", which envisioned strangling the Confederacy economically — blockading its ports and seizing control of the Mississippi River — rather than invading and occupying the whole South. Lincoln adopted the blockade while initially pursuing a more aggressive strategy on land.

Enforcement

At the war's start the U.S. Navy had few ships, but it expanded enormously, purchasing and converting merchant vessels and building new warships; by 1865 it had hundreds of vessels on blockade duty. Blockading squadrons were stationed off major ports such as Charleston, Wilmington, Mobile, and Galveston. Although porous at first, the blockade grew progressively more effective; by the later war years, few blockade runners got through.

Blockade runners

Fast, low-profile steamers, often purpose-built in Britain, ran the blockade from neutral ports such as Bermuda, Nassau, and Havana, carrying arms and luxury goods in and cotton out. Blockade running was highly profitable but risky; runners that were captured became prizes of the Union Navy. The last major Confederate port, Wilmington, North Carolina, was closed when Fort Fisher fell in January 1865.

Effects and diplomacy

The blockade choked the Confederacy's ability to export cotton and import war materiel, contributing significantly to Southern economic collapse and inflation. It also created friction with Britain and France, whose merchants were affected and whose governments had to decide whether the blockade was legally effective under international law. The Union's declaration of a "blockade" rather than simply closing ports implicitly treated the Confederacy as a belligerent, a point of diplomatic sensitivity.
