Swallowing

Swallowing (deglutition)
SystemDigestive and respiratory coordination
Key structuresTongue, soft palate, pharynx, larynx, esophagus
ControlVoluntary (oral phases); brainstem swallowing center (pharyngeal/esophageal phases)
NervesCranial nerves V, VII, IX, X, XII

Swallowing, also called deglutition, is the process in the human or animal body which makes something pass from the mouth into the pharynx and onward into the esophagus, while shutting off the airway. It is a complex neuromuscular act coordinating some 50 pairs of muscles and several cranial nerves, and a typical adult swallows hundreds of times a day, including during sleep.

Stages

Oral preparatory and transit stages

Under voluntary control, food is chewed and mixed with saliva to form a bolus, which the tongue gathers and propels backward against the palate toward the oropharynx.

Pharyngeal stage

Once triggered, this rapid involuntary stage (about one second) seals the nasopharynx with the soft palate, elevates and closes the larynx, and uses the epiglottis and vocal folds to protect the airway while the pharyngeal constrictors squeeze the bolus downward. Breathing pauses momentarily (deglutition apnea), and the upper esophageal sphincter (cricopharyngeus) relaxes to admit the bolus.

Esophageal stage

Primary peristalsis sweeps the bolus through the esophagus in roughly 8–20 seconds; secondary peristalsis clears any residue. The lower esophageal sphincter then relaxes to let the bolus enter the stomach.

Neural control

Sensory input from the oropharynx travels via the trigeminal, glossopharyngeal, and vagus nerves to a central pattern generator in the medulla (nucleus tractus solitarius and nucleus ambiguus), which sequences the motor output. Cortical areas initiate voluntary swallows and can modulate the reflex.

Disorders

Difficulty swallowing is called dysphagia; leakage of food into the airway (aspiration) can cause pneumonia. Causes include stroke, Parkinson's disease, motor neurone disease, head and neck cancers, and structural esophageal disease such as achalasia or strictures. Assessment uses clinical examination and videofluoroscopy (modified barium swallow), and management involves speech-language therapists, diet modification, compensatory maneuvers, and sometimes tube feeding.
