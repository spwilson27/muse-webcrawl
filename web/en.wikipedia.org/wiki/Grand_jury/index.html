Grand jury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A grand jury is a jury — a group of citizens — empowered by law to conduct legal proceedings, investigate potential criminal conduct, and determine whether criminal charges should be brought (an indictment). It may compel testimony and documents by subpoena and examine physical evidence. Grand juries originated in England (the Assize of Clarendon, 1166) and survive mainly in the United States, where the Fifth Amendment requires grand-jury indictment for federal capital or "infamous" crimes (a requirement not applied to the states).

Operation and debate

US grand juries typically comprise 16–23 members, sit in secret, hear only the prosecution (targets and defence counsel have limited rights), and indict on probable cause by supermajority — leading to the famous gibe that a prosecutor could persuade a grand jury to "indict a ham sandwich". Special grand juries investigate public corruption and organised crime. Defenders see a citizen shield between the state and the accused; critics note near-automatic indictments in ordinary cases and, conversely, grand juries' frequent decisions not to indict police officers — flashpoints in protests over police killings. Most other common-law countries abolished grand juries in the 20th century, relying on professional prosecutors and preliminary hearings.
