Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), was a United States Supreme Court case about class-action certification and employment discrimination. The Court ruled 5–4 that a class of roughly 1.5 million current and former female Wal-Mart employees alleging sex discrimination in pay and promotions could not be certified.

Background

Betty Dukes, a Wal-Mart greeter in California, and others sued under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, alleging Wal-Mart's decentralised discretion over pay and promotion produced a pattern of discrimination against women. The district court and Ninth Circuit certified the class under Rule 23(b)(2) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

Decision

Justice Scalia's majority held the plaintiffs failed Rule 23(a)(2) commonality: they had not shown a common contention capable of classwide resolution, because discretion exercised by thousands of managers, without a specific employment practice linking the claims, was not enough; statistical and anecdotal evidence did not suffice. The Court also held unanimously in relevant part that claims for individualised monetary relief (back pay) could not be certified under Rule 23(b)(2), which is limited to injunctive or declaratory relief. Justice Ginsburg dissented in part.

Impact

Dukes raised the bar for class certification, requiring rigorous analysis and proof of commonality, and influenced employment, consumer and mass tort litigation.
