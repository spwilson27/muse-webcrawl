Peine forte et dure

Peine forte et dure (French for "strong and harsh punishment") was a method of torture formerly used in the common law legal system of England, in which a defendant who refused to plead to a criminal charge ("standing mute") was subjected to having heavier and heavier stones placed upon his or her chest until either a plea was entered or the prisoner died of suffocation.

Why defendants stood mute

Under English law a felony trial could not proceed without a plea. Conviction for felony brought forfeiture of the defendant's property to the Crown and "corruption of the blood", disinheriting his family. A prisoner who died without pleading — even under the press — died unconvicted, so his estate passed to his heirs. Some defendants therefore chose to endure pressing to death rather than plead, an agonising but deliberate sacrifice for their families.

Practice

The practice developed from the Statute of Westminster I of 1275, which prescribed harsh imprisonment (prison forte et dure) for those who refused to plead; over the following centuries this hardened into literal pressing under boards loaded with weights. One of the earliest recorded cases is that of Margaret Clitherow, a Catholic recusant pressed to death at York in 1586; she was later canonised. In colonial America, the most famous victim was Giles Corey, pressed to death during the Salem witch trials in 1692 — the only such case in American history — whose legendary last words were "More weight!"

Abolition

From 1741 pressing was effectively replaced when standing mute was treated as a conviction, and the Felony and Piracy Act 1772 made standing mute equivalent to pleading guilty. In 1827 the law was changed so that the court would enter a plea of not guilty on behalf of a mute defendant, finally removing the reason for the practice.
