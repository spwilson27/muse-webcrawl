Littlewood's law

Littlewood's law states that a person can expect to experience an event with odds of one in a million (called a "miracle") at the rate of about one per month. The law was framed by Cambridge mathematician John Edensor Littlewood and published in his 1986 collection of essays, A Mathematician's Miscellany.

The argument

Littlewood supposed that a person experiences events at roughly one event per second during waking hours and is alert for about eight hours a day. This gives around 28,800 events per day, or roughly one million events in 35 days. If a "miracle" is defined as an event with probability one in a million, then purely by chance a person should expect about one such event every 35 days — roughly one per month.

Purpose

The law is intended to illustrate a general principle debated by skeptics: that apparently astonishing coincidences are statistically inevitable given the sheer number of events people experience. It is related to the law of truly large numbers, which states that with a large enough sample size, any outrageous thing is likely to happen.

Criticism

Critics note that the argument depends on arbitrary choices, such as the rate of one event per second and the one-in-a-million threshold, and that real events are not independent draws from a uniform distribution. Supporters reply that the law is an order-of-magnitude illustration rather than a precise model.
