The Scopes trial, formally The State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes and commonly known as the Scopes Monkey Trial, was an American legal case in July 1925 in which John Scopes, a high-school teacher, was accused of violating Tennessee's Butler Act, which outlawed the teaching of human evolution in public schools. The trial was deliberately staged in the small town of Dayton to attract publicity: the American Civil Liberties Union had offered to defend anyone prosecuted under the Act, and local civic leaders hoped the case would put Dayton on the map.

The trial

The prosecution was assisted by William Jennings Bryan, three-time Democratic presidential candidate and a leading voice of the anti-evolution movement; the defense included the celebrated trial lawyer Clarence Darrow. The trial became a contest over science and religion rather than Scopes's factual guilt. In the trial's most famous episode, Darrow called Bryan to the stand as an expert on the Bible and subjected him to a searching examination on literal interpretation — conducted outdoors, because of fears the courtroom floor would collapse under the crowds. The jury convicted Scopes after minutes of deliberation, and he was fined $100.

Aftermath

Bryan died five days after the trial ended. In 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the Butler Act but overturned Scopes's conviction on the technicality that the judge, not the jury, had set the fine — and recommended the case be dropped. The Butler Act itself remained in force until 1967. Although the defense technically lost, press coverage — notably H. L. Mencken's scathing reports — portrayed the anti-evolution cause as backward, and textbook publishers quietly reduced coverage of evolution for decades.

In popular culture

The trial was dramatised in the 1955 play Inherit the Wind and its 1960 film adaptation, which used the case as an allegory for McCarthyism. The broader conflict resurfaced in later American court cases over evolution and creationism, including Epperson v. Arkansas (1968), Edwards v. Aguillard (1987), and Kitzmiller v. Dover (2005).
