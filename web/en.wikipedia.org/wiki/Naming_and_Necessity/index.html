Naming and Necessity is a 1972 book by the philosopher Saul Kripke, based on three lectures delivered at Princeton University in 1970. It is one of the most influential works of twentieth-century analytic philosophy, reviving metaphysical questions about necessity and essence within the philosophy of language and reshaping debates about reference, identity, and the mind–body problem.

Main arguments

Kripke argues against descriptivist theories of names associated with Frege, Russell, and Wittgenstein, according to which a proper name is synonymous with a cluster of descriptions. Instead, he proposes that names are rigid designators: they pick out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. A name's reference is fixed by an initial "baptism" and then passed along a causal-historical chain of uses, not by descriptions speakers associate with it — a picture often called the causal theory of reference.

Kripke further distinguishes metaphysical from epistemic modalities: some truths are necessary but a posteriori (known only empirically), such as "Hesperus is Phosphorus" and "water is H2O", while some contingent truths are knowable a priori, such as the length of the standard metre. If theoretical identifications like heat and molecular motion are true, he argues, they are necessarily true. Applied to philosophy of mind, the argument challenges identity theories: because pain seems impossible to separate from its immediate felt quality, the apparent contingency of "pain = C-fibre stimulation" cannot be explained away as easily as in the heat case.

Influence

The lectures transformed the philosophy of language, helped launch the "new theory of reference", and spurred essentialism's return to mainstream metaphysics. Together with related work by Hilary Putnam and David Kaplan, Kripke's arguments underlie semantic externalism.
