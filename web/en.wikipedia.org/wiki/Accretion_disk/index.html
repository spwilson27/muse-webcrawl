Accretion disk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An accretion disk is a structure — usually a circumstellar disk — formed by diffuse material in orbital motion around a massive central body. The central body is typically a young star, a protostar, a white dwarf, a neutron star or a black hole. Gravity causes material in the disk to spiral inward; friction and compression heat it until it radiates thermal energy, observed across the electromagnetic spectrum from infrared (in young stellar objects) to X-rays and gamma rays (around black holes and neutron stars). Accretion disks are ubiquitous in astrophysics and are central to theories of planet formation, binary-star evolution and active galactic nuclei.

Physics

Gas in the disk orbits at nearly the Keplerian speed, which increases inward. Adjacent rings therefore shear against one another, and some effective viscosity converts orbital energy to heat while driving angular momentum outward and mass inward. Molecular viscosity is far too small to explain observed accretion rates; the leading mechanism is turbulence produced by the magnetorotational instability, identified by Balbus and Hawley in 1991, in which a weak magnetic field coupling faster inner gas to slower outer gas becomes unstable and generates sustained turbulence. The standard thin-disk model of Shakura and Sunyaev (1973) parametrises the unknown viscosity with the dimensionless α parameter and predicts a multicolour blackbody spectrum, hotter at smaller radii.

Temperature and emission

In the thin-disk model the effective temperature falls with radius roughly as r−3/4. Disks around stellar-mass black holes peak in X-rays; those around supermassive black holes peak in the ultraviolet (the "big blue bump" of quasars); protoplanetary disks shine in the infrared, partly from reprocessed starlight. Many disks also launch collimated jets along their rotation axes, and the hottest inner regions emit broadened spectral lines used to weigh the central object.

Types

  • Protoplanetary disks around young stars, the birthplaces of planets — closely related to, but distinguished from, the accretion disks of compact objects.
  • Disks in X-ray binaries and cataclysmic variables, fed by a companion star, prone to outbursts and state changes.
  • AGN disks around supermassive black holes, extending to light-days or more.
  • Radiatively inefficient flows (such as advection-dominated accretion flows) at very low or very high accretion rates, which are geometrically thick and dim for their inflow rate.
