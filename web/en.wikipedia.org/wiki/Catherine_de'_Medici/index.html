Catherine de' Medici (Italian: Caterina de' Medici; 13 April 1519 – 5 January 1589) was an Italian (Florentine) noblewoman born into the Medici family. She was Queen of France from 1547 until 1559 by marriage to Henry II, and the mother of three French kings — Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III. The years during which her sons reigned, 1559–1589, are known in France as "the age of Catherine de' Medici", because of her extensive, if at times varying, influence in the political life of the country.

Early life and marriage

Catherine was the daughter of Lorenzo II de' Medici, Duke of Urbino, and Madeleine de La Tour d'Auvergne. Orphaned in infancy, she was raised in Florence and Rome under the protection of her Medici relatives, including Pope Clement VII, who arranged her marriage in 1533 to Henri, Duke of Orléans, the future Henry II. For a decade she was overshadowed at court by her husband's mistress, Diane de Poitiers, but her position was secured by the births of ten children after years of infertility.

Power and the Wars of Religion

After Henry II's accidental death in 1559 and the brief reign of Francis II, Catherine became regent for the young Charles IX in 1560 and remained the dominant political figure of the court. Facing the French Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, she pursued a shifting policy of compromise and conciliation, including the Edict of January (1562) and the Colloquy of Poissy, punctuated by recourse to force. Her reputation was permanently damaged by the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed after the wedding of her daughter Margaret to Henry of Navarre; the extent of her responsibility remains debated by historians. She died at Blois in January 1589, months before the assassination of her last surviving son, Henry III, ended the Valois dynasty.

Patronage and legacy

Catherine was a major patron of the arts, sponsoring poets, musicians and architects; building projects associated with her include the Tuileries Palace and the Château de Chenonceau's bridge gallery. Later tradition, especially Protestant and romantic-era literature, cast her as a sinister Italian schemer, though modern historians generally depict a pragmatic ruler attempting to preserve the crown amid civil war.
