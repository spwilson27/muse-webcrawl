Anzac Cove (Turkish: Anzak Koyu) is a small cove on the Aegean coast of Turkey's Gallipoli peninsula, famous as the landing site of the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps (ANZAC) on 25 April 1915, the first day of the land phase of the Gallipoli campaign.

The landing

The cove, about 600 metres long and hemmed in by steep eroded cliffs and ridges, was not the intended landing place: the covering force was put ashore roughly two kilometres north of the planned beach at Gaba Tepe, probably through a combination of an uncharted current and navigational error. From about 4:30 a.m., waves of rowing boats brought the 3rd Australian Brigade ashore into the dark; men scrambled up the slopes — Plugge's Plateau, Russell's Top, The Nek — against scattered Ottoman defenders until Mustafa Kemal's 57th Regiment counter-attacked and sealed the perimeter.

The beachhead

For the next eight months the cove was the cramped heart of the Anzac position: a supply port of jetties and dumps, dugouts terraced into the hillsides, and shrapnel-swept tracks — Shrapnel Valley ran inland from it. Water was short, flies and disease rampant, and Turkish shells ("Beachy Bill" from the Olive Grove) plagued the beach. The August offensive aimed from here at the heights of Chunuk Bair while a new landing opened at Suvla Bay to the north. Anzac was finally evacuated on the nights of 19–20 December 1915.

Commemoration

The Turkish government officially recognised the name "Anzac Cove" in 1985, at Australia's request — a gesture reciprocated by the naming of Atatürk memorials in Canberra and Wellington. Each Anzac Day a dawn service is held at the cove's commemorative site; on the 75th anniversary in 1990 and the centenary in 2015, thousands of Australians and New Zealanders attended, and veterans' ashes are among those scattered in its cemetery-lined hills.
