Salmon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Salmon is the common name for several species of ray-finned fish in the family Salmonidae, prized as food fish. Atlantic salmon and the Pacific salmons (chinook, coho, sockeye, pink and chum) are famous for migrating from the ocean up rivers to spawn in the freshwater streams where they hatched.

Life cycle

Salmon are anadromous: they hatch in gravel nests (redds) in streams, migrate to sea to grow for one to several years, then navigate home — apparently by smell — to spawn. Pacific salmon die after spawning once; their carcasses feed forests and wildlife. The journey involves leaping rapids and waterfalls, and the fish transform dramatically in colour and shape.

Fisheries and farming

Wild salmon fisheries are economically and culturally central around the North Pacific and North Atlantic, and salmon are sacred and staple foods for Indigenous peoples. Atlantic salmon is now mostly farmed — salmon aquaculture, led by Norway and Chile, exceeds two million tonnes a year — raising issues of sea lice, escapes and feed. Wild stocks face dams, warming rivers and overfishing; many populations are endangered.
