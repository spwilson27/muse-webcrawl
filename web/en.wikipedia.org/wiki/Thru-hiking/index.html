Thru-hiking

Thru-hiking, or through-hiking, is the act of hiking an established long-distance trail end-to-end continuously, typically completing the entire route within a single season. The term is most commonly associated with the long trails of the United States, such as the Appalachian Trail, the Pacific Crest Trail, and the Continental Divide Trail, the three routes that together make up the so-called Triple Crown of Hiking.

Thru-hiking differs from section hiking, in which a trail is completed in separate segments over months or years, and from day hiking. A thru-hiker carries all necessary gear in a backpack, resupplying food and supplies in trail towns at intervals, and typically covers 20 to 30 miles (32 to 48 km) per day over a period of several months.

History

Long-distance hiking grew in popularity during the twentieth century as major trail systems were completed. The Appalachian Trail was conceived in 1921 and completed in 1937. Earl Shaffer is credited with the first reported thru-hike of the Appalachian Trail in 1948. In 1970, Ed Garvey's hike and subsequent book helped popularize thru-hiking, and the number of attempts grew steadily in the following decades. The completion of the Pacific Crest Trail and Continental Divide Trail gave rise to the Triple Crown challenge.

Gear and logistics

Thru-hikers place great emphasis on lightweight equipment, a philosophy known as ultralight backpacking. Reducing base weight—the weight of gear excluding food, water, and fuel—is considered essential for sustaining high daily mileage over months. Hikers typically resupply every three to ten days, either by purchasing food in towns near the trail or by mailing supply boxes to themselves. Many adopt "trail names," nicknames given by fellow hikers, and rely on the support of "trail angels," volunteers who provide food, rides, or lodging.

Notable trails

Beyond the American Triple Crown trails, popular thru-hiking routes include the Te Araroa in New Zealand, the Camino de Santiago routes in Europe, and the Great Divide Trail in Canada. In the United Kingdom, end-to-end walking of routes such as Land's End to John o' Groats is a similar tradition.
