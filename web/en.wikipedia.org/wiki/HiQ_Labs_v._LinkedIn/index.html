hiQ Labs v. LinkedIn

CourtU.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
DecidedSeptember 9, 2019; April 18, 2022
IssueWeb scraping and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act

hiQ Labs, Inc. v. LinkedIn Corp. is a United States legal case concerning web scraping and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA). The data analytics company hiQ Labs scraped publicly available profiles from LinkedIn to build workplace-analytics products; LinkedIn demanded that it stop and blocked its access, arguing that continued access violated the CFAA's prohibition on accessing a computer "without authorization".

Litigation history

hiQ sued for declaratory relief, and in 2017 the district court granted a preliminary injunction allowing it to continue accessing public profiles. In 2019 the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that scraping publicly available data likely does not constitute access "without authorization" under the CFAA, because the statute's concept of authorization presupposes a permission requirement such as a password gate. The Supreme Court granted certiorari, then in June 2021 — following its decision in Van Buren v. United States — vacated and remanded the case for reconsideration. In April 2022 the Ninth Circuit largely reaffirmed its original reasoning and the preliminary injunction.

Settlement

The parties settled in December 2022. LinkedIn prevailed on its breach-of-contract counterclaims based on hiQ's creation of fake accounts, and hiQ agreed to a permanent injunction and the destruction of scraped data; hiQ, which had ceased operating, stipulated to a judgment of about $500,000.

Significance

The case is a leading authority on the legality of scraping public websites in the United States and on the interpretation of "without authorization" in the CFAA, frequently cited in debates over data portability, competition and automated data collection.
