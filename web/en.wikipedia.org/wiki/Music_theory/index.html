Music theory is the study of how music works: the structures, systems and relationships by which sounds are organised into compositions and understood by listeners. It encompasses the analysis of pitch, rhythm, harmony, melody, form and notation, and includes both prescriptive traditions (how music "should" be written within a style) and descriptive analysis of actual works.

Fundamentals

Theory begins with the elements of sound: pitch (highness or lowness), duration, dynamics and timbre. Pitches are organised into scales and modes; the distance between pitches is measured in intervals; combinations of simultaneous pitches form chords. In Western tonal music the major and minor scales, the circle of fifths, key signatures and functional harmony — the pull of dominant to tonic — provide the basic grammar taught in conservatoires. Rhythm and metre organise durations; melody is a succession of pitches shaped by contour and phrasing; counterpoint governs the combination of independent melodic lines, codified from Renaissance polyphony through the species exercises of Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum (1725).

History

Formal theory is ancient: Pythagorean tuning derived intervals from simple ratios; Greek writers classified modes; Boethius transmitted Greek theory to the Latin Middle Ages, when music stood in the quadrivium beside arithmetic, geometry and astronomy. Medieval and Renaissance theorists (Guido d'Arezzo, Zarlino) built notation and modal systems; the Baroque era codified thoroughbass and then functional harmony, systematised by Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722). Nineteenth-century theory (Riemann, Schenker in the early 20th century) sought deep structures in the tonal masterworks, while the 20th century added set theory for atonal music, serial analysis, and theories of popular music and jazz.

Non-Western and contemporary theory

Other cultures theorise music on different axes: Indian theory organises melody by raga and rhythm by tala, described in texts from the Natya Shastra onward; Arab and Turkish theory uses the maqam system of melodic modes with microtonal intervals. Modern music theory also overlaps with music psychology, acoustics and computational analysis, and remains contested territory: whether theory describes universal perception or the conventions of particular repertoires is a standing debate.
