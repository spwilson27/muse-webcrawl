E. H. Harriman

Born: Edward Henry Harriman, 20 February 1848, Hempstead, New York
Died: 9 September 1909 (aged 61), Orange County, New York
Occupation: Railroad magnate, financier

Edward Henry Harriman (February 20, 1848 – September 9, 1909) was an American financier and railroad magnate, who, through the management of the Union Pacific Railroad, became one of the most powerful railroad magnates in United States history and a leading figure on Wall Street. At the time of his death he controlled a railroad empire of some 23,000 miles (the Union Pacific and Southern Pacific systems) and was a dominant force in American finance.

Early life and career

Harriman was born in Hempstead, Long Island, the son of an Episcopal clergyman. He left school at fourteen to work on Wall Street as an office boy, bought a seat on the New York Stock Exchange at twenty-two, and built a successful brokerage and investment banking career, specialising in railroad reorganizations. His rebuilding of the Illinois Central Railroad in the 1880s established his reputation for turning distressed rail properties into profitable ones.

The Union Pacific

In 1897 Harriman joined the syndicate that bought the bankrupt Union Pacific Railroad out of receivership and became chairman of its executive committee. He modernized the line — rebuilding track, reducing grades, ordering standardized equipment in huge quantities — and made it one of the best-run and most profitable railroads in the country. In 1901 he acquired control of the Southern Pacific Railroad, creating a combined system stretching from the Midwest to the Pacific.

Northern Pacific corner and Northern Securities

In 1901 Harriman, backed by Jacob Schiff of Kuhn, Loeb, fought James J. Hill and J. P. Morgan for control of the Northern Pacific Railway. The speculative "corner" in Northern Pacific stock briefly drove its price to $1,000 a share and triggered a market panic (the Panic of 1901). The combatants compromised by forming the Northern Securities Company holding company, which the U.S. Supreme Court ordered dissolved in Northern Securities Co. v. United States (1904), a landmark antitrust decision.

Later years

President Theodore Roosevelt's administration investigated Harriman's methods, and the Interstate Commerce Commission criticised his financial practices, though he remained a towering figure. In 1899 he financed the Harriman Alaska Expedition, a celebrated scientific voyage that documented the Alaskan coast. He died at his estate, Arden, in New York in 1909, leaving a fortune estimated at $70–100 million; his widow Mary used much of it for philanthropy, including founding the E. H. Harriman Award and funding the Pacific Coast Borax ventures and state parks. His sons W. Averell Harriman and E. Roland Harriman became prominent in finance and public life.
