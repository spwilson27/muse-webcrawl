Lahn

The Lahn is a river in western Germany, a right-bank tributary of the Rhine. It is 245.6 kilometres (152.6 mi) long, rising in the Rothaar Mountains of North Rhine-Westphalia and flowing through Hesse and Rhineland-Palatinate before joining the Rhine at Lahnstein, just south of Koblenz. Its valley, the Lahntal, is known for its historic towns, half-timbered architecture, castles, and the cathedral city of Limburg an der Lahn.

Course

The Lahn rises at the Lahnkopf (624 m) near Netphen in the Rothaar Mountains. It flows east past Biedenkopf to Marburg, then south to Gießen, where it turns southwest and passes Wetzlar. Downstream it meanders through a deepening valley past Weilburg — where a ship tunnel built in 1844–47 is a notable engineering monument — and Runkel to Limburg. From Limburg it flows westward through the Taunus and Westerwald foothills past Diez, Nassau, and Bad Ems, reaching the Rhine at Lahnstein. Major tributaries include the Dill, the Weil, the Ohm, and the Aar.

Navigation and use

The lower 143 km of the Lahn, from below Gießen to the Rhine, was made navigable in the 19th century with locks and weirs, and remains a federal waterway used mainly by recreational craft. The Lahn valley cycle path (Lahnradweg), following the river for roughly 245 km, is one of Germany's popular long-distance cycling routes. The valley is also known for the marble quarried near Villmar ("Lahn marble"), used in buildings including the Empire State Building's lobby panels and several German cathedrals.

History

The region was settled from early times, and the river formed a natural corridor and boundary — in the Roman period the Limes ran near its lower course. Medieval towns along the Lahn grew wealthy on trade, wine, and mining in the surrounding hills. Marburg and Gießen became university towns; the landgraves of Hesse left castles and residences throughout the valley. Goethe's stay in Wetzlar in 1772 inspired The Sorrows of Young Werther.
