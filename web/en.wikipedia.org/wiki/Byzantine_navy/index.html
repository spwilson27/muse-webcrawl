The Byzantine navy was the naval force of the Byzantine Empire. Like the state it served, it was a direct continuation of the Imperial Roman Navy, but played a far greater role in the defence and survival of the empire than its earlier predecessor. While the fleets of the Roman Empire faced few great naval threats, operating as a policing force vastly inferior in power and prestige to the legions, the sea became vital to the very existence of the Byzantine state, which several historians have called a "maritime empire."

History

The first great test came with the Arab conquests of the 7th century: the loss of Syria and Egypt threatened Constantinople itself, which twice survived Arab sieges (674–678 and 717–718) thanks in part to its fleet and to the newly invented incendiary weapon Greek fire, projected from siphons mounted on the Empire's warships. The navy was organised into a central imperial fleet at Constantinople and provincial squadrons, above all the maritime themes such as the Kibyrrhaiotai, who guarded the approaches against Arab raiders from Cilicia and Syria.

Zenith and decline

Under the Macedonian dynasty the navy supported the great reconquests of the 10th century — Crete was retaken in 961 by Nikephoros Phokas with a huge amphibious expedition — and Byzantine fleets dominated the eastern Mediterranean. From the later 11th century, however, emperors increasingly relied on the navies of Venice and Genoa, granting trading privileges in exchange for naval aid; the standing fleet withered, a decline lamented by contemporaries. After the Fourth Crusade shattered the empire in 1204, the successor states maintained only modest squadrons, and the restored empire of the Palaiologoi never rebuilt a serious fleet: by the 15th century the "navy" consisted of a handful of ships, and Constantinople's defence in 1453 depended on Italian vessels.

Ships and tactics

The characteristic Byzantine warship was the dromon, a galley developed from the Roman liburna with lateen sails, a fully decked hull, and a spur at the waterline for disabling enemy vessels rather than the ramming beak of antiquity. Dromons came in several sizes, from small galeai to large pampiloi with crews of 200–300 men. Catapults and cranes hurled stones, javelins, and pots of Greek fire or quicklime; the most feared weapon remained the bow-mounted siphon spraying Greek fire, an incendiary that burned on water. Tactics emphasised formation discipline, deception, and intelligence, codified in naval manuals such as Leo VI's Naumachica.
