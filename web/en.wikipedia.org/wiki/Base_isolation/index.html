TypeEarthquake engineering technique
Principal devicesLead-rubber bearings, friction pendulum bearings
EffectLengthens natural period; typically to 2–4 seconds

Base isolation, also known as seismic isolation, is a technique of earthquake engineering that decouples a building or other structure from its foundation so that ground motion during an earthquake is not fully transmitted to the structure above. By interposing flexible bearings between the building and the ground, the system greatly lengthens the structure's natural period of vibration and, with added damping, reduces the accelerations it experiences — shifting the design goal from merely preventing collapse to keeping the building functional and its contents undamaged.

How it works

The commonest devices are lead-rubber bearings — pads of alternating rubber and steel plates with a central lead plug that yields to dissipate energy — and friction pendulum bearings, in which the building slides on concave surfaces so that gravity both permits movement and re-centres the structure after a quake. A base-isolated building moves largely as a rigid body, swaying slowly on its isolators, while inter-storey drift and floor accelerations fall to a fraction of those in a fixed-base building. A clear "moat" around the building allows for the expected displacement, which can reach several tens of centimetres.

History and applications

The concept is old — ideas for sliding foundations appear in ancient and early twentieth-century sources — but modern base isolation began with the work of New Zealand engineer Bill Robinson, inventor of the lead-rubber bearing, first used in the William Clayton building in Wellington in 1981. The technique has since been applied to thousands of buildings in Japan, the United States, China, Italy and elsewhere, and is especially favoured for hospitals, emergency centres, museums and historic structures that must remain operational or protect irreplaceable contents. Notable examples include the Utah State Capitol, San Francisco City Hall (retrofitted), and Apple Park in California. Isolated buildings have performed well in major earthquakes, including the 1995 Kobe earthquake, where two isolated buildings near the epicentre survived with little damage.

Limitations

Base isolation is less effective for tall, flexible buildings and on very soft soils that amplify long-period motion, and it requires periodic inspection and eventual replacement of the bearings. It adds construction cost, which studies generally find is partly or wholly offset by avoided earthquake losses.
