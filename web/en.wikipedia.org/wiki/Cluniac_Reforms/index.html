Cluniac Reforms

The Cluniac Reforms (also called the Benedictine Reform) were a series of changes within medieval Western monasticism in the Western Church, focused on restoring the traditional monastic life as prescribed by the Rule of Saint Benedict. The reforms are named after the Abbey of Cluny in Burgundy, founded in 910, and spread through a network of monasteries dependent on Cluny.

Origins

Cluny was founded by William I, Duke of Aquitaine, whose charter of foundation unusually placed the abbey directly under the protection of the Pope rather than under local secular or episcopal control, and guaranteed the monks' free election of their abbot. Under a succession of long-lived and capable abbots — Berno, Odo, Odilo and Hugh of Semur — Cluny became renowned for an elaborate and solemn liturgy, strict observance, and freedom from lay interference.

Spread and principles

Daughter houses were organised as priories subject to the abbot of Cluny, creating one of the first true monastic "orders": at its height in the 12th century, the Cluniac network comprised well over a thousand houses across France, Spain, Italy, England and beyond. The reforms emphasised liturgical prayer (especially for the dead), clerical celibacy, opposition to simony, and the independence of the Church from lay control — ideas that fed into the wider Gregorian Reform of the 11th century.

Decline

From the 12th century, newer orders such as the Cistercians criticised Cluniac wealth and liturgical elaboration, and Cluny's influence waned. The abbey church — the largest church in Christendom until St Peter's Basilica in Rome — was largely demolished after the French Revolution.
