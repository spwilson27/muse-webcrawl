Virial expansion

The virial expansion expresses the pressure of a many-particle system in equilibrium as a power series in the density. It is a systematic correction to the ideal gas law, in which successive terms account for interactions between pairs, triples, and larger clusters of particles. The expansion was introduced into statistical mechanics by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1901 as an empirical equation of state, and later given a rigorous microscopic foundation by Joseph Edward Mayer in 1937.

Formulation

For a gas of number density ρ = N/V, with compressibility factor Z = p/(ρkBT), the virial expansion is:

Z = 1 + B2(T)ρ + B3(T)ρ2 + B4(T)ρ3 + ⋯

The temperature-dependent coefficients Bn(T) are called virial coefficients: B2 is the second virial coefficient, arising from pairwise interactions; B3 from three-body clusters, and so on. In molar form, the expansion is often written with coefficients B, C, D… in powers of the inverse molar volume:

pVm/(RT) = 1 + B/Vm + C/Vm2 + D/Vm3 + ⋯

Second virial coefficient

For a classical gas with pairwise potential u(r), the second virial coefficient is given by an integral over the Mayer f-function:

B2(T) = −2π ∫0∞ [e−u(r)/(kBT) − 1] r2 dr

For a hard-sphere gas of diameter σ this reduces to B2 = 2πσ³/3, four times the volume of a single sphere per particle pair — the same excluded-volume correction that appears in the van der Waals equation, with which the virial expansion agrees to second order in density. The temperature at which B2 = 0 is called the Boyle temperature, at which the gas mimics ideal behaviour over a range of low densities.

Applications

Virial expansions are used as accurate equations of state for gases at low and moderate densities, in the theory of polymer solutions (where the second virial coefficient measures solvent quality), in colloid science through the osmotic virial expansion, and in astrophysics. The expansion converges only below the critical density and fails to describe phase transitions directly, though truncations at high order can capture much of the thermodynamics of real fluids.
