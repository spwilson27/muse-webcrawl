Roman mile


Definition: 1,000 paces (mille passus)
Length: 1,000 passus ≈ 1,480 metres (0.92 modern miles)
Subdivisions: 5,000 Roman feet; 8 stadia

The Roman mile (Latin: mille passus, "a thousand paces", abbreviated m.p. or MP) was the standard unit of distance in ancient Rome and across the Roman Empire. It consisted of 1,000 passus (paces), where a pace was the distance from the heel of one foot to the heel of the same foot after a full stride — five Roman feet. The Roman mile therefore measured 5,000 Roman feet, approximately 1,480 metres, somewhat shorter than the modern statute mile of 1,609 metres.

Milestones

Distances on Roman roads were measured from the Milliarium Aureum, the "Golden Milestone" erected by Augustus in the Roman Forum, and marked by cylindrical stone milestones along the roads, typically inscribed with the name and titles of the reigning emperor, the distance to the nearest significant city, and sometimes the official responsible for the road's upkeep. Thousands of milestones survive across the former empire and are a major source for Roman geography and imperial titulature.

Legacy

The word "mile" in English and its cognates in other European languages derive from mille passus. After Rome's fall, local miles varied widely by region — the medieval London mile, the Scots mile, and the Irish mile all differed — until standardisation: in Britain the statute mile of 5,280 feet was defined in 1593. In much of continental Europe, the term survived in league-like units before being displaced by the kilometre after the adoption of the metric system.
