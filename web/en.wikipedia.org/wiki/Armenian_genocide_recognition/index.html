Armenian genocide recognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Armenian genocide recognition is the formal public affirmation, by governments, legislatures, and international bodies, that the mass killing and deportation of Armenians by the Ottoman Empire in 1915–1917 constituted genocide. The overwhelming consensus of historians and genocide scholars affirms this characterisation, while the Republic of Turkey — successor state to the Ottoman Empire — officially disputes the term, arguing that the deaths occurred amid the broader upheaval of the First World War.

Background

Beginning in April 1915, Ottoman authorities arrested Armenian intellectuals in Constantinople and organised massacres and death marches toward the Syrian desert. Estimates of the dead range from several hundred thousand to around 1.2–1.5 million. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word "genocide", cited the Armenian case as a formative example when framing the 1948 Genocide Convention.

Recognition by states

Uruguay was the first country to formally recognise the genocide, in 1965. As of the mid-2020s more than thirty countries have done so, including France (2001), Canada (2004), Germany (2016), and the Netherlands. The United States Congress passed recognition resolutions in 2019, and on 24 April 2021 President Joe Biden formally recognised the Armenian genocide, breaking with decades of presidential avoidance of the term. The European Parliament, the Council of Europe, and several sub-national legislatures have also adopted recognition resolutions. Turkey has typically responded with diplomatic protests and temporary recalls of ambassadors.

Significance

Recognition campaigns, led by the worldwide Armenian diaspora, are framed by supporters as a matter of historical justice and prevention; the Armenian Apostolic Church canonised the victims collectively in 2015, on the centenary. Commemorations centre on 24 April, marked at the Tsitsernakaberd memorial in Yerevan.
