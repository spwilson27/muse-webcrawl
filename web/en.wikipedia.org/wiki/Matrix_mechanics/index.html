Matrix mechanics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of the rocky island of Heligoland, where Heisenberg developed matrix mechanics in June 1925]
Heligoland, where Heisenberg's 1925 breakthrough occurred.

Matrix mechanics is the formulation of quantum mechanics created by Werner Heisenberg, Max Born and Pascual Jordan in 1925. It was the first complete and correct formulation of quantum mechanics, preceding Erwin Schrödinger's wave mechanics by months; the two were soon shown equivalent (Schrödinger, 1926), and Paul Dirac's transformation theory unified them.

Heisenberg's starting point was radical: to build mechanics only from observable quantities — the frequencies and intensities of spectral lines — abandoning unobservable electron orbits. The resulting position and momentum are represented by infinite arrays (matrices) of transition amplitudes whose non-commutativity, pq − qp = (h/2πi)I, is the algebraic heart of quantum theory and the source of the Uncertainty principle. Max Born recognised Heisenberg's multiplication rule as matrix algebra from his student days — reportedly remarking that the matrices had been 'dead' formalism to him until then.

The 'three-man paper' and aftermath

Born and Jordan formalised the scheme in September 1925; the joint Born–Heisenberg–Jordan 'Dreimännerarbeit' (three-man paper) completed the theory that year, including perturbation methods and angular momentum. Pauli immediately used it to derive the hydrogen spectrum from first principles — the calculation that convinced many physicists the strange new mechanics was right. In modern teaching the Schrödinger picture dominates, but matrix mechanics survives as the Heisenberg picture (operators evolving, states fixed) and as the natural language of quantum optics and spin systems.
