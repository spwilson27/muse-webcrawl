King's Men (playing company)

The King's Men was the acting company to which William Shakespeare belonged for most of his career. Formerly known as the Lord Chamberlain's Men under Elizabeth I, the company took its new name when James I became its patron in 1603. It was the foremost theatrical company in Jacobean London and continued under Charles I until the theatres were closed by Parliament in 1642.

History

The company was formed in 1594 as the Lord Chamberlain's Men, built around the actor-manager Richard Burbage and including Shakespeare as actor, playwright and shareholder ("sharer"). It performed at The Theatre and the Curtain before building the Globe Theatre on Bankside in 1599. On the accession of James I in 1603, the company was granted a royal patent and renamed the King's Men; its members became Grooms of the Chamber.

Venues and repertory

The company played summers at the Globe and, from 1608, winters at the indoor Blackfriars Theatre, allowing higher prices and year-round performance. Its repertory included Shakespeare's plays — the great tragedies and late romances were written for this company — and works by Ben Jonson, John Fletcher (who succeeded Shakespeare as house playwright), John Webster and Philip Massinger.

Personnel and organisation

The King's Men was organised as a joint-stock company of sharers who divided profits and owned the playbooks and costumes. Leading members included Richard Burbage, its tragedian; John Heminges and Henry Condell, who later published the First Folio of Shakespeare's plays in 1623; and the clown Robert Armin.

End

When Parliament ordered the theatres closed at the outbreak of the English Civil War in 1642, the King's Men effectively ceased to exist; the Globe was demolished in 1644.
