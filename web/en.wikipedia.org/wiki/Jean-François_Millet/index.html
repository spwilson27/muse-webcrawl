Jean-François Millet (4 October 1814 – 20 January 1875) was a French artist and one of the founders of the Barbizon school in rural France. Millet is noted for his paintings of peasant farmers and can be categorized as part of the Realism movement. His monumental, dignified treatment of agricultural labor — in works such as The Gleaners (1857) and The Angelus (1857–59) — made him one of the most famous and widely reproduced French painters of the nineteenth century.

Life and work

Millet was born to a farming family in Gruchy, Normandy, and grew up working the land before local patrons sent him to study art in Cherbourg and then Paris. After early struggles and conventional portraits and mythologies, the revolution of 1848 and a government commission led him to his true subject: rural labor. In 1849 he settled in the village of Barbizon on the edge of the Forest of Fontainebleau, where he lived for the rest of his life alongside Théodore Rousseau and other Barbizon painters.

The Winnower (1848) and The Sower (1850) established his peasant imagery; The Gleaners, shown at the Salon of 1857, alarmed conservative critics, who read its large-scale depiction of the poorest rural women as a socialist statement. The Angelus — two peasants pausing in a field at the evening bell, heads bowed over a basket of potatoes — became one of the most famous images in the world, endlessly reproduced, and after Millet's death the subject of a famous auction battle that led France to treat it as a national treasure. His late pastels and drawings of landscape and labor are prized for their luminous economy.

Influence and legacy

Millet profoundly influenced Vincent van Gogh, who copied his compositions obsessively as a young artist, as well as Camille Pissarro, Georges Seurat, and generations of realist and social-realist painters worldwide — including the American poet Edwin Markham, whose "The Man with the Hoe" (1899) was inspired by Millet's painting. The bulk of his work is in the Musée d'Orsay, Paris, with a major group in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, reflecting his early popularity with American collectors.
