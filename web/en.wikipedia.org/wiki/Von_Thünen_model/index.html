Von Thünen model

The von Thünen model is an early economic model of agricultural land use, developed by the German economist Johann Heinrich von Thünen in his work The Isolated State (1826). It predicts how agricultural land around a central market city will be arranged in concentric rings, based on the balance of market prices, transport costs, and land rent.

Assumptions

Von Thünen imagined an "isolated state": a single city at the center of a featureless, uniformly fertile plain, with no navigable rivers or roads other than simple wagon transport, and farmers who act rationally to maximize profit. Transport cost increases with distance and with the weight and perishability of the product.

The rings

Under these assumptions, land use organizes into rings around the city:

  • First ring: intensive farming and dairy production — perishable, high-value goods such as milk and vegetables, which must reach market quickly.
  • Second ring: forest, providing heavy firewood and timber whose transport cost is high relative to its value.
  • Third ring: extensive field crops such as grain, grown with crop rotation.
  • Fourth ring: ranching and livestock grazing, using land of the lowest rent at the greatest distance.

Beyond the fourth ring, land is too far from the city for transport costs to leave any profit, and it remains wilderness. The model introduced the concept of economic rent declining with distance from a market and became a founding work of location theory and spatial economics, influencing later models of urban land use.
