Building material is any material used for construction purposes. Naturally occurring substances — clay, stone, sand, wood — have been used since prehistory, and manufactured products now range from brick, cement and concrete to steel, glass, plastics and engineered composites. Materials are selected for structural performance, durability, fire and thermal behaviour, cost, availability and environmental impact.

Principal materials

Concrete — cement binder with aggregate and water — dominates by volume for its strength in compression, versatility and low cost; reinforced with steel bars it forms reinforced concrete. Steel provides frames for tall buildings and long spans. Timber, long used for houses and roofs, has revived in engineered forms (glulam, cross-laminated timber) that allow multi-storey wooden buildings with low embodied carbon. Masonry (brick, stone and concrete block) provides durable walls. Gypsum products, such as the wallboard made famous by US Gypsum, line most modern interiors. Glass, aluminium, copper, bitumen, plastics and insulation materials (mineral wool, foams, cellulose) complete the typical palette.

Properties and standards

Materials are specified by standards covering strength, fire resistance, thermal conductivity (U-values), acoustic performance, moisture behaviour and durability under weathering. Testing laboratories certify products, and building codes prescribe minimum performance for safety and energy efficiency.

Sustainability

Materials account for a large share of construction's environmental footprint: cement production alone causes roughly 8% of global CO₂ emissions. Responses include supplementary cementitious materials (fly ash, slag), recycled aggregates and steel, design for disassembly and reuse, and bio-based materials such as straw, hemp-lime and even charcoal-derived biochar additives under study for carbon-storing concrete. Embodied-carbon accounting and environmental product declarations are becoming standard procurement requirements.

History

Early builders used mudbrick, thatch and timber; monumental cultures quarried stone and fired brick. Roman concrete, lost for centuries, was surpassed only by Portland cement (patented 1824), whose combination with steel created the modern built environment.
