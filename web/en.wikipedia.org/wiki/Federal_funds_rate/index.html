Federal funds rate

In the United States, the federal funds rate is the interest rate at which depository institutions lend reserve balances to one another overnight on an uncollateralised basis. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets a target range for the rate, and the Federal Reserve implements it through its monetary policy tools; it is the principal lever of U.S. monetary policy.

Mechanism

Banks must hold reserves, and those with excess balances lend to those needing funds in the federal funds market. The weighted-average market rate is the effective federal funds rate (EFFR), published daily by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Since 2008 the FOMC has announced a target range (25 basis points wide), steered primarily by interest on reserve balances (IORB) and the overnight reverse repurchase agreement (ON RRP) facility.

Importance

Changes in the federal funds rate ripple through the economy: they influence the prime rate, mortgage and credit-card rates, business borrowing costs, exchange rates and asset prices. Renowned episodes include the Volcker era peak of about 19–20% in 1981 to crush inflation, cuts to near zero during the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, and the rapid hiking cycle of 2022–23 against post-pandemic inflation.
