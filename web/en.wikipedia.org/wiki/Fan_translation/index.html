A fan translation is an unofficial translation of a creative work — most commonly a video game, manga, anime, film, or television series — produced by fans rather than by the copyright holder or a licensed publisher. Fan translations arise where no official translation exists, where an official version is considered unsatisfactory, or where fans wish to make a work available in a language the rights holder has neglected.

Types

Fansubs

Fansubs (a portmanteau of "fan" and "subtitles") are subtitled versions of audiovisual media made by fans. The practice began in the 1980s in the anime fandom of North America and Europe, where fans exchanged VHS tapes with subtitles produced using early home computers and genlock equipment. With the spread of broadband internet in the 2000s, fansub groups adopted digital workflows — translation, timing, typesetting, encoding, and quality checking performed by distributed volunteers — and releases moved to file-sharing networks and later streaming sites.

Scanlation

Scanlation (scan + translation) is the fan translation of comics, especially manga: pages are scanned, the original text is removed ("cleaning" and "redrawing"), and translated text is lettered in its place. Scanlation groups typically follow an ethic of dropping a series once it is licensed in their language, though enforcement varies widely.

Video games

Fan translations of video games usually involve modifying the game data (a "ROM hack" or patch for a disc image) so that text, and sometimes graphics, appear in the target language. Notable fan translation projects have covered role-playing games never released outside Japan in the 1990s and 2000s; some efforts took a decade or more and required bespoke reverse engineering of text compression and encoding.

Legal status

Fan translations are derivative works and are generally produced without the authorisation of the copyright holder, making their distribution an infringement in most jurisdictions. Rights holders have historically tolerated much fan translation on the grounds that it serves markets they do not, and sometimes because it builds audiences later monetised by official releases. The rise of simultaneous global publication and licensed simulcasts and simulpubs in the 2010s reduced unmet demand, and some publishers hired fan translators into professional localisation work.

Influence

Fan translation communities pioneered practices later adopted professionally, including honourific retention, translator's notes, styled subtitle typesetting, and rapid-turnaround subtitling pipelines. They have also preserved access to works that remain officially untranslated.
