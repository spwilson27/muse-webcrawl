The Federalist Papers is a collection of 85 articles and essays written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay under the collective pseudonym "Publius", to promote ratification of the United States Constitution. They appeared in New York newspapers between October 1787 and August 1788.

Contents

Hamilton wrote the plurality (about 51 essays), Madison about 29, and Jay five before illness intervened; disputed authorship of some essays was largely resolved by statistical analysis (Mosteller and Wallace, 1964) in Madison's favour. Famous numbers include No. 10 (Madison, on controlling faction in an extended republic), No. 51 (Madison, separation of powers and checks and balances — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"), No. 70 (Hamilton, an energetic unitary executive) and No. 78 (Hamilton, judicial review and an independent judiciary).

Influence

Though written as advocacy, the papers became the classic commentary on the Constitution, frequently cited by the US Supreme Court in interpreting its meaning. They stand with the Constitution itself and the Bill of Rights — and later amendments such as the Thirteenth Amendment — as foundational texts of American government.
