Effective demand

The Keynesian cross: equilibrium where aggregate demand meets output

Effective demand is a concept in macroeconomics denoting the level of demand for goods and services that is actually backed by the ability to pay and that determines output and employment in an economy. It is central to the economics of John Maynard Keynes.

Keynes's formulation

In The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), Keynes defined effective demand as the point where the aggregate demand function intersects the aggregate supply function — the expected proceeds that just make it worthwhile for entrepreneurs to provide a given level of employment. If expected demand falls short, firms cut production and jobs, and the economy can settle into an equilibrium with involuntary unemployment, contradicting Say's law.

Earlier writers

Keynes credited forerunners including Thomas Malthus, who argued against Ricardo that general gluts were possible, and later Michał Kalecki independently developed a similar theory of demand-determined output.

Implications

Because output is demand-determined in the short run, the principle of effective demand underlies fiscal policy prescriptions — government spending can raise demand and employment — and the multiplier analysis of consumption and investment.
