Late Ottoman genocides

Late Ottoman genocides is a scholarly term and periodisation used by many historians of genocide studies to refer to the collective treatment of the Armenian genocide (1915–1917), the Greek genocide (1914–1922), and the Assyrian genocide (Sayfo, 1914–1920), in which the government of the Ottoman Empire, under the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), systematically exterminated its Christian subjects during and after the First World War. Taken together, these campaigns killed an estimated several million people — roughly 600,000 to 1.5 million Armenians, 300,000 to 900,000 Ottoman Greeks, and 200,000 to 300,000 Assyrians — and effectively eliminated the empire's ancient Christian populations from most of Anatolia and its other territories.

Background

During the nineteenth century the multi-ethnic, multi-religious Ottoman Empire steadily declined, losing territories in the Balkans and Caucasus. Massacres and expulsions of Muslims from former Ottoman lands drove large refugee populations into Anatolia, while the empire's Christian communities — Armenians, Greeks, and Assyrians — increasingly became objects of suspicion, especially amid rising Turkish nationalism under the CUP, which seized power in 1908 and established a one-party regime in 1913. Earlier episodes of violence, including the Hamidian massacres of 1894–1896 and the Adana massacre of 1909, foreshadowed the later campaigns.

The genocides

During the First World War, the Ottoman government, fearing collaboration between its Christian subjects and the empire's enemies, ordered the deportation of these populations to the Syrian desert and elsewhere, accompanied by massacres, death marches, starvation, and the destruction of communities. The Armenian genocide began with the arrest of Armenian intellectuals in Constantinople on 24 April 1915 and proceeded through mass deportations and killings across Anatolia and Syria. Ottoman Greek communities in Pontus, western Anatolia, and eastern Thrace were subjected to deportations, labour battalions, and massacres from 1914 until the end of the Greco-Turkish War in 1922, culminating in the burning of Smyrna and the compulsory population exchange of 1923. Assyrian Christians in southeastern Anatolia and northwestern Persia were massacred beginning in 1914–1915 in what is remembered in Syriac as the Sayfo, the "Year of the Sword."

Historiography

Scholars such as Benny Morris and Dror Ze'evi, in their book The Thirty-Year Genocide: Turkey's Destruction of Its Christian Minorities, 1894–1924 (2019), argue that the campaigns of 1914–1924, viewed together with the massacres of the 1890s, formed a single continuous process of ethnic and religious cleansing that reduced the Christian population of Anatolia from around 20 per cent to about 2 per cent. Many genocide scholars treat the late Ottoman cases as connected episodes driven by a common ideology and perpetrator regime, and the International Association of Genocide Scholars has passed resolutions affirming the Armenian, Greek, and Assyrian genocides. The Republic of Turkey officially rejects the characterisation of these events as genocide, arguing instead that the deaths resulted from wartime conditions, civil conflict, and relocation measures — a position widely described by scholars as genocide denial.
