Blood feud

Blood feuds were common in clan-based societies

A blood feud is a feud, usually between families, in which the cycle of killings continues for a long period. Blood feuds, sometimes known as vendettas, were common in societies with a weak rule of law (or where the state did not consider itself responsible for resolving such disputes), and where family honour was regarded as more important than all else. The feuding parties consider that the only way to avenge a death or serious insult is by killing a member of the offending family, which in turn provokes a retaliatory killing, and so on.

History

Blood feuds were part of the legal systems of ancient Germanic peoples, governed by codes such as the wergild — a monetary compensation that could settle the debt of honour and end the feud. In medieval and early modern Europe, vendettas were especially associated with Corsica, Sicily, Sardinia, Montenegro, and the Scottish Highlands. In Albania, the traditional code known as the Kanun of Lekë Dukagjini regulated gjakmarrja (blood-taking) in fine detail, and feuds persisted into the twenty-first century in some northern districts.

Famous feuds

Among the most famous American blood feuds was that between the Hatfield and McCoy families along the Tug Fork of the Big Sandy River between West Virginia and Kentucky in the late nineteenth century, which claimed more than a dozen lives and became a byword for senseless clan violence. The feud between the Montagues and Capulets in Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet is a fictional example drawn from Italian vendetta culture.

Ending feuds

Feuds could be settled by payment of compensation (blood money, such as the Arabic diya), by marriage between the families, by ritual reconciliation ceremonies, or by the intervention of religious authorities or the state. Modern states generally suppress blood feuds by monopolising legitimate violence through criminal law.
