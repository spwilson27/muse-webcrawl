Penman–Monteith equation

Evapotranspiration measurement over a vegetated surface.

The Penman–Monteith equation estimates evapotranspiration from a vegetated surface by combining the surface energy balance with aerodynamic and surface (stomatal) resistances to vapour transfer. It builds on Howard Penman's 1948 combination equation for evaporation from open water and vegetation, extended by John Monteith in 1965 to account explicitly for the resistance to water loss imposed by plant canopies.

Formulation

The equation is commonly written as:

λE = [ Δ(Rn − G) + ρa cp (es − ea) / ra ] / [ Δ + γ (1 + rs/ra) ]

where λE is the latent heat flux, Rn net radiation, G soil heat flux, Δ the slope of the saturation vapour pressure curve, γ the psychrometric constant, ρa cp the volumetric heat capacity of air, (es − ea) the vapour pressure deficit, ra the aerodynamic resistance, and rs the bulk surface resistance of the canopy. Setting rs = 0 recovers evaporation from a wet surface.

FAO-56 standard

The Food and Agriculture Organization's Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 (1998) adopted a standardized Penman–Monteith method, defining a hypothetical reference crop (grass 0.12 m tall, surface resistance 70 s/m, albedo 0.23) so that reference evapotranspiration, ET0, can be computed from standard weather data—solar radiation, temperature, humidity, and wind speed. The FAO-56 method is the internationally recommended standard for irrigation scheduling and water balance studies, replacing earlier empirical formulas such as the modified Penman and Blaney–Criddle methods.

Applications

The equation is used in hydrology, agricultural meteorology, land-surface schemes within climate and weather models, and remote-sensing estimates of evaporation. Its physical basis makes it more transferable between climates than purely temperature-based methods, at the cost of requiring more input data.
