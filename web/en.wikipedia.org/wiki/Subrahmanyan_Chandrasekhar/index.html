Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar (19 October 1910 – 21 August 1995) was an Indian-American theoretical physicist who made major contributions to the understanding of the structure and evolution of stars. He shared the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics with William Fowler 'for his theoretical studies of the physical processes of importance to the structure and evolution of the stars'.

On the sea voyage from India to England in 1930, the 19-year-old Chandrasekhar combined relativity with quantum statistics to show that a white dwarf star above a certain mass — the Chandrasekhar limit, about 1.4 solar masses — cannot support itself against gravity and must collapse further, a conclusion that pointed toward neutron stars and black holes. The result was publicly demolished by Arthur Eddington, the era's dominant astrophysicist, in a controversy that shaped Chandrasekhar's career.

Career and work

Chandrasekhar took his doctorate at Cambridge (1933) and moved to the University of Chicago in 1937, remaining there until his death. Working in decade-long campaigns, he systematically rebuilt whole subjects: stellar structure, radiative transfer (his 1950 monograph remains a classic), stochastic processes, hydrodynamic stability, and the mathematical theory of black holes (1983), each closed with a definitive treatise. He edited the Astrophysical Journal for nearly two decades and trained generations of astrophysicists, among them Nobel laureates.

Legacy

The Chandra X-ray Observatory, launched by NASA in 1999, is named after him ('Chandra' also means 'moon' in Sanskrit). The Chandrasekhar limit is now understood as the dividing line in stellar death: stars below it end as white dwarfs; above it, their cores collapse to neutron stars or black holes, and accreting white dwarfs pushed over the limit explode as Type Ia supernovae — the standard candles that revealed the accelerating universe.
