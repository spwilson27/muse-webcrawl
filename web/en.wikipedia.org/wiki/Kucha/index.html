Kucha or Kuche (also Kuchar; Chinese: 龜茲, Qiūcí) was an ancient Buddhist kingdom located on the branch of the Silk Road that ran along the northern edge of the Taklamakan Desert in the Tarim Basin, in present-day Kuqa County, Xinjiang, China. For centuries it was the most populous oasis in the region and a major centre of Buddhism, music and trade.

History

Kucha enters Chinese records in the Han dynasty, when it was the largest of the "Thirty-six Kingdoms of the Western Regions". It alternately acknowledged Chinese suzerainty and the power of steppe confederations such as the Xiongnu and the Turks. In 384 CE the general Lü Guang conquered Kucha and brought back the celebrated monk-translator Kumārajīva — himself a native of Kucha, of an Indian father and Kuchean mother — to China, where his translations of Mahayana sutras shaped East Asian Buddhism. The pilgrim Xuanzang visited around 630 CE and described some 100 monasteries with 5,000 monks studying Sarvāstivāda and increasingly Mahayana doctrine.

Tang China established effective control after 648–49, making Kucha one of the Four Garrisons of Anxi. Following the An Lushan rebellion the region passed to Tibetan and then Uyghur dominance, and Kucha's Buddhist, Tocharian-speaking culture gradually gave way to Islam and Turkic languages by the Mongol era.

Culture

The Kuchean language, known today as Tocharian B, was an Indo-European language written in a Brahmi-derived script. Kucha was famed across Asia for its orchestral music — Kuchean ensembles performed at the Sui and Tang courts and influenced Chinese and Japanese court music. Nearby stand the Kizil Grottoes, some of the earliest Buddhist cave temples in China (3rd–8th centuries), with murals in a distinctive Indo-Iranian style.
