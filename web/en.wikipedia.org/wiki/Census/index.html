Census

A census is the procedure of systematically acquiring, recording and calculating population information about the members of a given population. The term is used mostly in connection with national population and housing censuses, though other censuses — of agriculture, businesses, or wildlife — are also conducted. The United Nations defines a population census as the total process of collecting, compiling, evaluating, analysing and publishing demographic, economic and social data pertaining to all persons in a country at a specified time.

History

Enumerations were conducted in ancient Egypt, China, and Rome — the Roman census, administered by censors, assessed property and citizenship for taxation and military service. The word derives from Latin censere, "to assess". Sweden began continuous national population statistics in 1749, often credited as the first modern census tradition, and the first United States census was held in 1790, as required by the Constitution to apportion seats in the House of Representatives.

Methods

Traditional censuses visit every household; many countries now use postal or online self-response, administrative registers, or rolling surveys. Results are used for political apportionment, allocation of public funds, planning of services, and social research. The UN recommends censuses be taken at least every ten years.

Challenges

Censuses face undercounts of marginalised groups, cost escalation, privacy concerns, and questions about sensitive items such as ethnicity, religion, and citizenship.
