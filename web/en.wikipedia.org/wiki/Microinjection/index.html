Microinjection

Microinjection is the delivery of substances into a living cell or organism using a very fine glass micropipette, typically under a microscope with the aid of micromanipulators. Volumes injected range from picolitres to nanolitres. The technique is a direct physical method of introducing DNA, RNA, proteins, dyes, or organelles into individual cells, and is widely used in developmental biology, genetics, and assisted reproduction.

Applications

The most famous use is pronuclear injection: DNA is injected into the pronucleus of a fertilized egg to create transgenic animals, a method pioneered in mice around 1980–81 and still a standard route to transgenic lines. Microinjection is also used to introduce CRISPR reagents, messenger RNA, morpholinos, and antibodies into embryos of model organisms such as zebrafish, frogs (Xenopus), and the nematode C. elegans, where injection into the gonad produces transgenic offspring.

In human medicine, intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), introduced in 1992, injects a single sperm directly into an oocyte and has become a routine treatment for male infertility. Microinjection is also used in somatic cell nuclear transfer (cloning), in electrophysiology, and to load individual cells with fluorescent indicators.

Technique

Pipettes are pulled from glass capillaries to tips of about 0.5–5 µm. The target cell is immobilized by a blunt holding pipette under gentle suction, the injection pipette penetrates the membrane, and pressure (from a microinjector pump) delivers the solution. Success depends heavily on operator skill; survival and delivery rates vary widely with cell type. Compared with bulk methods such as electroporation or viral transduction, microinjection is slow and low-throughput but offers precise delivery into defined compartments of single cells.
