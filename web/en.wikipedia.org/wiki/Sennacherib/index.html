Sennacherib

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Sennacherib enthroned receiving the submission of Lachish, from his palace reliefs]

Sennacherib (reigned 705–681 BC) was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, son and successor of Sargon II, famous for his campaigns in the Levant — above all the 701 BC invasion of Judah that besieged Hezekiah in Jerusalem ('like a bird in a cage', in Sennacherib's own annals) without taking the city, an event told from both sides in the Bible and Assyrian inscriptions — and for the destruction of Babylon (689 BC) after years of Babylonian revolt. He rebuilt Nineveh as the empire's greatest capital, girdled by walls and fed by canals and the aqueduct at Jerwan, and was assassinated in 681 BC by two of his sons — an event the Bible also records — triggering the succession war won by Esarhaddon.

Levant and Babylon

The 701 campaign crushed the Phoenician coast, Philistia (Ekron relieved too late) and Judah's fortified cities — Lachish's siege, immortalised in the reliefs from his palace (now in the British Museum) and in the excavated siege ramp, stands as antiquity's best-documented assault. Jerusalem survived: whether by Hezekiah's tribute (both accounts agree it was paid), plague in the Assyrian camp (the biblical angel narrative; Herodotus' mice and gnawed bowstrings), or strategic limits is irrecoverable. Babylon, by contrast, Sennacherib annihilated after his eldest son's murder there: the city razed, its god Marduk's statue carried off, the Euphrates diverted over the ruins — an impiety that haunted his house.

Nineveh and murder

Sennacherib's Nineveh — walls some 12 km around, the 'Palace Without Rival' with its programme of reliefs and lamassu colossi, gardens watered from the Jerwan canals (a system some have proposed as the true site of the 'Hanging Gardens') — was the ancient world's most engineered capital. His murder (681 BC) in a temple, by sons the sources name differently (Adrammelech and Sharezer in Kings; the Babylonian chronicle tradition fuller), and the parricides' flight to Armenia, opened Esarhaddon's reign of Babylonian restoration. Byron's poem ('The Assyrian came down like the wolf on the fold') fixed his name in English verse.
