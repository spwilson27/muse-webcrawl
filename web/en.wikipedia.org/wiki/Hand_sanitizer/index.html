Hand sanitizer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hand sanitizer (also known as hand antiseptic, hand disinfectant, hand rub or handrub) is a liquid, gel or foam applied to the hands to kill many viruses, bacteria and other microorganisms. Most hand sanitizers are alcohol-based, typically containing ethanol, isopropyl alcohol or n-propanol at concentrations of 60 to 95 percent, which denature microbial proteins and inactivate many enveloped viruses.

History

Alcohol has been used as an antiseptic since at least the late nineteenth century. Alcohol-based hand rubs became standard in European hospitals in the twentieth century, and gel formulations for consumers appeared in the 1980s and 1990s; the brand Purell, launched in 1997, popularised the product in the United States. During the COVID-19 pandemic, demand surged worldwide and distillers and other manufacturers converted production to sanitizer amid shortages.

Uses and effectiveness

Health authorities such as the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommend washing with soap and water when hands are visibly soiled, and using an alcohol-based sanitizer of at least 60 percent alcohol otherwise. Sanitizers are less effective against some pathogens, including norovirus and the spores of Clostridioides difficile, and do not remove chemical contaminants such as pesticides.

Safety

Alcohol-based sanitizers are flammable and can cause poisoning if ingested, particularly by children. Regulators have warned against products contaminated with methanol, which is toxic. Emollients such as glycerol are commonly added to reduce skin dryness from frequent use.
