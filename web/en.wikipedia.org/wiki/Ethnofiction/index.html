Ethnofiction
Genre: Film / ethnography
Key figures: Jean Rouch, Robert Flaherty
Related: Docufiction, Visual anthropology, Cinéma vérité

Ethnofiction is a genre of film that blends documentary and fiction in the study of people and cultures, in which subjects portray themselves in improvised or staged roles based on their own lives. The term is most closely associated with the French filmmaker and anthropologist Jean Rouch, who developed the form from the 1950s onward.

Origins

Precursors include Robert J. Flaherty's Man of Aran (1934), in which islanders re-enacted traditional practices. Rouch, working in West Africa, asked his subjects to perform fictionalised versions of themselves, as in Moi, un noir (1958), in which young migrants in Abidjan narrate invented personas drawn from their real experience, and Jaguar (1967). Rouch described the camera as a catalyst that provokes a reality rather than merely recording one.

Method and influence

Ethnofiction involves the filmed community in constructing the narrative, blurring the line between observer and participant. It was a major influence on cinéma vérité—Rouch's Chronique d'un été (1961, with Edgar Morin) applied the approach in Paris—and on later participatory and reflexive approaches in visual anthropology and docufiction.
