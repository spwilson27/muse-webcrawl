In mathematics, an algebraic number field (or simply number field) is a field extension of the field of rational numbers Q such that the extension has finite degree. Equivalently, a number field is a field that contains Q and has finite dimension when considered as a vector space over Q. Number fields are the central objects of study in algebraic number theory.

By the primitive element theorem, every number field can be written as Q(θ) for some algebraic number θ, obtained by adjoining a root of an irreducible polynomial over Q. The degree of the field equals the degree of that polynomial.

Ring of integers

Inside a number field K lies its ring of integers OK, consisting of the elements of K that are roots of monic polynomials with integer coefficients (the algebraic integers in K). Unlike the integers in Z, the ring OK need not have unique factorization of elements. However, OK is a Dedekind domain, so every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. The failure of unique factorization of elements is measured by the ideal class group; its size, the class number, equals 1 precisely when OK is a unique factorization domain.

Examples

  • Quadratic fields Q(√d) for squarefree d ≠ 1 — the simplest nontrivial number fields; their arithmetic depends delicately on d.
  • Cyclotomic fields Q(ζn), obtained by adjoining roots of unity, central to the theory of Fermat's Last Theorem and to class field theory.
  • The rationals Q itself, of degree 1, whose ring of integers is Z.

Structure theorems

The Dirichlet unit theorem describes the unit group of OK: the units form a finitely generated abelian group whose rank is r1 + r2 − 1, where r1 is the number of real embeddings and r2 the number of pairs of complex embeddings. The discriminant of the field measures ramification of rational primes, and Minkowski's bound shows that every ideal class contains an ideal of small norm, proving that the class number is finite. The analytic class number formula relates the class number to the residue of the Dedekind zeta function at s = 1.

Ramification

A rational prime p can split, remain inert, or ramify in OK; only finitely many primes ramify — exactly those dividing the discriminant. The decomposition of primes is governed, in Galois extensions, by Frobenius elements and is the starting point for Chebotarev's density theorem and class field theory, which describes abelian extensions of number fields in terms of the fields themselves.
