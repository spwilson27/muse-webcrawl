DefinitionSugar bound by a glycosidic bond to a non-carbohydrate group (aglycone)

In chemistry, a glycoside is a molecule in which a sugar is bound by its anomeric carbon to another group via a glycosidic bond. The sugar part is the glycone; the non-sugar part is the aglycone or genin. Glycosides occur abundantly in plants, where they often serve as stored, inactive forms of active compounds that are released by hydrolysis when tissue is damaged.

Classification

Glycosides are classified by the atom linking sugar to aglycone: O-glycosides (oxygen), S-glycosides (sulfur, e.g. glucosinolates in mustard), N-glycosides (nitrogen, e.g. nucleosides), and C-glycosides (carbon, resistant to hydrolysis). They are also grouped by aglycone chemistry, as in the classes below.

Important classes

  • Cardiac glycosides (digoxin, digitoxin, ouabain) from foxglove and other plants inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase and are used, with care, in heart failure.
  • Cyanogenic glycosides (amygdalin in bitter almonds, linamarin in cassava) release hydrogen cyanide on hydrolysis.
  • Anthraquinone glycosides (sennosides from senna) are stimulant laxatives.
  • Saponins are triterpene or steroidal glycosides that foam in water.
  • Steviol glycosides from Stevia rebaudiana are intensely sweet and used as sugar substitutes.
  • Salicin from willow bark is metabolised to salicylic acid, the basis of aspirin.

Hydrolysis

Glycosides are cleaved by heating with dilute acid or by specific glycosidase enzymes. In the body, gut bacteria hydrolyse many plant glycosides, releasing the active aglycone — a step essential to the action of several traditional medicines.
