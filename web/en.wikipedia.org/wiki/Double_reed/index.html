A double reed is a type of reed used to produce sound in various wind instruments. It consists of two pieces of cane (or synthetic material) bound together that vibrate against each other when air is blown between them, generating the instrument's tone. In the Western orchestra, the principal double-reed instruments are the oboe, cor anglais (English horn), bassoon and contrabassoon; many folk and historical instruments — shawms, crumhorns, the duduk, the zurna, the hne, and the chanters of many bagpipes — also use double reeds.

Construction

Orchestral double reeds are fashioned mostly by the players themselves from cane of the giant reed Arundo donax, grown chiefly in southern France. For the oboe, a piece of cane is gouged, folded, shaped and tied onto a metal staple (tube) with thread, then the tip is scraped thin with a reed knife so it vibrates freely; the whole oboe reed is only about 7 cm long. Bassoon reeds are larger, tied onto a crook (bocal) and similarly scraped. Because cane is hygroscopic and wears out, players maintain a rotating stock of reeds, adjusting them for response, pitch and tone — reed-making is considered a core professional skill, and few players use commercial reeds unmodified.

Playing characteristics

The player places the reed between the lips (for oboe and bassoon, both lips fold over the teeth in an embouchure) or, in many folk traditions, entirely within the mouth or against a pirouette. Air pressure sets the two blades beating together many hundreds of times per second, chopping the airflow into pulses that excite the air column. Double-reed instruments are notably loud and penetrating — an adaptation to outdoor use in their folk ancestors — and the oboe's carrying tone is why orchestras traditionally tune to its A. The instruments are mostly conical-bored, overblowing at the octave.

History

Double-reed pipes are ancient: the Greek aulos and Roman tibia, played in pairs, used double reeds, as did Egyptian and Mesopotamian instruments. The medieval shawm family spread across Europe and Asia along trade routes, giving rise to descendants from the Moroccan ghaita to the Chinese suona. The modern oboe emerged in mid-17th-century France at the court of Louis XIV, refined from the shawm by makers such as the Hotteterre family.
