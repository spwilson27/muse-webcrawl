Five Ways (Aquinas)

AuthorThomas Aquinas
WorkSumma Theologiae, I, q. 2, a. 3
Datec. 1265–1274
SubjectArguments for the existence of God

The Five Ways (Latin: quinque viae) are five arguments for the existence of God set out by the 13th-century theologian Thomas Aquinas in his Summa Theologiae. They are a posteriori arguments, reasoning from observed features of the world to a first cause whom Aquinas identifies as God.

The arguments

  1. Motion: things in motion must be moved by something else; an infinite regress is impossible, so there must be an unmoved mover.
  2. Efficient cause: nothing causes itself; the chain of causes requires a first, uncaused cause.
  3. Contingency: contingent beings depend on something necessary; there must be a being necessary of itself.
  4. Degrees: things are more or less good, true and noble, implying a maximum that is the cause of all such perfections.
  5. Design (teleology): natural bodies act toward ends as if guided, implying an intelligent director.

Criticism and influence

The arguments drew on Aristotle and on Jewish and Islamic predecessors such as Maimonides and Avicenna. They have been debated for centuries: Hume and Kant raised influential objections, while Thomist philosophers continue to defend versions of the first three ways. The arguments remain standard material in philosophy of religion.
