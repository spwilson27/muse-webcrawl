Hyperoperation

In mathematics, the hyperoperation sequence is an infinite sequence of arithmetic operations (called hyperoperations) that starts with addition (the zeroth or first operation depending on convention) and continues with multiplication, exponentiation, tetration, pentation, and so on. Each operation in the sequence is defined as the iteration of the preceding one. The sequence was generalized from constructions of Wilhelm Ackermann and Reuben Goodstein in the 1920s–1940s and underlies the Ackermann function.

Definition

The nth hyperoperation, written Hn(a, b) or a[n]b in Goodstein's notation, satisfies

Hn+1(a, b) = Hn(a, Hn+1(a, b − 1))

with H0(a, b) = b + 1 (succession), H1 addition, H2 multiplication, H3 exponentiation, and H4 tetration (a tower of b copies of a). Thus 2[4]4 = 2222 = 65,536, while 3[4]3 exceeds 7.6 trillion digits in its tower height relationships and is astronomically large.

Knuth's up-arrow notation

Donald Knuth's up-arrow notation, introduced in 1976, expresses the same hierarchy: a↑b is exponentiation, a↑↑b is tetration, a↑↑↑b is pentation, and each additional arrow raises the level. The notation is used to write very large numbers such as Graham's number, which requires 64 layers of arrows.

Properties and extensions

Hyperoperations beyond exponentiation are not associative or commutative, and their results grow faster than any primitive recursive function; the Ackermann function built from them is a standard example of a computable function that is not primitive recursive. Mathematicians have proposed extending hyperoperations to non-integer and even complex ranks ("fractional tetration"), though no single extension commands consensus, and defining inverses (hyper-logarithms and hyper-roots) leads to the theory of superfunctions and Abel functions.
