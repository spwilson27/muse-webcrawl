Mediterranean Revival architecture

Mediterranean Revival is an architectural style introduced in the United States in the waning 19th century, peaking in popularity during the 1920s and 1930s. The style drew from the architecture of Mediterranean countries — Spain, Italy, Portugal, southern France, and the coastal regions of North Africa and the Levant — blending elements of Spanish Colonial, Spanish Renaissance, Italian Renaissance, Moorish, and Byzantine architecture.

Characteristics

Typical features include stuccoed wall surfaces (often in warm pastel tones), low-pitched red clay tile roofs, arched windows and doorways, wrought-iron balconies and grilles, decorative tile work, courtyards with fountains, loggias, and asymmetrical façades. Interiors frequently feature exposed wooden ceiling beams, tiled floors, and ornate plasterwork.

History

The style gained prominence in Florida and California, where the climate and Spanish colonial heritage made it especially appropriate. Architects such as Addison Mizner in Florida — whose work shaped Palm Beach and Boca Raton — and Bertram Goodhue, whose designs for the 1915 Panama–California Exposition in San Diego popularized Spanish Colonial Revival, were leading figures. The Florida land boom of the 1920s produced extensive Mediterranean Revival suburbs, hotels, and movie palaces.

The style was applied to houses, hotels, railway stations, churches, and public buildings. It declined after the Second World War as modernist and ranch styles became dominant, but surviving examples are widely preserved in historic districts in Florida, California, Texas, and Arizona.

Related styles

  • Spanish Colonial Revival — emphasizing Spanish and Mexican colonial precedents.
  • Moorish Revival — drawing on Islamic architecture of Spain and North Africa.
  • Italian Renaissance Revival — more formal classical Italian models.
  • Mission Revival — based on the Spanish missions of the American Southwest.
