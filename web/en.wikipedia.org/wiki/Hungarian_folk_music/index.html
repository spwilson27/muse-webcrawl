Hungarian folk music

Hungarian folk music (Hungarian: magyar népzene) encompasses the traditional music of Hungary and of Hungarian communities in neighbouring regions including Transylvania, Slovakia, and Moldavia (the Csángó people). It is notable for its distinctive pentatonic scales, characteristic rhythms, and a rich repertoire of dance music.

Styles and repertoire

The pioneering ethnomusicologists Béla Bartók and Zoltán Kodály began collecting Hungarian folk songs in the early 1900s, recording thousands of melodies on wax cylinders in peasant villages. Bartók classified the repertoire into an "old style" of slow, rhythmically free (parlando rubato) songs based on anhemitonic pentatonic scales — a legacy he traced to ancient Finno-Ugric and Turkic connections across Eurasia — and a "new style" of later songs in strict tempo influenced by Western major and minor scales. Dance music (tánczene) includes the csárdás, the verbunkos (recruiting dance music), and circle and couple dances of Transylvania.

Instruments

The core ensemble is a string band led by a prímás (lead violinist), with second violin or viola (kontra) and double bass providing rhythmic accompaniment. The cimbalom, a large hammered dulcimer, is characteristic of professional urban "Gypsy bands" that performed Hungarian popular songs (magyar nóta) in cafés and restaurants. Rural instruments include the tekerőlant (hurdy-gurdy), the citera (zither), the tárogató, and the furulya (shepherd's flute).

Revival

In the 1970s the táncház ("dance house") movement revived village music and dance as participatory community events, led by musicians such as Ferenc Sebő and Béla Halmos and bands including Muzsikás and Kaláka. The táncház method of transmitting traditional culture was inscribed by UNESCO in its Register of Good Safeguarding Practices in 2011.
