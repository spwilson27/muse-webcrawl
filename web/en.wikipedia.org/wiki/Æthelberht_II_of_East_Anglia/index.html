Æthelberht II (Old English: Æðelbrihte; died 20 May 794), also known as Saint Ethelbert the King, was King of East Anglia from the late 770s until his death. He was beheaded by order of Offa of Mercia while visiting the Mercian royal court, and was soon venerated as a saint and martyr. Hereford Cathedral, where he was buried, is dedicated to him together with St Thomas of Hereford.

Reign

Little is known of Æthelberht's accession or family. His coinage — silver pennies of a type influenced by Offa's reformed currency, including one remarkable issue bearing the she-wolf suckling Romulus and Remus — indicates that East Anglia retained a degree of independence in the 790s, perhaps asserting freedom from Mercian overlordship. A unique gold coin or medallion in his name has also been reported.

Death and legend

According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Æthelberht was beheaded in 794. Later medieval accounts, embroidered by hagiographers such as Gerald of Wales, tell that he travelled to Offa's palace at Sutton Walls to marry Offa's daughter Ælfthryth, against the wishes of Queen Cynethryth, who is said to have persuaded Offa to have the young king murdered. The king's head, separated from his body, was said to have performed miracles on its journey to Hereford, revealing a spring and restoring sight to a blind man.

Cult

Æthelberht's body was buried at Hereford, and by the early ninth century a church dedicated to him stood there; the later cathedral continued the dedication. A second shrine at Westminster Abbey also claimed relics. He is commemorated on 20 May, and several churches, chiefly in Norfolk and Herefordshire, bear his dedication.
