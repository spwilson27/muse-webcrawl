Gay liberation

Gay liberation is a social and political movement that emerged in the late 1960s and flourished through the 1970s, urging lesbian, gay, bisexual and other non-heterosexual people to reject shame, come out publicly, and engage in direct action to counter societal prejudice. Its adherents sought not merely tolerance but a fundamental transformation of society's attitudes toward sexuality, gender and the family. The movement is generally considered the opening phase of the modern LGBT rights movement.

Background

In the decades before liberation, homosexuality was criminalised in most Western countries, classified as a mental disorder by psychiatric authorities, and treated as grounds for dismissal from government and military service. Early "homophile" organisations such as the Mattachine Society (founded 1950) and the Daughters of Bilitis (1955) pursued a cautious strategy of respectability and legal reform. By the late 1960s, influenced by the civil rights movement, feminism, and opposition to the Vietnam War, a younger generation of activists concluded that assimilationist tactics were insufficient.

Stonewall and the Gay Liberation Front

The movement is conventionally dated from the Stonewall riots of June 1969, when patrons of the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York City's Greenwich Village, resisted a police raid over several nights. Within weeks, activists founded the Gay Liberation Front (GLF), deliberately echoing the names of anti-colonial liberation movements. Chapters spread rapidly across the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and continental Europe.

On 28 June 1970, the first anniversary of Stonewall was marked by the Christopher Street Liberation Day march in New York and parallel demonstrations in other cities — the origin of the annual pride parades now held worldwide.

Ideas and tactics

The GLF and successor groups such as the Gay Activists Alliance combined consciousness-raising, "zaps" (confrontational protests targeting discriminatory institutions), dances, newspapers and communes. Liberationist thought linked gay oppression to capitalism, patriarchy and compulsory heterosexuality. A signature tactic was encouraging individuals to come out — to be openly gay to family, employers and the public — on the theory that visibility would dissolve prejudice.

The movement won an early landmark victory in 1973, when the American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders.

Legacy

By the mid-1970s the radical liberationist organisations had largely dissolved, their place taken by reform-oriented gay rights groups focused on anti-discrimination law, and by a growing commercial gay subculture. Nevertheless, the liberation era established enduring elements of LGBT politics: pride as a public affirmation, the language of coming out, coalition-building, and the insistence that sexual minorities were an oppressed group entitled to full equality.
