Thixotropy


A thixotropic fluid liquefies under shear

Thixotropy is a time-dependent shear-thinning property of certain gels and fluids. A thixotropic material is viscous under static conditions but flows — becoming less viscous — when shaken, agitated, or otherwise stressed, and it recovers its viscosity over time once the stress is removed. The word derives from the Greek thixis ("touch") and tropos ("turning").

Explanation

In many gels the microstructure consists of weak physical bonds that break down progressively under shear and rebuild at rest. Because the breakdown and rebuilding take finite time, viscosity depends not only on the instantaneous shear rate but also on the material's recent history: a plot of shear stress against shear rate forms a hysteresis loop.

Examples

  • Paints and inks — formulated to be thixotropic so they flow under the brush but do not drip afterward.
  • Ketchup and yoghurt — common food examples that thin when shaken or stirred.
  • Drilling muds — engineered so that cuttings stay suspended when circulation stops.
  • Clays — certain clay soils liquefy when disturbed, a factor in landslides and in "quick clay" disasters in Scandinavia and Canada.
  • Cytoplasm and synovial fluid — biological fluids with thixotropic behaviour.

Thixotropy vs. related phenomena

Thixotropy differs from simple shear thinning (pseudoplasticity), in which viscosity falls immediately with shear rate and recovers instantly. The opposite behaviour — thickening over time under stress — is called rheopexy or anti-thixotropy. Thixotropy should also not be confused with dilatancy, where viscosity increases with shear rate itself.
