Cost of capital

In economics and corporate finance, the cost of capital is the cost of a company's funds — the return it must pay to the investors who supply its debt and equity capital. From the investor's side it is an opportunity cost: the expected return foregone by investing in the firm rather than in alternatives of comparable risk. Firms use the cost of capital as a hurdle rate: a project creates value only if its expected return exceeds the cost of the capital used to finance it, and the rate serves as the discount rate in net present value and discounted cash flow valuation.

Components

The cost of debt is the effective interest rate a company pays on its borrowings, adjusted for the tax deductibility of interest: after-tax cost of debt = pre-tax yield × (1 − marginal tax rate). The cost of equity is unobservable directly and is usually estimated with the capital asset pricing model, re = rf + β(rm − rf), where rf is the risk-free rate, β measures the stock's sensitivity to the market, and the bracketed term is the market risk premium; alternatively the dividend growth model infers the cost of equity from the share price, expected dividend and growth rate.

Weighted average cost of capital

Because firms combine sources of finance, the standard summary measure is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC):

WACC = (E/V)·re + (D/V)·rd·(1 − T)

where E and D are the market values of equity and debt, V = E + D, and T is the marginal corporate tax rate. WACC is the standard discount rate for valuing a firm or a project whose risk matches the firm's overall risk.

Capital structure theory

The Modigliani–Miller theorem (1958) showed that, in frictionless markets, a firm's value — and hence its weighted cost of capital — is independent of how it is financed. Introducing corporate taxes makes debt attractive because of the interest tax shield, predicting a falling WACC with leverage, but the trade-off theory argues that expected bankruptcy and financial-distress costs rise with debt, producing an optimal capital structure at which WACC is minimised. The pecking order theory instead explains financing choices by information asymmetry, with firms preferring retained earnings first, then debt, then new equity.

Uses and limitations

Beyond project appraisal, regulators use an allowed cost of capital to set prices for utilities and other rate-of-return-regulated industries. Practitioners debate the estimation of beta, the equity risk premium and the appropriate capital structure weights; small changes in the assumed cost of capital can dominate a valuation's outcome, and a single firm-wide rate may misprice projects whose risk differs markedly from the firm's average.
