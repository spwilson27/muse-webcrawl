Diatomaceous earth

Diatomaceous earth, also known as diatomite or kieselguhr, is a naturally occurring, soft, siliceous sedimentary rock that can be crumbled into a fine white to off-white powder. It consists of the fossilised remains of diatoms, a type of hard-shelled microalgae.

Diatomaceous earth
CompositionMostly amorphous silica (SiO2·nH2O)
Particle size3 µm to more than 1 mm
UsesFiltration, mild abrasive, insecticide, absorbent, stabiliser for dynamite

Formation

Diatomite forms by the accumulation of siliceous frustules of dead diatoms in marine or lacustrine sediments. Major deposits occur in the United States (California), Denmark, France, Germany and China.

Uses

Its high porosity makes it an excellent filter aid for beer, wine, oils and swimming-pool water. Alfred Nobel used it to stabilise nitroglycerin in dynamite. As a mechanical insecticide, its sharp particles damage insect exoskeletons and absorb their lipids, causing death by dehydration; it is used in grain storage and against household pests. It also serves as a mild abrasive, an absorbent for spills, a soil conditioner and a source of plant-available silicon.

Safety

Food-grade material is low in crystalline silica, but inhalation of calcined grades containing cristobalite is a respiratory hazard and is regulated occupationally.
