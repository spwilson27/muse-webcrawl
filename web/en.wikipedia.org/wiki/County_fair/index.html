County fair

TypeAgricultural show / festival
Typical seasonLate summer and early autumn
OriginUnited States, early 19th century

A county fair is an annual public event, held mainly in the United States and Canada, at which the residents of a county gather to exhibit livestock and agricultural products, compete in contests, and enjoy entertainment. County fairs are typically held in late summer or early fall, around harvest time, on a permanent fairground with exhibition halls, barns, a grandstand and a midway.

History

The American county fair grew out of agricultural societies founded in the early 1800s; Elkanah Watson is often credited with organising early fairs in Berkshire County, Massachusetts, from 1811. The idea combined livestock shows, ploughing matches and displays of produce and domestic manufactures with speeches and prizes intended to spread improved farming methods. In Britain and Ireland, similar functions are served by county agricultural shows.

Exhibits and competitions

Classic fair events include judging of cattle, pigs, sheep, poultry and horses; 4-H and FFA youth competitions; displays of giant vegetables, preserves, baking, quilts and crafts; harness racing and equestrian events; and contests such as pie-eating, tractor pulls and demolition derbies. Ribbons — with blue for first place — are the traditional awards. State fairs are larger, statewide versions of the same institution; the State Fair of Texas and the Minnesota State Fair are among the largest fairs in the world.

Entertainment and food

Modern fairs add carnival rides and games on the midway, concerts, monster trucks and fireworks. Fair food is a celebrated genre of its own: corn dogs, funnel cakes, cotton candy, deep-fried novelties of every description, and regional specialities such as Minnesota's cheese curds or Iowa's pork chop on a stick. County fairs remain an important fixture of rural and small-town American life, though many have declined or diversified as agriculture's share of employment has shrunk.
