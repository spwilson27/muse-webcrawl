Horizontal coordinate system

Horizontal coordinate system
Also calledAlt-azimuth (altaz) coordinate system
CoordinatesAltitude (a), azimuth (A)
Fundamental planeThe observer's horizon
Reference directionsZenith and the north (or south) point of the horizon

The horizontal coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system that uses the observer's local horizon as the fundamental plane to define the position of a celestial object. It divides the sky into the upper hemisphere (visible) and the lower hemisphere (obstructed by the Earth) and expresses a body's position by two angles: altitude and azimuth.

Definitions

  • Altitude (a), sometimes called elevation, is the angle between the object and the observer's horizon, from 0° at the horizon to +90° at the zenith (directly overhead) and −90° at the nadir. Zenith distance is the complement, 90° − a.
  • Azimuth (A) is measured in the plane of the horizon from a reference direction — in modern astronomy and navigation usually north, increasing eastward — so that north is 0°, east 90°, south 180°, and west 270°. (Some traditions measure azimuth from the south.)

Properties

The system is intuitive and directly tied to pointing telescopes and theodolites — altazimuth mounts move in altitude and azimuth — but the coordinates of a fixed star change continuously with time as the Earth rotates, and depend on the observer's latitude and longitude. An object rises in the east, crosses the meridian at maximum altitude (culmination), and sets in the west. Atmospheric refraction raises apparent altitudes near the horizon by roughly half a degree, and must be corrected for precise work.

Conversions

Conversion to the equatorial coordinate system (hour angle H, declination δ) uses spherical trigonometry with the observer's latitude φ, for example: sin a = sin δ sin φ + cos δ cos φ cos H. The reverse conversion locates objects for observers and underpins the pointing models of computerised alt-az telescopes.

Uses

The horizontal system is used in naked-eye astronomy, satellite tracking, navigation, surveying, solar-energy engineering (solar position), and radar and antenna pointing. Its main limitation — time- and place-dependence — is why star catalogues use equatorial rather than horizontal coordinates.
