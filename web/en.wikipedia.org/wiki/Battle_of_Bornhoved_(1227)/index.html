The Battle of Bornhöved was fought on 22 July 1227 near Bornhöved in Holstein between the army of King Valdemar II of Denmark and a coalition of north German princes and free cities. The Danish defeat ended a generation of Danish expansion and supremacy along the southern shore of the Baltic Sea.

Background

In the early thirteenth century Denmark under Valdemar II controlled Holstein, Mecklenburg, Pomerania, the city of Lübeck, and had conquered Estonia in 1219. Danish dominance collapsed after Valdemar and his son were kidnapped in 1223 by Henry of Schwerin and held for ransom; the terms of his release required concessions that his German vassals and the cities soon repudiated. In 1226–27 a coalition formed to throw off Danish rule entirely, led by Count Adolf IV of Schauenburg and Holstein and supported by Lübeck, Hamburg, the Archbishop of Bremen and other princes.

The battle

The armies met on the heath at Bornhöved on the feast day of St Mary Magdalene. Fighting was long and bloody. According to later tradition, the turning point came when the Ditmarshers — peasant levies in the Danish service — deserted or changed sides (aided, legend said, by the sun breaking through and blinding the Danes after prayers to Mary Magdalene). The Danish army broke, and Valdemar was rescued from the field almost alone. Combined losses were reported in the thousands.

Aftermath

Bornhöved confirmed the independence of Lübeck as a free imperial city, restored Holstein to Adolf IV, and confined Danish power to Denmark itself (with Rügen and Estonia retained for a time). The battle opened the southern Baltic to German eastward expansion and the growth of the Hanseatic League's cities.
