The Manitoba Schools Question (French: La question des écoles du Manitoba) was a political crisis in the Canadian province of Manitoba between 1890 and 1897. It arose from the provincial government's abolition of public funding for Catholic schools and the removal of French as an official language of instruction, measures that struck at rights the largely French-speaking Catholic community believed were guaranteed by the Manitoba Act of 1870 and the British North America Act of 1867.

Background

When Manitoba entered Confederation in 1870, its population was roughly half French-speaking Catholic (largely Métis) and half English-speaking Protestant. The Manitoba Act protected denominational schools and the use of both English and French in the legislature and courts. Over the following two decades, immigration from Ontario transformed the province's demographics, and by 1890 French Catholics were a small minority.

The 1890 legislation

In 1890 the government of Premier Thomas Greenway passed legislation establishing a single non-denominational public school system, ending tax support for Catholic (and other denominational) schools, and an accompanying act made English the sole official language of the province. Archbishop Alexandre-Antonin Taché and the Catholic community challenged the laws in court.

Legal battles and federal intervention

The courts produced shifting results. The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council ultimately held in 1895 (in the Barrett and Brophy appeals) that while Manitoba could operate its own school system, Catholics had a right of appeal to the federal government, which had power to pass remedial legislation. The Conservative federal government of Sir Charles Tupper prepared a remedial bill in 1896, but it was filibustered by the Liberal opposition, and the ensuing election brought Wilfrid Laurier to power.

The Laurier–Greenway compromise

In November 1896 Laurier and Greenway reached a compromise: religious instruction could be given at the end of the school day where parents requested it, Catholic teachers could be hired where numbers warranted, and instruction in French (or another non-English language) was permitted under certain conditions. The settlement avoided coercive federal legislation and set a precedent for negotiated federal-provincial accommodation, though French-language rights in Manitoba remained restricted for decades afterwards; French was not restored as an official language of the legislature until required by the Supreme Court in 1979.
