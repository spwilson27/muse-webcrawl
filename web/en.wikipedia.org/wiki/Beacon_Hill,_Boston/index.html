Beacon Hill is a historic neighborhood in Boston, Massachusetts, covering approximately one square mile just west of downtown. It is known for its narrow, gaslit streets, Federal-style brick row houses, and its long association with Boston's political and social elite — the so-called "Boston Brahmins". The Massachusetts State House, designed by Charles Bulfinch and completed in 1798, crowns the hill and gives the neighborhood much of its character.

History

The area takes its name from a beacon placed on the summit in 1635 to warn of invasion. Originally one of three peaks (the "Trimountain"), the hill was reduced in height by about 60 feet during early 19th-century land-making projects, the earth being used to fill in the Mill Pond. Development in the early 1800s produced the elegant row houses for which the district is famous. The north slope was historically home to a significant African American community, centred on the African Meeting House (1806), the oldest surviving Black church building in the United States, and was an important station on the Underground Railroad.

Landmarks

Landmarks include Louisburg Square, an elegant private square; Acorn Street, often called one of the most photographed streets in America, with its original cobblestones; Charles Street's antique shops; and the Nichols House Museum. The neighborhood was designated a historic district in 1955, and its architecture is protected by the Beacon Hill Architectural Commission.
