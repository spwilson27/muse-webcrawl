The Radio Act of 1927 (Public Law 69-632) was United States federal legislation that created the Federal Radio Commission (FRC) and established the basic regulatory framework for American broadcasting. It replaced the Radio Act of 1912, which had proved unable to manage the chaos of the 1920s broadcasting boom.

Background

Under the 1912 Act the Secretary of Commerce licensed stations but lacked authority to refuse licences or assign frequencies effectively; court decisions in 1926 stripped even that power, and broadcasters jumped frequencies, boosted power, and interfered with one another in what Secretary Herbert Hoover called a situation of "chaos". Congress responded with the 1927 Act, declaring the radio spectrum a public resource: broadcasters would receive licences for limited terms only, with no property right in a channel, and would operate in the "public interest, convenience, or necessity" — a phrase adapted from public-utility law that remains the cornerstone of US broadcast regulation.

Provisions and effects

The Act empowered the five-member Federal Radio Commission to assign frequencies, set power and hours of operation, and award or deny licences by the public-interest standard. Its General Order 40 (1928) reallocated the band, clearing channels largely for high-power network-affiliated commercial stations and marginalising many educational, religious, and non-profit broadcasters — a settlement that shaped the advertiser-supported character of American radio. The Davis Amendment (1928) required equal allocation of licences among five zones and the states. In 1934 the Act's framework was absorbed into the Communications Act, which replaced the FRC with the Federal Communications Commission.
