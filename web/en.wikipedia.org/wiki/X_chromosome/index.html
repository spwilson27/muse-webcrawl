The X chromosome is one of the two sex chromosomes in many organisms, including mammals, and is found in both males and females. It is part of the XY sex-determination system, in which females typically have two X chromosomes (XX) and males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). The X chromosome was named for its unique properties by early researchers, the letter X signifying "unknown."

The human X chromosome spans about 154 million base pairs—around 5% of the total DNA in a female cell—and contains roughly 800 to 900 protein-coding genes, considerably more than the much smaller Y chromosome. It was among the last human chromosomes to be fully sequenced, with the sequence published in 2005; a complete telomere-to-telomere assembly followed in 2020.

X-inactivation

Because females carry two X chromosomes and males only one, mammals equalise gene dosage through X-inactivation: early in embryonic development, one X chromosome in each female cell is largely silenced and condenses into a Barr body. The choice of which X is inactivated is generally random in placental mammals, making females mosaics of cell lines expressing one or the other X. The process is controlled by the long non-coding RNA XIST, which coats the chromosome to be inactivated. Roughly 15% of human X-linked genes escape inactivation to some degree.

X-linked disorders

Many genetic disorders are X-linked. Because males have only one X chromosome, recessive X-linked conditions affect males far more often. Well-known examples include red–green colour blindness, haemophilia A and B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and fragile X syndrome (caused by expansion of a CGG repeat in the FMR1 gene). The pseudoautosomal regions at the tips of the chromosome pair with the Y chromosome during meiosis and behave like autosomes.
