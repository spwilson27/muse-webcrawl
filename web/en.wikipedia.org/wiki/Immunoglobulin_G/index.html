Immunoglobulin G

The Y-shaped structure of an IgG antibody

Immunoglobulin G (IgG) is a type of antibody. Representing approximately 75% of serum antibodies in humans, IgG is the most common type of antibody found in blood circulation. IgG molecules are created and released by plasma B cells. Each IgG antibody has two paratopes.

Structure

IgG antibodies are large molecules with a molecular weight of about 150 kDa. They consist of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains joined by disulphide bonds in a Y shape. The tips of the Y (the Fab regions) bind antigen, while the stem (the Fc region) interacts with cell surface receptors and complement proteins. Humans have four IgG subclasses, IgG1 through IgG4, which differ in their hinge regions and effector functions.

Function

IgG is the main type of antibody found in blood and extracellular fluid, allowing it to control infection throughout the body. By binding many kinds of pathogens — such as viruses, bacteria, and fungi — IgG protects the body from infection through opsonisation, agglutination, activation of the classical complement pathway, and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. IgG also neutralises toxins. It is the only antibody class able to cross the human placenta, providing the newborn with protection in the first months of life.

Clinical significance

Measuring IgG levels is used in diagnosing immune deficiencies and autoimmune conditions. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), prepared from pooled donor plasma, is used to treat antibody deficiencies, certain autoimmune diseases, and inflammatory disorders. Pathogenic IgG autoantibodies drive diseases such as myasthenia gravis and immune thrombocytopenia.
