Daryl Bem

Daryl J. Bem (born June 10, 1938) is an American social psychologist and professor emeritus at Cornell University. He is the originator of self-perception theory (1972), an alternative account of attitude change to cognitive dissonance theory, and of the "exotic becomes erotic" theory of sexual orientation (1996). Bem gained wide attention outside psychology in 2011 for publishing experimental evidence of precognition, a paper that became a flashpoint in the replication crisis in psychology.

Career

Bem studied physics at Reed College before switching to psychology, earning his PhD in social psychology from the University of Michigan in 1964. He taught at Carnegie Mellon, Stanford, Harvard (visiting), and Cornell, where he spent most of his career. With his wife, psychologist Sandra Bem, he was an early public advocate of egalitarian marriage and wrote on gender roles.

Self-perception theory

Bem's self-perception theory (1967, 1972) proposes that people infer their own attitudes from observing their behaviour, much as an outside observer would — challenging Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory, particularly in explaining the effects of insufficient justification. The theory remains a standard topic in social psychology textbooks.

Exotic becomes erotic

In 1996 Bem proposed the "exotic becomes erotic" (EBE) theory, arguing that children perceive peers of the sex they feel different from as exotic, that this produces physiological arousal, and that the arousal is later transformed into erotic attraction — an attempt to explain the development of sexual orientation through a single mechanism for heterosexuality and homosexuality.

Psi research and the replication crisis

A long-time investigator of psi phenomena, Bem published "Feeling the Future" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2011, reporting nine experiments purporting to show retroactive influences on cognition and affect. Subsequent failed replications and methodological critiques — concerning exploratory analysis, publication bias and statistical practice — made the paper a catalyst for reforms such as pre-registration and helped ignite psychology's replication crisis. Bem responded to critics and published a meta-analysis defending the effect.
