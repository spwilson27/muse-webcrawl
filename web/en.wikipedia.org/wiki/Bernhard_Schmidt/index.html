Bernhard Woldemar Schmidt (30 March 1879 – 1 December 1935) was an Estonian-German optician and astronomer who invented the Schmidt camera, a wide-field telescope design that revolutionised astronomical survey work.

Early life

Schmidt was born on the island of Nargen (Naissaar), off the Estonian coast. As a teenager he lost his right hand and forearm in an accident with gunpowder while experimenting, a handicap that did not prevent him from becoming a master optician. He trained in precision optics and worked in Mittweida, Germany, making mirrors and lenses prized by professional astronomers.

The Schmidt camera

In 1929, while working at the Hamburg Observatory in Bergedorf, Schmidt devised a telescope combining a spherical primary mirror with a thin aspheric correcting plate placed at the mirror's centre of curvature. The design eliminated coma and astigmatism over a very wide field of view while remaining fast, making it ideal for photographing large areas of sky. The first Schmidt camera was completed in 1930. Derivatives of the design powered the great photographic sky surveys of the twentieth century, including the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey, which used the 48-inch Samuel Oschin Schmidt telescope.

Legacy

Schmidt died in Hamburg in 1935, only a few years after his invention began to transform astronomy. The lunar crater Schmidt and the asteroid 1743 Schmidt are named in his honour. Schmidt corrector plates are also used in the widely produced Schmidt–Cassegrain telescopes popular with amateur astronomers.
