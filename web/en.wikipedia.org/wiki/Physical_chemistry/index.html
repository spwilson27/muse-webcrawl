Physical chemistry is the branch of chemistry that applies the principles and methods of physics to chemical problems: how matter behaves, why reactions happen, and how fast. It bridges chemistry with physics and underlies most of the theory used by the other branches.

Its core areas are chemical thermodynamics (energy, enthalpy, entropy and equilibrium), chemical kinetics (reaction rates and mechanisms), quantum chemistry (electronic structure and bonding from the Schrödinger equation) and spectroscopy (how matter interacts with light).

[Image: A spectrometer and a calorimeter in a physical chemistry laboratory]

Methods

Physical chemists use calorimetry, spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography, electrochemistry and computation to measure molecular properties and test theories. The Gibbs energy criterion (ΔG < 0 for a spontaneous process at constant temperature and pressure) is one of its most used results.

History

The field coalesced in the late 19th century around Ostwald, van 't Hoff and Arrhenius — the 'Ionists' — and the founding of the first physical chemistry journal in 1887.
