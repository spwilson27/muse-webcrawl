Wainscoting

Wainscoting is wooden panelling that lines the lower part of the walls of a room. Originally used to insulate rooms against damp stone walls and to protect plaster from damage by chairs and everyday wear, wainscoting became a decorative architectural feature found in homes, public buildings and institutions.

Construction

Traditional wainscoting consists of stiles and rails framing raised or flat panels, capped by a chair rail moulding and finished at the floor with baseboard (skirting board). A common form is beadboard, made of narrow vertical boards with a beaded groove. Modern wainscoting may use solid wood, plywood, medium-density fibreboard (MDF) or PVC. Heights vary: classic wainscoting covers the lower third of a wall, while in dining rooms it may rise higher, traditionally to the height at which chair backs would strike the wall.

History

The word derives from the Dutch wagenschot, referring to fine oak boards imported for panelling. Wood panelling became widespread in English interiors in the 16th and 17th centuries; in Georgian and colonial American houses, wainscoting was often painted, in contrast to earlier stained oak. In the 19th and 20th centuries it remained popular in halls, dining rooms and bathrooms, and it continues to be used as a durable, traditional wall treatment.
