Oil shale is an organic-rich fine-grained sedimentary rock containing kerogen (a solid mixture of organic chemical compounds) from which liquid hydrocarbons can be produced. Oil shale can be burned directly as a low-grade fuel for power generation and heating, or processed by heating (retorting) to yield shale oil, a substitute for conventional crude oil.

Despite its name, oil shale is not necessarily shale and does not contain oil in its pores; the hydrocarbons are locked in solid kerogen and are released only when the rock is heated to roughly 450–500 °C. It should not be confused with "shale oil" produced by hydraulic fracturing of tight oil reservoirs such as the Bakken or Permian Basin, which is ordinary crude oil trapped in low-permeability rock.

Geology and reserves

Oil shales formed in ancient lakes, shallow seas and wetlands, where organic matter accumulated faster than it could decay. The largest known deposit is the Eocene Green River Formation of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming in the United States, which contains an estimated resource on the order of trillions of barrels of oil equivalent, though most of it has never been economic to extract. Commercial industries have operated in Estonia — where oil shale has fuelled power stations for a century — and in China and Brazil.

Processing and environment

Retorting can be done in surface vessels after mining, or in situ by heating the rock underground for months or years. Both approaches are energy- and water-intensive, and producing a barrel of shale oil emits substantially more greenhouse gas than a barrel of conventional oil. Mining generates large volumes of spent shale, which must be landfilled; in Estonia, ash and semi-coke heaps form artificial hills. These costs and impacts have repeatedly caused oil shale booms to collapse when conventional oil prices fall.
