Neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) are intracellular aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau protein that are a primary pathological hallmark of Alzheimer's disease and related disorders collectively known as tauopathies. NFTs were first described by Alois Alzheimer in 1906 in the brain of his patient Auguste Deter, alongside amyloid plaques.

Formation

Tau normally stabilises microtubules in neuronal axons. In disease, tau becomes abnormally hyperphosphorylated, detaches from microtubules, and misfolds into insoluble paired helical filaments (with straight filaments), which accumulate in the cell body and dendrites as tangles. After the neuron dies, residual "ghost tangles" may persist extracellularly. The precise triggers — and the relative contributions of tau versus amyloid-β to neurodegeneration — remain active research questions; genetic evidence from MAPT mutations, which cause frontotemporal dementia with tangles but no plaques, shows tau dysfunction alone can drive neurodegeneration.

Pathology and distribution

Tangles typically appear first in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, structures essential for memory, and spread in a stereotyped pattern into limbic regions and eventually the neocortex. Tangle burden correlates more closely with cognitive impairment and neuronal loss than amyloid plaque burden does. NFTs also occur in other tauopathies, including progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, Pick's disease, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, though tangle ultrastructure and distribution vary.

Braak staging

Heiko and Eva Braak (1991) described six stages of neurofibrillary pathology in Alzheimer's disease: stages I–II (transentorhinal), III–IV (limbic), and V–VI (neocortical). Braak staging, together with plaque scores, underpins the modern neuropathological diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (the "ABC" score).

Clinical significance

Tau pathology can now be visualised in living patients with tau PET imaging tracers, and CSF/plasma phosphorylated tau (p-tau) biomarkers are used in diagnosis and trials. Therapies aimed at tau — immunotherapies, aggregation inhibitors, and antisense approaches reducing tau production — are under investigation, alongside approved anti-amyloid treatments.
