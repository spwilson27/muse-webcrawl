Serotiny

Serotiny in botany simply means "following or accompanying serotine", that is, the late development of offspring, but serotiny is more generally the ecological adaptation of some seed plants in which seeds are retained in a protective structure on the parent plant for years and are released only after an environmental trigger — most famously the heat of a wildfire. This behaviour is also known as pyriscence when the trigger is fire.

Mechanism

Serotinous cones or fruits are sealed shut with resin or lignified tissues. When a fire passes, temperatures melt the resin bonds or dry out the scales, allowing the cone to open and shed its seeds onto a freshly cleared, nutrient-rich ash bed with little competition. Classic examples include the lodgepole pine (Pinus contorta) and jack pine of North America, many Australian Banksia and Hakea species, and South African proteas.

Evolutionary significance

Serotiny is most common in fire-prone ecosystems such as boreal forests, chaparral, and fynbos. Its prevalence tends to increase with the frequency and predictability of stand-replacing fires, and to decline where fire intervals are so long that parent trees die before reproducing, or so short that juveniles cannot mature between burns.

Other triggers

Not all serotiny is fire-triggered. Some species release seeds in response to the death of the parent branch or plant (necriscence), to wetting and drying cycles (hygriscence), or to freezing conditions.
