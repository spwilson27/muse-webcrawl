Vastitas Borealis

The Vastitas Borealis (Latin for "northern waste") is the largest lowland region of Mars. It surrounds the north pole of the planet, extending from roughly 50°N latitude to the polar cap, and dominates the northern hemisphere. The name derives from an albedo feature recorded by Eugène Antoniadi and was formally adopted by the International Astronomical Union in 1973.

The smooth northern plains lie several kilometres below the southern highlands.

Geography and the Martian dichotomy

The Vastitas Borealis sits on the low side of the Martian hemispheric dichotomy: the northern lowlands lie some 4–6 km lower than the cratered southern highlands. The boundary between the two is marked in places by escarpments and fretted terrain. Within the lowland, smaller named plains include Utopia Planitia (site of the Viking 2 landing), Arcadia Planitia, Acidalia Planitia and the Scandia region. Proposed explanations for the dichotomy include a single colossal impact — the "Borealis basin" hypothesis — or ancient plate-tectonic-like processes.

An ancient ocean?

The smoothness and low elevation of the plains have long suggested that they were once the floor of an ocean, sometimes called Oceanus Borealis, fed during the Noachian and Hesperian periods by outflow channels and a warmer, wetter climate. Proposed shorelines have been mapped around the dichotomy boundary, though their interpretation remains debated. Radar sounders on Mars Express and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter have found extensive buried ice deposits within the lowlands.

Exploration

Two landers have visited the region: Viking 2 touched down in Utopia Planitia in 1976, and NASA's Phoenix lander descended into the Green Valley of the Vastitas Borealis in May 2008, near 68°N. Phoenix confirmed water ice just beneath the surface, scooped and heated soil samples, and observed falling snow — providing ground truth for the icy character of the northern plains.
