Counterforce

[Image: Counterforce]

In nuclear strategy, a counterforce target is a military target, such as an intercontinental ballistic missile silo, a bomber airbase, a ballistic missile submarine base, or a command and control installation. A counterforce strategy targets the adversary's weapons and military infrastructure rather than its population and industry, which are known as countervalue targets.

Concept

A counterforce first strike aims to destroy the enemy's nuclear forces before they can be launched, thereby limiting the damage of any retaliatory attack. Such a capability depends on accurate delivery systems, sufficient numbers of warheads, and the ability to locate targets — including, most difficultly, mobile missiles and submarines at sea.

History

Early Cold War American war plans were primarily countervalue, aimed at Soviet cities and industry. From the late 1950s, improving missile accuracy made counterforce options increasingly prominent in United States planning, a trend formalised in the 1974 Schlesinger Doctrine and Presidential Directive 59 of 1980, which emphasised flexible targeting of Soviet military forces. Critics argued that counterforce capabilities were destabilising, because they created incentives to strike first in a crisis and pressured adversaries to adopt launch-on-warning postures.

Distinction from countervalue

Counterforce targeting is sometimes presented as more discriminate than attacks on cities, but hardened military targets often require ground bursts of high yield, producing enormous fallout, and many military installations are near population centres — so a "pure" counterforce strike would still kill millions of civilians.
