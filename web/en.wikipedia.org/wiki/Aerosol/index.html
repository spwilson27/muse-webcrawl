Aerosol

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atmospheric aerosol haze over a landscape (image placeholder)

An aerosol is a suspension of fine solid particles or liquid droplets in air or another gas. Examples of aerosols include fog, clouds, dust, smoke, and haze. The liquid or solid particles have diameters typically less than 1 μm, though larger particles can remain suspended for significant periods. Aerosols can be natural — such as sea salt, volcanic ash, pollen, and desert dust — or anthropogenic, such as sulfate and soot particles from combustion.

Definitions and size

The aerosol particle sizes range from a few nanometres to about 100 micrometres. Particles are commonly grouped into ultrafine, fine (PM2.5), and coarse (PM10) fractions. A suspension of solid particles in gas is sometimes called a fume or dust depending on how it forms; fog and mist are aerosols of liquid droplets.

Atmospheric aerosols and climate

Atmospheric aerosols affect climate directly by scattering and absorbing sunlight and indirectly by acting as cloud condensation nuclei, changing cloud brightness and lifetime. Sulfate aerosols from fossil-fuel burning exert a cooling influence that partly masks greenhouse warming, whereas black carbon absorbs sunlight and warms the atmosphere. Large volcanic eruptions inject sulfur gases into the stratosphere, producing a global aerosol veil capable of cooling the planet for several years, as after the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo.

Health effects

Fine particulate matter penetrates deep into the lungs and is strongly associated with respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Air quality standards in many countries regulate concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10. Aerosols also transmit respiratory pathogens when droplets and droplet nuclei carrying viruses or bacteria are inhaled.

Applications

Engineered aerosols are used in spray cans (historically with chlorofluorocarbon propellants), inhalers for drug delivery, spray painting, pesticide application, and aerosol-jet printing of electronics.
