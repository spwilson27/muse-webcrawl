Phenols

In organic chemistry, phenols, sometimes called phenolics, are a class of chemical compounds consisting of one or more hydroxyl groups (−OH) bonded directly to an aromatic hydrocarbon group. The simplest member of the class is phenol itself, C6H5OH. Depending on the number of hydroxyl groups, phenols may be classified as monohydric, dihydric, or polyhydric; well-known examples include catechol, resorcinol, hydroquinone, and the polyphenols abundant in plants.

Properties

Phenols are more acidic than ordinary alcohols because the phenoxide anion is resonance-stabilized by the aromatic ring; phenol has a pKa of about 10. They typically form strong hydrogen bonds, have higher boiling points than comparable arenes, and undergo electrophilic aromatic substitution readily, with the hydroxyl group activating the ring and directing substitution to ortho and para positions. Many phenols are prone to oxidation, forming colored quinones.

Occurrence and uses

Phenols occur widely in nature: they contribute to the antioxidant properties of plant foods and beverages such as tea and wine, and compounds such as thymol, eugenol, and capsaicin are phenolics. Industrially, phenol is produced on a scale of millions of tonnes per year, chiefly by the cumene process, and is used to make phenolic resins (Bakelite), bisphenol A for polycarbonates and epoxies, caprolactam for nylon, and pharmaceuticals including aspirin. Phenols also serve as disinfectants and antiseptics, following Joseph Lister's pioneering use of carbolic acid (phenol) in surgery.

Health and safety

Phenols can be toxic and are readily absorbed through the skin, causing burns and systemic effects at high exposures; chlorinated phenols such as pentachlorophenol have been restricted as wood preservatives due to environmental persistence. Conversely, dietary polyphenols are widely studied for potential health benefits.
