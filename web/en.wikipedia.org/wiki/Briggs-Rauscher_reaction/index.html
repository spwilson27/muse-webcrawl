Briggs-Rauscher reaction

The Briggs–Rauscher oscillating reaction is one of the best-known chemical oscillators, demonstrated by high-school teachers Thomas S. Briggs and Warren C. Rauscher in 1973. A solution containing hydrogen peroxide, iodate, malonic acid, a manganese catalyst, and starch indicator cycles repeatedly through colourless, amber, and deep blue-black phases for several minutes.

Mechanism

The oscillation arises from two competing pathways for processing iodate and peroxide. When iodide concentration is low, a radical process dominates, producing iodine, which is consumed by malonic acid (amber phase). As iodide builds up, a non-radical process takes over, and the sudden production of iodine with rising iodide forms the dark blue starch–iodine complex; the cycle resets as iodine is depleted. Roughly ten coupled reactions are needed to model the behaviour, in the tradition of the Field–Noyes model for the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction.

Demonstration and uses

Because its ingredients are accessible and the colour changes dramatic, the reaction is a staple chemistry demonstration of far-from-equilibrium dynamics. A practical application exploits its sensitivity: antioxidants added to the mixture delay the oscillation in proportion to their activity, giving a simple assay for antioxidant capacity of foods and other samples.
