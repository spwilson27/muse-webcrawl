A superheterodyne receiver, often shortened to superhet, is a radio receiver design that uses frequency mixing to convert a received signal to a fixed intermediate frequency (IF) which can be more conveniently processed than the original carrier frequency. Nearly all modern radio and television receivers — and most radar, communications and measurement equipment — use the superheterodyne principle.

History

The heterodyne technique originated with Reginald Fessenden around 1901. The superheterodyne itself was invented by American engineer Edwin Howard Armstrong in 1918, while serving in France during the First World War; he sought a way to amplify weak high-frequency signals using the vacuum tubes of the day. A parallel patent claim by French engineer Lucien Lévy led to litigation, and Armstrong's patent was eventually upheld in the United States. Commercial superheterodyne sets, sold by RCA under licence from 1924, displaced the tuned radio frequency (TRF) receiver by the early 1930s.

Operation

The incoming radio-frequency (RF) signal from the antenna is optionally amplified, then mixed with a sine wave from a tunable local oscillator. The mixer output contains sum and difference frequencies; a fixed-frequency IF filter selects the difference, so every station is converted to the same intermediate frequency — for example 455 kHz in AM broadcast receivers and 10.7 MHz in FM receivers. Because the IF never changes, its amplifiers and filters can be optimised once for high gain and sharp selectivity. The signal is then demodulated and the recovered audio (or data) amplified. Tuning is accomplished by varying only the local oscillator (ganged with the RF preselector).

Advantages and drawbacks

The architecture offers excellent sensitivity, selectivity and stability compared with TRF designs, and gain can be distributed across frequencies where it is easiest to obtain. Its principal drawback is the image response: a second frequency, offset from the desired signal by twice the IF on the other side of the oscillator frequency, also converts to the IF and must be rejected by RF filtering before the mixer. Other issues include local-oscillator radiation and spurious mixing products. Dual- and triple-conversion designs mitigate these problems in high-performance receivers, and modern implementations often hand the IF to digital signal processing.
