International Atomic Time

International Atomic Time (abbreviated TAI, from the French Temps Atomique International) is a high-precision atomic coordinate time standard based on the notional passage of proper time on Earth's geoid. TAI is a weighted average of the time kept by hundreds of atomic clocks in national laboratories around the world, coordinated by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) in Sèvres, France.

Realisation

TAI is not realised directly in real time. National laboratories compute local time scales, UTC(k), steered toward UTC, and the BIPM combines the data from roughly 450 atomic clocks in more than 80 laboratories to form a free atomic time scale, which is then steered using measurements from primary frequency standards (caesium fountains and other primary standards) to produce TAI. Results are published retrospectively in the BIPM's monthly Circular T.

History

Atomic timekeeping began in the 1950s with the first caesium atomic clocks. TAI was formally established in 1971 by the 14th General Conference on Weights and Measures, building on earlier atomic time scales maintained by the Bureau International de l'Heure. Its epoch was set so that TAI agreed with Universal Time at the start of 1958.

Relation to other time scales

Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), the basis of civil time, is derived from TAI by the addition of leap seconds: UTC runs behind TAI by an integer number of seconds, which stood at 37 seconds from 2017. Because leap seconds are inserted to keep UTC aligned with the irregular rotation of the Earth, TAI advances uniformly while UTC does not. Other time scales based on TAI include Terrestrial Time (TT), which runs exactly 32.184 seconds ahead of TAI, and the time scales used by satellite navigation systems: GPS time is offset from TAI by a fixed 19 seconds.
