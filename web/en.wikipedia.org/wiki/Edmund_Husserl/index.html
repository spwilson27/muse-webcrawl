Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) was a German philosopher who founded Phenomenology, the philosophical movement devoted to the rigorous description of the structures of conscious experience. Trained in mathematics and influenced by Franz Brentano's notion of intentionality — the mind's directedness toward objects — Husserl taught at Göttingen and Freiburg, where his students and assistants included Martin Heidegger, Edith Stein and Eugen Fink.

Thought

Husserl's Logical Investigations (1900–01) attacked psychologism in logic. His mature method calls for the epoché or "bracketing" of the natural attitude — suspending assumptions about the external world — so that consciousness and its intentional structures can be described as they are given. Later work developed the concepts of the lifeworld (Lebenswelt), intersubjectivity, and the internal temporality of consciousness. His final work, The Crisis of European Sciences (1936), argued that modern science had lost touch with its grounding in lived experience.

Legacy

Husserl left some 45,000 pages of shorthand manuscripts, rescued to Leuven in Belgium before the Second World War, where the Husserl Archives continue to publish his works. Phenomenology, through Heidegger, Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and others, became a founding movement of 20th-century continental philosophy and influenced psychology, sociology and cognitive science.
