Sacred Heart

The Sacred Heart is a Roman Catholic devotion to the physical heart of Jesus Christ as a symbol of his divine love for humanity. It is one of the most widely practised and well-known Catholic devotions, depicted in art as a heart aflame, wounded, encircled by a crown of thorns and surmounted by a cross, shining with light.

History

Devotion to Christ's wounds and heart grew in the Middle Ages among mystics including Gertrude the Great and, later, John Eudes. Its modern form stems from the visions of the French nun Margaret Mary Alacoque at Paray-le-Monial in the 1670s, in which Christ was said to have promised graces to those honouring his heart, including the practice of receiving Communion on the first Fridays of nine consecutive months. Long controversial, the devotion was officially endorsed in the 18th and 19th centuries; Pope Pius IX extended the Feast of the Sacred Heart to the universal Church in 1856, and Leo XIII consecrated the world to the Sacred Heart in 1899. Pius XII's encyclical Haurietis aquas (1956) gave the devotion its classic theological exposition.

Practice

The feast is celebrated on the Friday after the second Sunday after Pentecost. Devotional practices include the First Fridays, enthronement of the Sacred Heart image in homes, and consecration by families and nations. The Basilica of Sacré-Cœur in Paris, built after the Franco-Prussian War, is the devotion's most famous monument; the related devotion to the Immaculate Heart of Mary is often paired with it.
