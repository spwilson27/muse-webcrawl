Brigham Young

Brigham Young (June 1, 1801 – August 29, 1877) was an American religious leader and politician. He was the second president of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS Church) from 1847 until his death, succeeding founder Joseph Smith, and led the Mormon pioneers west to the Salt Lake Valley in 1847. He founded Salt Lake City, served as the first governor of the Utah Territory, and directed the settlement of hundreds of communities across the American West, earning the sobriquets "the Mormon Moses" and "the American Moses."

Early life and succession

Born in Vermont and raised in upstate New York, Young worked as a carpenter and joiner. Baptized into Smith's church in 1832, he became an apostle in 1835 and led missions in the eastern states and Britain. After Smith's murder in Nauvoo, Illinois, in 1844, Young, as president of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles, prevailed in the succession crisis and organized the migration west.

Utah

Young reached the Salt Lake Valley in July 1847 and immediately directed the platting of Salt Lake City around the Salt Lake Temple site. Over three decades he founded more than 350 settlements from Idaho to Arizona and into Nevada, California, and Mexico, organized irrigation agriculture, and governed the provisional State of Deseret and then Utah Territory. His tenure included conflict with the federal government — the Utah War of 1857–58, during which U.S. troops marched on Utah and Young was replaced as governor — and the era's practice of plural marriage, which Young himself practised and publicly defended from 1852. Brigham Young University and the University of Utah trace their founding to institutions he established or supported.
