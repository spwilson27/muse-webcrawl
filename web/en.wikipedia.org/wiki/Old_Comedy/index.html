Old Comedy

Old Comedy (Greek: archaia) is the first phase of ancient Greek comedy, flourishing in 5th-century BCE Athens and represented above all by the eleven surviving plays of Aristophanes. It is characterized by topical political satire, obscenity, fantasy, and a chorus that directly addresses the audience.

Features

Old Comedy plays were performed at the City Dionysia and Lenaea festivals. Their structure typically includes an agon (contest or debate between opposing principals), a parabasis in which the chorus steps forward to speak for the poet, and a series of loosely connected farcical scenes ending in a festive revel (komos). Actors wore grotesque padded costumes with phallic props and masks. Plots often present a fantastical scheme — a private peace with Sparta in Acharnians, a sex strike in Lysistrata, a city in the sky in The Birds — and mock real Athenians by name, from the demagogue Cleon to Socrates in The Clouds.

Development

Comedy became an official part of the Dionysia in 486 BCE. Aristophanes' predecessors included Cratinus, Crates, and Eupolis, whose works survive only in fragments. Late 4th-century scholars divided Greek comedy into Old, Middle, and New phases; New Comedy, exemplified by Menander, abandoned political satire for domestic plots, profoundly influencing Roman comedy and, through it, Western theatre.
