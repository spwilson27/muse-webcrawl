Critical care medicine, also known as intensive care medicine, is a medical specialty that deals with seriously or critically ill patients who have, are at risk of, or are recovering from conditions that may be life-threatening. Care is delivered in intensive care units (ICUs) by multidisciplinary teams led by physicians called intensivists.

The specialty encompasses diagnosis and management of multi-organ failure, sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, shock, major trauma, and complications after major surgery. Core interventions include mechanical ventilation, vasoactive drugs, renal replacement therapy, advanced monitoring, nutrition, and sedation.

History

Modern intensive care is often traced to the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic, when anaesthetist Bjørn Ibsen organised manual ventilation of patients with respiratory paralysis, dramatically reducing mortality and leading to the first dedicated ICU in 1953. Florence Nightingale's practice of grouping the sickest patients near the nursing station during the Crimean War is an earlier antecedent.

Systems of care

ICUs may be "closed", where the intensivist team assumes primary responsibility for patients, or "open", where admitting physicians retain control. Research associates closed, intensivist-led models with improved outcomes. Critical care is resource-intensive and raises significant questions of triage and rationing, which came to prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic.
