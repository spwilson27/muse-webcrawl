Carl Gustav Jung (26 July 1875 – 6 June 1961) was a Swiss psychiatrist and psychoanalyst who founded analytical psychology. His work influenced psychiatry, anthropology, archaeology, literature, philosophy, psychology, and religious studies.

Life and career

Jung studied medicine at the University of Basel and worked at the Burghölzli psychiatric hospital in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler. His word-association studies brought him into contact with Sigmund Freud in 1907; the two collaborated closely until theoretical disagreements — especially over the nature of the unconscious and of libido — caused a bitter break in 1912–1913.

Key concepts

Jung proposed the collective unconscious, a layer of the psyche shared by all humans, populated by archetypes such as the Self, the Shadow, the Anima and Animus, and the Persona. He described the process of individuation, the integration of these elements into a whole personality. His typology of psychological types introduced the terms introversion and extraversion, later developed into the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator. He also coined synchronicity for meaningful coincidences not causally connected.

Later work and legacy

After a period of intense self-exploration recorded in The Red Book, Jung developed his mature system, travelled widely studying indigenous cultures, and wrote extensively on alchemy, religion, and symbolism. Whatever the scientific controversies around his theories, his vocabulary — complexes, archetypes, the collective unconscious — has entered general culture.
