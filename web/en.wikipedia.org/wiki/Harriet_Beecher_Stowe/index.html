Harriet Elisabeth Beecher Stowe (14 June 1811 – 1 July 1896) was an American author and abolitionist. She came from the religious Beecher family and became best known for her novel Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), which depicts the harsh conditions experienced by enslaved African Americans. The book reached an audience of millions as a novel and play, and became influential, even in Britain, helping to fuel anti-slavery feeling on the eve of the American Civil War.

Life

Stowe was born in Litchfield, Connecticut, the daughter of the prominent Congregational minister Lyman Beecher; her siblings included the preacher Henry Ward Beecher and educator Catharine Beecher. She attended the Hartford Female Seminary run by her sister, then moved with her family to Cincinnati, Ohio, across the river from the slave state of Kentucky, where she witnessed slavery's realities closely and assisted fugitive slaves. In 1836 she married Calvin Ellis Stowe, a professor at Lane Theological Seminary.

Uncle Tom's Cabin

Prompted by outrage at the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 and by the death of the Stowes' infant son, Uncle Tom's Cabin; or, Life Among the Lowly was serialised in the anti-slavery journal The National Era in 1851–52 and published in book form in March 1852. It sold 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year and over a million in Britain. Southerners attacked it as abolitionist propaganda; Stowe responded with A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin (1853), documenting her sources. According to legend, President Abraham Lincoln greeted her in 1862 as "the little woman who wrote the book that started this great war" — an anecdote first reported decades later and of doubtful authenticity.

Later life

Stowe wrote some 30 books, including the novels Dred (1856) and The Minister's Wooing (1859), the travel memoir Sunny Memories of Foreign Lands, and the controversial Lady Byron Vindicated (1870). After the Civil War she settled in Hartford, Connecticut, and wintered in Mandarin, Florida, where she promoted education for freed people. She died in Hartford in 1896.
