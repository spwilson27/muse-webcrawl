Fraunhofer diffraction

In optics, the Fraunhofer diffraction equation is used to model the diffraction of waves when the diffraction pattern is viewed at a long distance from the diffracting object, and also when it is viewed at the focal plane of an imaging lens. In contrast, diffraction observed close to the diffracting object is described by Fresnel diffraction. The phenomenon is named after the German physicist Joseph von Fraunhofer.

Theory

In the far field, the wavefronts arriving at the observation plane are effectively plane waves, and the diffraction pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture function. For a single slit of width a, the intensity at angle θ follows

I(θ) = I₀ sinc²(π a sinθ / λ)

with the first minimum at sin θ = λ/a. For a circular aperture of diameter D, the pattern is the Airy pattern — a bright central disk surrounded by rings — whose first zero lies at sin θ ≈ 1.22 λ/D, the basis of the Rayleigh criterion for the resolving power of telescopes and microscopes.

Applications

Fraunhofer diffraction governs the images formed by optical instruments, the double-slit fringes in Young's experiment at large distances, and the interpretation of far-field scattering; its mathematics also underlies Fourier optics and antenna theory.
