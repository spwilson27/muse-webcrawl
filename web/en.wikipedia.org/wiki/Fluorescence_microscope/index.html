Fluorescence microscope

An upright epifluorescence microscope.

A fluorescence microscope is an optical microscope that uses fluorescence instead of, or in addition to, scattering, reflection, and attenuation or absorption, to study the properties of organic or inorganic substances. A fluorescence microscope can resolve the distribution of a single molecular species and is one of the most important tools in modern cell biology and materials science.

Principle

The specimen is illuminated with light of a specific wavelength (or wavelengths) which is absorbed by fluorophores, causing them to emit light of longer wavelengths (the Stokes shift). The illumination light is separated from the much weaker emitted fluorescence through the use of a spectral emission filter, so that only the emission light reaches the eye or detector. Fluorescent areas shine against a dark background, providing high contrast and sensitivity down to single molecules.

Epifluorescence microscopy

In the most common configuration, epifluorescence, excitation light passes through the objective lens onto the specimen, and the same objective collects the emitted fluorescence. A dichroic mirror (dichromatic beamsplitter) reflects the shorter-wavelength excitation light toward the specimen while transmitting the longer-wavelength emission to the detector. Excitation source, excitation filter, dichroic mirror, and emission filter are typically combined in a removable filter cube.

Light sources include mercury-vapour and xenon arc lamps, and increasingly high-power LEDs and lasers.

Fluorophores

Specimens may fluoresce naturally (autofluorescence, e.g. chlorophyll) or be labelled with fluorescent dyes and probes. Classic fluorophores include fluorescein (FITC), rhodamine derivatives (TRITC), and the DNA stain DAPI. The development of fluorescent proteins, beginning with green fluorescent protein (GFP) isolated from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria, allows specific proteins to be tagged genetically and observed in living cells.

Techniques

  • Immunofluorescence — antibodies conjugated to fluorophores locate specific antigens.
  • FISH — fluorescence in situ hybridization maps nucleic acid sequences.
  • Confocal microscopy — a pinhole rejects out-of-focus light, enabling optical sectioning and three-dimensional reconstruction.
  • Two-photon microscopy — near-infrared excitation for deep imaging in living tissue.
  • TIRF — total internal reflection fluorescence restricts excitation to a thin layer near the coverslip.
  • FRAP — fluorescence recovery after photobleaching measures molecular mobility.
  • Super-resolution methods — STED, PALM and STORM surpass the diffraction limit of resolution.

Limitations

Fluorophores undergo photobleaching — irreversible destruction under intense illumination — and can be phototoxic to living cells. These effects limit observation time and motivate the use of sensitive cameras and low-light imaging strategies.
