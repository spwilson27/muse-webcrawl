Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

MEN1 classically affects the "three Ps": parathyroid, pancreas, and pituitary.

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), also known as Wermer's syndrome, is an inherited disorder characterized by tumours arising in multiple endocrine glands. It is caused by inactivating mutations in the MEN1 tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 11q13, which encodes the protein menin, and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with high penetrance. Prevalence is roughly 1 in 30,000.

Clinical features

The classic triad involves the parathyroid glands, the pancreatic islet cells, and the anterior pituitary — the "three Ps". Primary hyperparathyroidism due to multi-gland parathyroid hyperplasia or adenomas is the most common manifestation, present in the great majority of patients by age 50, and causes hypercalcemia with kidney stones and bone loss. Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours include gastrinomas (causing Zollinger–Ellison syndrome of recurrent peptic ulcers), insulinomas (causing hypoglycemia), and non-functioning tumours; gastrinoma is the most common functioning pancreatic tumour in MEN1. Pituitary adenomas, most often prolactinomas, occur in a substantial minority. Additional features include adrenal cortical tumours, carcinoid tumours of the foregut, facial angiofibromas, collagenomas, and lipomas.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is made clinically (tumours in two or more of the classic glands), by family history plus one characteristic tumour, or by genetic testing showing a pathogenic MEN1 variant. Because tumours may be multiple and metachronous, management favours surveillance over aggressive surgery in some settings: periodic biochemical screening (calcium, parathyroid hormone, prolactin, gastrin, glucose/insulin) and imaging begin in childhood for known carriers. Parathyroidectomy, surgery or medical therapy (proton pump inhibitors for gastrinoma; somatostatin analogues) for pancreatic tumours, and standard pituitary tumour treatment are tailored to each lesion.

History

The syndrome was described by Paul Wermer in 1954, who recognized its hereditary nature; the MEN1 gene was cloned in 1997. It is distinguished from MEN type 2 (caused by RET mutations, with medullary thyroid cancer and pheochromocytoma) by genetics and tumour spectrum.
