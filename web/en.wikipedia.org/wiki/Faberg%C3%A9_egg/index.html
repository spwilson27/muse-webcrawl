Fabergé egg

A Fabergé egg is a jewelled egg created by the jewellery firm House of Fabergé in Saint Petersburg, Russia. The most famous are the 50 "Imperial" eggs commissioned by the Russian emperors Alexander III and Nicholas II as Easter gifts for their wives and mothers between 1885 and 1916. The eggs are among the most celebrated works of decorative art and have become symbols of the opulence of the Romanov court.

History

In 1885, Emperor Alexander III commissioned the jeweller Peter Carl Fabergé to create an Easter egg for his wife, Empress Maria Feodorovna. The result, the Hen Egg, is an apparently plain white enamel egg that opens to reveal a golden yolk, inside which is a golden hen, which in turn concealed a replica of the Imperial Crown and a ruby pendant. The Empress was so delighted that the commission became an annual tradition. Alexander's son Nicholas II continued it after 1894, presenting one egg each year to his mother and one to his wife, Empress Alexandra Feodorovna — two per year until 1916.

The Imperial eggs

Fifty Imperial eggs were made. Each opened to reveal a "surprise" — miniature portraits, clockwork automata, tiny models of palaces, trains, and carriages — executed in gold, enamel, and precious stones by Fabergé's workmasters, including Mikhail Perkhin and Henrik Wigström. Famous examples include the Coronation Egg (1897), containing a precise miniature of the imperial coronation coach; the Lilies of the Valley Egg (1898); the Trans-Siberian Railway Egg (1900); and the Winter Egg (1913), one of the most expensive, made of rock crystal engraved to resemble ice. Seven of the Imperial eggs are currently missing.

Other eggs

Fabergé also made eggs for private clients, including the Kelch eggs (seven, commissioned by Alexander Kelch for his wife Barbara), and single eggs for the Duchess of Marlborough, the Rothschild family, and Emanuel Nobel. In total, about 69 jeweled eggs are known to have been produced by the firm.

After the Revolution

Following the Russian Revolution, the eggs were confiscated by the Bolsheviks; many were later sold abroad by the Soviet government to raise foreign currency. Today the largest collection is held by the Fabergé Museum in Saint Petersburg, which acquired nine Imperial eggs assembled by the collector Malcolm Forbes when they were purchased by Viktor Vekselberg in 2004 and returned to Russia. Other eggs are held by the Kremlin Armoury, the Royal Collection in London, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Walters Art Museum, and the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts. In 2014 the long-lost Third Imperial Egg resurfaced, having been bought for its scrap gold value by an American dealer; it was later sold to a private collector.
