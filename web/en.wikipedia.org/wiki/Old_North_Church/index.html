The Old North Church (officially Christ Church in the City of Boston) is an Episcopal parish church in the North End of Boston, Massachusetts. Built in 1723, it is the oldest church building in Boston still in use and a National Historic Landmark. It is famous for the lantern signal displayed in its steeple on the night of 18 April 1775, warning of the British march that began the American Revolutionary War.

The lantern signal

On the evening of 18 April 1775, sexton Robert Newman — possibly with vestryman John Pulling — hung two lanterns briefly in the steeple, the prearranged signal that British regulars were leaving Boston "by sea" (crossing the Charles River to Cambridge), rather than marching out over Boston Neck. The signal was intended for patriots in Charlestown awaiting word, since Paul Revere himself might be detained. Revere then began his famous ride. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow immortalized the episode in his 1861 poem "Paul Revere's Ride": "One, if by land, and two, if by sea."

The building

The church is a brick Georgian building inspired by the London churches of Christopher Wren; its 191-foot (58 m) steeple, capped by a weathervane, is the tallest in Boston of its era. The original steeple was destroyed in the Great Gale of 1804 and its replacement toppled by Hurricane Carol in 1954; the present steeple follows the original design. Inside are high box pews, a bust of George Washington — said by Lafayette to be the best likeness of him — and one of the earliest American peals of bells, cast in Gloucester, England, in 1744.

Today

Old North Church remains an active Episcopal congregation and one of the most visited sites on Boston's Freedom Trail, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors annually. In April 2025 it marked the 250th anniversary of the lantern signal with ceremonies including a reading of Longfellow's poem.
