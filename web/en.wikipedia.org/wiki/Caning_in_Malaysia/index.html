Caning in Malaysia

Caning is a widely used form of corporal punishment in Malaysia. It is imposed under three distinct systems: judicial caning ordered by the civil courts as an additional penalty for criminal offences; Sharia caning ordered by Syariah courts for offences against Islamic law; and disciplinary caning in schools and the military.

Judicial caning

Judicial caning is inherited from British colonial law and is prescribed for a wide range of offences — more than forty under the Penal Code and other statutes — including rape, drug trafficking, robbery, and immigration offences. It is inflicted with a rattan cane about 1.09 metres long, applied to the offender's bare buttocks by trained prison officers in a single session, with a maximum of 24 strokes for an adult. Only men between 18 and 50 certified fit by a medical officer may be caned; women, men sentenced to death and men over 50 are exempt. Medical officers attend and may halt the procedure. Unlike in neighbouring Singapore, Malaysian practice does not permit the offender's sentence to be carried out in instalments.

Sharia caning

Under state Islamic enactments, Syariah courts may impose up to six strokes, a cap set by federal law. Sharia caning is much lighter: the offender remains clothed, the cane is smaller and the officer may not raise the arm above the shoulder, so the blows are intended to be symbolic rather than injurious. In 2010 three women were caned under the Sharia system for illicit sex, the first such cases involving women in Malaysia; in 2018 two women were caned in Terengganu after pleading guilty to attempting same-sex relations, drawing international criticism.

School caning

Caning with a light rattan cane may be administered in Malaysian schools by head teachers or authorised staff, officially only to male students, on the palm or over clothing, with restrictions set by the Ministry of Education.

Debate

Human rights organisations such as Amnesty International have condemned judicial caning as cruel and degrading, documenting lasting physical and psychological injury; Malaysian governments have defended it as a lawful deterrent supported by the public.
