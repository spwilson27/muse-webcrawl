In statistics, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) is a method of estimating the parameters of an assumed probability distribution, given some observed data. This is achieved by maximising a likelihood function so that, under the assumed statistical model, the observed data is most probable. The point in the parameter space that maximises the likelihood function is called the maximum likelihood estimate.

Definition

For independent observations x1, …, xn from a density f(x; θ), the likelihood is the joint density viewed as a function of the parameter θ:

L(θ) = ∏i=1..n f(xi; θ),    θ̂ = argmaxθ L(θ)

In practice the log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = log L(θ) is maximised instead, since it is easier to differentiate and numerically more stable; both have the same maximiser.

History

Early versions of the idea appear in the work of Gauss and Laplace, but the method was developed and popularised in its modern form by Ronald Fisher between 1912 and 1922, who also established much of its theoretical justification.

Properties

Under regularity conditions, maximum likelihood estimators are consistent (converging to the true parameter value as the sample grows), asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient, attaining the Cramér–Rao lower bound. The MLE is also invariant under reparameterisation: the MLE of a function g(θ) is g(θ̂). Finite-sample bias can nevertheless occur, and for some models the likelihood has no closed-form maximiser, requiring numerical optimisation such as the Newton–Raphson method, Fisher scoring, or the EM algorithm for latent-variable models.

Applications

MLE underlies most parametric statistical models, including linear and logistic regression, survival analysis, and the training of many machine learning models, where minimising cross-entropy loss is equivalent to maximising likelihood.
