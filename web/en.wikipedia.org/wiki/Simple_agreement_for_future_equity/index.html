A simple agreement for future equity (SAFE) is a financing contract used by startup companies to raise seed capital. Introduced by the accelerator Y Combinator in 2013, a SAFE gives the investor the right to receive equity in the company at a future priced financing round, in exchange for capital provided now. It was designed as a simpler, cheaper, and more founder-friendly alternative to the convertible note.

A SAFE converts into equity when the startup raises a priced round of financing.

How it works

Under a SAFE, the investor pays money to the company immediately but receives no shares and no debt. Instead, when the company later raises a priced equity round (such as a Series A), the SAFE automatically converts into shares of the same class sold in that round. The conversion price is usually determined by a valuation cap — a ceiling on the valuation at which the SAFE converts — and/or a discount (commonly around 20%) to the price paid by the new investors, rewarding the early investor for taking risk sooner. Most-favoured-nation and pro-rata side provisions are also common.

Unlike a convertible note, a SAFE is not debt: it carries no interest rate and no maturity date, and it does not need to be repaid if no financing occurs. If the company is sold or liquidated before conversion, the SAFE holder is typically entitled to get their money back (or convert at the cap) ahead of common shareholders, depending on the form used.

History and adoption

Y Combinator published the first standard SAFE documents in late 2013; entrepreneur and investor Adeo Ressi had earlier proposed a similar instrument. In 2018 Y Combinator revised the forms to "post-money" SAFEs, which calculate the investor's ownership against a capitalization that includes all converting SAFEs, making dilution clearer to both sides. SAFEs have become the dominant instrument for pre-seed and seed fundraising in the United States and are widely used internationally, though regulators and some investors note that SAFE holders lack the protections of shareholders until conversion and may be wiped out or heavily diluted in adverse outcomes.
