Interstellar travel is the hypothetical travel of spacecraft between star systems. Because the distances are enormous — the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.24 light-years (40 trillion kilometres) away — and no propulsion technology can approach the speed of light, interstellar travel is far more demanding than interplanetary travel and remains a subject of scientific study and science fiction rather than practice.

Challenges

The core difficulty is energy: accelerating even a modest spacecraft to a few percent of light speed requires more energy than humanity currently produces, and at relativistic speeds collisions with interstellar dust and gas become hazardous, while communications delays stretch to years. Proposed solutions include slow "generation ships" or world ships carrying self-sustaining populations for centuries; sleeper ships with crews in suspended animation; and uncrewed probes, which sidestep life-support requirements.

Proposed propulsion

Concepts studied by engineers and physicists include nuclear pulse propulsion (Project Orion, 1950s–60s), fusion rockets (the British Interplanetary Society's Project Daedalus, 1970s), antimatter rockets, ramjets collecting interstellar hydrogen (the Bussard ramjet), and beamed-energy light sails — the approach of Breakthrough Starshot (announced 2016), which aims to accelerate gram-scale probes to about 20% of light speed toward Alpha Centauri. Speculative faster-than-light ideas, such as the Alcubierre warp drive, require forms of matter and energy not known to exist.
