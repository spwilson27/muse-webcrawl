Cultural mosaic


The mosaic metaphor: distinct pieces composing one whole

Cultural mosaic (French: la mosaïque culturelle) is the metaphor used to describe the mixture of ethnic groups, languages, and cultures that coexist within Canadian society. The term is often contrasted with the older American ideal of the melting pot, in which immigrants are expected to assimilate into a single dominant culture: in the mosaic, by contrast, groups retain their distinctive identities while contributing to the whole.

Origins and use

The metaphor was popularised in Canada in the mid-twentieth century; the writer John Murray Gibbon used the image in Canadian Mosaic (1938), and the term was taken up by sociologists and later embedded in official discourse. With the adoption of federal multiculturalism policy in 1971 and the Canadian Multiculturalism Act of 1988, the mosaic became a defining element of Canadian national identity and of Canada's self-presentation abroad.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether the metaphor captures real integration outcomes, pointing to "vertical mosaic" analyses of ethnic stratification (the phrase is from John Porter's 1965 study The Vertical Mosaic), concerns that it celebrates difference while masking inequality, and arguments that in practice Canadian integration resembles assimilation more than the metaphor suggests. Defenders argue the mosaic expresses a genuine policy commitment and a pluralist ideal distinguishing Canada from the United States.
