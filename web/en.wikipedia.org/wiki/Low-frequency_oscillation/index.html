Low-frequency oscillation

Low-frequency oscillation (LFO) is an electronic frequency that is usually below 20 Hz and creates a rhythmic pulse or sweep. It is used to modulate other signals in synthesisers and audio effects equipment rather than being heard directly as a tone.

Overview

An LFO generates a periodic waveform — commonly sine, triangle, square, sawtooth or random (sample-and-hold) — which is routed to control a parameter of another module. Introduced in modular synthesisers of the 1960s, such as those of Moog and Buchla, LFOs are now a standard feature of synthesisers, samplers and effects units.

Applications

Typical uses include modulating oscillator pitch to produce vibrato, modulating amplitude to produce tremolo, and modulating filter cutoff frequency to produce wah-like sweeps and rhythmic timbral movement. In effects pedals and processors, LFOs drive chorus, flanger, phaser and rotary-speaker simulations. At very low rates an LFO can generate slowly evolving textures; when its rate is raised into the audio range it can act as a source of sideband-rich FM or AM tones.
