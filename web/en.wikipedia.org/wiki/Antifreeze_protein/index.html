Antifreeze protein

Antifreeze proteins (AFPs), also called ice-structuring proteins, are a class of polypeptides produced by certain animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria that permit their survival in subzero environments. Unlike conventional antifreezes, which lower the freezing point colligatively in proportion to concentration, AFPs bind directly to small ice crystals and inhibit their growth at very low concentrations.

Mechanism

AFPs adsorb onto specific faces of ice crystals. By the adsorption–inhibition model, the bound proteins force ice to grow in highly curved fronts between them; the Kelvin effect makes such growth energetically unfavorable, depressing the freezing point without affecting the melting point. This separation between freezing and melting temperatures is called thermal hysteresis and is the standard measure of AFP activity.

Distribution and applications

AFPs were first discovered in Antarctic notothenioid fishes in the late 1960s by Arthur DeVries. Different structural types evolved independently in fish, insects (where some are "hyperactive"), plants, and microorganisms — a striking case of convergent evolution. Applications under study include improved frozen-food texture, cryopreservation of organs and cells, frost protection for crops, and transgenic cold-tolerant organisms.
