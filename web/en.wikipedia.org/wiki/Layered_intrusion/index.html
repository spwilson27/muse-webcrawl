Layered intrusion

A layered intrusion is a large sill-like body of igneous rock which exhibits vertical layering or differences in composition and texture. These intrusions can be many kilometres in area covering from around 100 km² to over 50,000 km² and several hundred metres to over a kilometre in thickness. Most layered intrusions are Archean to Proterozoic in age (for example, the Paleoproterozoic Bushveld complex), although a few are much younger, such as the Tertiary Skaergaard intrusion of East Greenland.

Formation

Layered intrusions form when basaltic magma is emplaced into the crust and crystallises slowly in a large magma chamber. As crystals form, they may settle under gravity, float, or be redistributed by convection currents and magma flows, producing cumulate layers enriched in particular minerals. The sequence of crystallisation — olivine and pyroxene early, plagioclase later, for example — together with processes such as fractional crystallisation, magma replenishment, and contamination, generates the rhythmic layering characteristic of these bodies. The mechanisms remain debated: in-situ crystallisation at the chamber margins and crystal-liquid separation both play roles in modern models.

Notable examples

  • The Bushveld Igneous Complex of South Africa, the largest layered intrusion on Earth and the source of most of the world's platinum-group metals, chromite, and vanadium.
  • The Stillwater Complex of Montana, USA, known for its platinum-group element reef.
  • The Skaergaard intrusion of Greenland, a classic, relatively small and beautifully exposed example studied since L. R. Wager's pioneering work.
  • The Great Dyke of Zimbabwe, the Rum intrusion of Scotland, the Muskox intrusion of Canada, and the Kiglapait intrusion of Labrador.

Economic significance

Layered intrusions are of major economic importance. Individual layers (reefs) can be rich in platinum-group elements, chromium, nickel, copper, and vanadium. The Merensky Reef and UG2 chromitite layer of the Bushveld Complex, and the J-M Reef of the Stillwater Complex, are among the world's principal sources of platinum and palladium. Some layered intrusions also host major iron-titanium oxide deposits in their upper zones.
