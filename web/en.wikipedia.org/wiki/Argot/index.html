Argot

An argot is a language or vocabulary used by an in-group that outsiders are not supposed to understand — most famously the secret speech of criminals, thieves, and vagrants, but also the specialized vocabularies of trades, occupations, and subcultures. The word comes from French, where argot originally referred to the jargon of Parisian beggars and thieves.


French argot was first systematically recorded in studies of the Parisian underworld.

Characteristics

An argot typically shares the grammar of the surrounding language but substitutes a distinctive vocabulary, built through metaphor, borrowing from other languages, truncation, and word-formation games. French verlan, which reverses syllables, and English back slang are examples of such playful coding. Unlike a jargon, which serves professional precision, an argot's purpose is partly concealment and partly the marking of group identity.

History

Recorded thieves' cants go back to the early modern period: French lexicographer sources described the jobelin of fifteenth-century vagabonds, and English pamphleteers such as Thomas Harman published glossaries of "pedlar's French" in the sixteenth century. François Villon wrote ballads in the argot of the Coquillards. In the nineteenth century, writers such as Victor Hugo, in Les Misérables, treated argot as the language of the outcast and devoted chapters to its study. Modern sociolinguistics treats such varieties under broader terms such as anti-language and register.
