Typhoon Nina was a deadly tropical cyclone that struck China in August 1975. Although its winds caused damage in Taiwan and coastal China, Nina is remembered chiefly for the catastrophe that followed its landfall: stalling over Henan province, it dumped extraordinary rainfall that triggered the failure of the Banqiao Dam and dozens of other dams, in one of the deadliest disasters of the twentieth century.

Nina's remnant stalled over Henan, producing record rainfall in the Huai River basin.

Meteorological history

Nina formed east of the Philippines on 30 July 1975 and intensified into a super typhoon with sustained winds of 250 km/h (155 mph) before striking Hualien, Taiwan, on 3 August. Weakened by Taiwan's mountains, it crossed the Taiwan Strait and made a second landfall in Fujian, China. Instead of dissipating, its circulation lodged against a cold front and remained nearly stationary over Henan for several days, wringing out staggering rainfall: at Linzhuang, 1,631 mm fell in about three days, including 830 mm in six hours — among the most extreme short-period rainfalls recorded anywhere.

The Banqiao Dam failure

The rain overwhelmed the Banqiao Dam on the Ru River and the Shimantan Dam on the Hong River, both of which failed in the early hours of 8 August 1975, releasing a wall of water roughly 10 km wide across the plains. A total of 62 dams failed in a cascade. The flooding directly drowned tens of thousands of people, and epidemics and famine in the aftermath raised total death estimates to between roughly 126,000 and 230,000; about 11 million people were affected and millions of homes destroyed. The Chinese government long kept the scale of the disaster restricted; details emerged publicly in the 1990s and 2000s.

Legacy

The 1975 flood remains one of the deadliest weather-related disasters in recorded history and a byword for the dangers of dam failure. In its wake, China reviewed the design standards and safety of its reservoirs, and the Banqiao Dam was eventually rebuilt in the 1990s.
