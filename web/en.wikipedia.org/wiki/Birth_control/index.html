Birth control

Birth control, also known as contraception, anticonception, and fertility control, is the use of methods or devices to prevent pregnancy. Birth control has been used since ancient times, but effective and safe methods of birth control only became available in the 20th century. Planning, making available, and using human birth control is called family planning. Some cultures limit or discourage access to birth control because they consider it to be morally, religiously, or politically undesirable.

Methods

The World Health Organization and United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provide guidance on the safety of birth control methods among women with specific medical conditions. The most effective methods are those that are long acting and do not require frequent visits to a healthcare provider, including:

  • Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) — intrauterine devices (IUDs) and contraceptive implants, with failure rates below 1% per year;
  • Sterilization — vasectomy in males and tubal ligation in females;
  • Hormonal methods — combined oral contraceptive pills, patches, vaginal rings, injectables, and progestin-only methods;
  • Barrier methods — condoms (which also protect against sexually transmitted infections), diaphragms, and cervical caps;
  • Fertility awareness methods, emergency contraception, and lactational amenorrhoea.

History

Ancient methods included withdrawal, barrier devices of various materials, and herbal preparations. The modern birth control movement began in the early 20th century, driven by campaigners such as Margaret Sanger in the United States and Marie Stopes in Britain. The first oral contraceptive pill, Enovid, was approved in the United States in 1960, developed from research by Gregory Pincus, John Rock, and others. The Dalkon Shield IUD scandal of the 1970s led to stronger device regulation. Safe and effective modern IUDs and implants followed in later decades.

Effects

Access to contraception reduces unintended pregnancy and maternal mortality, allows spacing of pregnancies, and has broad socioeconomic effects on women's education and earnings. Globally, use of modern contraception has risen steadily; the United Nations estimates that most women of reproductive age who wish to avoid pregnancy are using some method, though unmet need persists in many regions.

Ethics and society

Attitudes to birth control vary by religion and culture. The Roman Catholic Church permits only natural family planning methods, while most other religious traditions accept contraception. Public policy debates continue over funding, adolescent access, and conscientious objection by providers; see also bioethics.
