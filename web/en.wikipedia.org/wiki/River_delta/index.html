A river delta is a landform created by deposition of sediment that is carried by a river as the flow leaves its mouth and enters slower-moving or still water. This occurs where a river enters an ocean, sea, estuary, lake, reservoir, or (more rarely) another river that cannot carry away the supplied sediment. It is so named because its triangle shape resembles the uppercase Greek letter delta, Δ.

Formation

As a river flows toward its mouth, its velocity falls, and with it the flow's competence to carry sediment. The coarsest material is deposited first, building a gently sloping fan of channels, levees, crevasse splays, and floodplain deposits. Over time the main channel may abandon its course and switch to a shorter, steeper route — a process called avulsion — building a new delta lobe while the old one subsides and erodes.

Whether a delta forms, and its shape, depends on the balance between the river's sediment supply and the reworking power of waves and tides. Geologists classify deltas as river-dominated (elongate, "bird-foot" form, such as the Mississippi), wave-dominated (arcuate, such as the Nile), or tide-dominated (with wide tidal channels, such as the Ganges–Brahmaputra).

Notable deltas

  • Ganges–Brahmaputra Delta — the world's largest delta, in Bangladesh and India, home to over 100 million people and the Sundarbans mangrove forest.
  • Nile Delta — the archetypal delta of Egypt, described by Herodotus, who is credited with coining the term.
  • Mississippi River Delta — a bird-foot delta in Louisiana, losing land rapidly through subsidence and sediment starvation.
  • Mekong Delta — a major rice-growing region of Vietnam.
  • Danube Delta — a major European wetland and UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Ecology and human significance

Deltas are fertile, low-lying, and rich in wetland habitats, making them centres of agriculture and dense settlement since antiquity. They are also highly vulnerable: damming upstream traps sediment, relative sea-level rise drowns shorelines, and groundwater extraction accelerates subsidence. Many of the world's great deltas — including the Nile, Mekong, and Mississippi — are shrinking despite their large sediment loads.
