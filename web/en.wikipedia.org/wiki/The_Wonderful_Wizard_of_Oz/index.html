The Wonderful Wizard of Oz

The Wonderful Wizard of Oz is an American children's novel written by L. Frank Baum and illustrated by W. W. Denslow, first published by the George M. Hill Company in Chicago in May 1900. It tells the story of Dorothy Gale, a girl from Kansas carried by a cyclone to the magical Land of Oz, and her journey with the Scarecrow, the Tin Woodman and the Cowardly Lion to seek the help of the Wizard of Oz. The book was an immediate bestseller and has since become one of the best-known stories in American literature; the Library of Congress has declared it "America's greatest and best-loved homegrown fairytale".

Plot

Dorothy lives on a Kansas farm with her Aunt Em, Uncle Henry and her little dog Toto. A cyclone carries their house to the Land of Oz, where it lands on — and kills — the Wicked Witch of the East. The Good Witch of the North and the Munchkins greet Dorothy, who takes the dead witch's silver shoes. To return home, she must follow the yellow brick road to the Emerald City and ask the great Wizard of Oz for help. Along the way she frees the Scarecrow (who wants brains), oils the rusted Tin Woodman (who wants a heart), and meets the Cowardly Lion (who wants courage).

In the Emerald City, the Wizard — appearing to each visitor in a different terrifying form — agrees to help only if they kill the Wicked Witch of the West. The companions survive her wolves, crows, bees and Winkie soldiers, but the Witch's Winged Monkeys capture Dorothy. Enslaved in the Witch's castle, Dorothy finally destroys her by throwing a bucket of water over her. Back in the Emerald City they discover the Wizard is a humbug — an ordinary old man from Omaha who arrived by balloon. He gives the Scarecrow, Tin Woodman and Lion symbolic tokens of the qualities they possessed all along, and tries to take Dorothy home by balloon, but it departs without her. Dorothy finally learns from Glinda, the Good Witch of the South, that the silver shoes can carry her anywhere; she clicks her heels three times and wishes herself home.

Publication and adaptations

Baum and Denslow shared the copyright and production costs; the lavish colour plates made the first edition expensive to produce but the book sold some 15,000 copies in its first month and remained in print for years. Baum went on to write thirteen sequels, and other authors continued the series after his death. A hugely successful 1902 Broadway musical adaptation preceded the book's most famous adaptation, MGM's 1939 film The Wizard of Oz starring Judy Garland, in which the silver shoes became ruby slippers to exploit Technicolor. The story has generated further adaptations including The Wiz, Gregory Maguire's novel Wicked (1995) and its stage musical.

Interpretations

Since Henry Littlefield's 1964 article, many readers have interpreted the novel as a parable of 1890s American Populism — the yellow brick road as the gold standard, the silver shoes as free silver, and so on — although Baum's own politics and the evidence for the reading remain debated. The book entered the public domain in 1956 and has never been out of print in some form since.
