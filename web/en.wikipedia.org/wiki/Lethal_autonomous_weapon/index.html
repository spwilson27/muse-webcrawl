Lethal autonomous weapon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lethal autonomous weapons (LAWs) are a type of military system that can independently search for and engage targets based on programmed constraints and descriptions. They are also known colloquially as "killer robots" or slaughterbots. Such systems operate in the air, on land, on water, under water, or in space, and range from automatic defensive systems to loitering munitions that linger over an area before striking a detected target.

Types and examples

Autonomy in weapons exists on a spectrum. Defensive systems such as the Phalanx CIWS can automatically engage incoming projectiles, while some air-defence systems can engage aircraft without direct human initiation of each shot. Offensive examples commonly discussed include loitering munitions such as the IAI Harpy, which searches for and destroys radar emitters, and experimental platforms designed to select and engage targets with limited human oversight.

Ethical and legal debate

The development of lethal autonomous weapons has prompted an international debate over whether machines should be permitted to make life-and-death decisions. Critics argue that delegating the decision to kill to algorithms violates principles of human dignity and creates an accountability gap under international humanitarian law, since it may be unclear who is responsible when such a system acts unlawfully. Supporters counter that autonomous systems could, in principle, react faster and more precisely than humans and might reduce some harms of war.

Since 2013, discussions have taken place under the framework of the United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW), where a group of governmental experts adopted guiding principles including the position that human responsibility for decisions on the use of weapons systems must be retained. The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of non-governmental organisations, advocates a pre-emptive treaty ban on fully autonomous weapons. In 2015, an open letter signed by researchers in artificial intelligence and robotics called for a ban on offensive autonomous weapons beyond meaningful human control.
