Sphincter

A sphincter is an anatomical structure, a circular muscle that normally maintains constriction of a natural body passage or orifice and which relaxes as required by normal physiological functioning. Sphincters are found in many animals; there are over 60 types in the human body, some microscopically small, in particular the millions of precapillary sphincters.

Function

Sphincters control the passage of liquids, solids, and gases through orifices such as the digestive tract and urinary system. Many are under involuntary (autonomic) control, while some, like the external anal and external urethral sphincters, are voluntary. At death, sphincters relax, sometimes releasing fluids.

Examples

Major sphincters include the lower esophageal (cardiac) sphincter at the stomach entrance; the pyloric sphincter at the stomach outlet; the sphincter of Oddi controlling bile and pancreatic juice flow into the duodenum; the ileocecal sphincter between small and large intestine; the internal and external anal sphincters; the urinary sphincters controlling continence; and the pupillary sphincter of the iris, which constricts the pupil in bright light.

Clinical significance

Sphincter dysfunction underlies conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (a weakened lower esophageal sphincter), achalasia (failure of that sphincter to relax), sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, and urinary or fecal incontinence from sphincter damage or weakness. Treatments range from medication and endoscopic dilation to surgical repair or artificial sphincter implants.
