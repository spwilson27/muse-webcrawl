Franck–Condon principle

The Franck–Condon principle describes the intensities of vibronic transitions, or the absorption or emission of a photon. It states that when a molecule is undergoing an electronic transition, such as ionization, the nuclear configuration, which is determined by the position of the nuclei, does not change significantly. This is attributed to the relatively large mass of nuclei compared to electrons and the consequently much slower motion of the nuclei. The principle is named after James Franck and Edward U. Condon.

Overview

Classically, the principle is explained by the observation that electronic transitions occur on a timescale of about 10−15 seconds, during which the comparatively massive nuclei barely move. The transition is therefore drawn as a vertical line on a diagram of potential energy versus internuclear separation (representing constant nuclear coordinates), consistent with the Born–Oppenheimer approximation.

Quantum mechanically, the intensity of a vibronic transition is proportional to the square of the overlap integral between the vibrational wavefunctions of the two states involved — the Franck–Condon factor. This explains the observed progressions of vibrational bands in the electronic spectra of molecules, and applies to absorption, fluorescence, and photoelectron spectroscopy.
