Hydration reaction

In chemistry, a hydration reaction is a chemical reaction in which a substance combines with water. In organic chemistry, water typically adds across a double or triple bond of an unsaturated substrate; in inorganic chemistry, hydration may refer to the incorporation of water into a crystal structure (forming a hydrate) or the surrounding of ions by water molecules in solution.

Organic hydration

The classic example is the acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes to give alcohols, following Markovnikov's rule: ethylene is hydrated industrially to ethanol using a phosphoric acid catalyst, and propylene gives isopropanol. The hydration of alkynes (the Kucherov reaction) yields carbonyl compounds — an enol intermediate that tautomerises to a ketone, or acetaldehyde from acetylene. Hydration of nitriles gives amides, and the hydration of carbon dioxide gives carbonic acid, a reaction catalysed in biology by carbonic anhydrase.

Mineral and cement hydration

Hydration reactions are central to the setting of cement: the calcium silicates in Portland cement react with water to form calcium silicate hydrate gel and calcium hydroxide, which give concrete its strength. Similarly, the conversion of hemihydrate plaster (plaster of Paris) to gypsum is a hydration reaction used in casts and moulds.
