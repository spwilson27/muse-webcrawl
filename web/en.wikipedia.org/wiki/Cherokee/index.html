Overview

The Cherokee (Cherokee: ᎠᏂᏴᏫᏯᏍᏗ, romanised Aniyvwiyaʔi, or ᏣᎳᎩ, Tsalagi) are one of the Indigenous peoples of North America. Historically they lived in the southeastern United States, in present-day North Carolina, Tennessee, Georgia, South Carolina and Alabama, and were among the largest and most politically integrated Native American groups of the Southeast.

History

In the early nineteenth century the Cherokee adopted a written constitution, published a bilingual newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, and developed a syllabary created by Sequoyah in 1821 that made the Cherokee the first North American Indigenous people to achieve widespread literacy in their own language. Despite legal victories in the United States Supreme Court in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), the Indian Removal Act and the disputed Treaty of New Echota led to the forced removal of some 16,000 Cherokee to Indian Territory in 1838–39, a journey on which thousands died, remembered as the Trail of Tears. Those who remained or returned in North Carolina formed the basis of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians.

Government and nations

Today the three federally recognised Cherokee tribes are the Cherokee Nation, headquartered at Tahlequah, Oklahoma — the largest tribe in the United States by enrollment — the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina, and the United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians in Oklahoma.

Language and culture

The Cherokee language, a member of the Iroquoian family, is written in Sequoyah's syllabary of 85 characters. It is classified as endangered, and the Cherokee Nation has invested in immersion schools and master-apprentice programmes to revitalise it. Traditional culture includes the stomp dance, clan kinship reckoning through the mother, and crafts such as rivercane basketry and carving.
