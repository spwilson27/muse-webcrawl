Dating creation

Dating creation is the attempt at an absolute chronology of the events of the early history of the world, extending from the act of creation to subsequent events known from sacred texts and traditional histories. Numerous chronologies have been constructed in Jewish, Christian, and other traditions, generally by summing the lifespans and genealogies recorded in scripture.

[ Hero image: James Ussher, Archbishop of Armagh, author of the Ussher chronology ]

Notable chronologies

  • Ussher chronology — James Ussher, Archbishop of Armagh, calculated in 1650 that creation occurred on the evening preceding 23 October 4004 BC. His dates were printed in the margins of many editions of the King James Bible and became the best-known creation chronology in the English-speaking world.
  • Byzantine era — the Eastern Roman world dated creation to 1 September 5509 BC, a date used in the Byzantine calendar for centuries.
  • Hebrew calendar — the Jewish Anno Mundi era counts years from a creation date of 3761 BC.
  • Masoretic and Septuagint traditions — differing figures in the Hebrew and Greek texts of Genesis yield dates roughly a millennium apart; chronologers such as Eusebius of Caesarea and the Venerable Bede (who arrived at 3952 BC) worked from these sources.

Context

Creation dating was a serious scholarly discipline through the early modern period — figures including Johannes Kepler and Isaac Newton produced their own chronologies. It was progressively displaced from the eighteenth century onward as geology established the great antiquity of the Earth, and later by radiometric dating and cosmology, which place the Earth's age at about 4.54 billion years and the universe's at about 13.8 billion. Dates for creation remain significant within young-Earth creationism, which accepts biblical chronologies of roughly 6,000 years.
