John Milton Cage Jr. (September 5, 1912 – August 12, 1992) was an American composer, music theorist, artist, and philosopher. A pioneer of indeterminacy in music, electroacoustic music, and the non-standard use of musical instruments, Cage was one of the leading figures of the post-war avant-garde. Critics have lauded him as one of the most influential composers of the 20th century.

Life

Cage was born in Los Angeles and briefly attended Pomona College before traveling to Europe. Returning to California, he studied composition with Richard Buhlig, Adolph Weiss, Henry Cowell, and Arnold Schoenberg. He taught at the Cornish School in Seattle, where he met the dancer Merce Cunningham, his lifelong collaborator and partner. In New York in the 1940s he formed close friendships with artists including Marcel Duchamp and became a central figure in experimental music and the emerging Fluxus movement. He also developed a serious passion for mycology, becoming a respected amateur mushroom expert.

Music

Cage invented the prepared piano — a piano with objects placed between or on its strings — for the dance piece Bacchanale (1940) and composed the celebrated Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48) for the instrument. Influenced by Zen Buddhism and the I Ching, he began using chance operations to compose, producing works such as Music of Changes (1951). His most famous and controversial composition is 4′33″ (1952), in which performers do not play their instruments for four minutes and thirty-three seconds, framing ambient sound itself as music. Other notable works include Imaginary Landscape No. 4 for twelve radios, Williams Mix, and the late "number pieces."

Writings and influence

Cage's lectures and writings, collected in books such as Silence (1961) and A Year from Monday (1967), spread his ideas about sound, silence, and chance far beyond music. His famous dictum "I have nothing to say and I am saying it" encapsulated his playful approach to philosophy. He taught at Black Mountain College and the New School for Social Research, influencing generations of composers, visual artists, and choreographers.

Death and legacy

Cage died of a stroke in New York City on August 12, 1992. His works continue to be performed worldwide, and his radical redefinition of music — treating all sound as potentially musical — remains foundational to experimental art.
