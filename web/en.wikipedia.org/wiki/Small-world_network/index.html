Small-world network

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Small-world network
A small-world network: mostly local links, a few long-range shortcuts

A small-world network is a graph in which most nodes are not direct neighbours of one another, yet most nodes can be reached from every other node by a small number of steps. Formally, a network is said to show the small-world effect if its average shortest path length grows proportionally to the logarithm of the number of nodes, while its clustering coefficient remains much higher than that of a comparable random graph.

History

The phenomenon was popularised by Stanley Milgram's 1967 "small world experiment", which found short chains of acquaintances connecting random pairs of people in the United States — the origin of the "six degrees of separation" idea. In 1998, Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz published an influential model in Nature showing that a regular lattice rewired with a small probability of long-range edges exhibits both high clustering and short path lengths, unifying features of ordered and random graphs.

Properties

Small-world networks are characterised by (i) a small average shortest path length, comparable to that of an Erdős–Rényi random graph of the same size, and (ii) a large clustering coefficient, comparable to that of a regular lattice. The Watts–Strogatz model interpolates between a ring lattice and a random graph as the rewiring probability increases; even tiny rewiring probabilities collapse the path length while barely reducing clustering.

Examples

Many real networks display small-world structure, including social networks, the World Wide Web's hyperlink graph in some measures, airline route networks, power grids, and neural networks such as the connectome of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans analysed by Watts and Strogatz.

Applications

Small-world structure affects how quickly information, influence, or disease spreads through a network, how robust the network is to node failure, and how synchronisation emerges in coupled oscillators. The concepts underpin much of modern network science and the analysis of social and biological systems.
