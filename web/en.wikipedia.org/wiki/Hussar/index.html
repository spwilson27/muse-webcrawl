Hussar

A hussar was a member of a class of light cavalry that originated in Central Europe in the 15th and 16th centuries, first in Hungary, and whose colourful uniforms and dash made them the most glamorous mounted arm of European armies through the 19th century.

Origins

The name derives from Hungarian huszár. Hungarian hussars served as raiders and scouts in the wars against the Ottoman Empire; the celebrated winged hussars of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, by contrast, developed into heavy shock cavalry famous for breaking the Ottoman army at the Battle of Vienna in 1683.

18th–19th centuries

Most European armies raised hussar regiments — Prussia under Frederick the Great, France, Russia, Austria and Britain — used for reconnaissance, outpost duty, raiding and pursuit. Their distinctive dress comprised a short braided jacket (dolman), a fur-trimmed pelisse worn slung over the shoulder, a busby or shako, tight breeches and a sabretache, with a curved sabre and carbines or pistols as arms.

Later history

Light cavalry declined with modern firepower in the First World War, though hussar regiments survive ceremonially and as armoured reconnaissance units in several armies today, retaining the historic title and uniform features.
