Pixilation

Pixilation (from pixilated, as if "possessed by pixies") is a stop-motion animation technique in which live actors are used as frame-by-frame subjects, in the same way as objects in conventional stop motion. Because each frame is exposed separately, actors can appear to glide along the ground, disappear and reappear, move objects by "magic", or interact impossibly with their surroundings. Not to be confused with pixelation, the visible display of pixels, or with image censoring.

Pixilation lets actors move in impossible ways

History

The technique is nearly as old as cinema: early trick films by pioneers such as James Stuart Blackton and Segundo de Chomón used frame-by-frame exposure of actors. The Scottish-Canadian animator Norman McLaren popularised and named the technique with his National Film Board shorts Neighbours (1952) — which won an Academy Award — and A Chairy Tale (1957), in which a man struggles with an animated chair. Grant Munro, who worked with McLaren, used pixilation extensively.

Later use

Pixilation has been used in feature films, music videos and commercials: Jan Švankmajer's films blend it with object animation; the video for Peter Gabriel's "Sledgehammer" (1986) combined pixilation with claymation; and the technique appears in the work of PES and in the opening of the television series The Goodies era British comedy. The Czech animator's tradition and advertising have kept the method alive into the digital era, where it can now be shot on ordinary cameras.

Technique

Actors hold a pose while one or a few frames are exposed, then shift slightly and hold again. Movement is laborious: gliding requires the actor to shuffle tiny distances with feet flat, and "flying" can be achieved by photographing mid-jump poses. The method's slightly jerky, dreamlike quality is part of its appeal.
