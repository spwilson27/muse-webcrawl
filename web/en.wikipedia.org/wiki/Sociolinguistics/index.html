Sociolinguistics is the descriptive study of the effect of any or all aspects of society, including cultural norms, expectations, and context, on language, and the effect of language on society. It overlaps considerably with the sociology of language, which focuses on language's effect on society, whereas sociolinguistics focuses on society's effect on language. The field is founded on the observation that language varies systematically — between speakers, groups, and situations — and that this variation is socially meaningful rather than random "free variation".

History

Although dialectologists and scholars such as Mikołaj Kruszewski had noted social aspects of language earlier, sociolinguistics emerged as a recognisable field in the 1960s. William Labov's 1966 study of the social stratification of English in New York City — including his famous department-store survey comparing Saks, Macy's, and Klein's — demonstrated that the use of variables such as post-vocalic /r/ correlated precisely with social class and with the formality of the occasion ("style-shifting"). Dell Hymes proposed the ethnography of communication and the notion of communicative competence, and John Gumperz developed interactional sociolinguistics, analysing how contextualisation cues guide interpretation in conversation.

Variation studies

The variationist tradition studies sociolinguistic variables — features with two or more variants that say the "same thing" but are distributed differently across speakers and styles, such as (ING), the alternation between walking and walkin'. Labov's study of Martha's Vineyard showed islanders centralising diphthongs as a marker of local identity in opposition to summer tourists. Peter Trudgill's Norwich study documented class stratification in England and introduced the idea of covert prestige — the positive value speakers attach to non-standard forms that signal solidarity and local belonging, as against the overt prestige of the standard. Penelope Eckert's work on "jocks and burnouts" in a Detroit high school shifted attention to communities of practice and the construction of social meaning through style.

Key concepts

  • Speech community — a group sharing norms for the use and evaluation of language.
  • Register and style — varieties of language according to situation, from formal to intimate; speakers shift along this range.
  • Code-switching — alternating between languages or varieties within a conversation, often to signal identity, solidarity, or topic change.
  • Diglossia — the stable use of a "high" variety for official and written purposes alongside a "low" vernacular, as with Standard Arabic and regional dialects.
  • Dialect, accent, and sociolect — varieties tied to region, pronunciation alone, and social group respectively.
  • Language attitudes — the evaluations hearers attach to varieties, studied with the matched-guise technique, in which judges rate the same speaker recorded in different guises.

Language and identity

Sociolinguists study how language indexes gender, ethnicity, age, occupation, and group membership. Janet Holmes and others have documented gendered patterns of politeness, hedging, and conversational support, while cautioning against essentialism. Basil Bernstein's distinction between "elaborated" and "restricted" codes in the speech of British schoolchildren — often misread as a deficit theory — argued that schools privilege the code of the middle class, disadvantaging working-class pupils; Labov's "The Logic of Nonstandard English" (1972) famously countered deficit views by demonstrating the systematic grammar of African American Vernacular English. Contemporary work examines slang, language online, global Englishes, and the linguistic construction of identity in social media.

Methods

Data come from sociolinguistic interviews (designed to elicit casual speech despite the "observer's paradox" — the need to observe how people speak when they are not being observed), rapid anonymous surveys, participant observation, corpora, and experiments such as matched-guise tests. Quantitative variationists analyse variable data statistically; interactional sociolinguists use close discourse analysis of recorded encounters such as job interviews and medical consultations.
