Chicago Convention on International Civil Aviation

The Convention on International Civil Aviation, also known as the Chicago Convention, was signed on 7 December 1944 by 52 signatory states in Chicago, Illinois. It received the requisite 26th ratification on 5 March 1947 and went into effect on 4 April 1947, the same date that the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) came into being. The convention has since been revised eight times (in 1959, 1963, 1969, 1975, 1980, 1997, 2000 and 2006) and, as of 2023, has 193 signatory states.

Contents of the convention

The convention establishes the core principles of international air transport: the sovereignty of each state over the airspace above its territory, the prohibition of scheduled international air services operating over or into a state's territory without permission, and the obligation of states to collaborate to secure the highest possible degree of uniformity in regulations and standards. It sets out rules on aircraft nationality and registration, facilitation, and the duties of states in the event of accidents.

ICAO

The convention created the International Civil Aviation Organization, a specialised agency of the United Nations headquartered in Montreal, Canada. ICAO adopts Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs) contained in the convention's 19 annexes, covering matters from personnel licensing and rules of the air to aviation security and the safe transport of dangerous goods.

Freedoms of the air

Two companion agreements signed alongside the convention — the International Air Services Transit Agreement and the International Air Transport Agreement — set out the so-called freedoms of the air. The Transit Agreement, widely ratified, grants the first two freedoms: overflight and technical stops. The broader Transport Agreement, which would have granted commercial freedoms, was ratified by few states and later denounced by the United States; commercial traffic rights are instead negotiated bilaterally.
