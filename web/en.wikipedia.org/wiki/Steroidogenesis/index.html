Steroidogenesis

Steroidogenesis is the biological process by which steroids are generated from cholesterol and transformed into other steroids. The pathways take place mainly in the adrenal cortex, the testes, the ovaries and the placenta, and produce the glucocorticoids, mineralocorticoids, androgens, estrogens and progestogens that regulate metabolism, salt balance, reproduction and development.

The pathway

All steroidogenesis begins with cholesterol, which is transported into mitochondria with the help of the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR). There the enzyme CYP11A1 (cholesterol side-chain cleavage enzyme, P450scc) converts it to pregnenolone, the common precursor of all steroid hormones. Pregnenolone then follows tissue-specific routes: in the adrenal zona fasciculata it is converted mainly to cortisol; in the zona glomerulosa to aldosterone; in the gonads to testosterone and, through the enzyme aromatase, to estradiol.

Key enzymes include 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase, 21-hydroxylase (CYP21A2), 11β-hydroxylase and 17α-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase. Each step modifies the steroid nucleus by adding or removing hydroxyl groups and double bonds, progressively changing the molecule's biological activity and the receptor it activates.

Regulation and disorders

Adrenal steroidogenesis is driven by ACTH from the pituitary; gonadal steroidogenesis by LH and FSH. Defects in steroidogenic enzymes cause congenital adrenal hyperplasia — most often 21-hydroxylase deficiency — which can lead to cortisol deficiency, excess androgen production and virilisation. Excess or deficient steroid production also underlies Cushing's syndrome, Conn's syndrome and some disorders of sex development, so the pathway is a major target of endocrine pharmacology.
