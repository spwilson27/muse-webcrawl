Overview

The Mercator projection is a cylindrical map projection presented by the Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator in 1569. It is conformal: at every point, angles and small shapes are shown correctly, and — its great purpose — any line of constant compass bearing, a rhumb line, comes out straight, so a navigator could draw a course with a ruler and steer it.

The price is scale. Meridians are drawn parallel and equally spaced, so east–west distances are progressively stretched away from the equator; to keep angles true, north–south spacing stretches equally, and areas inflate with the square of that stretch. Greenland looks the size of Africa, though Africa is some fourteen times larger, and the poles themselves can never be shown, lying at infinity.

Use and criticism

Mercator ruled nautical charting for four centuries and, in its Web Mercator variant, became the default of online maps, where its squareness suits tiled zooming. As a general world map it has been criticised since the twentieth century for the false importance it gives to high-latitude lands, a debate that made the equal-area Gall–Peters projection famous. Professional chart-makers long ago moved to other projections for world maps, while keeping Mercator for the sea charts it was designed for.
