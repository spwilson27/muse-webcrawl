Diana Nemorensis

Diana Nemorensis ("Diana of the Wood"), also known as Diana of Nemi, was a Roman goddess venerated at a sanctuary on the shores of Lake Nemi in the Alban Hills near Aricia (modern Ariccia), Italy. Her cult was notable for the strange custom governing its priesthood.

The Rex Nemorensis

The priest of Diana at Nemi bore the title Rex Nemorensis, "King of the Wood". According to ancient sources such as Strabo and Servius, the priest was always a fugitive slave who obtained the office by killing his predecessor in single combat, having first plucked a branch — identified in later tradition with the Golden Bough — from a sacred tree in the grove. He then held the priesthood until he himself was slain by a stronger challenger.

The Golden Bough

This ritual is the starting point of Sir James George Frazer's comparative study of religion, The Golden Bough (1890–1915), which opens with the scene of the priest-king prowling the grove sword in hand. The cult is also linked in tradition with Orestes and Iphigenia, said to have brought the image of Tauric Diana to Nemi, and the festival of the goddess, the Nemoralia, was held in mid-August and associated with women, childbirth and slaves.

Sanctuary

Excavations at the site have revealed a large terraced sanctuary with a temple, theatre, baths and votive deposits, indicating that the shrine was a major healing and pilgrimage centre from the late Republic into the Imperial period.
