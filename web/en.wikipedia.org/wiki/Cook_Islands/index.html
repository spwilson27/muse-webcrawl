Cook Islands
Capital: Avarua
Largest island: Rarotonga
Official languages: English, Cook Islands Māori
Government: Unitary parliamentary constitutional monarchy in free association with New Zealand
Population: about 17,000

The Cook Islands (Cook Islands Māori: Kūki 'Airani) is a self-governing island country in the South Pacific Ocean, in free association with New Zealand. It comprises 15 islands spread over nearly two million square kilometres of ocean, divided into a northern group of low coral atolls and a southern group of mostly volcanic islands, including Rarotonga, where the capital Avarua is located.

History

The islands were first settled by Polynesian voyagers around AD 1000. Spanish explorers reached them in the 16th and 17th centuries, and Captain James Cook visited in the 1770s, giving the group its European name. The Cook Islands became a British protectorate in 1888 and were annexed to New Zealand in 1901. Self-government in free association began in 1965; Cook Islanders hold New Zealand citizenship, and New Zealand retains responsibility for defence, while the Cook Islands conducts its own foreign affairs and has joined international organisations in its own right.

Politics and economy

The head of state is the monarch of New Zealand, represented by the King's Representative; the head of government is the prime minister, leading a parliament of 24 members. Tourism is the dominant industry, followed by offshore banking, pearls, and marine and fruit exports. The currency is the New Zealand dollar, alongside a local Cook Islands dollar.

Culture

Cook Islands culture is Polynesian, with Cook Islands Māori and English as official languages. Traditional dance, drumming and wood carving are prominent, and Christianity — brought by London Missionary Society missionaries from 1821 — is practised by the great majority of the population.
