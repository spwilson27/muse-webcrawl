Belote

Players4 (2 teams of 2); variants for 2–3
Cards32 (Piquet deck: 7–A in each suit)
TypeTrick-taking, point-trick game
OriginFrance, c. 1920s
Related gamesKlaverjas, Jass, Coinche

Belote is a 32-card trick-taking game played in France and French-speaking regions, and one of the most popular card games in France. Played by four players in two partnerships, it descends from the Dutch game Klaverjas via the French game Bel Atout and took its modern form in the 1920s. Closely related variants include Coinche (with bidding) and Belote in the Balkans and the former Soviet Union (where it became hugely popular in Armenia, Georgia and elsewhere, sometimes as Bazaar Belote).

Rules

The game uses a 32-card deck. In the trump suit the ranking is unusual: the Jack ("Valet") is the highest card and the Nine second, followed by Ace, Ten, King, Queen, Eight and Seven; in plain suits the order is Ace, Ten, King, Queen, Jack, Nine, Eight, Seven — giving trump Jack 20 points and trump Nine 14 points, which makes capturing them decisive. A card is turned up for trump; players in turn may accept it or pass, after which trump may be chosen from another suit.

Play consists of eight tricks; partners must follow suit if able and must trump if they cannot follow and an opponent is winning the trick. The game is named for the bonus combination belote: the King and Queen of trumps held by one player score 20 points when declared ("Belote!" then "Rebelote!"). Sequences and sets (such as four Jacks, worth 200) may also be declared in classic belote with announcements. A team normally needs at least 82 of the 162 card points to score; failing to reach one's contract puts the opponents' total to one's own score ("être dedans").

Cultural role

Belote is a fixture of French café and family life, widely played in bars, village festivals and online. Coinche, its bidding variant, is dominant in the south-east of France. The game also spread throughout the francophone world and the Armenian diaspora.
