Psychosis involves a loss of contact with reality.

Psychosis is a condition of the mind that results in difficulties determining what is real and what is not real. Symptoms may include delusions (false beliefs) and hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that others do not see or hear). Additional symptoms may include incoherent speech and behaviour that is inappropriate for the situation. There may also be sleep problems, social withdrawal, lack of motivation, and difficulties carrying out daily activities.

Psychosis can have serious adverse outcomes. As with many psychiatric phenomena, psychosis has several different causes, which can include mental disorders such as schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, bipolar disorder, sensory deprivation, hypoglycemia, and medical conditions such as brain tumours or dementia. It may also be caused by the use of, or abrupt discontinuation of, certain drugs and medications. Brief psychotic disorder can also occur in response to extreme stress.

Signs and symptoms

The symptoms of psychosis are traditionally divided into positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and disorganised symptoms. Positive symptoms are those added to normal experience, principally hallucinations and delusions. Hallucinations may affect any of the senses, but auditory hallucinations — hearing voices — are the most common in psychotic disorders. Delusions are fixed false beliefs held despite contrary evidence; common themes include persecution, grandiosity, and reference.

Negative symptoms include diminished emotional expression, avolition (lack of motivation), alogia (poverty of speech), and anhedonia. Disorganisation may appear as tangentially organised or incoherent speech ("thought disorder") and grossly disorganised behaviour. Psychosis may also involve catatonia, a syndrome of motor abnormality.

Causes

Psychosis is associated with a range of psychiatric diagnoses. It is a defining feature of schizophrenia spectrum disorders and occurs frequently in the manic phase of bipolar disorder. Other causes include severe depression, postpartum changes, substance intoxication or withdrawal (including alcohol, cannabis, and stimulants such as methamphetamine), prescription medications such as high-dose steroids, sleep deprivation, and neurological conditions including Parkinson's disease and temporal lobe epilepsy.

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that dysregulation of dopaminergic signalling — particularly excess dopamine activity in mesolimbic pathways — contributes to positive psychotic symptoms. Genetic factors play a substantial role: having a first-degree relative with a psychotic disorder significantly increases risk. Developmental and environmental factors, including obstetric complications, childhood trauma, urban upbringing, and migration, have also been associated with increased incidence.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical interview and mental state examination, together with exclusion of medical and substance-related causes through history, physical examination, and laboratory testing where indicated. Structured criteria in the DSM-5 and ICD-11 define the psychotic disorders by the pattern and duration of symptoms. A first episode of psychosis typically prompts investigation for underlying medical causes and assessment of risk to self or others.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the underlying cause. Antipsychotic medications, which principally block dopamine D2 receptors, are the mainstay of treatment for most psychotic disorders; they are divided into first-generation (typical) and second-generation (atypical) agents, the latter having a lower risk of extrapyramidal side effects. Clozapine is uniquely effective for treatment-resistant schizophrenia but requires blood monitoring because of the risk of agranulocytosis.

Psychosocial interventions — including cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis (CBTp), family interventions, supported employment, and early intervention services — improve outcomes and reduce relapse. Early treatment of a first psychotic episode is associated with better long-term prognosis, which has driven the establishment of early intervention in psychosis services in many countries.

Epidemiology and history

Psychotic disorders affect roughly 3% of people at some point in their lives, with schizophrenia itself having a lifetime prevalence of about 0.3–0.7%. The word psychosis was introduced in 1845 by Ernst von Feuchtersleben and derives from the Greek psyche (mind) and -osis (abnormal condition). Emil Kraepelin's distinction between dementia praecox and manic-depressive illness in the late 19th century laid the foundations of modern classification of the psychoses.
