Targeted therapy

Targeted therapy is a form of cancer treatment that uses drugs designed to act on specific molecular targets — usually proteins involved in the growth, progression, and spread of cancer — rather than killing all rapidly dividing cells as conventional chemotherapy does. It is a cornerstone of precision medicine.

Types

Most targeted therapies are either small-molecule drugs, which can enter cells and inhibit enzymes such as tyrosine kinases (for example imatinib against BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukaemia), or monoclonal antibodies, which bind targets on cell surfaces (for example trastuzumab against HER2 in breast cancer). Other classes include proteasome inhibitors, PARP inhibitors, and angiogenesis inhibitors.

Use and limitations

Treatment is often guided by biomarker testing of a tumour's mutations or protein expression. Targeted drugs generally have different, sometimes milder, side-effect profiles than cytotoxic chemotherapy, but cancers frequently develop resistance through new mutations, so combinations and successive lines of therapy are common.
