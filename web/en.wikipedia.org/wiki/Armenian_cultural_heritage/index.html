Armenian cultural heritage encompasses the architecture, art, language, literature, music, and religious traditions created by the Armenian people over more than two millennia, in their highland homeland between Anatolia, the Caucasus, and Persia, and in the worldwide Armenian diaspora.

Architecture and stone carving

Armenia, the first state to adopt Christianity (traditionally in 301 AD), developed a distinctive church architecture of central domes on cruciform plans, exemplified by the 7th-century cathedral of Zvartnots, the monasteries of Haghpat, Sanahin, and Geghard, and the cathedral of Etchmiadzin. The khachkar — an intricately carved cross-stone — is the emblematic Armenian art form, inscribed on UNESCO's intangible heritage list. Illuminated manuscripts, preserved at the Matenadaran in Yerevan, form another celebrated tradition.

Language, music, and cuisine

The Armenian alphabet (c. 405 AD) enabled a rich literature from the 5th-century golden age onward. Musical heritage ranges from sacred chant and the ashugh troubadour tradition to the duduk, a double-reed woodwind whose playing is UNESCO-listed. Culinary symbols include lavash flatbread and dolma.

Preservation and loss

Armenian heritage has suffered extensive destruction, including during the Armenian genocide, when churches and monuments in western Armenia were ruined or repurposed, and in later conflicts in Nagorno-Karabakh, where the fate of Armenian monuments has been a subject of international monitoring and dispute.
