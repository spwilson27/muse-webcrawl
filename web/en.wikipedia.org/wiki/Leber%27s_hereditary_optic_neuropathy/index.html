Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a maternally inherited (transmitted from mother to offspring) degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and their axons that leads to an acute or subacute loss of central vision. It predominantly affects young adult males. LHON is transmitted only through the mother, as it is primarily due to mutations in the mitochondrial (not nuclear) genome, and only the egg contributes mitochondria to the embryo.

Causes

Three point mutations in mitochondrial DNA account for the great majority of cases: m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A and m.14484T>C, which affect the ND1, ND4 and ND6 subunit genes of complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. The 11778 mutation is the most common worldwide. Men are affected roughly four to five times more often than women, and not all carriers of a mutation develop the disease, a phenomenon known as incomplete penetrance.

Symptoms and course

Vision loss typically begins in one eye with painless blurring and loss of colour vision, followed within weeks or months by the fellow eye. A dense central scotoma develops, and visual acuity commonly falls to counting fingers or worse. In a minority of patients, particularly those with the 14484 mutation, some spontaneous recovery of vision can occur.

Treatment

Idebenone, a synthetic analogue of coenzyme Q10, has been approved in Europe for the treatment of LHON and may improve outcomes when started early. Patients are generally advised to avoid smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, which may act as environmental triggers. Gene therapy trials targeting the ND4 mutation have been investigated.

History

The condition is named after the German ophthalmologist Theodor Leber (1840–1917), who described the characteristic clinical picture in 1871.
