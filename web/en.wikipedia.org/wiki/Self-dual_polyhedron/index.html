Self-dual polyhedron

The regular tetrahedron is dual to itself

A self-dual polyhedron is a polyhedron whose dual polyhedron — obtained by interchanging vertices and faces — is congruent or combinatorially identical to the original. A necessary condition is that the polyhedron has the same number of vertices as faces, with corresponding arrangements of edges and vertex figures.

Examples

The best-known example is the regular tetrahedron: its four vertices and four triangular faces correspond exactly, and joining the centres of its faces produces another tetrahedron. All regular pyramids with polygonal bases are self-dual in the combinatorial sense, as are many canonical forms. Families of self-dual polyhedra include the elongated square pyramid and various Johnson solids, and infinitely many irregular examples can be generated from self-dual planar graphs.

Self-dual graphs and polytopes

By Steinitz's theorem the concept extends to planar graphs: a graph is self-dual if it is isomorphic to its dual graph; wheel graphs are standard examples. In higher dimensions, self-dual polytopes include the 24-cell in four dimensions and certain regular polygons (all of which are self-dual) in two dimensions. Euler's formula implies that for a self-dual polyhedron the number of edges is exactly twice the number of vertices minus two.
