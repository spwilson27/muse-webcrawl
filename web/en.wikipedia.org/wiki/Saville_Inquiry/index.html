Saville Inquiry

Chairman: Lord Saville of Newdigate
Established: 1998
Report published: 15 June 2010
Subject: Bloody Sunday, Derry, 30 January 1972

The Guildhall, Derry, principal venue of the inquiry.

The Saville Inquiry was a public inquiry established by the British government in 1998 to reinvestigate the events of Bloody Sunday, when British soldiers shot 26 unarmed civilians during a civil rights march in Derry, Northern Ireland, on 30 January 1972, killing 14. It was chaired by Lord Saville of Newdigate, sitting with two other judges, and replaced the earlier Widgery Report, which had been widely rejected by the families of the victims.

Proceedings

The inquiry was announced by Prime Minister Tony Blair in January 1998, shortly before the Good Friday Agreement. It heard evidence over several years, principally in the Guildhall in Derry and later in London, taking statements from thousands of witnesses including soldiers, civilians, politicians and officials, and it became the longest-running and most expensive public inquiry in British legal history, costing nearly £200 million.

Report and aftermath

The report was published on 15 June 2010. It concluded that none of those killed or wounded was posing a threat, that the soldiers fired first, that no warning was given, and that some of the soldiers' evidence had been knowingly untruthful. Prime Minister David Cameron, presenting the report to Parliament, formally apologised, describing the killings as "unjustified and unjustifiable". The findings vindicated the campaign of the victims' families; subsequent police investigations considered prosecutions of individual soldiers.
