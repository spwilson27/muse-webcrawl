Schwann cell

Schwann cells (named after the physiologist Theodor Schwann) are the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system (PNS). They support neurons by wrapping around axons, forming the myelin sheath in myelinated fibres, and by enclosing groups of unmyelinated axons (Remak bundles). Their counterparts in the central nervous system are oligodendrocytes.

A Schwann cell forming myelin around a peripheral axon.

Function

Myelinating Schwann cells each insulate a single segment of one axon, greatly increasing conduction velocity through saltatory conduction between the gaps (nodes of Ranvier). Non-myelinating Schwann cells provide metabolic support and protection to small-diameter axons, including pain fibres.

Nerve repair

Unlike central neurons, peripheral nerves can regenerate effectively. After injury, Schwann cells clear debris, form bands of Büngner that guide regrowing axons, and secrete growth factors. Because of this supportive role they are a focus of cell-therapy research for spinal cord injury.

Associated diseases

Disorders involving Schwann cells include Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome, and tumours such as schwannomas (including vestibular schwannoma) and neurofibromas.
