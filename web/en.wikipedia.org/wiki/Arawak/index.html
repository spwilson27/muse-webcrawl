Arawak

Arawak peoples
RegionsCaribbean; northern South America (Amazonia, the Guianas)
LanguagesArawakan languages (Taíno, Lokono, Wayuu, Garífuna, and others)

The Arawak are a group of Indigenous peoples of northern South America and the Caribbean whose languages belong to the Arawakan (Maipurean) family, one of the largest language families of the Americas at European contact. The term is applied particularly to the Lokono of the Guianas — who call themselves Lokono, "the people", and whose language is sometimes called "true" Arawak — and historically to the Taíno of the Greater Antilles, the first Indigenous Americans encountered by Christopher Columbus in 1492.

History

Arawakan-speaking peoples are thought to have originated in the Orinoco basin and spread from about 2,500 years ago through Amazonia, the Guianas, and the Caribbean island chain, carrying with them sophisticated agriculture based on bitter manioc (cassava), ceramics of the Saladoid tradition, and ball-game ceremonialism. Taíno society in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico was organised into chiefdoms (cacicazgos) ruled by caciques, with a religion centred on zemí spirit figures.

Within decades of 1492, the Taíno population collapsed catastrophically from epidemic disease, enslavement, and violence under the Spanish encomienda system — a demographic disaster documented by Bartolomé de las Casas. Nevertheless Taíno ancestry persists substantially in modern Caribbean populations, and Taíno words entered world languages in abundance: hurricane, hammock, tobacco, maize, canoe, iguana, barbecue, and cacique.

Modern peoples

Surviving Arawakan-speaking peoples include the Wayuu of the Guajira Peninsula (the largest Indigenous group in Colombia and Venezuela), the Lokono of Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana, the Wapishana and Terêna, the Asháninka of Peru, and — through mixed Arawak and Carib heritage — the Garífuna of Central America. Taíno descendant and revival movements are active in Puerto Rico and the diaspora.
