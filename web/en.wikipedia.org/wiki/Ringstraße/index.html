The Ringstraße (German pronunciation: [ˈʁɪŋˌʃtʁaːsə]; lit. "ring road") is a grand circular boulevard in Vienna, Austria, encircling the Innere Stadt (First District). Built in the second half of the 19th century on the site of the city's demolished fortifications, it is lined with many of Vienna's most famous public buildings and monuments, built in the historicist style known as Ringstraßenstil, and is regarded as one of the great urban-planning achievements of the 19th century.

History

In 1857 Emperor Franz Joseph I issued his famous decree Es ist Mein Wille ("It is my will"), ordering the demolition of the medieval city walls and moats that separated the old town from its suburbs, and the creation of a broad ring boulevard on the cleared ground, including the glacis beyond. The project was financed by selling building plots along the new boulevard. The Ringstraße was officially opened by the Emperor on 1 May 1865, although construction of its monumental buildings continued into the 1890s.

The planners deliberately grouped different functions along the ring's segments: the area near the Hofburg became a new imperial forum; the university and city hall anchored a civic quarter; and parks such as the Stadtpark and the Burggarten softened the street with greenery. Private palaces (Ringstraßenpalais) built by the wealthy industrial and banking aristocracy filled many of the remaining plots.

Notable buildings

  • Vienna State Opera (Staatsoper), opened 1869
  • Academy of Fine Arts
  • Austrian Parliament Building
  • City Hall (Rathaus), in neo-Gothic style
  • University of Vienna main building
  • Burgtheater
  • Vienna Stock Exchange (Börse)
  • Votive Church (Votivkirche)
  • Museum of Fine Arts (Kunsthistorisches Museum) and Natural History Museum, facing each other across Maria-Theresien-Platz
  • Palace of Justice
  • Museum of Applied Arts (MAK), the first museum building on the Ring

Style and criticism

The buildings of the Ringstraße deploy historicist styles chosen to symbolise each institution's function: Greek Revival for the Parliament (democracy), Gothic for the City Hall (municipal freedom), Renaissance for the University (learning and rebirth), and Baroque for the Imperial theatres and museums. Critics such as Camillo Sitte praised its urban qualities while questioning its eclectic styling; the architect Adolf Loos famously satirised the decorated façades as a "Potemkin city". Nevertheless, the Ringstraße ensemble is protected as part of Vienna's UNESCO World Heritage–listed historic centre.

The Ring today

The boulevard's named segments (among them Opernring, Burgring, Dr.-Karl-Renner-Ring, Universitätsring, and Schottenring) carry car traffic and tram lines that circle the old town. The Ringstraße remains a ceremonial axis of the city, used for parades, demonstrations, marathons, and the annual Life Ball and Rainbow Parade events routed along it.
