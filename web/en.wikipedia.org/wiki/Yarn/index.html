Yarn

Yarn is a long continuous length of interlocked fibres, suitable for use in the production of textiles, sewing, crocheting, knitting, weaving, embroidery, or ropemaking. The word yarn is usually reserved for products that have been spun — drawn out and twisted together — from staple (short) fibres such as wool or cotton, although continuous-filament yarns made of silk or synthetic fibres are also called yarn.

Production

Yarn is made by spinning raw fibre. Traditionally this was done on the drop spindle and the spinning wheel; the Industrial Revolution brought the spinning jenny, water frame, and spinning mule. Modern mills use ring spinning, which produces the strongest yarns, and open-end (rotor) spinning, which is faster and cheaper. Spun yarn is wound onto bobbins, then often doubled (plied) — two or more singles twisted together — to make stronger, more balanced yarn for knitting or weaving.

Types

Yarns are classified by fibre (animal: wool, mohair, cashmere, alpaca, silk; plant: cotton, linen, hemp, bamboo; synthetic: acrylic, nylon, polyester), by structure (single, plied, cabled, novelty yarns such as boucle and chenille), and by weight — from lace weight through fingering, sport, DK, worsted, to super bulky. Yarn "count" measures fineness: in the cotton count system, higher numbers mean finer yarn; the direct tex and denier systems instead measure mass per unit length.

History

Spinning is one of humanity's oldest crafts; drop spindles are found in Neolithic archaeological sites, and linen yarns from the Paleolithic have been recovered from a Georgian cave. The hand-spinning of wool and cotton was central to pre-industrial textile economies and to resistance movements — notably the use of the charkha spinning wheel in Gandhi's khadi campaign in India.
