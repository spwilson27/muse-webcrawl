Chinese classifiers

Chinese classifiers (量詞, liàngcí), also called measure words, are words that must be used between a numeral or demonstrative and a noun when counting or specifying objects in Chinese: for example, 三本書 (sān běn shū, "three books"), where 本 is the classifier for bound volumes. Mandarin Chinese has dozens of classifiers in common use, each conventionally associated with particular classes of nouns.

Types

Linguists distinguish sortal classifiers, which categorise nouns by inherent properties such as shape, animacy or function — 張 zhāng for flat objects, 條 tiáo for long flexible things, 隻 zhī for many animals, 輛 liàng for vehicles — from mensural classifiers (true measure words) denoting containers, groups and standard units, such as 杯 bēi "cupful" or 群 qún "group". The classifier 個 gè serves as a general-purpose default and in colloquial speech often replaces more specific classifiers.

Syntax

Classifiers occur in the pattern numeral–classifier–noun and demonstrative–classifier–noun (這本書, "this book"). In many contexts the classifier can appear with a demonstrative without a numeral, and some classifier–noun combinations can omit the noun when it is understood. Classifiers are obligatory in Mandarin but vary across Chinese varieties in inventory and usage.

History

The classifier system developed gradually during the Han dynasty and the following centuries, expanding as Chinese grammar shifted toward analytic structures. Similar numeral classifier systems are found in neighbouring languages such as Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese and Thai, partly through contact and areal influence.
