Atom probe

The atom probe is a microscopy technique that provides three-dimensional, atom-by-atom maps of the chemical identity and position of atoms within a small volume of material. In atom probe tomography (APT), a needle-shaped specimen with a very sharp tip (radius below 100 nm) is subjected to a high electric field, sometimes combined with laser or voltage pulses, so that atoms are ionized and evaporated from the tip one by one.

Principle

Evaporated ions are accelerated toward a position-sensitive detector. The identity of each ion is determined from its mass-to-charge ratio by time-of-flight mass spectrometry, while the detector records the impact position; combining these data, and the sequence in which ions arrive, allows a computer to reconstruct the original three-dimensional arrangement of atoms in the specimen with near-atomic resolution and chemical sensitivity down to parts per million.

History

The atom probe grew out of the field ion microscope developed by Erwin Wilhelm Müller in the 1950s. In 1967–1968 Müller, John Panitz and S. Brooks McLane combined field ion microscopy with a mass spectrometer to create the first atom probe. Subsequent developments—position-sensitive detectors, voltage- and laser-pulsed commercial instruments (such as the LEAP series)—turned APT into a routine, if specialized, microanalysis method.

Applications

Atom probe tomography is used in metallurgy and semiconductor science to study solute segregation at grain boundaries, nanoscale precipitates, dopant distributions, and radiation damage, and increasingly in geology, battery research and catalysis. Its limitations include the demanding specimen preparation—usually by focused ion beam milling—and the small analysed volume, typically tens to hundreds of nanometres across.
