Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624) was an Indian Islamic scholar, Hanafi jurist and a prominent member of the Naqshbandi Sufi order. He is revered in South Asian Islam as Mujaddid Alf Thani, the "Reviver of the Faith in the Second Millennium", for his efforts to revive orthodox Sunni Islam in Mughal India in reaction against the religious policies of the emperor Akbar.

Life

Sirhindi was born in Sirhind in the Punjab and educated there and in Sialkot. He joined the Naqshbandi order under Khwaja Baqi Billah in Delhi and quickly rose to prominence as a teacher. He wrote hundreds of letters — collected as the Maktubat — to disciples, nobles and officials, setting out his theological and political views.

Views

Sirhindi opposed Akbar's policy of sulh-i-kul ("peace with all") and the syncretic Din-i Ilahi, insisting on the supremacy of the sharia and the separation of Islam from other faiths in public life. In Sufi metaphysics he challenged the popular doctrine of wahdat al-wujud ("unity of being") associated with Ibn Arabi, proposing instead wahdat ash-shuhud ("unity of witness"), which held that the experience of unity is subjective and that God and creation remain distinct.

Imprisonment and influence

The emperor Jahangir, suspicious of Sirhindi's following and offended that he would not perform the prostration before the throne, imprisoned him in Gwalior Fort in 1619; he was released after about a year and thereafter kept close to the court. His Naqshbandi-Mujaddidi lineage became one of the most influential Sufi branches in India, Central Asia and the Ottoman world.
