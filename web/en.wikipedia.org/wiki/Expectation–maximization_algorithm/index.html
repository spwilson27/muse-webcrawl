Expectation–maximization algorithm

The expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm is an iterative method in statistics for finding maximum likelihood or maximum a posteriori estimates of parameters in models that depend on unobserved latent variables. The algorithm alternates between an expectation (E) step, which computes the expected value of the log-likelihood using the current parameter estimates, and a maximization (M) step, which updates the parameters to maximise that expected log-likelihood. Each iteration is guaranteed not to decrease the likelihood, and the procedure is repeated until convergence.

History

Special cases of the method were used for decades before it was unified and named by Arthur Dempster, Nan Laird, and Donald Rubin in their landmark 1977 paper, which also proved the monotone convergence property under general conditions. C. F. Jeff Wu later refined the convergence theory, showing convergence to a stationary point rather than necessarily the global maximum — EM commonly converges to a local optimum, so initialisation matters.

Applications

EM is widely used for fitting mixture models such as Gaussian mixtures (for clustering), training hidden Markov models (where the E-step is implemented by the forward–backward algorithm and EM is known as the Baum–Welch algorithm), estimating parameters with missing data, and in medical imaging, natural language processing, and bioinformatics. Variants include generalised EM, which only needs to increase rather than maximise the expected log-likelihood in the M-step, and online EM for streaming data.
