Market maker

A market maker or liquidity provider is a company or an individual that quotes both a buy and a sell price in a tradable asset held in inventory, hoping to make a profit on the bid–ask spread, or turn. The firm benefits from the difference between the price at which it is willing to buy a security (the bid price) and the price at which it is willing to sell it (the ask price).

A market maker continuously quotes bid and ask prices to provide liquidity.

Function

Market makers provide liquidity and depth to markets and profit from the difference between the bid and ask prices. Because markets need participants willing to trade at all times, exchanges often designate official market makers for particular securities. In the United States, the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) uses designated market makers, formerly known as "specialists", while Nasdaq market makers compete with one another through an electronic network.

By continuously quoting two-sided prices, market makers guarantee that buyers and sellers can transact immediately, rather than waiting for a counterparty to appear. This reduces volatility caused by thin order books and lowers transaction costs for other market participants.

Profit and risk

The market maker's profit comes from the spread: buying at the bid and selling at a slightly higher ask. However, the market maker faces inventory risk — if it accumulates a large position in an asset whose price moves against it, it can suffer losses. It also faces adverse selection: informed traders may trade with the market maker precisely because they know the price is about to move. Market makers manage these risks by adjusting quotes, widening spreads in volatile conditions, and hedging positions.

High-frequency trading

Modern market making is dominated by high-frequency trading firms such as Citadel Securities, Virtu Financial, Jane Street and Optiver, which use automated algorithms to quote prices across thousands of instruments simultaneously. These firms handle a substantial share of retail and institutional order flow in equities, options, futures and foreign exchange.
