Dirichlet's unit theorem

In mathematics, Dirichlet's unit theorem is a basic result in algebraic number theory due to Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet. It determines the rank of the group of units in the ring of algebraic integers of a number field. The group of units is finitely generated and has rank equal to r = r1 + r2 − 1, where r1 is the number of real embeddings of the field and r2 is the number of conjugate pairs of complex embeddings.

Statement

Let K be a number field and let 𝒪K be its ring of integers. Dirichlet's unit theorem states that the group of units 𝒪K× is a finitely generated abelian group of the form

𝒪K× ≅ μ(K) × ℤ(r1 + r2 − 1),

where μ(K) is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity contained in K. A set of units whose images generate the free part is called a set of fundamental units.

Examples

  • For K = ℚ, the rank is 0 and the unit group is just {±1}.
  • For a real quadratic field such as ℚ(√5), the rank is 1: the units are ±εn, where ε is the fundamental unit; for ℚ(√5), ε = (1+√5)/2.
  • For an imaginary quadratic field, the rank is 0, so the unit group is finite, consisting only of roots of unity.

The regulator

The volume of the fundamental domain of the lattice obtained by mapping units into Euclidean space via their logarithms is measured by the regulator of the field, which appears in the analytic class number formula. A proof of the theorem combines this logarithmic embedding with Minkowski's theorem on linear forms.
