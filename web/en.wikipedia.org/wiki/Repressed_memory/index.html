Repressed memory

Repressed memory is a controversial hypothetical concept originating in psychoanalytic theory, according to which the mind unconsciously blocks memories of traumatic events — particularly childhood sexual abuse — so that they become inaccessible to ordinary recall, yet continue to influence behaviour and may later be recovered, sometimes during psychotherapy.

History

The idea derives from Sigmund Freud's theory of repression, a proposed defence mechanism by which distressing thoughts are excluded from consciousness. In the 1980s and early 1990s, a "recovered memory" movement within psychotherapy held that many psychological symptoms were caused by repressed memories of abuse, and techniques such as hypnosis, guided imagery and age regression were used to recover them.

Controversy

The concept became the subject of intense scientific and legal dispute in the "memory wars" of the 1990s. Memory researchers, notably Elizabeth Loftus, demonstrated that suggestive therapeutic techniques could create vivid false memories of events that never occurred. A number of criminal convictions and civil suits based on recovered memories were later overturned, and some patients retracted their accusations. Professional bodies have generally concluded that while genuinely forgotten traumatic experiences can occur, memories "recovered" through suggestive techniques are unreliable, and that there is little controlled scientific evidence for repression as Freud described it.

Status

Recovered-memory therapy is now widely discouraged by mainstream psychological associations. Related concepts, such as dissociative amnesia, remain in diagnostic manuals, but their mechanisms and prevalence are still debated among researchers.
