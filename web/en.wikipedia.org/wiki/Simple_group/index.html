Simple group

In mathematics, a simple group is a nontrivial group whose only normal subgroups are the trivial group and the group itself. A group that is not simple can be broken into two smaller groups, namely a nontrivial normal subgroup and the quotient group; this process can be repeated, and for finite groups one eventually arrives at uniquely determined simple groups by the Jordan–Hölder theorem.

Definition. A group G is called simple if it is not the trivial group and its only normal subgroups are {e} and G itself.

Examples

The cyclic group Z/5Z of integers modulo 5 under addition is simple: it has no nontrivial subgroups at all, hence no nontrivial normal subgroups. More generally, the cyclic groups of prime order are the only finite abelian simple groups. The smallest non-abelian simple group is the alternating group A5, of order 60; all non-abelian finite simple groups have even order by the Feit–Thompson theorem.

Classification

The classification of finite simple groups, completed in the early 1980s after decades of work by hundreds of mathematicians, states that every finite simple group belongs to one of the following families:

  • cyclic groups of prime order;
  • alternating groups of degree at least five;
  • groups of Lie type (including the classical Chevalley groups and the twisted groups);
  • one of 26 exceptional groups, the sporadic groups, the largest of which is the Monster group.

The proof runs to tens of thousands of journal pages and is widely regarded as one of the largest collaborative achievements in mathematics; a "second-generation" proof has been undertaken to condense and verify it.

Infinite simple groups

The definition applies equally to infinite groups. The alternating group on an infinite set is simple, as is the group PSLn over infinite fields in most cases. Thompson's groups T and V are examples of finitely presented infinite simple groups.
