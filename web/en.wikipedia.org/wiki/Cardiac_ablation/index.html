Cardiac ablation

Cardiac ablation is a procedure that uses energy — most commonly radiofrequency heat or cryotherapy (extreme cold) — to create small scars in heart tissue and block the abnormal electrical pathways that cause arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and supraventricular tachycardia.

Cardiac ablation
Field: Cardiology / electrophysiology
Methods: Radiofrequency, cryoablation, pulsed field
Treats: AFib, flutter, SVT, WPW

Procedure

A cardiologist threads thin catheters through a vein, usually in the groin, to the heart. After mapping the heart's electrical activity, targeted tissue is ablated. For atrial fibrillation, pulmonary vein isolation is the cornerstone technique. Most patients are sedated or under general anaesthesia, and the procedure typically takes two to four hours.

Risks and outcomes

Risks include bleeding, stroke, damage to the heart's conduction system, and, rarely, atrio-oesophageal fistula. Success rates vary by arrhythmia type; ablation is generally recommended when medication fails or is not tolerated.
