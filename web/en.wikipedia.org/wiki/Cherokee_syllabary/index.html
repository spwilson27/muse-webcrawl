The Cherokee syllabary is a writing system invented by the Cherokee silversmith Sequoyah in the late 1810s and early 1820s to write the Cherokee language. It is a syllabary: each character represents a syllable of the language, and in its modern form it contains 85 characters.

Invention

Sequoyah, who spoke only Cherokee, became convinced of the value of writing — the "talking leaves" of the settlers — and spent about twelve years developing a system, first attempting logographic characters and then arriving at a syllabary. He completed it around 1821 and demonstrated it with his daughter Ayoka. The Cherokee Nation adopted the syllabary officially in 1825, and literacy spread with remarkable speed: within a few years a large proportion of the Cherokee population could read and write.

Use

In 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix began publication using type cast for the syllabary, and the system has remained in continuous use in books, newspapers, signage, and language-revitalisation programmes in Oklahoma and North Carolina. Some of the printed letterforms, adapted to typographic conventions, resemble Latin capital letters (such as Ꭰ, Ꭱ, Ᏻ) but with entirely different sound values. The syllabary is encoded in Unicode in the Cherokee block.
