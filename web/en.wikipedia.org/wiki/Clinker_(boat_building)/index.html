Clinker (boat building)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The stepped, overlapping strakes of a clinker-built hull

Clinker-built (also known as lapstrake) is a method of boat building in which the edges of hull planks overlap each other. Where necessary in larger craft, shorter planks can be joined end to end, creating a longer strake. Each strake overlaps the one below it and is fastened with copper or iron rivets clenched over roves (small square washers), producing a distinctive stepped surface on the outside of the hull.

History

The technique is strongly associated with northern Europe. It was used for the wooden boats of the Vikings — longships and knarrs — whose light, flexible, shallow-draught hulls were strong in a seaway yet could be beached. Finds such as the Gokstad and Oseberg ships demonstrate the method. Clinker construction continued into the modern era for lifeboats, dinghies, and working boats.

Advantages and disadvantages

Clinker hulls are light and resilient because the riveted laps act as continuous longitudinal stiffeners. However, the method requires more labour and skill than carvel construction (planks laid edge to edge), and the lapped surface creates extra drag, so large vessels eventually favoured carvel or steel hulls.

Modern use

Traditional wooden clinker boats are still built for heritage and pleasure use, and the appearance is imitated in moulded plywood, GRP, and strip-planked boats ("clinker-effect" hulls).
