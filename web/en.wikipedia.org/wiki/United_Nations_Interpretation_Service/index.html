The United Nations Interpretation Service provides simultaneous interpretation for meetings of the United Nations in the organization's six official languages: Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish. It forms part of the Department for General Assembly and Conference Management and services the General Assembly, the Security Council, ECOSOC, and hundreds of other intergovernmental meetings each year.

Working methods

Interpreters work in soundproof booths, one booth per language, in teams of two or three, alternating roughly every 20–30 minutes. English and French booths traditionally work from all other languages, while the other booths often work through "relay"—taking their input from the English or French interpretation when they cannot work directly from the language spoken. Interpreters generally work into their mother tongue. Because delegates may speak at up to 160–180 words per minute, interpreters rely on advance texts, terminology databases, and preparation on highly technical subject matter.

Recruitment and history

Staff interpreters are recruited through competitive language examinations administered by the UN. Simultaneous interpretation at the UN grew out of the consecutive interpretation used at the League of Nations and the Nuremberg trials, where pioneering equipment and interpreters demonstrated the feasibility of the simultaneous method; it became standard practice at the young United Nations in the late 1940s. Alongside staff, the service engages freelance interpreters for peak periods such as the General Assembly's general debate each September.
