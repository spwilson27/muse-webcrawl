Xiang Army

The Xiang Army (Chinese: 湘軍; pinyin: Xiāngjūn) was a standing army organized by Zeng Guofan, a scholar-official of the Qing dynasty, drawn largely from his home province of Hunan (whose abbreviation is Xiang). Raised from 1853 onward, it became the principal force that suppressed the Taiping Rebellion, the deadliest civil war of the 19th century.

Organization

Unlike the hereditary Banner and Green Standard forces of the Qing, the Xiang Army was recruited personally by its commanders. Soldiers were selected mainly from rural Hunan, officers were often scholars from the gentry class, and units were bound by personal loyalty to their commanders rather than to the imperial bureaucracy. Pay was comparatively generous, funded through lijin transit taxes and provincial revenues rather than the imperial treasury.

Campaigns

The Xiang Army fought a long, grinding war against the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. After setbacks near the Xiang River region and in Jiangxi, Zeng's forces, with allied armies under commanders such as Zuo Zongtang and Zeng's brother Zeng Guoquan, tightened their grip on the Yangtze valley. In July 1864 Xiang Army troops captured Nanjing, the Taiping capital, effectively ending the rebellion.

Legacy

The Xiang Army model — regionally recruited, personally commanded forces financed by provincial taxes — was imitated by the Huai Army of Li Hongzhang and weakened the Qing court's direct control of military power. Historians often see in it a precedent for the warlord armies of the early 20th century. Zeng disbanded much of the force after 1864, but its officers dominated Chinese military and civil affairs for decades.
