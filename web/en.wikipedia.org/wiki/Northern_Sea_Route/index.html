Northern Sea Route

Icebreaker escort on the Northern Sea Route

The Northern Sea Route (Russian: Северный морской путь, Severnyy morskoy put) is a shipping route along the Arctic coast of Russia, from the Kara Sea to the Bering Strait. It forms the Russian-administered portion of the Northeast Passage and is defined in Russian law as extending from the Novaya Zemlya straits in the west to the Bering Strait in the east, a distance of some 5,600 kilometres.

Advantages and traffic

For voyages between Northern Europe and East Asia the route can be roughly 40 percent shorter than the Suez Canal route; the sailing distance from Rotterdam to Yokohama falls from about 11,200 to about 6,500 nautical miles. Traffic grew from a few million tonnes in the 2010s to over 30 million tonnes annually by the early 2020s, dominated by Russian liquefied natural gas from Yamal, oil and bulk cargoes rather than transit container shipping.

Navigation conditions

Even with declining Arctic sea ice, the route is ice-covered for much of the year and passages generally require ice-class vessels and often nuclear icebreaker escort; Russia charges fees and requires permits. Harsh weather, shallow straits, remoteness and limited search-and-rescue infrastructure constrain commercial use, and insurers price Arctic voyages accordingly.

History and governance

The route was developed by the Soviet Union from the 1930s as a national supply line for Arctic settlements and resources, but was closed to foreign shipping until 1991. Russia regards much of it as internal waters, a position not fully accepted by other states. Climate change is expected to lengthen the navigation season, and the route remains central to Russian Arctic strategy.
