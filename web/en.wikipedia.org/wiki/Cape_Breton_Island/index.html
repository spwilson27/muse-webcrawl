Cape Breton Island (French: île du Cap-Breton, formerly île Royale; Scottish Gaelic: Ceap Breatainn or Eilean Cheap Breatainn; Mi'kmaq: Unama'ki) is an island on the Atlantic coast of North America and part of the province of Nova Scotia, Canada.

The 10,311 km² island accounts for 18.7% of Nova Scotia's total area. Although the island is physically separated from the Nova Scotia peninsula by the Strait of Canso, it is artificially connected to mainland Nova Scotia by the rock-filled Canso Causeway.

Geography

Cape Breton Island is composed mainly of a rocky shore, rolling farmland, glacial valleys, barren headlands, mountains, woods and plateaus. The island's interior is dominated by the Bras d'Or Lake, a large saltwater estuary system. The northern portion is dominated by the Cape Breton Highlands, an extension of the Appalachian mountain chain.

History

The island's first residents were the ancestors of the Mi'kmaq, who have inhabited the island for thousands of years. French colonists established settlements in the early 17th century, and the fortress of Louisbourg became one of the most important French strongholds in North America before falling to the British in 1758. Large numbers of Scottish Gaelic settlers arrived in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, and Gaelic culture remains influential on the island.

Economy and culture

Coal mining and steel production around Sydney once anchored the economy; today tourism, fishing, and culture predominate. The island is renowned for its Celtic music traditions and the scenic Cabot Trail, one of Canada's most famous drives.
