The coronation of the British monarch is a ceremony (specifically, an initiation rite) in which the monarch of the United Kingdom is formally invested with regalia and crowned at Westminster Abbey. It corresponds to the coronations that formerly took place in other European monarchies, many of which have adopted simpler inauguration ceremonies. The coronation usually takes place several months after the death of the previous monarch, following a period of mourning. A coronation is not legally necessary for a monarch to reign; indeed, the ceremony usually happens many months after accession, and Edward VIII was never crowned yet was undoubtedly king.

The service

The service, based substantially on the texts used since the coronation of Edgar at Bath in 973 and codified in the Liber Regalis, is conducted by the Archbishop of Canterbury. Its main elements are: the recognition and oath, in which the sovereign promises to govern according to law and to maintain the Protestant religion and the Church of England; the anointing with consecrated oil on hands, breast and head — the most sacred moment, traditionally shielded from view; the investiture with the spurs, sword, armills, robe and orb, the ring, the sceptres; the crowning with St Edward's Crown on the Coronation Chair, which encloses the Stone of Scone; and the enthroning and homage. Communion is celebrated, and the sovereign departs wearing the Imperial State Crown.

Regalia and participants

The Honours of the United Kingdom (Crown Jewels) used include St Edward's Crown (1661), the Imperial State Crown, the Sceptre with the Cross, the Sceptre with the Dove, the Orb, the Ampulla and Spoon used for the anointing oil, and the Jewelled Sword of Offering. Peers and peeresses traditionally wore coronets and robes, and representatives of the other Commonwealth realms whose head of state the monarch is attend. The coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953 was the first to be fully televised; that of Charles III in 2023 was shorter and smaller, with about 2,000 guests, and included the involvement of other faith leaders for the first time.

History

English coronations have been held at Westminster Abbey since William the Conqueror in 1066. Notable coronations include that of William III and Mary II (1689), the only joint coronation of co-monarchs; the disastrously extravagant coronation of George IV (1821), from which his estranged queen was excluded; and the modest "half-crownation" of William IV (1831), whose cheapness set a more restrained precedent. The last coronation of a separate Scottish rite was Charles I's at Holyrood in 1633.
