Hans Spemann

Born27 June 1869, Stuttgart, Kingdom of Württemberg
Died9 September 1941 (aged 72), Freiburg, Germany
FieldsDevelopmental biology, embryology
Known forEmbryonic induction; the Spemann–Mangold organizer
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1935)

Hans Spemann (27 June 1869 – 9 September 1941) was a German embryologist, founder of the school of experimental developmental biology known as Entwicklungsmechanik, and the first embryologist to receive the Nobel Prize. He was awarded the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering embryonic induction — the phenomenon by which one group of cells directs the development of neighbouring tissue — centred on the region he called the "organizer".

Life and career

Spemann studied medicine and zoology at Heidelberg, Munich, and Würzburg, working with Theodor Boveri. He held professorships at Würzburg, Rostock, and the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Biology in Berlin-Dahlem, before settling at the University of Freiburg in 1919, where he led a celebrated school of developmental biology until his retirement in 1937.

Experiments

Spemann pioneered microsurgical techniques on amphibian embryos, using tools such as loops of baby's hair to constrict newt eggs. His constriction experiments on zygotes suggested that nuclei remained equipotent early in development — an early glimpse of the principle behind cloning. In famous work of 1918, he showed that tissue from a region fated to become skin could form a neural plate when transplanted, and vice versa, depending on position.

The organizer

In 1924 his doctoral student Hilde Mangold performed the crucial experiment: transplanting the dorsal lip of the blastopore from one newt gastrula to another produced a conjoined secondary embryo, largely organised from host tissue. Spemann and Mangold interpreted the graft as an "organization centre" — the Spemann–Mangold organizer — that induces the nervous system and body axis in surrounding cells. Mangold died in a household accident in 1924 and could not share the 1935 Nobel Prize, which was awarded to Spemann alone; he acknowledged her contribution in his Nobel lecture.

Legacy

The organizer effect guided the hunt for inducing molecules that culminated, decades later, in the identification of signalling proteins such as noggin and chordin and their antagonists of BMP signalling. Spemann's monograph Embryonic Development and Induction (1938) summarised the field he had created.
