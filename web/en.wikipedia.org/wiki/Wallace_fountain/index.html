Wallace fountain

A Wallace fountain is a type of public drinking fountain designed by the sculptor Charles-Auguste Lebourg and funded by the English philanthropist Sir Richard Wallace. Installed across Paris from 1872, the fountains are one of the most recognisable pieces of Parisian street furniture, and examples have since been installed in cities around the world.

DesignerCharles-Auguste Lebourg
DonorSir Richard Wallace
First installed1872, Paris
MaterialCast iron, painted green
Height2.71 m (large model)

History

After the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune of 1871, much of Paris's water infrastructure was damaged and the price of water rose sharply, leaving the poorest Parisians without reliable access to drinking water. Richard Wallace, a wealthy English collector who had remained in Paris during the siege, donated funds for the construction of public drinking fountains. The first were installed in 1872.

Design

The large model stands 2.71 metres tall and weighs about 600 kilograms. Its octagonal dome is supported by four caryatids representing kindness, simplicity, charity, and sobriety, and it is topped by a dolphin-like finial. Water flows in a thin stream from the centre, designed so that a cup or bottle could be filled without blocking the flow. Smaller wall-mounted models were also produced for narrow streets.

Distribution

More than 100 Wallace fountains survive in Paris, and others are found in cities including London, Lisbon, Montreal, and San Sebastián. In Paris they remain in use, maintained by the city, and they are painted the characteristic dark green of Parisian street furniture. The fountains are listed as historic monuments in some locations.
