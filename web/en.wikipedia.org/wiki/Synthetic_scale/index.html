Synthetic scale

In music theory, a synthetic scale is a scale that derives from a traditional musical scale (or another synthetic scale) by altering one or more of its notes by a chromatic semitone. The composer Ferruccio Busoni is closely associated with the concept: in his Sketch of a New Esthetic of Music (1907) he enumerated more than a hundred such scales derived by modifying the major and melodic minor scales, arguing that composers should invent the scalar material their works require rather than rely only on inherited patterns.

Derivation

Any alteration of a diatonic scale produces a synthetic scale. For example, raising the fourth degree of the major scale yields the Lydian mode's sharpened variant sometimes called the Lydian dominant scale when applied to melodic minor (C–D–E–F♯–G–A–B♭), a scale widely used in jazz over dominant seventh chords. Lowering the second degree of the major scale gives the Phrygian dominant family of sonorities. Because each degree of a heptatonic scale can independently be raised, lowered or left alone, the number of possible synthetic scales is very large, though only a fraction are in common use.

Examples in practice

  • The prometheus scale used by Alexander Scriabin (C–D–E–F♯–A–B♭), a six-note synthetic scale built from stacked fourths.
  • The blues scale, an adaptation of the minor pentatonic with an added flattened fifth.
  • Nicolas Slonimsky's thesaurus patterns, and the symmetric scales of Olivier Messiaen's "modes of limited transposition" — the whole-tone and octatonic scales — function compositionally as synthetic material.
  • Jazz improvisers routinely use altered scales such as the super-Locrian (altered) scale, the seventh mode of melodic minor, over altered dominant chords.
