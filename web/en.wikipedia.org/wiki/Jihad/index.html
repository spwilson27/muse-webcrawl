Jihad (Arabic: جهاد, jihād [dʒɪˈhaːd]; "struggle, striving") is an Arabic word that literally means "striving or struggling, especially with a praiseworthy aim." In Islamic tradition it denotes striving in the path of God, encompassing the internal struggle against sin and vice, moral and social effort, and — in classical jurisprudence — armed struggle under specified conditions. The term appears in the Quran and hadith and is elaborated in the legal literature of all major schools of Islamic law.

Muslims and scholars describe varying degrees and kinds of jihad: the "greater jihad" of self-discipline and the "lesser jihad" of external defence appear in later tradition and Sufi teaching, while classical jurists chiefly codified the military sense, with rules on declaration, conduct, non-combatants, and truces. In the modern era, the term has been invoked both by states and by movements — including militant Islamist groups whose use of it is contested by mainstream scholars — and it is one of the most debated Islamic concepts in public discourse.

Etymology and Quranic usage

Jihād derives from the root j-h-d, "to strive, exert effort." The Quran uses the term and its derivatives dozens of times, frequently in the phrase "striving in the path of God" (al-jihād fī sabīl Allāh), sometimes with wealth and self (e.g., Q 9:41; 49:15), sometimes in contexts of persecution and migration (Q 29:69; 22:78), and sometimes in connection with fighting (qitāl), which has its own terminology and rules (Q 2:190–193; 9:29; 47:4). Quranic commands are commonly read by classical jurists alongside rules limiting targets, honouring agreements, and prohibiting transgression (Q 2:190).

Classical jurisprudence

Classical legal manuals (fiqh) treat military jihad in books of siyar. Jurists generally classified it as a collective duty (farḍ kifāya) for expansion or defence under the caliph/imam's authority, becoming individually obligatory (farḍ ʿayn) when Muslim territory was attacked. Conditions and rules included: legitimate authority to declare it; prior invitation to Islam or treaty terms in the offensive case (per most schools); prohibition of killing non-combatants (women, children, the elderly, monks) based on prophetic instructions; prohibitions on mutilation and destruction of crops and animals beyond necessity (codified in Abu Bakr's instructions to armies); observance of treaties and safe-conducts (amān); and rules for prisoners, booty, and concluding truces. The world was analytically divided into dār al-Islām and dār al-ḥarb (with various intermediate categories), a framework later jurists adapted to changed circumstances.

The concept of martyrdom (shahāda) is closely associated with dying in lawful jihad, with extensive hadith literature on its rewards; classical scholars simultaneously warned that intention and lawful conditions determine the act's validity.

Spiritual and moral senses

Sufi and ethical literature emphasise the struggle against the lower self (jihād al-nafs). A widely cited hadith reports the Prophet, returning from battle, calling the fight against the self the "greater jihad" — its chain of transmission is classified weak by hadith critics, but the idea was influential through figures such as al-Ghazali, whose Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn structures the religious life as struggle against vice. Other standard classifications (e.g., Ibn al-Qayyim's) divide jihad into struggle against the self, against Satan, against wrongdoers and hypocrites (by tongue, wealth, and hand), and against armed aggressors.

Historical practice

Jihad provided one legal-ideological frame for the early Muslim conquests (7th–8th centuries) and for frontier warfare on the Byzantine, Iberian, Central Asian, and Indian frontiers. Over the centuries it was proclaimed by states and movements in varying circumstances: Ottoman sultans as ghazi-caliphs; anti-colonial resistances in the 19th and 20th centuries (e.g., in Algeria, the Caucasus under Imam Shamil, Sudan under the Mahdi, Libya under the Sanusis); and the Ottoman call of 1914, which had limited effect. Other mobilisations — such as the Soviet–Afghan war (1979–89) — drew transnational volunteers and reshaped modern militancy.

Modern interpretations

Modernist scholars (e.g., Muhammad Abduh, and many contemporary muftis and bodies such as al-Azhar) generally hold that Quran-sanctioned fighting is defensive and rule-bound, that the sword verses are context-specific, and that terrorism targeting civilians violates classical conditions; the 2004–05 Amman Message and the 2014 open "Letter to Baghdadi" by Muslim scholars set out such arguments in detail. Jihadist ideologues (from Sayyid Qutb's writings to Abdullah Azzam's defence doctrine and the manifestos of al-Qaeda and ISIS) propound expansive, individualised duty theories that mainstream jurists reject. Western media often translate jihad simply as "holy war," a rendering scholars criticise as imprecise: the Arabic for "war" is ḥarb, and jihad's semantic field is broader.
