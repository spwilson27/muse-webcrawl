Primary sclerosing cholangitis

Primary sclerosing cholangitis
Specialty: Gastroenterology, hepatology
Symptoms: Fatigue, itching, jaundice
Complications: Cirrhosis, liver failure, cholangiocarcinoma
Causes: Unknown; associated with ulcerative colitis

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a long-term progressive disease of the liver and gallbladder characterised by inflammation and scarring of the bile ducts, which normally allow bile to drain from the liver. The scarring progressively narrows the ducts and can lead to cirrhosis and liver failure.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with PSC have no symptoms for years. When symptoms occur they may include fatigue, itching (pruritus), jaundice, abdominal pain, and episodes of bacterial cholangitis. Fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies can develop because bile flow is impaired.

Causes and associations

The cause is unknown, but PSC is strongly associated with inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis; roughly 70% of people with PSC also have ulcerative colitis. Genetic and immune factors are believed to play a role.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically made with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), which show the characteristic beaded appearance of narrowed bile ducts. Liver biopsy is rarely needed.

Treatment

There is no proven medical therapy that alters the course of PSC. Management focuses on treating complications, and liver transplantation is the only definitive treatment for advanced disease. People with PSC have an increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma and colorectal cancer and require surveillance.
