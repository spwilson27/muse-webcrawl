Dark horse

A dark horse is a previously lesser-known person, team or thing that emerges to prominence in a situation, especially in a competition involving multiple rivals, apparently coming from nowhere or succeeding against the odds. The term originated in horse racing, where a dark horse was one whose form and abilities were unknown to bettors.

Origin

The phrase was popularised by Benjamin Disraeli in his novel The Young Duke (1831), in which a dark horse unexpectedly wins a race. From racing it spread to politics, where a dark horse candidate is a compromise or surprise nominee who emerges at a deadlocked convention; James K. Polk, nominated by the Democratic Party in 1844, is a classic American example. Abraham Lincoln and Warren G. Harding have also been described as dark horse nominees.

Other uses

In sport, an unfancied team that wins a tournament may be called a dark horse. The term is also used in entertainment and business for an unexpected success, and gave its name to Dark Horse Comics, a major American comic book publisher, and to George Harrison's record label Dark Horse Records.
