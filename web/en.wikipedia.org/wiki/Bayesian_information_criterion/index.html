Bayesian information criterion

The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) or Schwarz information criterion (also SIC, SBC, SBIC) is a criterion for model selection among a finite set of models; models with lower BIC are generally preferred. It is based, in part, on the likelihood function and it is closely related to the Akaike information criterion (AIC).

When fitting models, it is possible to increase the maximum likelihood by adding parameters, but doing so may result in overfitting. Both BIC and AIC attempt to resolve this problem by introducing a penalty term for the number of parameters in the model. The penalty term is larger in BIC than in AIC for typical sample sizes, so BIC tends to favour simpler models.

Definition

BIC = k ln(n) − 2 ln(L̂)

where L̂ is the maximised value of the likelihood function of the model, n is the number of data points, and k is the number of parameters estimated by the model. The BIC was developed by Gideon Schwarz, who gave a Bayesian argument for adopting it, in a 1978 paper.

Properties

  • The BIC is an asymptotic result derived under the assumption that the data distribution can be represented by a model in the candidate set.
  • Unlike AIC, BIC is consistent: as the sample size grows, the probability of selecting the true model approaches one (when the true model is among the candidates).
  • BIC penalises free parameters more strongly than AIC whenever ln(n) > 2, i.e. for n > 7.
  • Differences in BIC between two models can be interpreted roughly as evidence: differences of 0–2 are weak, 2–6 positive, 6–10 strong, and above 10 very strong evidence in favour of the model with the lower value.

Applications

BIC is widely used for model selection in regression, time-series analysis, clustering (for example in choosing the number of components in a mixture model), and in computational linguistics and phylogenetics. It is one of the most commonly reported information criteria in applied statistics.
