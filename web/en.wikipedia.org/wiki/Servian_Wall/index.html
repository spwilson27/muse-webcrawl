Servian Wall

The Servian Wall (Latin: Murus Servii Tullii) was an ancient Roman defensive wall surrounding the city of Rome. Tradition attributes it to the sixth king of Rome, Servius Tullius, in the 6th century BC, but the surviving stone circuit dates mainly to the Roman Republic, rebuilt in the 4th century BC after the sack of Rome by the Gauls in 390 BC.

Description

The wall was about 11 km long and enclosed roughly 426 hectares, incorporating the traditional seven hills of Rome. It was built of large blocks of tuff (Grotta Oscura stone) laid in alternating headers and stretchers, rising to about 10 metres in places. On the more vulnerable eastern side, across the Esquiline plain, the wall was supplemented by a broad ditch (fossa) and an earthen rampart (agger), creating a formidable defensive line.

The circuit had numerous gates, including the Porta Collina, Porta Viminale, Porta Esquilina, Porta Querquetulana, Porta Caelimontana, Porta Capena, Porta Naevia and Porta Raudusculana, several of which are known from literary sources.

History

The wall served Rome's defence during the Republic, resisting Hannibal's approach in 211 BC during the Second Punic War. As the city expanded beyond the circuit during the Empire, the wall lost military importance and buildings were erected against and over it. It was eventually superseded by the Aurelian Walls, built in the 3rd century AD.

Remains

Substantial sections survive today, most famously beside Roma Termini railway station and on the Aventine Hill, where a portion incorporating an arch for defensive artillery (catapult) can still be seen.
