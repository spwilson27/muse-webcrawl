Head arrangement

In jazz, a head arrangement is a musical arrangement formed from a spontaneous rehearsal or musical performance, remembered by all the players (that is, kept "in the head"), and not written down. Head arrangements are essentially arrangements worked out by ear, built by a band collectively through trial and error during rehearsals, and retained in memory rather than on paper.

Practice

Head arrangements were especially common in the territory bands and big bands of the 1920s and 1930s, many of whose members did not read music fluently and travelled constantly. A tune's simple written "head" — the melody and chord changes — would be elaborated in rehearsal: riffs were invented for the brass and saxophone sections, background figures devised to support soloists, and shout choruses constructed for climaxes. Because the arrangement belonged to the band's collective memory, it could be highly distinctive and difficult for rival bands to steal.

Notable examples

The Count Basie Orchestra was celebrated for its head arrangements, developed in its Kansas City period with minimal written music — standards such as "One O'Clock Jump" and "Jumpin' at the Woodside" grew out of riff-based heads. Fletcher Henderson's and Bennie Moten's bands also relied heavily on the practice before staff arrangers became standard in swing-era orchestras.
