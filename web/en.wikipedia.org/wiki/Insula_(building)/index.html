Insula (building)

In Roman architecture, an insula (Latin for "island", plural insulae) was a kind of apartment building that housed most of the urban population of ancient Rome. The term originally referred to a city block surrounded by streets, and by extension to the multi-storey tenement buildings filling such blocks. Insulae typically had shops and workshops (tabernae) on the ground floor and apartments on the floors above, and they provided housing for ordinary Romans, in contrast to the freestanding domus of the wealthy.

Description

Insulae were built of brick-faced concrete, timber, and mudbrick; quality varied greatly, and the cheapest upper rooms were cramped and hazardous. Ground-floor apartments could be relatively spacious, sometimes with running water, but upper floors usually lacked water, heating, and sanitation, requiring trips to public fountains and latrines. Staircases were often external. Because of poor construction and the use of wooden beams and braziers, insulae were notorious for collapse and fire; the satirist Juvenal mocked the danger of life in Rome's teetering tenements, and landlords such as Cicero owned insulae as income property.

Regulation

Augustus limited building heights to about 70 Roman feet (20.7 m), a limit reduced by Trajan to 60 feet (17.75 m) after disasters such as the Great Fire of 64 AD prompted stricter building codes. Regionary catalogues of the fourth century list more than 44,000 insulae in Rome against only about 1,800 domus, though the figures may count individual units or blocks and remain debated by scholars.

Surviving examples

The best evidence comes from Ostia Antica, Rome's port, where substantial insulae with courtyards survive, such as the House of Diana. In Rome itself, the Insula dell'Ara Coeli, excavated at the foot of the Capitoline Hill, preserves several floors of a second-century block.
