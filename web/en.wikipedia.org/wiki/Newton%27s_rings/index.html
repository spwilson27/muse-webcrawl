Newton's rings are a pattern of concentric bright and dark rings produced by the interference of light reflecting between two surfaces — typically a convex lens resting on a flat glass plate, separated by a thin film of air whose thickness grows with radius.

Explanation

Light reflected from the top of the air film and from the plate beneath it travels slightly different paths; where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths the waves reinforce, and where it is a half-integer they cancel. Because the air-film thickness increases quadratically with radius, the rings are closely spaced near the centre and their radii grow with the square root of the ring number. The centre spot is dark because reflection from the denser medium introduces a half-wave phase change.

Uses

The phenomenon, described by Isaac Newton in his Opticks (1704), is used to test the flatness and quality of optical surfaces and to measure the wavelength of light and the radius of curvature of lenses.
