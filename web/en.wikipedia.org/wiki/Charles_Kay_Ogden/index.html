Charles Kay Ogden (1 June 1889 – 20 March 1957) was an English linguist, philosopher and writer. As a Cambridge undergraduate he co-founded the Heretics Society and the magazine Cambridge Magazine. With I. A. Richards he wrote the influential book The Meaning of Meaning (1923), a pioneering work in semantics. He is best remembered as the inventor and propagator of Basic English, a controlled language of 850 words designed as an international auxiliary language.

The Meaning of Meaning

Written with I. A. Richards and supplemented by essays by the anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski and others, The Meaning of Meaning (1923) proposed the "semiotic triangle" relating symbols, thoughts (reference), and referents, and argued that words have no meaning in themselves but only through their users. It influenced literary criticism, philosophy of language, and the young discipline of semantics.

Basic English

Ogden presented Basic English in Basic English: A General Introduction with Rules and Grammar (1930). By restricting vocabulary to 850 words — 600 nouns, 150 adjectives, and 100 "operations" (verbs and function words) — and simplifying grammar, he claimed anything could be said. The system attracted the support of Winston Churchill and was promoted by the Orthological Institute, which Ogden founded; H. G. Wells's vision of a world language in The Shape of Things to Come drew on it. Critics, including George Orwell (whose Newspeak in Nineteen Eighty-Four is sometimes seen as a dark parody of such projects), doubted its clarity and universality.

Other work

Ogden edited the Psyche Miniatures and International Library book series, published Jeremy Bentham scholarship (he coined the term "deontology" in its modern sense while editing Bentham), and supervised translations of works including Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1922).
