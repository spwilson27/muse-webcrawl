Helper engine

A helper engine (also called a banking engine or pusher) is a railway locomotive temporarily assigned to assist a train over a steep gradient or "bank" that the train's own locomotives cannot climb unassisted, or cannot climb at an acceptable speed. Helpers are added at the foot of the grade — at the front, in the middle, or most commonly at the rear — and removed at the summit.

Operation

In the steam era, helper districts were a routine feature of mountain railroading, with locomotives kept at helper stations awaiting assignments; famous examples include the climb over the Alleghenies at Horseshoe Curve and the approaches to Alpine tunnels in Europe. The helper crew had to coordinate carefully with the train's crew using whistle signals, since pushing too hard could cause derailments or break couplings.

Modern practice

Diesel and electric helpers remain in use on heavy-haul freight railways. Radio-controlled distributed power units placed within or at the end of a train have largely supplanted separately crewed helpers, because the lead engineer can control all locomotives simultaneously. Where traffic is predictable, some railroads instead assign permanent additional locomotives for the whole route. In the United Kingdom, banking engines were once common on inclines such as the Lickey Incline near Birmingham, among the steepest sustained main-line gradients in the country.
