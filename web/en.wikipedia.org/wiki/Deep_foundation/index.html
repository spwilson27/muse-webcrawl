Deep foundation

A piling rig installing deep foundations to transfer building loads to stronger ground.

A deep foundation is a type of foundation that transfers the loads of a structure to the earth at considerable depth below the ground surface, as opposed to a shallow foundation, which spreads loads to soil near the surface. Deep foundations are used when surface soils are too weak or compressible to support the structure, when loads are very large — as under skyscrapers, bridges and offshore structures — or where scour or erosion threatens shallow footings.

Types

  • Driven piles are prefabricated elements — timber, steel H-sections or tubes, or precast concrete — hammered or vibrated into the ground, displacing the soil.
  • Bored piles (drilled shafts or caissons) are cast in place in a drilled hole, often with a reinforcing cage, and may be belled at the base to increase bearing area.
  • Micropiles are small-diameter drilled and grouted piles used for underpinning and in restricted access.
  • Caissons and cofferdams support bridge piers in water; large open caissons are sunk by excavating from within.
  • Barrettes are rectangular bored piles excavated with a clamshell grab, common in diaphragm-wall construction.

Load transfer

A pile resists load through a combination of end bearing at its toe and skin friction along its shaft; a foundation may rely on either or both. Piles are usually installed in groups joined by a pile cap, which distributes the structural load among them. Design must consider not only capacity but settlement, group effects, buckling in very soft soils, and negative skin friction where surrounding soil consolidates and drags the pile downward. Load testing — by static loading, dynamic measurements during driving, or bi-directional cell tests — verifies performance.
