Euclid's lemma

In number theory, Euclid's lemma is a fundamental result about divisibility and prime numbers. It states that if a prime number p divides the product ab of two integers a and b, then p must divide at least one of a or b. Equivalently: if p divides ab and is coprime to a, then p divides b.

History and proof

The result appears as Proposition 30 in Book VII of Euclid's Elements. It is a key step in proving the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — the uniqueness of prime factorisation. A standard modern proof uses Bézout's identity: if p does not divide a, then since p is prime, gcd(p, a) = 1, so there exist integers x and y with px + ay = 1. Multiplying through by b gives pbx + aby = b; since p divides both terms on the left, it divides b.

Generalisations

The property that prime elements satisfy Euclid's lemma is taken as part of the definition of a prime element in a general integral domain, and it distinguishes prime elements from irreducible elements. In a unique factorisation domain the two notions coincide. Euclid's lemma also fails in rings that lack unique factorisation, which is precisely what makes such rings interesting counterexamples in algebraic number theory.
