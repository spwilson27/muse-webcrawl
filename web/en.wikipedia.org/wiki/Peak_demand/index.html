Peak demand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A typical daily load curve with morning and evening peaks

FieldElectric power systems
Related conceptsLoad factor, base load, demand response, peaking power plant

Peak demand on an electrical grid is simply the highest electrical power demand that has occurred over a specified time period, or, in everyday usage, the periods when electrical power is expected to be provided for a sustained period at a significantly higher than average supply level. Peak demand fluctuations may occur on daily, monthly, seasonal and yearly cycles.

Description

Electricity cannot be stored economically in large quantities, so supply must meet demand instantaneously. A grid's load curve typically rises in the morning, peaks during hot afternoons when air conditioning runs, and reaches its highest levels in early evening as people return home — the pattern behind the solar-era "duck curve," in which abundant midday photovoltaic generation pushes the net peak into the evening ramp. Because generating and network capacity must be built to serve the peak, a small number of peak hours can drive a large share of system cost: in many systems the top 10 per cent of demand occurs in only 1 per cent of hours.

Managing peak demand

Utilities meet peaks with peaking power plants that can start quickly, such as gas turbines and hydroelectric units, and increasingly with grid-scale batteries and demand response programmes that pay customers to reduce consumption at critical times. Large customers are often billed partly on their maximum ("demand") charge, creating incentives to flatten their load. Time-of-use pricing performs the same role for households. Reducing peak demand defers investment in generation and networks and lowers emissions, since peaking plants are usually the least efficient and most polluting on the system.
