Eureka Flag

DesignWhite cross on a blue field, with a white eight-pointed star at the centre and white stars at the ends of the cross

The Eureka Flag is the battle flag flown at the Eureka Stockade in Ballarat, Victoria, Australia, on 3 December 1854, during an uprising of gold miners against the colonial authorities. The miners, protesting against the cost of mining licences, heavy-handed licence hunts, and their lack of political representation, swore an oath of allegiance beneath the flag two days earlier: "We swear by the Southern Cross to stand truly by each other and fight to defend our rights and liberties."

The Eureka Flag, with its cross and five white stars evoking the Southern Cross.

Design

The flag consists of a blue field bearing a white horizontal-and-vertical cross, with a large white eight-pointed star at the centre of the cross and a white star at each of its four ends. The arrangement evokes the constellation of the Southern Cross, though rendered as a cross rather than in the constellation's natural configuration. According to tradition, the flag was designed by a Canadian miner, "Lieutenant" Ross, and sewn by three women — Anastasia Withers, Anne Duke, and Anastasia Hayes — from bunting and other materials available on the goldfields. The original flag, measuring about 2.6 by 4 metres, was made of woollen bunting.

History

The flag was first raised at Bakery Hill on 29 November 1854 and then flown over the stockade the miners constructed at Eureka. When government troops and police stormed the stockade before dawn on Sunday 3 December, at least 22 miners and several soldiers were killed. During the assault the flag was torn down; trooper John King is said to have climbed the flagpole and seized it. Fragments of the original were kept as souvenirs by participants and their families, and portions were later cut away and distributed.

The surviving original flag is held in Ballarat and displayed at the Eureka Centre. In 2013 it was loaned to, and conserved in association with, the Museum of Australian Democracy at Eureka (M.A.D.E.), which stood on the site until its closure in 2018.

Legacy

The Eureka Flag has become one of Australia's most enduring symbols of protest and democratic defiance. It has been adopted by trade unions, republicans, and various political movements across the spectrum, and it appears on buildings, in marches, and — controversially — in the logos of some organisations. Debates over its meaning and appropriate use continue: for many it stands for the "fair go" and resistance to unjust authority; others note its appropriation by groups far removed from the miners' cause.
