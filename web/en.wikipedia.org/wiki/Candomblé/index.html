Candomblé

Candomblé is an African diasporic religion that developed in Brazil, mainly in the 19th century, among enslaved West and Central African people, especially Yoruba, Fon and Bantu communities. It is centred on veneration of deities known as orixás (Yoruba tradition), voduns or inkices, depending on the "nation" (nação).

Beliefs and practice

Practitioners believe in a supreme creator (Olorum/Olodumare) and in orixás who govern forces of nature and aspects of human life, such as Oxalá, Iemanjá (queen of the sea), Xangô (thunder) and Oxum (fresh waters and love). Worship takes place in temples called terreiros, led by a mãe de santo or pai de santo, and involves drumming, singing in African languages, dance, offerings, and possession trance in which the orixá is believed to mount the devotee.

History

Candomblé survived persecution under slavery and later police repression by syncretising orixás with Catholic saints. It is especially strong in Bahia, whose capital Salvador is a centre of Afro-Brazilian culture.

Influence

Candomblé has deeply influenced Brazilian music, cuisine, festivals and literature, and is recognised as part of Brazil's cultural heritage.
