Tort law

A tort is a civil wrong that causes someone else to suffer loss or harm, resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. Tort law — the area of law dealing with such wrongs — aims principally to provide relief to injured parties in the form of damages, impose liability on those responsible for causing harm, and deter others from committing harmful acts. Unlike contract law, tort duties are imposed by law rather than agreed by the parties; unlike criminal law, tort actions are brought by private parties and (with exceptions such as punitive damages) are not intended to punish.

Tort law provides compensation for harm caused by civil wrongs.

Categories of tort

Intentional torts arise from deliberate conduct: battery, assault, false imprisonment, trespass to land, conversion, defamation, and intentional infliction of emotional distress. Negligence, the largest and most practically important category, requires proof of a duty of care, breach of that duty, causation, and compensable damage; the modern tort was crystallised in Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], where Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" established a general duty to take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable injury to others. Strict liability imposes liability without proof of fault for certain activities — classically, the escape of dangerous things accumulated on land (Rylands v Fletcher), keeping wild animals, and (in modern statute and doctrine) defective products.

Key doctrines

Tort law is structured by doctrines of causation (factual "but-for" cause and legal or proximate cause, limited by foreseeability as in Palsgraf), defences (contributory and comparative negligence, consent, illegality), vicarious liability of employers for employees' torts, joint and several liability, and rules on recoverable loss — pure economic loss and psychiatric injury being specially restricted in English law. Statutes modify the common law in many areas, for example occupiers' liability, employers' liability and consumer protection.

Purposes and criticism

Legal theory identifies several purposes of tort: compensation, corrective justice between the parties, deterrence, and the vindication of rights. Critics argue the system is slow, costly and capricious — litigation as a "lottery" — prompting no-fault compensation schemes (notably New Zealand's Accident Compensation Corporation) and periodic "tort reform" campaigns, especially in the United States, seeking caps on damages and restrictions on class actions. Defenders respond that tort uniquely forces injurers to internalise the costs of their conduct and provides a public forum in which corporate and official wrongdoing is exposed.
