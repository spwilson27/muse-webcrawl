Sybil attack

A Sybil attack is a type of attack on a computer network service in which an attacker subverts the service's reputation or consensus system by creating a large number of pseudonymous identities and using them to gain a disproportionately large influence. It is named after the title character of the 1973 book Sybil, a case study of a woman diagnosed with dissociative identity disorder; the term was proposed by Microsoft researcher Brian Zill and formalised in John R. Douceur's 2002 paper "The Sybil Attack".

Description

Sybil attacks exploit systems that assume each identity corresponds to a distinct participant: peer-to-peer networks, voting and reputation systems, social networks (where mass fake accounts support astroturfing and spam), and anonymity networks such as Tor, where a Sybil adversary may run many relays to attempt deanonymisation. In permissionless blockchain systems, Sybil attacks threaten consensus if an attacker can cheaply create enough apparent nodes or voters to outvote honest participants.

Defences

Because Douceur showed that, absent a trusted authority, purely local validation cannot prevent Sybil identities, defences raise the cost of identity creation or exploit structure in trust relationships:

  • Resource testing / proof of work — require each identity to demonstrate computation, storage or burned currency (proof of burn); Bitcoin's proof-of-work and proof-of-stake systems make influence proportional to a scarce resource rather than to identity count.
  • Identity certification — a central authority, CAPTCHA, phone verification, or "proof of personhood" schemes vet identities.
  • Social-graph defences — algorithms such as SybilGuard and SybilLimit assume the honest region of a social graph is fast-mixing and connected to Sybil regions by few "attack edges", bounding the number of Sybils that can be accepted.
  • Reputation and stake-weighted voting — weight influence by history, stake or fees, so fresh identities have little power.
