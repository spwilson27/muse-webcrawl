Japanese-American life before World War II

Before the Second World War, Japanese-American communities took root along the Pacific Coast and in Hawaii from the 1880s onward. The immigrant generation (Issei), barred by law from naturalised citizenship, built farms, businesses and neighbourhoods — the "Little Tokyos" and "Japantowns" of Los Angeles, San Francisco, Seattle and other cities — while their U.S.-born children (Nisei) grew up as American citizens straddling two cultures. This pre-war world of language schools, Buddhist and Christian churches, prefectural associations (kenjinkai) and Japanese-language newspapers was largely destroyed by the mass incarceration of 1942–1945.

Immigration and settlement

Significant Japanese immigration began in the 1880s–1890s, with labourers recruited to Hawaii's sugar plantations and, increasingly, to the mainland for railroad, mining, cannery and agricultural work. The Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907–1908 restricted new labour migration from Japan but allowed wives and family members to join men already in America, enabling the formation of settled families — often through "picture bride" marriages arranged by photograph and proxy. By 1920 Japanese farmers, though a small fraction of California's population, produced a strikingly large share of its truck crops, leasing and later buying land despite discriminatory laws.

Discrimination and law

Anti-Japanese agitation, driven by labour unions, politicians and newspapers, produced the San Francisco school segregation crisis of 1906, California's Alien Land Laws of 1913 and 1920 (which barred "aliens ineligible to citizenship" from owning agricultural land), and the Immigration Act of 1924, which effectively ended Japanese immigration entirely. Issei responded with test cases, land transfers to their citizen children, and community self-organisation.

Community life

Pre-war communities were dense with institutions: Japanese Associations coordinated business and diplomacy with Japan; kenjinkai linked migrants from the same home prefecture; hundreds of Japanese-language schools taught Nisei children the language and ethics of their parents after regular school hours. Buddhist temples, Christian churches, martial-arts halls, baseball leagues, flower-arranging and koto classes, and festivals such as Obon structured social life. A vigorous Japanese-language press — papers like Los Angeles's Rafu Shimpo (founded 1903) and the English-language Japanese American Courier in Seattle — linked the generations.

The Nisei generation

By 1940 the Nisei, educated in American public schools and citizens by birth, were coming of age, forming the Japanese American Citizens League (founded 1929) and seeking full participation in American life. Their world ended with the attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, and Executive Order 9066 the following February, which led to the forced removal and incarceration of some 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, two-thirds of them U.S. citizens — a rupture from which the pre-war Japantowns never fully recovered.
