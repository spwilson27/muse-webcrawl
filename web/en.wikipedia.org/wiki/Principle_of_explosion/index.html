Principle of explosion

The principle of explosion (Latin: ex falso quodlibet, "from falsehood, anything [follows]"; also ex contradictione quodlibet) is a principle of classical logic according to which, from a contradiction, any proposition whatsoever can be validly inferred. In symbolic terms, for arbitrary propositions P and Q:

(P ∧ ¬P) → Q

A logic system in which this principle holds is called "explosive"; systems that reject it in order to tolerate some contradictions without triviality are paraconsistent logics.

[Image: Proof of explosion]

Proof sketch

In classical logic the principle follows from basic inference rules. From P, disjunction introduction gives P ∨ Q. From ¬P together with P ∨ Q, disjunctive syllogism then yields Q. Since P and ¬P were both assumed (the contradiction), any Q at all may be concluded. Variants of the proof use the law of excluded middle and other classical principles; intuitionistic logic also accepts explosion, since it is derivable from the rule for negation elimination into the empty type.

Significance

Explosion is the reason consistency is so important in classical mathematics and logic: a single contradiction, if derivable, renders a theory trivial — every statement becomes provable and the distinction between true and false collapses. It motivates the study of paraconsistent logic, relevance logic, and inconsistency-tolerant reasoning in computer science and philosophy. The principle was known to medieval logicians such as William of Soissons and was discussed by the schoolmen under the phrase ex impossibili sequitur quidlibet.
