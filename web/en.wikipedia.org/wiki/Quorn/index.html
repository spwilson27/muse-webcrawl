Quorn

Quorn
Product type: Meat substitute
Launched: 1985
Main ingredient: Mycoprotein from Fusarium venenatum
Named after: Quorn, Leicestershire

Quorn is a brand of meat substitute products, primarily sold in the United Kingdom and other markets, whose main ingredient is mycoprotein — a protein derived from the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown by fermentation. Quorn products are marketed to vegetarians and to people reducing their meat consumption, and mostly imitate the texture of meat dishes such as mince, fillets, nuggets, and sausages.

History

Mycoprotein was developed from the 1960s in a search for cheap protein sources, when Rank Hovis McDougall researchers screened thousands of soil organisms; the strain of Fusarium venenatum used (ATCC PTA-2684) was found in a garden in Marlow, Buckinghamshire, in 1967. After a decade of safety testing, the product was approved for sale in 1985, produced in a joint venture with ICI. The brand takes its name from the Leicestershire village of Quorn.

Production and nutrition

The fungus is grown in large air-lift fermenters on glucose, then harvested; its naturally filamentous hyphae give the product a fibrous, meat-like texture. The mycoprotein is mixed with a binder — usually egg white in vegetarian products, or potato protein in vegan lines introduced from 2015 — plus flavourings and colourings. Quorn is high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat, though it is relatively high in sodium in some prepared products.

Controversies

A small proportion of consumers report allergic or gastrointestinal reactions to mycoprotein, which led to labelling campaigns and legal disputes in the United States in the 2000s over whether the product should be described as "mushroom-based". Quorn was also involved in a 2017 ASA ruling in the UK over advertising claims. The brand has changed ownership several times, being acquired by Monde Nissin in 2015.
