Beekeeping

A beekeeper inspecting frames of a modern movable-frame hive.

Beekeeping (or apiculture, from Latin apis, "bee") is the maintenance of bee colonies, commonly in man-made beehives, by humans. A beekeeper (or apiarist) keeps bees in order to collect their honey and other products of the hive — beeswax, propolis, pollen and royal jelly — to pollinate crops, or to produce bees for sale to other beekeepers. A location where bees are kept is called an apiary.

History

Honey hunting is depicted in rock art thousands of years old, and organised beekeeping in clay, straw and wooden hives was practised in ancient Egypt, Greece and Rome. Modern beekeeping dates from the nineteenth century, when Lorenzo Langstroth's movable-frame hive (patented 1852), based on the principle of "bee space", and inventions such as the centrifugal honey extractor and the smoker, made large-scale, non-destructive hive management possible.

Modern practice

Beekeepers manage colonies of the western honey bee (Apis mellifera) and, in Asia, the eastern honey bee (Apis cerana). Seasonal work includes inspecting for disease and swarming, adding honey supers, harvesting honey and preparing colonies for winter. Major threats to managed bees include the parasitic mite Varroa destructor, bacterial foulbrood diseases, pesticides and colony collapse disorder. Commercial beekeepers transport hives over long distances to provide pollination services to agriculture.
