Chifa

Chifa is a Chinese Peruvian culinary tradition based on Cantonese elements fused with traditional Peruvian ingredients and traditions. The term also refers to restaurants serving this cuisine. Chifa developed with the arrival of Chinese immigrants to Peru beginning in 1849, and it has become one of the most popular types of food in the country. The word derives from the Cantonese chi fan (食飯), "to eat rice". Lima's Barrio Chino, founded in the 1920s, is the historic centre of chifa culture.

History

Chinese labourers, known as coolies, first arrived in Peru in 1849 to work in guano extraction, railways and plantations. As they gained freedom and settled in cities, they opened small eateries that adapted Cantonese cooking to local ingredients: Peruvian potatoes, ají peppers and lomo beef met soy sauce (called sillao), ginger (kión) and scallions. By the early 20th century, chifa restaurants were fixtures of Lima life across social classes.

Dishes

Chifa staples include arroz chaufa (fried rice, from Cantonese chow fan), tallarín saltado (stir-fried noodles), sopa wantán (wonton soup), kam lu wantán (fried wontons in sweet-sour sauce) and pollo chi jau kay. Its most famous export to general Peruvian cuisine is lomo saltado, a stir-fry of beef strips, onions, tomatoes and fried potatoes served with rice. Meals in Peruvian chifas are traditionally accompanied by Inca Kola, a sweet yellow soft drink.

Cultural significance

Chifa is central to the identity of the Tusán — Peruvians of Chinese descent — and to Peruvian gastronomy generally, celebrated by chefs such as Gastón Acurio. Related traditions exist in Ecuador and other countries that received Cantonese migration, such as chaulafán in Ecuador.
