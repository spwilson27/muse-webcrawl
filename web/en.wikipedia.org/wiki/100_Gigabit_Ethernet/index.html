100 Gigabit Ethernet

100 Gigabit Ethernet (100 GbE or 100G) is a group of computer networking technologies for transmitting Ethernet frames at a rate of 100 gigabits per second. The standards were developed by the IEEE 802.3 working group, principally in IEEE 802.3ba (approved in 2010), which defined both 40 Gigabit and 100 Gigabit Ethernet, and later amendments such as IEEE 802.3bm and 802.3cd that added lower-cost optical and electrical interfaces.

100 Gigabit Ethernet

Standard: IEEE 802.3ba-2010 and later

Speed: 100 Gbit/s

Common interfaces: 100GBASE-SR4, -LR4, -ER4, -CR4, -DR, -FR1

Form factors: CFP, CFP2, CFP4, QSFP28

Development

The IEEE Higher Speed Study Group began work in 2006 in response to demand from internet service providers and data centre operators for backbone links faster than 10 GbE. Unusually, the resulting project standardised two rates: 40 GbE for servers and 100 GbE for network aggregation and core links. The first generation of equipment used the large CFP transceiver module; successive generations (CFP2, CFP4 and QSFP28) shrank the optics and cut power consumption, making 100G the workhorse of cloud data centres.

Physical layer variants

Standards include 100GBASE-KR4 and -CR4 for backplanes and short twinaxial copper cables, 100GBASE-SR10 and -SR4 over multimode fibre, and 100GBASE-LR4 and -ER4 over single-mode fibre using four wavelength-division multiplexed "lanes" of 25 Gbit/s each, reaching 10 km and 40 km respectively. Later variants such as 100GBASE-DR and 100GBASE-FR1 use a single 100 Gbit/s PAM4 lane. The 100G line signalling evolved from 10×10 Gbit/s lanes to 4×25 Gbit/s and then 2×50/1×100 Gbit/s electrical lanes defined in IEEE 802.3ck.

Use

100 Gigabit Ethernet is deployed in internet exchange points, carrier cores, high-performance computing clusters and hyperscale data centres, where it has largely succeeded 10G and 40G for spine and aggregation links; in the 2020s it began in turn to be supplemented by 200G, 400G and 800G Ethernet.
