Carcinoma

[Image placeholder: Micrograph of a lung carcinoma showing malignant epithelial cells]

A carcinoma is a type of cancer that develops from epithelial cells — the cells that line the inner and outer surfaces of the body. Carcinomas occur when the DNA of epithelial cells is damaged or altered, causing the cells to grow uncontrollably and become malignant. They are by far the most common type of human cancer.

Classification

Carcinomas are classified by the type of epithelial cell from which they arise and by their microscopic appearance. Major categories include:

  • Adenocarcinoma — arising in glandular epithelium (e.g. many breast, lung, prostate and colorectal cancers).
  • Squamous cell carcinoma — arising in squamous epithelium (e.g. skin, lung, cervix).
  • Transitional cell (urothelial) carcinoma — arising in the urinary tract lining.
  • Basal cell carcinoma — the most common skin cancer, arising in the basal layer of the epidermis.
  • Carcinoma in situ — malignant cells confined to the epithelium without invasion of the basement membrane.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by biopsy and histopathological examination, with grading based on how abnormal the cells appear and staging (TNM) based on tumour size, lymph node involvement and distant metastasis. Treatment may include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy depending on type and stage.
