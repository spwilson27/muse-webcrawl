Redenomination

Redenomination is the process by which a currency's nominal value is changed, typically by exchanging old units for new units at a fixed conversion rate, usually a power of ten. Prices, wages and debts are converted at the same rate, so redenomination by itself does not change purchasing power or exchange value.

Reasons

Governments redenominate most often after periods of high inflation or hyperinflation have made denominations unwieldy, with many zeros in everyday prices. Removing zeros simplifies accounting, cash handling and price display, and can signal a commitment to monetary stability. Redenomination may accompany broader monetary reform or a change of currency name.

Examples

Well-known cases include France in 1960 (100 old francs = 1 new franc), Turkey in 2005 (1,000,000 old lira = 1 new lira), Ghana in 2007 (10,000 old cedis = 1 new cedi), and Zimbabwe's repeated redenominations during its hyperinflation of the 2000s. Brazil redenominated several times before introducing the real in 1994.
