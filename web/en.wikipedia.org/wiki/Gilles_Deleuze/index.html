Gilles Deleuze

Gilles Deleuze (French: [ʒil dəløz]; 18 January 1925 – 4 November 1995) was a French philosopher who, together with Félix Guattari, wrote influential works in philosophy, psychoanalysis, literature, film and political theory. His thought — centred on difference, becoming and immanence — has been taken up across the humanities and social sciences.

Life

Born in Paris, Deleuze studied philosophy at the Sorbonne and taught at the Lycée level and at CNRS before joining the experimental University of Paris VIII (Vincennes, later Saint-Denis) in 1969, where he taught until his retirement in 1987. He published monographs on Hume, Nietzsche, Kant, Bergson and Spinoza, and his doctoral works Difference and Repetition (1968) and The Logic of Sense (1969) established his reputation. With Guattari he wrote Capitalism and Schizophrenia — Anti-Oedipus (1972) and A Thousand Plateaus (1980) — and the late What Is Philosophy? (1991). He also wrote two books on cinema, Cinema 1: The Movement-Image (1983) and Cinema 2: The Time-Image (1985). Plagued by respiratory illness, he died by suicide in 1995.

Thought

Deleuze argued that Western philosophy had subordinated difference to identity, representation and recognition; he instead sought a philosophy in which difference is primary and being is univocal — said in one sense of everything that is, following Duns Scotus, Spinoza and Nietzsche. Concepts such as the rhizome (a non-hierarchical, multiply connected structure, contrasted with the arborescent "tree" model), deterritorialisation, assemblage, the body without organs, and schizoanalysis (his and Guattari's critique of psychoanalysis) became widely influential, as did his reading of art and sensation. Michel Foucault famously predicted that "one day the century will be Deleuzian."
