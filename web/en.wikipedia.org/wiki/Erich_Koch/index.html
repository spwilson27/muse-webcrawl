Erich Koch

Erich Koch (19 June 1896 – 12 November 1986) was a Nazi Party official, Gauleiter of East Prussia and Reichskommissar of Ukraine, notorious for the brutality of his rule.

Erich Koch (19 June 1896 – 12 November 1986) was a German Nazi Party politician. He was Gauleiter of East Prussia from 1928 to 1945 and, during the Second World War, Reichskommissar of the Reichskommissariat Ukraine, where his regime of ruthless exploitation and mass murder made him one of the most feared of Nazi administrators. He was also, unusually, a senior figure in the Protestant church, serving as praeses of the East Prussian provincial synod.

Early life and rise

Koch was born in Elberfeld (now part of Wuppertal), the son of a foreman. He worked as a railway clerk, served as a soldier in the First World War, and afterwards fought in the Freikorps in Upper Silesia. He joined the Nazi Party in 1922 and worked in the Ruhr district organisation under Albert Krebs and later in the orbit of Gregor Strasser. In 1928 he was appointed Gauleiter of East Prussia, where he built up the party organisation, and he entered the Reichstag in 1930. From 1933 he was also Oberpräsident (high president) of East Prussia, uniting party and state authority in his hands.

Ukraine

After the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, Koch was appointed Reichskommissar for Ukraine, ruling from Rivne. Against the relatively pragmatic policies favoured by Alfred Rosenberg's ministry, Koch treated the Ukrainian population purely as a resource to be starved and enslaved: grain quotas were seized regardless of local famine, millions of Ukrainians were deported to Germany as forced labourers (Ostarbeiter), and Koch lent full support to the mass murder of Ukraine's Jews. He famously declared that if he found a Ukrainian worthy of sitting at his table, he would have him shot. His disputes with Rosenberg over occupation policy were notorious within the regime.

Postwar trial and death

Koch fled East Prussia in 1945, was captured by the British in Hamburg in 1949 while living under a false identity, and was extradited to Poland in 1950. After years of delays he was tried in Warsaw in 1958–59 and sentenced to death for the murder of Polish citizens, but the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment — reportedly because of his failing health and unresolved questions about his knowledge of the fate of the looted Amber Room. He died in prison at Barczewo in 1986, aged 90, the last major Nazi war criminal still in Polish custody.
