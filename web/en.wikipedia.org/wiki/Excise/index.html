Excise

An excise, or excise tax (sometimes called an excise duty), is an indirect tax charged on the sale of, or imposed during the manufacture of, particular goods and services. Unlike a general sales tax, which applies broadly to retail transactions, an excise targets specific products — classically tobacco, alcohol and motor fuel — and is usually collected from the producer or importer rather than from the final consumer, though its cost is normally passed on in the price.

Characteristics

Excises may be specific (a fixed amount per unit — per litre of pure alcohol, per pack of cigarettes, per litre of fuel) or ad valorem (a percentage of price). Because they are levied upstream and embedded in the price, they are less visible to consumers than sales taxes itemised at the till, which historically made them an efficient and politically convenient revenue source.

Rationale

Governments justify excises on several grounds:

  • Revenue: demand for taxed goods is often price-inelastic, giving a stable yield.
  • Correcting externalities: "sin taxes" on tobacco and alcohol, and fuel duties, aim to make prices reflect social costs such as disease, accidents and pollution (a Pigouvian rationale).
  • Sumptuary policy: discouraging consumption of harmful products for public-health reasons.
  • Benefit taxation: fuel excises are often earmarked for road construction and maintenance, charging road users for the infrastructure they use.

History

The excise was developed in the Netherlands and introduced to England during the Civil War in 1643, modelled on Dutch practice, initially on beer, cider, spirits and other goods. Administered by the Board of Excise, it became a mainstay of British public finance and provoked lasting political controversy — Samuel Johnson's dictionary famously defined excise as "a hateful tax levied upon commodities". Attempts to extend it, such as Walpole's Excise Bill of 1733, triggered major political crises. In the young United States, a federal excise on distilled spirits provoked the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794. Britain's HM Customs and Excise administered such taxes until 2005, when it merged into HM Revenue and Customs.

Modern excises

Today most countries levy excises on tobacco products, alcoholic drinks, motor fuels and energy products; many also tax vehicles, air travel, gambling, sugary drinks, carbon emissions and luxury goods. Within the European Union, minimum excise rates on alcohol, tobacco and energy are harmonised by directive. Economists debate the regressivity of excises (they take a larger share of income from the poor) against their health and environmental benefits.
