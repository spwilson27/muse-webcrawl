Chaim Weizmann

Chaim Azriel Weizmann (27 November 1874 – 9 November 1952) was a Russian-born biochemist, Zionist leader and Israeli statesman who served as president of the Zionist Organization and later as the first President of Israel from 1949 until his death. He was instrumental in obtaining the Balfour Declaration of 1917, which committed the British government to support a Jewish national home in Palestine.

Weizmann, chemist and statesman, first President of Israel.

Early life and chemistry

Weizmann was born in the village of Motal near Pinsk in the Russian Empire and studied chemistry in Germany and Switzerland, receiving his doctorate from the University of Fribourg in 1899. In 1904 he moved to England, joining the University of Manchester. There he developed the acetone–butanol–ethanol (ABE) fermentation process using the bacterium Clostridium acetobutylicum — later dubbed the "Weizmann organism". During the First World War the process became strategically vital for producing acetone, a solvent needed to manufacture cordite, and Weizmann became director of the British Admiralty laboratories.

Zionist leadership

His scientific service gave him access to British leaders, and he pressed the case for Zionism with Foreign Secretary Arthur Balfour and Prime Minister David Lloyd George. The result was the Balfour Declaration of November 1917. In 1919 Weizmann met Emir Faisal and signed the Faisal–Weizmann Agreement on Arab–Jewish cooperation. He served as president of the World Zionist Organization from 1920 to 1931 and again from 1935 to 1946, though his moderate, pro-British diplomacy was increasingly challenged by younger, more militant activists. He founded the Hebrew University of Jerusalem's supporting institutions and the Daniel Sieff Research Institute at Rehovot in 1934, which after his death became the Weizmann Institute of Science.

Presidency

When the State of Israel was proclaimed in May 1948, Weizmann was elected chairman of the Provisional State Council and, in February 1949, the first President of Israel. His role was largely ceremonial, and he chafed at its limits, but his international prestige lent legitimacy to the new state. He died in office in 1952 and is buried at his home in Rehovot, on the grounds of the institute that bears his name.
