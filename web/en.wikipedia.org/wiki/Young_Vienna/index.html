Young Vienna

German: Jung-Wien
Active: c. 1890–1900s
Key figures: Arthur Schnitzler, Hugo von Hofmannsthal, Hermann Bahr, Richard Beer-Hofmann, Felix Salten
Setting: Viennese coffeehouses, especially Café Griensteidl

Young Vienna (German: Jung-Wien) was a circle of Viennese writers of the 1890s who spearheaded literary modernism in Austria. Gathering in coffeehouses — above all the Café Griensteidl, nicknamed the "Café Megalomania" — the group rejected the naturalism of Berlin letters in favour of impressionism, aestheticism, and psychological refinement, producing the characteristic literature of fin-de-siècle Vienna.

Members and programme

The critic Hermann Bahr acted as the group's theorist and impresario, proclaiming the "overcoming of naturalism" and importing successive European fashions — symbolism, decadence, impressionism. The circle's most enduring figures were Arthur Schnitzler, whose plays and prose anatomised the erotic and social life of the Viennese bourgeoisie, and the prodigy Hugo von Hofmannsthal, who published luminous lyric poems as a teenager under the pseudonym Loris. Other members included Richard Beer-Hofmann, Peter Altenberg, Leopold Andrian, and Felix Salten, later famous for Bambi.

Style and themes

Young Vienna writing favoured mood over plot: the monologue, the one-act play, the lyrical sketch, and the novella of consciousness. Themes included the fragility of the self, nervous sensitivity, the aesthetics of decay, and the death-haunted elegance of a declining empire — qualities that made Vienna around 1900 a laboratory of modernism alongside the Secession in art and Freud's psychoanalysis.

Decline

The demolition of the Café Griensteidl in 1897, lampooned by Karl Kraus in his pamphlet Die demolierte Literatur, symbolised the group's dispersal. Its members went on to individually distinguished careers — Hofmannsthal to his librettos for Richard Strauss, Schnitzler to international fame — while "Young Vienna" survived as the name for a defining moment in Austrian literature.
