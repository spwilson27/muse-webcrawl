The AK-74 (Russian: Автомат Калашникова образца 1974 года, "Kalashnikov automatic rifle model 1974") is an assault rifle designed by Mikhail Kalashnikov in the early 1970s and adopted by the Soviet Union as the successor to the AKM. Its defining change was a new small-calibre, high-velocity cartridge, the 5.45×39mm, developed in response to the American 5.56×45mm round used in the M16.

Development

Soviet experience in Vietnam-era reporting and ballistics research convinced planners that a smaller, lighter cartridge offered advantages: soldiers could carry more ammunition, recoil was lower, and hit probability in automatic fire improved. The 5.45×39mm round was designed with a long, slender bullet with a steel core and a small air cavity in the nose that shifts the centre of gravity rearward, promoting early yaw in tissue. The AK-74 adapted the proven AKM action to the new round, adding a distinctive large multi-chamber muzzle brake that significantly reduces recoil and muzzle rise, at the cost of increased blast to the sides.

Design details

The AK-74 is gas-operated with a long-stroke piston and rotating bolt, sharing the AK family's reputation for functioning in mud, sand and extreme cold. Early production rifles had wooden furniture; later models used plum- and black-coloured polymer. The side-folding AKS-74 served airborne and vehicle crews, while the shortened AKS-74U carbine became widely recognised. In 1991 Russia adopted the AK-74M, with a folding solid polymer stock and a side rail for optics, as its standard rifle; the AK-12 was adopted in 2018 as its successor, though AK-74 variants remain in enormous numbers worldwide.

Service

The AK-74 saw its first combat in the Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989), where the 5.45 mm round was nicknamed the "poison bullet" by Afghan fighters because of its wounding characteristics. It has since been used in the Chechen wars, the Russo-Georgian War, the Syrian civil war and the Russo-Ukrainian War, as well as by dozens of states and non-state forces; licensed and unlicensed copies were produced in Bulgaria, East Germany, Poland, Romania and elsewhere.
