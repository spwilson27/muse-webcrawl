Blazon

A blazon is the formal verbal description of a coat of arms

In heraldry and heraldic vexillology, a blazon is a formal description of a coat of arms, flag, or similar emblem, from which the reader can reconstruct the appropriate image. The word derives from the French blason, "shield." Blazon uses a specialized vocabulary, grammar, and word order, traditionally in a hybrid of French and English, that allows a design to be specified unambiguously in words alone. Two arms with the same blazon are considered identical, however differently artists may render them, because heraldic identity lies in the description, not in any single depiction.

Principles

A blazon begins with the field (background) of the shield, then describes the ordinaries (geometric bands and shapes), then the charges (objects and creatures), working from the background outward and from the chief (top) to the base. Colours are named by heraldic tinctures: the metals or (gold/yellow) and argent (silver/white); the colours gules (red), azure (blue), sable (black), vert (green), and purpure (purple); and furs such as ermine and vair. Sides are given from the bearer's point of view, so the shield's right side is termed dexter (the viewer's left) and its left sinister.

Examples

The arms of England are blazoned: "Gules, three lions passant guardant in pale or armed and langued azure" — on a red field, three gold lions walking with faces toward the viewer, arranged vertically, with blue claws and tongues. The flag of the city of Amsterdam, by contrast, might be blazoned in plainer language: three black saltires (St Andrew's crosses) on a red band across a black field.

Grammar and conventions

Blazon avoids repeating a tincture name where possible, referring to a repeated colour as "of the field," "of the first," and so on. Attitudes of animals are specified precisely: a lion rampant rears up; passant walks; statant stands. The system developed in the 12th and 13th centuries so that heralds could record arms at tournaments from purely verbal notes. Modern heraldic authorities in Britain, Canada, South Africa, and elsewhere still grant arms by blazon, and vexillologists write blazons for flags as well.
