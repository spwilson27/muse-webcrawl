International Gothic

International Gothic is a period of Gothic art that developed in late medieval Europe from about 1375 to about 1425. It is characterised by an elegant, courtly style shared across the courts of Europe — in France, Burgundy, Bohemia, Austria, Italy and elsewhere — in painting, sculpture, manuscript illumination and the decorative arts. In France the style is sometimes called the style courtois, and in older literature it is also known as "International Style" or "Soft Style" (in German, Weicher Stil, especially for sculpture).

Characteristics

International Gothic works feature graceful, elongated figures in courtly dress, arranged in flowing S-curves; rich surface pattern, especially gold backgrounds tooled with decorative motifs; exquisitely detailed observation of plants, animals and fabrics; and a generally decorative, aristocratic tone even in religious subjects. Saints and the Virgin appear as refined courtiers, and biblical scenes are often set amid the pageantry of contemporary court life.

Centres and artists

The style flourished at courts linked by dynastic marriage and the movement of artists: the Valois courts of Paris and Burgundy, the imperial court of Charles IV and Wenceslaus in Prague, the Visconti court in Milan, and the courts of Naples, Aragon and England (where the Wilton Diptych is the supreme surviving example). Leading artists include the Limbourg brothers, illuminators of the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry; Melchior Broederlam; Jacquemart de Hesdin; the Master of the Parement de Narbonne; in Italy, Gentile da Fabriano and Pisanello; and in sculpture, Claus Sluter at the Burgundian court. The Wilton Diptych, made for Richard II of England around 1395–1399, exemplifies the style's jewel-like refinement.

Decline

From around 1425 the International Gothic manner gave way in the Netherlands to the more robust naturalism of Early Netherlandish painting (Robert Campin, Jan van Eyck) and in Italy to the Early Renaissance. Elements of the courtly style persisted in some regions, notably in Spain and parts of Germany, into the mid-15th century.
