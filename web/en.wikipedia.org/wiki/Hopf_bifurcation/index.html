Hopf bifurcation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Birth of a limit cycle in a supercritical Hopf bifurcation

In the mathematical theory of bifurcations, a Hopf bifurcation is a local bifurcation in which a fixed point of a dynamical system loses stability as a pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues of the linearised system cross the imaginary axis, and a limit cycle branches from the equilibrium. It is named after the mathematician Eberhard Hopf, who proved an early version of the bifurcation theorem in 1942; related results were obtained by Aleksandr Andronov, and the bifurcation is sometimes called the Andronov–Hopf bifurcation.

Overview

Consider a smooth system of ordinary differential equations depending on a parameter. As the parameter passes a critical value, the equilibrium changes stability and a small-amplitude periodic orbit appears around it. If the emerging limit cycle is stable and the equilibrium becomes unstable, the bifurcation is supercritical; if the cycle is unstable and coexists with a stable equilibrium, the bifurcation is subcritical, and the system may jump to a large-amplitude attractor. The distinction is determined by the sign of the first Lyapunov coefficient, computed from the nonlinear terms of the system at the critical point.

Applications

Hopf bifurcations provide the standard mathematical model for the onset of oscillation in many fields: the firing of neurons, the Belousov–Zhabotinsky chemical reaction, aeroelastic flutter, predator–prey dynamics, laser thresholds, and the transition of business-cycle models from steady growth to endogenous cycles. The normal form of the bifurcation, after reduction to a centre manifold and near-identity change of coordinates, is a planar system in polar coordinates in which the radial equation governs the amplitude of the oscillation.
