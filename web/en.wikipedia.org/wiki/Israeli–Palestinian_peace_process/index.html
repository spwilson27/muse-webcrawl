The Israeli–Palestinian peace process refers to the intermittent negotiations, agreements and initiatives since the early 1990s aimed at resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict through a two-state settlement. It began in earnest with the Madrid Conference of 1991 and produced the Oslo Accords, under which Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) mutually recognised each other and created the Palestinian Authority as an interim self-governing body.

Oslo era

Secret talks in Oslo, Norway, produced the 1993 Declaration of Principles, signed on the White House lawn by Yitzhak Rabin and Yasser Arafat with US President Bill Clinton presiding. The 1995 Interim Agreement (Oslo II) divided the West Bank into Areas A, B and C and set a timetable for final-status negotiations. The process was badly damaged by violence — the 1994 Cave of the Patriarchs massacre, Hamas suicide bombings — and by the assassination of Rabin in November 1995 by an Israeli extremist opposed to the accords.

Camp David and aftermath

In July 2000, Clinton convened Rabin's successor Ehud Barak and Arafat at Camp David to settle final-status issues: borders, Jerusalem, refugees and security. The summit ended without agreement, each side blaming the other. The failure was followed within months by the Second Intifada (2000–2005). Further frameworks — the Clinton Parameters, the Taba talks, the 2002 Arab Peace Initiative, the Quartet's Road Map (2003), the Annapolis Conference (2007) and the Kerry negotiations (2013–14) — failed to produce a final settlement. The expansion of Israeli settlements, Palestinian political division between Fatah and Hamas after 2007, and repeated wars in Gaza are widely cited as obstacles; no sustained direct final-status talks have taken place since 2014.

Core issues

  • Borders — whether a Palestinian state would follow the 1967 lines with agreed land swaps.
  • Jerusalem — claimed as capital by both sides.
  • Refugees — the Palestinian claim of a right of return for 1948 refugees and descendants.
  • Security — Israeli demands for demilitarisation and border controls.
  • Settlements — Israeli communities in the West Bank and East Jerusalem, considered illegal under international law by most states.
