Climate change feedback

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Schematic of major climate feedback loops in the Earth system

In climate science, a climate change feedback (or climate feedback) is a process in the Earth system that can either amplify or diminish the effects of an initial change in climate forcing. A positive feedback amplifies warming (or cooling), while a negative feedback dampens it. Feedbacks are a major reason why climate sensitivity — the eventual warming from a doubling of CO2 — exceeds the warming expected from the greenhouse effect alone.

Major feedbacks

FeedbackSignMechanism
Ice–albedoPositiveMelting ice exposes darker ocean/land, absorbing more sunlight
Water vapourPositiveWarmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas
Lapse rateNegativeUpper-atmosphere warming pattern increases outgoing radiation
CloudUncertain (net positive)Shifts in cloud cover and altitude alter reflection and trapping
Permafrost carbonPositiveThawing permafrost releases CO2 and methane
Planck responseNegativeWarmer bodies radiate more energy to space

Quantification

Feedbacks are often expressed as a feedback factor or gain. The water vapour and ice–albedo feedbacks are the best understood and are strongly positive; cloud feedback remains the largest source of uncertainty in model projections, though recent research points to a net positive value.

Tipping points

Strong positive feedbacks can push parts of the climate system past tipping points — thresholds beyond which change becomes self-reinforcing, such as the collapse of the West Antarctic ice sheet or dieback of the Amazon rainforest.
