General epistles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The general epistles, also called the catholic epistles, are seven books of the New Testament in the form of letters. They are so called because, unlike the Pauline epistles, they are addressed not to a particular church or individual but to a more general audience — the word "catholic" here carries its older sense of "universal". In the canonical order of most Bibles they appear after the Pauline letters and before the Book of Revelation.

The Epistle of James is traditionally placed first among them and attributed to James the Just, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church. Second and Third John are the two shortest books of the Bible, each consisting of a single chapter written in the style of a private letter, though they are grouped with the general epistles by tradition.

Authorship and reception

The authorship of several of the general epistles was disputed in the early church. The church historian Eusebius of Caesarea placed James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, and Jude among the antilegomena, the "spoken-against" books whose canonicity was questioned, even though they were widely read. Second Peter in particular is regarded by most modern critical scholars as pseudepigraphal, written in Peter's name by a later author, partly because of its stylistic differences from 1 Peter and its apparent dependence on the Epistle of Jude.

The Epistle to the Hebrews is sometimes grouped with the general epistles in manuscripts because it is not addressed to a specific church, but it is more usually treated separately or counted among the Pauline corpus, to which tradition attached it.

Themes

Despite their diverse origins, the general epistles share a practical, pastoral character. James emphasises the necessity of faith expressed through works and warns against partiality toward the rich; 1 Peter comforts persecuted Christians with the hope of resurrection; the Johannine letters stress love, truth, and warnings against false teachers, introducing the term "antichrist"; Jude and 2 Peter both warn against corrupt teachers and recall divine judgment.
