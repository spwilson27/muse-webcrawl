Persistence of vision

Persistence of vision is the optical phenomenon in which an afterimage persists for a brief fraction of a second on the retina after the stimulus has been removed, so that a rapid sequence of still images is perceived as continuous. It was traditionally invoked to explain the perception of motion in film and animation, though modern vision science attributes apparent motion mainly to phenomena such as beta movement and the phi phenomenon, and flicker fusion to the temporal limits of visual processing.

History

The effect was described in various forms from antiquity; Lucretius noted the lingering of images, and in 1824 Peter Mark Roget presented his observations on the appearance of wheel spokes seen through apertures to the Royal Society. In the 1830s the principle was popularised by philosophical toys: the thaumatrope, Joseph Plateau's phenakistoscope, and William George Horner's zoetrope, all of which create the illusion of motion or fused images from rapidly presented pictures.

Applications

Persistence of vision underlies many display technologies. POV displays sweep a row of LEDs through space to paint an image in mid-air; scanning displays in oscilloscopes and radar rely on phosphor persistence and visual afterimages; and the term is used in computer graphics for the lingering image on certain display types. The related concept of flicker fusion threshold — the frequency at which an intermittent light appears steady, typically around 50–90 Hz depending on brightness — determines frame and refresh rates in cinema and electronic displays.

Criticism of the traditional account

Film scholars and vision scientists (notably Joseph and Barbara Anderson) have argued that "persistence of vision" as commonly taught is a myth as an explanation of motion perception: positive afterimages do not fill the gaps between frames, and motion is instead constructed by the visual system's apparent-motion mechanisms. The term survives in popular usage and in the names of display techniques.
