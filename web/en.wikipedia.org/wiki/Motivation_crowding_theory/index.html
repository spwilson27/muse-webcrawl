Motivation crowding theory

Motivation crowding theory
FieldBehavioural economics, psychology
Key proponentsBruno S. Frey, Reto Jegen, Edward Deci, Richard Ryan
Related conceptsIntrinsic motivation, overjustification effect, self-determination theory

Motivation crowding theory is a theory in behavioural economics and psychology holding that offering external incentives — such as monetary rewards or penalties — for a behaviour can undermine a person's intrinsic motivation to perform that behaviour, sometimes reducing overall performance. The phenomenon is known as the crowding-out effect. Conversely, external interventions can under some conditions strengthen intrinsic motivation, a process called "crowding-in".

Origins

The idea grew out of psychological experiments in the early 1970s. Edward Deci found that paying participants to solve enjoyable puzzles reduced their subsequent unpaid engagement with them, and Mark Lepper and colleagues demonstrated a similar "overjustification effect" in children rewarded for drawing. In economics, Richard Titmuss had argued in The Gift Relationship (1970) that paying blood donors could displace voluntary donation. The economist Bruno S. Frey developed these strands into a general theory, notably in his 1997 book Not Just for the Money and a 2001 survey with Reto Jegen.

Mechanism

According to the theory, external intervention crowds out intrinsic motivation when it is perceived as controlling: it impairs self-determination and self-esteem, and shifts the perceived locus of control so that the person feels the behaviour is performed for the reward rather than out of interest or duty. Interventions perceived as supportive or acknowledging — verbal praise, for example — tend instead to crowd motivation in.

Evidence and applications

A well-known field study by Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini found that introducing a small fine for parents who collected children late from daycare increased late pickups, and the increase persisted after the fine was removed — the fine had reframed a social obligation as a priced service. The theory has been applied to tax compliance, environmental policy (payments for conservation can erode civic virtue), siting of unwanted facilities (compensation reduced acceptance of a nuclear waste repository in a Swiss study), education and management pay-for-performance schemes.

Critics note that crowding effects depend on context and task type: for dull or routine tasks with little intrinsic motivation to begin with, incentives generally work as standard economics predicts.
