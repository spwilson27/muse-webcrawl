Frank Bunker Gilbreth

Frank Bunker Gilbreth (July 7, 1868 – June 14, 1924) was an American engineer, consultant, and author known as an early advocate of scientific management and a pioneer of time and motion study. Together with his wife, Lillian Moller Gilbreth, he developed methods for improving industrial efficiency by reducing unnecessary motions.

Early life and bricklaying

Gilbreth was born in Fairfield, Maine. Rather than attend college, he began work as a bricklayer's apprentice at age 17 and rose quickly through the construction trades. Observing that bricklayers used inconsistent and wasteful motions, he developed improved methods — including an adjustable scaffold and techniques that cut the motions required to lay a brick from 18 to about 5 — dramatically increasing productivity.

Motion study

Gilbreth founded his own contracting and consulting firms and generalized his bricklaying work into "motion study." He broke manual work into fundamental elements he called therbligs ("Gilbreth" nearly spelled backwards), such as search, find, grasp, transport loaded, and position. Using motion-picture cameras and chronocyclegraphs — small lights attached to workers' hands photographed over time — he analyzed work in fine detail to find "the one best way" to perform a task with minimum fatigue.

Collaboration with Lillian Gilbreth

Frank married Lillian Moller in 1904; she became his full professional partner, contributing psychological insight into worker satisfaction and fatigue. Their work complemented Frederick Winslow Taylor's time study, though the two men later clashed over credit and methods. After Frank's sudden death in 1924, Lillian carried on their consulting practice for decades.

Family and legacy

The Gilbreths had twelve children, whose upbringing — run partly as an experiment in household efficiency — was chronicled humorously in the memoir Cheaper by the Dozen (1948) by Frank Jr. and Ernestine Gilbreth Carey, later adapted into films. Gilbreth's writings include Motion Study (1911), Primer of Scientific Management (1912), and Applied Motion Study (1917).
