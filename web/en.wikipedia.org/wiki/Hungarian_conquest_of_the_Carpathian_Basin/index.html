The Hungarian conquest of the Carpathian Basin (Hungarian: honfoglalás, "taking of the homeland") was the series of events in the late ninth and early tenth centuries by which the Magyars (Hungarians), a semi-nomadic people from the Eurasian steppe, settled in the Carpathian Basin. Tradition dates the main occupation to 895–896, under the leadership of Árpád, founder of the dynasty that would rule Hungary for four centuries.

The arrival of the Magyars as depicted in the Feszty Panorama.

Background

The Magyars had migrated westward across the steppe for centuries, living successively in regions known as Magna Hungaria, Levédia, and Etelköz, under Khazar influence and then in alliance with various powers. In the 890s, pressed by the Pechenegs and involved in wars between the Bulgars and Byzantines, the seven Hungarian tribes — according to tradition bound by a blood oath — moved through the passes of the Carpathians into the basin, which was then divided among East Francia, Moravia, and Bulgarian and local Slavic and Avar-descended populations.

The conquest and its aftermath

Over the following decade Magyar forces defeated Moravian and Frankish opponents, notably at the Battle of Pressburg in 907, securing their hold on the basin. From this base, Hungarian mounted archers raided widely across Europe — into Germany, France, Italy, and the Balkans — until their defeat by Otto I at the Battle of Lechfeld in 955 ended the western raids. The tribal federation was consolidated into the Principality of Hungary, and under Géza and Stephen I the kingdom adopted Christianity and joined the political order of medieval Europe.

Sources and scholarship

The main narrative sources are the Gesta Hungarorum of the anonymous notary of King Béla, Constantine VII's De administrando imperio, and various Frankish annals. Archaeology of tenth-century cemeteries and grave goods has been used to trace settlement patterns, and modern genetic studies examine the relationship between the conquering Magyars and the basin's earlier inhabitants.
