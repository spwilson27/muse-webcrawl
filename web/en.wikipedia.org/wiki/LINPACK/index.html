LINPACK

LINPACK is a software library for performing numerical linear algebra on digital computers. Written in Fortran in the 1970s by Jack Dongarra, Jim Bunch, Cleve Moler and Gilbert Stewart, it solves systems of linear equations, least-squares problems, and eigenvalue-adjacent tasks using matrix factorizations such as LU decomposition with partial pivoting, QR and Cholesky factorization. LINPACK was designed for the machines of its era and does comparatively little blocking for cache hierarchies, which is why its successor, LAPACK, restructured the algorithms around the level-3 BLAS.

The LINPACK benchmark

LINPACK is best known today through the LINPACK benchmark, introduced by Dongarra in 1979: a report of the speed, in floating-point operations per second, at which a computer solves a dense system of linear equations of size n = 100, later n = 1000, and eventually for arbitrary problem sizes. Because the benchmark measures sustained performance on a real, widely understood computation, it became a standard yardstick for comparing supercomputers and is the basis of the TOP500 list of the world's fastest machines (using the highly parallel HPL variant), ranked by their maximum achieved performance, Rmax.

Criticism and successors

Critics note that dense Gaussian elimination is not representative of many real workloads, which are limited by memory bandwidth, sparsity or irregular communication; the HPCG benchmark was introduced as a complement for the TOP500. Nevertheless, LINPACK scores remain the headline metric of supercomputing performance, and machines such as Frontier exceeded an exaflop per second on it.
