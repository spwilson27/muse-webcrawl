Atlantis

Atlantis (Ancient Greek: Ἀτλαντὶς νῆσος, Atlantìs nêsos, 'island of Atlas') is a fictional island mentioned in Plato's works Timaeus and Critias as part of an allegory on the hubris of nations.

First mentionedPlato, c. 360 BC
GenreAllegory / legend
LocationBeyond the Pillars of Hercules (disputed)

Plato's account

In Plato's dialogues, Atlantis is a naval power lying in front of the Pillars of Hercules that conquered many parts of Western Europe and Africa 9,000 years before the time of Solon. After a failed attempt to invade Athens, Atlantis sank into the ocean in a single day and night of misfortune.

Interpretations

Scholars generally regard Atlantis as a literary invention by Plato to illustrate his political theories. Others have proposed real inspirations, including the Minoan eruption of Thera (Santorini), the Sea Peoples, or other Bronze Age disasters. See Caldera.

In popular culture

From the Renaissance onward, and especially after Ignatius Donnelly's 1882 book Atlantis: The Antediluvian World, Atlantis became a staple of pseudohistory, occultism, fiction, film and comics, often depicted as an advanced lost civilisation.
