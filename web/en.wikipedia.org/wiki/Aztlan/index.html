Aztlan (Nahuatl: Aztlān, "Place of Whiteness" or "Place of the Herons") is the ancestral home of the Aztec peoples in Mesoamerican mythology. Aztec oral and pictorial traditions describe the Mexica migrating from Aztlan, an island in a lake, southward to the Valley of Mexico, where they founded Tenochtitlan after a journey said to have lasted generations or centuries. The word gives the Aztec ("people from Aztlan") their name.

The migration legend

According to sources such as the Codex Boturini and the Codex Aubin, the Mexica left Aztlan under the guidance of their patron god Huitzilopochtli, whose priests carried his image on the journey. The travellers were told they would know their destination when they saw an eagle perched on a prickly-pear cactus growing from a stone in a lake — the sign fulfilled at Tenochtitlan, founded traditionally in 1325. Accounts differ on the migration's duration and itinerary, with stops at places such as Chicomoztoc ("Seven Caves") and Coatepec.

Proposed locations

Because no archaeological site has been conclusively identified as Aztlan, scholars and enthusiasts have proposed many locations, most in west-central or northwestern Mexico — including the island of Mexcaltitán in Nayarit — and some as far afield as the southwestern United States. Most historians treat Aztlan primarily as a mythological construct with symbolic and political significance for Mexica identity rather than a recoverable historical site.

Chicano movement

In the 1960s and 1970s, "Aztlán" was adopted by the Chicano movement in the United States as the name for the lands of the southwestern US annexed from Mexico after the Mexican–American War (1848). El Plan Espiritual de Aztlán (1969) proclaimed the region the spiritual homeland of Chicanos, and the name came to symbolise Mexican-American cultural nationalism and self-determination.
