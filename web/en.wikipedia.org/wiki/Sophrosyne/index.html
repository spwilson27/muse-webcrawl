Sophrosyne

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sophrosyne (Greek: σωφροσύνη) is an ancient Greek concept of an ideal of excellence of character and soundness of mind, which when combined in one well-balanced individual leads to other qualities such as temperance, moderation, prudence, purity, and self-control. It is often translated as "temperance" or "self-restraint," though its meaning is broader: a harmonious, rational mastery of one's desires and emotions. Its opposite is hubris.

In Greek literature and philosophy

The concept appears early in Greek poetry and in the Delphic maxims "Know thyself" and "Nothing in excess," with which it is closely associated. Plato devoted the dialogue Charmides to defining sophrosyne, where it is variously proposed as modesty, doing one's own business, self-knowledge and a kind of knowledge of knowledge itself; the dialogue ends in aporia. In the Republic, sophrosyne is the virtue of the producing class and of the city as a whole—an agreement among all classes about who should rule—while in Aristotle's ethics the related virtue of temperance (sophrosyne) is the mean concerning bodily pleasures between self-indulgence and insensibility.

Later usage

Sophrosyne became one of the four cardinal virtues in later Greek and Roman thought and was absorbed into Christian moral teaching as temperance. It is personified as a goddess in some later Greek sources, a daughter of Erebus and Nyx in certain lists of abstractions. The term continues to be used in philosophical and classical scholarship to denote an excellence of self-knowledge and measured conduct not fully captured by any single English word.
