"The Use of Knowledge in Society" is a scholarly article by Austrian-British economist Friedrich Hayek, published in the September 1945 issue of the American Economic Review. Written in response to the socialist calculation debate sparked by Ludwig von Mises and debated with Oskar R. Lange and Abba Lerner, it is regarded as one of the most influential economics papers of the twentieth century.

Argument

Hayek argues that the central economic problem of society is not the allocation of "given" resources but the utilization of knowledge that is not given to anyone in its totality. The knowledge relevant to economic decisions is dispersed among countless individuals as local, contextual, often tacit knowledge of "the particular circumstances of time and place". No central planning authority can gather and process all of it. The price system, Hayek contends, solves this problem economically: prices act as signals that condense information and coordinate the separate actions of different people, as in his example of a rise in the price of tin prompting economizing behaviour by users who need know nothing of why tin has grown scarcer.

Legacy

The essay is a foundational statement of the case against comprehensive central planning and a landmark in information economics. It is frequently cited alongside Hayek's related essay "Economics and Knowledge" (1937) and his book The Road to Serfdom (1944).
