Fingerprint recognition is an automated method of verifying or identifying a person based on the comparison of friction ridge patterns of their fingers. It is one of the most mature and widely deployed biometric technologies, used in law enforcement, border control, national identity systems and consumer devices such as smartphones.

History

Friction ridge patterns were used for identification in ancient China and were scientifically studied by Francis Galton, whose 1892 book Finger Prints established their permanence and near-uniqueness. Classification systems developed by Juan Vucetich in Argentina and Edward Henry in British India enabled large manual fingerprint files. Computerised matching arrived with Automated Fingerprint Identification Systems (AFIS) from the 1970s–80s onward; the FBI's IAFIS database holds hundreds of millions of records.

How it works

Sensors capture the print optically, capacitively or ultrasonically. Algorithms extract minutiae — points where ridges end or split — along with the overall pattern class (arch, loop or whorl). A stored template of minutiae geometry is compared with a probe print, producing a match score against a threshold. Verification is one-to-one; identification searches one print against many. Performance is measured by false match and false non-match rates, and latent prints from crime scenes often require expert examination because of distortion and partial coverage.

Concerns

Fingerprint systems can be spoofed with artificial fingers, templates cannot be revoked like passwords, and large-scale databases raise privacy questions. Scientific critiques have also challenged absolute claims of individualisation for partial latent prints.
