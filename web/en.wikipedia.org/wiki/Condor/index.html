Condor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Condor is the common name for two species of New World vultures, each in its own monotypic genus: the Andean condor (Vultur gryphus) of the Andes mountains of South America, and the California condor (Gymnogyps californianus) of the mountains of western North America. They are among the largest flying birds in the world, with the Andean condor having the largest wing area of any bird and a wingspan reaching about 3.3 metres (10 ft 10 in).

Description and behaviour

Condors are scavengers that feed on carrion, favouring large carcasses such as those of deer, cattle, and other big animals. Their plumage is mainly black, with white patches; the California condor has striking orange-yellow bare skin on the head of adults, while male Andean condors have a fleshy comb and wattles. Like other New World vultures, they have excellent eyesight and a strong sense of their soaring flight, riding thermals for hours with minimal flapping. Condors are slow breeders: a pair typically raises a single chick every other year, and young birds take six to eight years to reach maturity, though individuals may live 60 years or more.

Conservation

The California condor became extinct in the wild in 1987, when the last wild individuals were captured for a captive-breeding programme; it has since been reintroduced to California, Arizona, Utah, and Baja California, and remains critically endangered, threatened especially by lead poisoning from ammunition in carrion. The Andean condor is listed as Vulnerable, declining from persecution and secondary poisoning but still widespread along the Andes, where it is a national symbol of several countries and figures prominently in Andean mythology and folklore.
