The Gnetophyta are a division of gymnosperm plants comprising three highly distinctive genera: Ephedra (desert shrubs), Gnetum (mostly tropical lianas and trees), and Welwitschia (a single species of the Namib desert with just two ever-growing leaves). Together they number around 100 species.

Characteristics

Gnetophytes are unusual among gymnosperms for possessing vessel elements in their xylem — a feature otherwise characteristic of flowering plants — and for reproductive structures that superficially resemble flowers. Gnetum has broad angiosperm-like leaves, and some species show a form of double fertilisation. These similarities once led botanists (the "anthophyte hypothesis") to place gnetophytes as the closest relatives of angiosperms; molecular phylogenies instead nest them firmly within the gymnosperms, usually as sister to the pines (the gnepine hypothesis).

Genera

  • Ephedra — jointed, photosynthetic stems; source of the alkaloid ephedrine (ma huang).
  • Gnetum — tropical vines and trees of Africa, Asia and South America; some seeds are eaten.
  • Welwitschia mirabilis — Namib and Angola deserts; individuals may live over 1,000 years with only two leaves.

Evolution

The group has a fossil record reaching back to the Permian and diversified during the Cretaceous, when gnetophyte pollen was widespread. Today the three genera are relicts of this once broader distribution.
