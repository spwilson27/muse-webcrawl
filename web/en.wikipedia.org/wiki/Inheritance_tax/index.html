Inheritance tax

An inheritance tax is a tax paid by a person who inherits money or property of a person who has died, whereas an estate tax is a levy on the estate (money and property) of a person who has died. In international tax law, there is a distinction between an estate tax and an inheritance tax: an estate tax is assessed on the deceased person's estate before distribution, while an inheritance tax is assessed on each beneficiary's share after distribution.

History

Taxes on inheritance date back to antiquity. In ancient Rome, a tax called the vicesima hereditatium was levied at a rate of 5 percent on inheritances received by Roman citizens, with close relatives exempt. Modern inheritance taxes emerged in Europe in the nineteenth century and spread widely; the Netherlands introduced an inheritance tax in 1805, and the United Kingdom introduced "death duties" in the form of probate duty, legacy duty and succession duty.

Application by country

The practice of taxing inheritances varies considerably between countries. Many European countries, including France, Germany, and Japan, levy inheritance taxes with rates and exemptions depending on the relationship between the deceased and the heir, with spouses and close family generally taxed more lightly or exempted. In Japan the top marginal rate reaches 55 percent, among the highest in the world.

The United States has no federal inheritance tax, though the federal government levies an estate tax, and several states — including Iowa (being phased out), Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania — impose their own inheritance taxes on beneficiaries. The United Kingdom levies inheritance tax at 40 percent on estates above a threshold (£325,000 for many years), with an additional residence nil-rate band for homes left to direct descendants.

Arguments

Supporters argue that inheritance taxes reduce wealth inequality, promote meritocracy, and raise revenue with relatively low economic distortion compared with taxes on income or consumption. Critics contend that they tax savings that have already been taxed, create liquidity problems for heirs of illiquid assets such as farms and family businesses, and are easily avoided by the wealthy through estate planning. Empirical studies suggest inheritance taxes have modest effects on overall inequality.
