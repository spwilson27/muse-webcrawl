Reichstag fire
Date27 February 1933
LocationReichstag building, Berlin, Germany
PerpetratorMarinus van der Lubbe (convicted); wider responsibility disputed
ConsequencesReichstag Fire Decree; mass arrests; acceleration of Nazi consolidation of power

The Reichstag fire (German: Reichstagsbrand) was an arson attack on the Reichstag building, home of the German parliament in Berlin, on Monday, 27 February 1933, precisely four weeks after Adolf Hitler was sworn in as Chancellor of Germany. Marinus van der Lubbe, a Dutch council communist, was arrested at the scene and eventually executed for the crime. The Nazi government immediately blamed the fire on a Communist conspiracy to seize power, and persuaded President Paul von Hindenburg to sign the Reichstag Fire Decree the next day, suspending most civil liberties in Germany. The decree, never repealed, became a cornerstone of the Nazi dictatorship and the Gleichschaltung that followed.

The fire and arrests

The fire was reported at about 9:14 pm and spread rapidly through the debating chamber, whose wooden panelling and furnishings burned fiercely; the great glass dome shattered in the heat. Inside, police found van der Lubbe, a 24-year-old unemployed bricklayer and former Dutch Communist who had recently arrived in Berlin hoping to provoke a working-class uprising. Fire experts testified he could have set the blaze alone with firelighters. That night and in the following days, some 4,000 Communists and other opponents were arrested under emergency powers; the Communist Party (KPD) was effectively suppressed days before the federal election of 5 March 1933, in which the Nazis still failed to win an outright majority.

Trial

Van der Lubbe was tried at Leipzig alongside three Bulgarian Communists — Georgi Dimitrov, Blagoy Popov and Vasil Tanev — and the German Communist Ernst Torgler. The trial embarrassed the regime: Dimitrov, defending himself, cross-examined Hermann Göring to great effect, and all defendants except van der Lubbe were acquitted. Van der Lubbe was beheaded on 10 January 1934. In 2008, Germany posthumously pardoned him under a law annulling unjust Nazi-era verdicts. The Reichstag building itself, its dome destroyed, was not restored during the Nazi era; the parliament never again met there.

Who set the fire?

Whether van der Lubbe acted alone has been disputed ever since. The Communists' Brown Book blamed the Nazis themselves, alleging SA men entered through a tunnel from Göring's residence; Nazi involvement would have provided a perfect pretext, and the speed of the government's reaction struck many contemporaries as suspicious. Most modern historians — following Fritz Tobias's research in the 1960s, though challenged by scholars such as Benjamin Carter Hett — conclude that van der Lubbe most likely acted alone, while agreeing that the Nazis exploited the fire with premeditated speed to destroy the Weimar constitution's protections. Debate continues, and the fire remains the classic case study of a crisis used to justify emergency rule.
