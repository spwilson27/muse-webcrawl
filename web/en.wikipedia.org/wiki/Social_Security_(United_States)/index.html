Social Security (United States)

Social Security is the commonly used term for the Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI) program of the United States federal government, administered by the Social Security Administration (SSA). Created by the Social Security Act of 1935 as part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, the program provides retirement income, disability benefits, and survivors' benefits to eligible workers and their families, funded primarily through dedicated payroll taxes.

History

The Social Security Act was signed into law on 14 August 1935. It grew out of the work of the Committee on Economic Security, chaired by Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins, and responded to mass poverty among the elderly during the Great Depression, as well as popular movements such as Dr. Francis Townsend's old-age pension plan. The original act provided old-age benefits for retired workers in commerce and industry, along with federal support for state unemployment insurance and aid programs.

Payroll taxes began to be collected in 1937, and the first monthly retirement benefit was paid in January 1940 to Ida May Fuller of Vermont, who received the famous first check, numbered 00-000-001, for $22.54. Amendments in 1939 added benefits for spouses and survivors and shifted the program toward a family-based, pay-as-you-go system. Disability insurance was added in 1956, and cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) were made automatic in 1972. Medicare, enacted in 1965, is administered separately but is closely associated with the Social Security system.

How it works

Workers and employers each pay a payroll tax — under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) — of 6.2% of wages up to an annually adjusted taxable maximum, for a combined 12.4%; self-employed workers pay both halves. A separate 1.45% Medicare tax applies to all wages. Contributions are credited to workers' earnings records; eligibility and benefit amounts depend on years of covered work and lifetime earnings.

Retirement benefits can be claimed as early as age 62 at a permanently reduced rate, with the full retirement age gradually rising from 65 for earlier cohorts to 67 for those born in 1960 or later. Benefits are computed from the worker's highest 35 years of indexed earnings, using a progressive formula that replaces a higher share of income for low earners. The program is largely pay-as-you-go: current taxes pay current benefits, with surpluses held in the Social Security trust funds invested in special U.S. Treasury securities.

Programs

Retirement and survivors insurance

Retired workers, their spouses, divorced spouses in some cases, and surviving family members may receive monthly benefits. Survivors benefits can support widows and widowers, minor children, and dependent parents.

Disability insurance (SSDI)

Workers who become unable to engage in substantial gainful activity because of a qualifying medical condition, and who have sufficient work credits, may receive disability benefits; family members may also qualify.

Supplemental Security Income (SSI)

SSI, created in 1972 and administered by the SSA, is a separate means-tested program funded from general revenues that provides payments to aged, blind, and disabled people with limited income and resources.

Finances and reform debates

The annual Trustees Report projects the program's long-term finances. Because of population aging and lower birth rates, the combined trust funds have been projected to be depleted at some point in the 2030s, after which continuing payroll taxes would cover roughly three-quarters to four-fifths of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts. Proposals for reform include raising the payroll tax rate or cap, adjusting the retirement age, modifying the benefit formula, or means-testing benefits. Social Security remains one of the largest federal programs and a central element of retirement security in the United States, credited with dramatically reducing poverty among the elderly.
