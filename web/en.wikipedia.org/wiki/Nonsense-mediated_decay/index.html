Nonsense-mediated decay
Other namesNonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD)
FunctionmRNA surveillance pathway

Overview

Nonsense-mediated decay (NMD), also called nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, is a cellular surveillance pathway found in eukaryotes that detects and degrades messenger RNA transcripts containing premature termination codons (nonsense mutations). By destroying these faulty transcripts, NMD prevents the production of truncated proteins that could be harmful to the cell.

Mechanism

In mammals, NMD is closely connected to pre-mRNA splicing. During splicing, protein complexes called exon junction complexes are deposited near exon–exon boundaries. If a ribosome encounters a stop codon well upstream of one of these complexes during the first round of translation, factors including UPF1, UPF2, and UPF3 recognise the arrangement as abnormal and trigger degradation of the mRNA.

In addition to policing mutant transcripts, NMD regulates the abundance of many normal cellular mRNAs and thereby influences gene expression more broadly.

Medical relevance

Mutations that introduce premature stop codons underlie many genetic diseases, including forms of cystic fibrosis, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, and beta-thalassemia. Because NMD efficiency varies between individuals and tissues, it can modify the severity of such diseases, and it is a target of therapeutic strategies aimed at read-through of premature stop codons.
