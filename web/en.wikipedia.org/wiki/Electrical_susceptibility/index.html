Electrical susceptibility

In electricity (electromagnetism), the electric susceptibility (χe; Latin: susceptibilis "receptive") is a dimensionless proportionality constant that indicates the degree of polarization of a dielectric material in response to an applied electric field. The greater the electric susceptibility, the greater the ability of a material to polarize in response to the field, and thereby reduce the applied electric field inside the material.

Definition

The electric susceptibility χe is defined by the relation P = ε0χeE, where P is the polarization density (the electric dipole moment per unit volume), E is the electric field, and ε0 is the vacuum permittivity. The relative permittivity (dielectric constant) of the material is then εr = 1 + χe.

Properties

In vacuum, χe = 0. In linear dielectrics it is a constant, but in anisotropic materials it becomes a tensor, and at high field strengths or near resonances it depends on the field and frequency. Frequency-dependent susceptibility, described by models such as the Lorentz oscillator and Debye relaxation, governs dispersion and absorption in dielectric spectroscopy.
