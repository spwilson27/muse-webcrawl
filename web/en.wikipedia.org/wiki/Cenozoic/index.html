The Cenozoic (from Greek kainós, "new", and zōḗ, "life") is Earth's current geological era, beginning 66 million years ago with the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event that ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs, and continuing to the present day. It is often called the "Age of Mammals", because terrestrial faunas came to be dominated by mammals after the extinction.

Subdivisions

The Cenozoic is divided into three periods: the Paleogene (66–23 million years ago), the Neogene (23–2.6 million years ago), and the Quaternary (2.6 million years ago to the present). These are subdivided into epochs: Paleocene, Eocene, and Oligocene in the Paleogene; Miocene and Pliocene in the Neogene; and Pleistocene and Holocene in the Quaternary.

Climate and geography

The early Cenozoic was warm — the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum around 56 million years ago saw near-tropical conditions at high latitudes — followed by long-term cooling as Antarctica glaciated (~34 million years ago) and, much later, Northern Hemisphere ice sheets grew, producing the Quaternary ice ages. Plate tectonics carried the continents close to their modern positions: India collided with Asia, raising the Himalayas; Australia separated from Antarctica; and the Americas were joined by the Isthmus of Panama about 3 million years ago.

Life

Mammals and birds radiated into niches vacated by the dinosaurs. Grasslands spread in the later Cenozoic, accompanied by grazing mammals with high-crowned teeth. In the Quaternary, megafauna such as mammoths and giant ground sloths flourished and then largely vanished, and the genus Homo evolved in Africa, giving rise to modern humans.
