Nose

The nose is the protruding part of the face of vertebrates that bears the nostrils and houses the organ of olfaction (smell); in humans and many other animals it also forms the entrance to the respiratory tract. The human nose consists of bone and cartilage covered by skin, with an internal nasal cavity divided by the nasal septum.

Anatomy

The external nose includes the root and bridge, the dorsum, the apex (tip), and the alae (wings) flanking the nostrils (nares). Inside, the nasal cavity is lined with mucous membrane and folded bony shelves called conchae or turbinates, which warm, humidify, and filter inhaled air. Hair-like cilia and mucus trap dust and pathogens. The paranasal sinuses—frontal, maxillary, sphenoid, and ethmoid—open into the cavity.

Functions

Beyond breathing, the nose is the principal organ of smell: olfactory receptors in the upper cavity detect odorant molecules and send signals to the olfactory bulbs of the brain. The nose also resonates the voice and contributes to the sense of taste through aroma. In other animals the nose may be elaborated into a trunk (elephants), a snout for rooting (pigs), or a highly sensitive olfactory organ (dogs, whose sense of smell is vastly more acute than humans').

Clinical aspects

Common conditions include rhinitis (inflammation), sinusitis, nasal polyps, a deviated septum, and nosebleeds (epistaxis). The nose is the most frequently fractured facial bone, and reconstructive surgery (rhinoplasty) is among the most common cosmetic operations.
