Transcranial magnetic stimulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive form of brain stimulation in which a changing magnetic field, generated by a coil held against the scalp, induces an electric current in a localised region of the cerebral cortex. Single pulses can momentarily activate or disrupt cortical activity, while repetitive trains of pulses (rTMS) can produce longer-lasting increases or decreases in cortical excitability. TMS is used both as a research tool in neuroscience and as an approved medical treatment, notably for major depressive disorder that has not responded to medication.

Principles

A brief, strong current passed through the stimulation coil produces a magnetic field pulse of about 1–2 tesla that passes through the skull essentially unimpeded and induces a current in the underlying cortex sufficient to depolarise neurons. When applied over the motor cortex, a pulse evokes a measurable muscle response (a motor evoked potential) in a corresponding muscle; over the visual cortex it can produce flashes of light (phosphenes); and over language or cognitive areas it can transiently interfere with performance, allowing researchers to test the causal role of a region in a task — a "virtual lesion" method complementing correlational imaging.

Medical uses

rTMS applied to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2008 for treatment-resistant depression, delivered in daily sessions over several weeks. It has since received approvals or guideline support for obsessive-compulsive disorder (deep TMS targeting prefrontal–cingulate circuits), migraine with aura (single-pulse devices), and smoking cessation in some jurisdictions. Evidence is being gathered for chronic pain, stroke rehabilitation, tinnitus and other conditions, with mixed results.

Safety

TMS is generally well tolerated. Common side effects are scalp discomfort, headache and light-headedness. The most serious risk is induction of a seizure, which is rare and minimised by screening and by following published safety guidelines on stimulation intensity and frequency. The technique must be used cautiously or not at all in people with metallic or electronic implants in or near the head, such as cochlear implants or aneurysm clips.

History

Modern TMS began with the stimulator built by Anthony Barker and colleagues at the University of Sheffield in 1985, the first device able to stimulate the human motor cortex reliably and relatively painlessly. Its descendants — especially figure-of-eight coils, which focus the field — made TMS a standard tool of cognitive neuroscience and, later, of clinical psychiatry and neurology.
