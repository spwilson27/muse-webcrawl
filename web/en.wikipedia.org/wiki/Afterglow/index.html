Afterglow

An afterglow is a broad arch of whitish or pinkish sunlight in the sky that is scattered by fine particulates, such as dust, suspended in the atmosphere. It occurs after sunset (and in reverse before sunrise) when the Sun is just below the horizon, and forms the first stage of twilight's coloured display; a particularly intense, purple-hued phase is known as the purple light.

Formation

As the Sun sinks, direct sunlight ceases to illuminate the lower atmosphere, but dust and aerosols high in the troposphere and stratosphere continue to catch reddened sunlight and scatter it toward the observer, producing the soft arch opposite and around the Sun's azimuth. The phenomenon is intensified after major volcanic eruptions, when stratospheric aerosols spread globally — producing spectacular afterglows worldwide, as after Krakatoa (1883) and Pinatubo (1991).

Related phenomena

The afterglow is accompanied by the dark blue-grey segment of Earth's own shadow rising in the east, bordered by the pink antitwilight arch (the Belt of Venus). "Afterglow" is also used technically for the fading emission following gamma-ray bursts and for phosphorescent emission after excitation ceases.
