Robinson Crusoe is a novel by Daniel Defoe, first published on 25 April 1719. Written as the autobiography of the title character — a castaway who spends 28 years on a remote tropical island near the coast of South America, encountering cannibals, captives and mutineers before being rescued — it is often credited as one of the first novels in English and the origin of the castaway or "Robinsonade" genre.

Plot summary

Robinson Crusoe, born in York in 1632, defies his father's wish that he study law and goes to sea. After adventures including enslavement in Morocco and success as a plantation owner in Brazil, he joins a slave-trading voyage and is shipwrecked — the sole survivor — on an uninhabited island near the Orinoco. Salvaging tools, guns and supplies from the wreck, Crusoe builds a fortified dwelling, grows barley and rice, domesticates goats, makes pottery and furniture, and keeps a journal and calendar, gradually converting despair into industry and religious reflection.

Years later Crusoe discovers a footprint in the sand and learns that cannibals visit the island. He rescues one of their intended victims, whom he names Friday, and teaches him English and Christianity; Friday becomes his loyal companion. Together they later rescue Friday's father and a Spanish castaway. When an English ship arrives whose crew has mutinied, Crusoe helps the captain retake the vessel and is carried home after 28 years on the island. He returns to find his Brazilian plantation has made him wealthy, marries, and — in the sequel — revisits his island colony.

Inspiration

Defoe is thought to have been inspired by the real-life ordeal of Alexander Selkirk, a Scottish sailor marooned for over four years on an island in the Juan Fernández archipelago, whose story had been published by Woodes Rogers in 1712. Crusoe's island is, however, placed near the Caribbean rather than the Pacific.

Themes and reception

The novel's plain, factual style and detailed account of Crusoe's industry made it an immediate success; four editions appeared within months, and it has never been out of print. It has been read as a spiritual autobiography, a celebration of individualism and colonial enterprise, and a fable of economic rationality — Karl Marx and later economists treated Crusoe's island economy as a model case. It spawned countless imitations, including Johann David Wyss's The Swiss Family Robinson (1812).
