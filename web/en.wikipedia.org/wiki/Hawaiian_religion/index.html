Hawaiian religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hawaiian religion is the indigenous religious tradition of Native Hawaiians. It is polytheistic and animistic, centred on four principal gods — Kū, Kāne, Lono and Kanaloa — and a multitude of lesser deities (akua), guardian spirits (ʻaumākua) and deified ancestors. Hawaiian religion formed the spiritual framework of the Hawaiian chiefdoms and kingdom until its state structures were dismantled beginning in 1819, though many beliefs and practices persist and have been revived in modern times.

Beliefs and deities

The four great male gods (ke akua) are Kāne, creator and god of sunlight and fresh water; Kū, god of war and governance; Lono, god of agriculture, fertility and peace, honoured in the annual Makahiki festival; and Kanaloa, god of the ocean and the underworld. Well-known deities also include Pele, the volcano goddess whose home is Kīlauea; her sister Hiʻiaka; the trickster demigod Māui; and the moon goddess Hina. Family guardian spirits, ʻaumākua, often took animal forms such as sharks, owls or lizards.

Kapu system and ritual

Society was ordered by the kapu system — sacred prohibitions regulating eating, contact between men and women, and the sanctity of chiefs (aliʻi), whose bodies embodied divine mana (spiritual power). Temples called heiau served ritual functions ranging from agriculture (heiau hoʻouluʻai) to war; the largest war temples (luakini) involved human sacrifice. Priests (kāhuna) mediated ritual, medicine, and crafts. The New Year Makahiki season, dedicated to Lono, suspended warfare for feasting, sport and taxation.

Abolition and revival

In 1819, months after the death of Kamehameha I, his son Kamehameha II, with the regent Kaʻahumanu and queen mother Keōpūolani, publicly broke the eating kapu (the ʻAi Noa), abolishing the state religion a year before American Protestant missionaries arrived. Images were burned and heiau abandoned, though a battle at Kuamoʻo saw defenders of the old order defeated. Christianity and later other faiths spread, but Hawaiian practices survived in hula, chant (mele), healing and oral tradition, and a Hawaiian cultural renaissance since the 1970s has revived ceremonies at heiau and devotion to Pele and other akua.
