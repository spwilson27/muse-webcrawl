Orientation (vector space)

The orientation of a real vector space is a notion that formalizes "handedness" — the distinction between an ordered basis and its mirror image. An orientation of a finite-dimensional real vector space V is an equivalence class of ordered bases, where two bases are equivalent if the change-of-basis matrix between them has positive determinant. Every real vector space has exactly two orientations (with the trivial exception of the zero-dimensional space, which has one).

The right-hand and left-hand rules define the two orientations of R³.

Definition and examples

A linear map preserves orientation if its determinant is positive and reverses it if negative; reflections are the prototypical orientation-reversing maps, while rotations preserve orientation. In the plane, an orientation picks out which of clockwise or counterclockwise rotation is "positive." In three dimensions, the standard orientation of R³ is the one in which the standard basis (e1, e2, e3) points along the thumb, index, and middle fingers of the right hand.

Relation to exterior algebra

Equivalently, an orientation can be specified by choosing a nonzero element of the top exterior power of the dual space — a volume form up to positive scalar multiple. The sign of a determinant, of the scalar triple product, and of cross products all depend on the chosen orientation. On manifolds, a smooth choice of orientation for every tangent space is exactly the orientability of the manifold; vector spaces themselves are always orientable, and the subtleties arise only when stitching local orientations together globally.

Conventions

Chemistry and physics rely on orientation in defining chirality, the sign of optical rotation, and conventions for angular momentum and electromagnetic quantities. Computer graphics uses orientation (winding order of vertices) to decide which faces of a polygon point outward.
