Klepht

Era15th–19th centuries
RegionOttoman Greece (Balkans)
ActivityGuerrilla warfare, brigandage

Klephts (Greek: κλέφτης, kléftis, plural κλέφτες, kléftes, meaning "thief") were highwaymen turned self-appointed armatoloi, anti-Ottoman insurgents, and warlike mountain-dwellers in the Greek countryside during Ottoman rule. They were the descendants of Byzantine Greeks who retreated to the mountains in the 15th century to avoid Ottoman oppression.

Origins

The klephts were originally Greeks who fled to the mountains following the Ottoman conquest, evading taxes, debts and Ottoman reprisals. By the 17th century they had become a significant military force in the Greek highlands, raiding Ottoman convoys and occasionally serving as mercenaries. Klephtic bands were led by a kapetanios (captain) and organised under codes of honour celebrated in folk songs.

Role in the War of Independence

Klephts and their sometime adversaries, the armatoloi (Christian militia guards), formed the experienced fighting core of the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829). Many celebrated heroes of the revolution, such as Theodoros Kolokotronis and Odysseas Androutsos, began as klephts or armatoloi. After independence, klephtic traditions passed into Greek national mythology and literature, and brigandage persisted in the mountains for decades afterward.

Culture

Klephtic ballads (κλέφτικα τραγούδια) are a major genre of Greek folk song, praising the klephts' deeds, love of freedom and defiance of authority. Their distinctive dress, including the fustanella skirt and embroidered waistcoat, influenced the uniform of the Evzones.
