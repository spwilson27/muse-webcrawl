Stabat Mater (Pergolesi)

The Stabat Mater is a musical setting of the Stabat Mater sequence, composed by Giovanni Battista Pergolesi in 1736 — the last year of his short life. Scored for soprano and alto soloists, strings and basso continuo, it is the composer's most celebrated work and one of the most popular sacred compositions of the Baroque era.

Background

Pergolesi was commissioned by a Neapolitan confraternity, the Cavalieri di San Luigi di Palazzo, to write a new Stabat Mater to replace the by-then old-fashioned setting by Alessandro Scarlatti — scored, like Pergolesi's, for the same modest forces — for performance on Good Friday in the church of Santa Maria dei Sette Dolori. Pergolesi completed the work at the Franciscan monastery in Pozzuoli, where he had retreated in failing health; he died of tuberculosis in March 1736, aged twenty-six, shortly after finishing it. The poignant circumstances — a dying young composer's swansong on a text of maternal grief — helped to build its posthumous legend.

Music

The work is divided into twelve short movements alternating duets and solo arias. The opening duet is renowned for its chains of expressive suspensions over a walking bass in F minor; later movements include the soprano aria "Vidit suum dulcem natum", the duet "Fac ut ardeat cor meum", the vigorous alto aria "Fac ut portem Christi mortem" answered by "Inflammatus et accensus", and a brief final "Amen" fugue. Despite the sorrowful subject, the style is the graceful, lyrical galant manner of Pergolesi's operas, which some contemporaries found too cheerful for the text.

Reception

The Stabat Mater was the most printed piece of music of the 18th century. It was admired across Europe: Jean-Jacques Rousseau praised the opening duet as "the most perfect and touching duet to come from the pen of any musician", and J. S. Bach adapted the work as the cantata Tilge, Höchster, meine Sünden, BWV 1083, substituting a German paraphrase of Psalm 51. Arrangements by Hiller, Paisiello and many others, and performances for ever larger forces, kept it in the repertory into the Romantic era; modern historically informed performance has returned it to its original intimate scale.
