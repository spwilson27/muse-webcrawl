Moral dilemma

A moral dilemma is a situation in which an agent has moral reasons to perform each of two or more actions, can perform each of them, but cannot perform all of them — so that whichever the agent chooses, she will fail to satisfy some moral requirement. Moral dilemmas are central thought experiments in ethics and raise the question of whether an agent can be blamelessly trapped by morality itself.

Classic cases

The best-known example is the trolley problem, devised by Philippa Foot in 1967 and expanded by Judith Jarvis Thomson: a runaway tram will kill five workers unless the agent diverts it onto a track where it will kill one. Variants — pushing one person off a footbridge to stop the tram — elicit sharply different intuitions. Other classic cases include Jean-Paul Sartre's student torn between caring for his mother and joining the Resistance, and "Sophie's choice," in which a mother must choose which of her children will die.

Philosophical debate

Some philosophers argue that genuine dilemmas show moral theory must admit conflicting, non-overridable duties (prima facie duties in the sense of W. D. Ross); others argue that apparent dilemmas always resolve under a correct ordering of principles, whether Kantian, utilitarian, or otherwise. Psychologists use dilemmas — notably in Lawrence Kohlberg's studies of moral development — to probe how people reason about right and wrong.
