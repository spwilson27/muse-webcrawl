Operaismo

Key figures: Raniero Panzieri, Mario Tronti, Antonio Negri, Paolo Virno

Origin: Italy, early 1960s

Related: Autonomism, post-Marxism, workers' inquiry

Operaismo ("workerism") is a Marxist theoretical and political current that emerged in Italy in the early 1960s. It is associated with journals such as Quaderni Rossi (founded by Raniero Panzieri) and Classe Operaia, and with thinkers including Mario Tronti, Antonio Negri, Rita di Leo and Romano Alquati.

Core ideas

Operaismo inverted orthodox Marxist analysis by arguing that working-class struggle, rather than capital's own laws, is the driving force of capitalist development: capital reorganises production reactively, in response to workers' resistance. Tronti's Workers and Capital (1966) argued that the working class must be studied first and capital second.

A second distinctive method was the workers' inquiry (conricerca), pioneered by Alquati's studies of FIAT and Olivetti factories, in which researchers and workers jointly investigated the labour process and the "class composition" of the workforce.

Development

During the strikes of Italy's "Hot Autumn" of 1969, operaisti ideas influenced groups such as Potere Operaio. In the 1970s the current broadened into autonomism, shifting attention beyond the factory to the "social factory", unpaid domestic labour and the refusal of work. Negri's later work with Michael Hardt (Empire, 2000) carried operaist themes — the multitude, immaterial labour — to a global readership.

Criticism

Critics have charged operaismo with romanticising spontaneous struggle, underestimating institutions and mediation, and providing intellectual cover for the armed fringes of the 1970s Italian movement — accusations its leading figures rejected.
