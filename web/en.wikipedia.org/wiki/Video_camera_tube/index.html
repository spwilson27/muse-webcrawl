Video camera tube

Video camera tubes were devices based on the cathode-ray tube that were used from the 1930s until the 1980s to capture television images before being replaced by solid-state image sensors such as CCDs. A camera tube converts an optical image into an electrical signal by scanning a photosensitive target with an electron beam.

Types

Early development produced the iconoscope, patented by Vladimir Zworykin, and Farnsworth's image dissector. The image orthicon, introduced in the 1940s, offered greatly improved sensitivity and became standard for studio cameras. Later designs included the vidicon, which used a photoconductive target and became ubiquitous in industrial, medical, and space applications, and the Plumbicon (a lead-oxide vidicon developed by Philips) and Saticon, widely used in broadcast color cameras for their low lag and good color rendition.

Operation

Light from the scene is focused onto a target inside an evacuated glass envelope. An electron gun at the rear scans the target in a raster pattern, and variations in the target's stored charge or conductivity modulate the returned beam or output current, producing a video signal synchronized to the scanning.

Decline

Camera tubes were bulky, fragile, power-hungry, and prone to image burn-in and comet-tail artifacts on bright highlights. CCD sensors, commercialized in the 1980s, offered better performance in a smaller package and displaced tubes in nearly all applications.
