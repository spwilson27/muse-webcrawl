Tur Abdin

Tur Abdin (Syriac: ܛܘܼܪ ܥܒ݂ܕܝܼܢ, Ṭur ʿAḇdin, "Mountain of the Servants [of God]") is a hilly limestone region in southeastern Turkey, in the upper Tigris basin near the borders with Syria and Iraq, centred on the town of Midyat. It is the historic heartland of the Syriac Orthodox Church and one of the oldest continuously inhabited Christian regions in the world, home to dozens of ancient churches and monasteries.

History

Christianity reached the region in the earliest centuries of the faith; by late antiquity Tur Abdin was dense with monastic communities founded, according to tradition, by disciples of the apostles and early ascetics. The monastery of Mor Gabriel, founded in 397, remains one of the oldest functioning monasteries in the world and the seat of the Metropolitan of Tur Abdin. The region's Syriac (Aramaic-speaking) population endured repeated invasions and massacres over the centuries, most devastatingly the Sayfo — the Assyrian genocide of 1915 during the First World War — in which much of the local Christian population was killed, though Tur Abdin's villagers famously mounted armed resistance at villages such as Azakh (İdil).

Recent times

During the late 20th century, conflict between the Turkish state and Kurdish insurgents, together with economic hardship, drove most of Tur Abdin's remaining Syriac Christians to emigrate to Europe and elsewhere, leaving only a few thousand in the region by the 2000s — down from tens of thousands a century earlier. Since the 2000s some emigrants have returned to restore villages and monasteries, though disputes over confiscated monastery lands, notably involving Mor Gabriel, have drawn international attention.
