Centrifugation

[Image: A laboratory centrifuge with sample tubes in its rotor]

Centrifugation is a mechanical process which involves the use of centrifugal force to separate particles from a solution according to their size, shape, density, medium viscosity and rotor speed. A device that performs this, called a centrifuge, spins samples at high speed so that denser components migrate outward, away from the axis of rotation, while less dense components are displaced toward the centre.

Principle

When a suspension is rotated rapidly, particles experience an effective force many times gravity, expressed as relative centrifugal force (RCF, in units of ×g). Under this force, particles sediment at rates described by their sedimentation coefficient, measured in svedbergs (S). Separation can be by differential pelleting — successive spins at increasing speeds to collect fractions of decreasing size — or by density-gradient centrifugation, in which samples settle into bands within a gradient medium such as sucrose or caesium chloride.

Applications

Centrifugation is fundamental in laboratories: separating blood cells from plasma, isolating organelles and macromolecules in cell biology, and purifying viruses and DNA (as in the Meselson–Stahl experiment). Industrially, centrifuges clarify liquids, separate cream from milk, dewater sludge, concentrate uranium hexafluoride gas for isotope enrichment, and are used in sugar refining, oil processing and astronaut training (human centrifuges).

History

The principle was applied in cream separators in the 1860s–1870s, notably by Gustaf de Laval. Theodor Svedberg developed the analytical ultracentrifuge in the 1920s, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1926; modern ultracentrifuges reach speeds above 100,000 rpm and forces approaching 1,000,000 ×g.
