Residency (medicine)

Residency or postgraduate training is a stage of graduate medical education. It refers to a qualified physician (one who holds the degree of MD, DO, DPM, MBBS, or MBChB) who practises medicine, usually in a hospital or clinic, under the direct or indirect supervision of a senior medical clinician registered in that specialty, an "attending physician". In other countries the equivalent posts may be called "registrar" or "specialty trainee". The term residency is named for the historical practice of resident physicians living in the hospital where they trained.

Structure

Upon successful completion of a residency programme, physicians are eligible to practise independently in their specialty, and many proceed to a fellowship for subspecialty training. Residencies last from three years (e.g. internal medicine, family medicine, paediatrics) to five or more years (e.g. general surgery, neurosurgery up to seven). In the United States, positions are allocated principally through the National Resident Matching Program ("the Match"), and programmes are accredited by the ACGME. Work-hour rules in the US limit residents to a maximum of 80 hours per week averaged over four weeks — a reform introduced after the Libby Zion case.
