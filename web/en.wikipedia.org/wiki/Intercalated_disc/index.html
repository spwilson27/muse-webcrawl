Intercalated disc


Intercalated disc joining two cardiac muscle cells

Intercalated discs or lines of Eberth are microscopic identifying features of cardiac muscle. They are complex structures that connect adjacent cardiac muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) end to end, allowing the heart muscle to function as a syncytium — a single coordinated contractile unit. Intercalated discs support synchronised contraction of cardiac tissue and are found at the Z line of the sarcomere.

Structure

Intercalated discs consist of three types of cell junction:

  • Gap junctions — channels formed by connexin proteins (mainly connexin43) that allow ions and electrical impulses to pass directly between cells, enabling rapid, coordinated depolarisation of the myocardium.
  • Desmosomes (macula adherens) — strong mechanical attachments that anchor the cytoskeleton and prevent cells from separating during contraction.
  • Adherens junctions (fascia adherens) — anchoring sites for actin filaments of the terminal sarcomeres, transmitting contractile force from cell to cell.

The transverse portions of the disc, running perpendicular to the muscle fibres, contain the desmosomes and adherens junctions, while the longitudinal portions contain most of the gap junctions.

Function

Because of the gap junctions, an action potential arriving at one cardiomyocyte spreads rapidly to its neighbours, so that the atria and ventricles each contract as a unit. The mechanical junctions withstand the repeated stress of the heartbeat — around three billion cycles in a typical lifetime.

Clinical significance

Mutations in desmosomal proteins cause arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia), a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. Remodelling of gap junctions and loss of connexin43 occur in heart failure and after myocardial infarction, contributing to arrhythmias.
