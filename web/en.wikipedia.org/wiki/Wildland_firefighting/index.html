Wildland firefighting

Wildland firefighting is the work of suppressing and managing fires in wildland areas such as forests, grasslands, and shrublands, as distinct from structural firefighting in built environments. It involves specialised personnel, tactics, and equipment adapted to remote terrain, heavy fuels, and fire behaviour driven by weather and topography.

A hand crew constructing fireline during a wildfire suppression effort.

Tactics and personnel

Suppression tactics include constructing firelines with hand tools and bulldozers, conducting burnout operations, applying water and retardant from engines, helicopters, and airtankers, and using natural or existing barriers. Specialised crews include hotshot crews, helitack crews, and smokejumpers, who parachute into remote fires. Incidents are managed under the Incident Command System, coordinated in the United States through the National Interagency Fire Center. Firefighters wear Nomex clothing, hard hats, and carry fire shelters as a last-resort safety device.

Management and prevention

Modern wildland fire policy combines suppression with prevention and fuel management, including prescribed burning and mechanical thinning, recognising that many ecosystems are fire-adapted and that decades of total suppression have allowed fuels to accumulate. Challenges include a lengthening fire season, more homes in the wildland–urban interface, and firefighter safety; tragedies such as the 2013 Yarnell Hill Fire, which killed 19 members of the Granite Mountain Hotshots, have prompted reviews of training and safety standards.
