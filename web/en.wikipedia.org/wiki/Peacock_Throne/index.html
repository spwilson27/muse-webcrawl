Peacock Throne

The original Peacock Throne, commissioned by Shah Jahan, was carried off to Persia in 1739.

The Peacock Throne (Takht-i Tāūs) was a famous jewelled throne built for the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in the early 17th century and installed in the Diwan-i-Khas (hall of private audience) of the Red Fort in Delhi. Named for the peacocks sculpted at its rear, it was among the most opulent thrones ever made, encrusted with gold, emeralds, rubies, pearls, and diamonds — by tradition including the Koh-i-Noor.

History

Work on the throne began shortly after Shah Jahan's accession in 1628 and took about seven years. Contemporary accounts estimated its cost in the millions of rupees; the French jeweller Tavernier described it in detail in the 1660s, noting the canopy on twelve pillars, the sheets of gold enamelled and gem-set, and a peacock with a ruby in its beak whose tail was made of blue sapphires and other coloured stones.

In 1739 the Persian ruler Nader Shah invaded India, defeated the Mughals at Karnal, and carried the throne back to Persia as war booty after the sack of Delhi. The original was lost — probably broken up after Nader Shah's assassination in 1747 — and the name "Peacock Throne" later attached itself to a successor throne of the Qajar shahs of Persia, now kept in Tehran. "Peacock Throne" consequently became a metonym for the Persian (and earlier Mughal) monarchy itself.
