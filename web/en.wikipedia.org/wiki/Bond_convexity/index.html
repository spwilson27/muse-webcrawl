Bond convexity

In finance, bond convexity is a measure of the non-linear relationship between a bond's price and interest rates — specifically, the second derivative of the price with respect to interest rates, or equivalently the rate at which the bond's duration changes as yields move. Convexity is used, together with duration, to approximate how a bond's price will respond to changes in interest rates and to manage interest-rate risk in portfolios.

Calculation

For a bond with cash flows CFt at times t and yield y, convexity can be computed as the present-value-weighted sum of t(t+1)/(1+y)2 over all cash flows, divided by the bond price. The second-order price approximation is then ΔP/P ≈ −D·Δy + ½·C·(Δy)2, where D is modified duration and C is convexity. Whereas duration alone (a straight-line approximation) always under-estimates the price of an option-free bond when yields move, adding the convexity term captures the curvature of the true price–yield relationship.

Interpretation

For a plain-vanilla bond, positive convexity is generally favourable to the holder: prices rise more when yields fall than they fall when yields rise by the same amount, so investors are willing to pay a premium for higher convexity, all else equal. Bonds with embedded options may display negative convexity: callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities, for example, see their prices compressed as yields fall because the issuer is more likely to call the bond, or homeowners to prepay, shortening the security's life.

Applications

Convexity enters portfolio immunisation, where matching both duration and convexity of assets and liabilities protects a portfolio against larger rate shifts, and into risk measures used by banks and insurers. It is closely related to the concept of gamma in options pricing: duration plays the role of delta, and convexity the role of gamma.
