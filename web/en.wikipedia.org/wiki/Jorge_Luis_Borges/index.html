Jorge Luis Borges

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Jorge Luis Borges in the National Library, Buenos Aires, in old age, nearly blind, among the stacks he directed]

Jorge Luis Borges (24 August 1899 – 14 June 1986) was an Argentine short-story writer, essayist and poet, one of the most influential writers of the twentieth century. In brief, perfect fictions he explored labyrinths, mirrors, infinite libraries, doubles and invented worlds — anticipating postmodernism, magical realism and much of modern fantasy and philosophy of language.

His central books are two story collections, Ficciones (1944) and El Aleph (1949), containing 'The Library of Babel', 'Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius', 'The Lottery in Babylon', 'Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote', 'The Garden of Forking Paths' and 'The Aleph'.

Life and blindness

Borges grew up bilingual in Buenos Aires, in a house with an English grandmother and a large library; youthful years in Geneva and Spain brought him into the avant-garde. Like his father, he lost his sight progressively, and was nearly blind when appointed director of Argentina's National Library in 1955 — he mused that God had given him, with magnificent irony, 800,000 books and darkness at once (paraphrasing his famous reflection). Peronism dismissed him to poultry inspector, a humiliation he bore wryly.

Influence

Translated into English in the 1960s, Borges transformed world fiction: Eco, Calvino, Sebald and the Latin American Boom all work in his shadow. He never wrote a novel — his longest works run to a dozen pages — and never won the Nobel Prize, an omission so famous it became a byword. He died in Geneva in 1986.
