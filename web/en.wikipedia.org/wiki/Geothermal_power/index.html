Geothermal power

Geothermal power is electrical energy generated from the Earth's internal heat. Wells drilled into geothermal reservoirs bring hot water or steam to the surface, where it drives a turbine connected to a generator. Because the heat flow is continuous, geothermal plants typically run as baseload power with high capacity factors, and they emit far less carbon dioxide per unit of electricity than fossil-fuel plants.

History

Hot springs have been used for bathing and heating since antiquity, and the first industrial district heating system drew on geothermal water at Chaudes-Aigues, France, in the 14th century. At Larderello in Tuscany, Prince Piero Ginori Conti lit electric bulbs from a steam-driven dynamo in 1904, and the first commercial geothermal power plant followed there in 1913. Large-scale development spread after mid-century to The Geysers in California, Wairakei in New Zealand, and fields in Japan, Mexico, Iceland, and elsewhere.

Plant types

Dry steam plants use steam directly from the reservoir to turn the turbine; they are the oldest and rarest type. Flash steam plants take high-pressure hot water and let part of it flash to steam in a low-pressure separator. Binary cycle plants pass moderately hot geothermal water through a heat exchanger to vaporize a secondary working fluid with a low boiling point, allowing generation from resources as cool as about 100 °C and keeping the geothermal fluid in a closed loop.

Resources and sustainability

Commercial reservoirs are concentrated near tectonic plate boundaries — the Pacific "Ring of Fire", the East African Rift, Iceland, and Italy. Extracting heat faster than it is replenished can cool a reservoir over decades, so operators reinject cooled water to maintain pressure and extend field life; reinjection also disposes of brines and dissolved gases. Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) aim to create artificial reservoirs in hot dry rock by hydraulic stimulation, greatly expanding the potential resource, though induced seismicity has halted some projects, notably at Basel, Switzerland in 2006. Geothermal energy is also used directly for district heating, greenhouses, aquaculture, and industrial processes, and through ground-source heat pumps.

Economics and emissions

Geothermal plants have high upfront drilling costs — exploration risk is a major barrier — but low running costs and no fuel price exposure. Their lifecycle CO2 emissions are a small fraction of those of coal or gas generation, though some fields release naturally dissolved CO2 and hydrogen sulfide that must be managed.
