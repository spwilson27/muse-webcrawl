Specific impulse

Specific impulse (commonly abbreviated Isp) is a measure of how efficiently a reaction mass engine, such as a rocket using propellant or a jet engine using fuel, generates thrust relative to the amount of propellant consumed. It is proportional to the effective exhaust velocity of the propellant.

Definition

When expressed in velocity units, specific impulse equals the effective exhaust velocity: Isp = ve / g0, where g0 is standard gravity (9.80665 m/s²). In the convention common in the United States, specific impulse is quoted in seconds: the thrust produced per unit weight of propellant consumed per second. The two conventions are related by the factor g0, so an engine with an exhaust velocity of 4,400 m/s has a specific impulse of about 450 seconds. Higher specific impulse means less propellant is needed for a given change in velocity (delta-v), as expressed by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation.

Typical values

Chemical rockets achieve the highest thrust but modest specific impulse: solid propellants roughly 250–290 s, kerosene/LOX engines such as the RD-180 about 311 s at sea level, and hydrogen/LOX engines such as the RS-25 about 366 s at sea level and 452 s in vacuum. Nuclear thermal rockets have demonstrated about 850–900 s. Electric propulsion systems reach far higher values — gridded ion thrusters 3,000 s or more — but produce very low thrust. Air-breathing jet engines, which do not carry oxidizer, have effective specific impulses in the thousands of seconds when measured against fuel alone.

Considerations

Specific impulse in seconds is formally independent of unit system because it arises from dividing thrust by weight flow rate; quoted values for rockets vary with altitude because nozzle expansion and ambient pressure change thrust. Density impulse — the product of specific impulse and propellant density — matters when tank volume is constrained, favouring dense propellants such as kerosene over hydrogen despite hydrogen's higher specific impulse.
