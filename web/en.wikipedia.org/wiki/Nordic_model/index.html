Nordic model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Nordic model comprises the economic and social policies common to the Nordic countries (Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden): a comprehensive welfare state financed by high taxes, combined with market economies, strong trade unions and collective bargaining, and active labour-market policies. Its pillars are universal (not means-tested) welfare services — health, education, childcare — generous social insurance, flexicurity in Denmark's version (easy hiring and firing, generous benefits, activation), and among the world's highest female labour-force participation rates.

The model delivers top rankings on income equality, social mobility, life satisfaction and competitiveness simultaneously, which partisans of several camps cite selectively. Its strains are debated: ageing populations, the integration of immigrants into high-trust labour markets, housing shortages in the capitals, and tax burdens near or above 40 per cent of GDP (Denmark's is among the world's highest). Variants exist — Norway's petroleum fund (the world's largest sovereign wealth fund) cushions its model; Sweden's school vouchers and pension reforms show a more marketised edge. Political scientists treat the model as a distinct variety of capitalism (in the Esping-Andersen “social democratic” welfare regime), historically anchored by social-democratic parties and encompassing labour movements (Socialist International parties among them).
