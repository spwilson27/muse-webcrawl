Karl Guthe Jansky (October 22, 1905 – February 14, 1950) was an American physicist and radio engineer who, in 1932, made the first discovery of radio waves emanating from beyond the Earth — from the centre of the Milky Way — thereby founding the science of radio astronomy. Working for Bell Telephone Laboratories on sources of static that interfered with transatlantic radio telephone service, he identified a steady hiss that rose and fell with the sidereal day, which he traced to the constellation Sagittarius. The unit of radio flux density, the jansky (Jy), is named in his honour. [Image: Karl Jansky with his rotating directional antenna at Holmdel, New Jersey]

Early life and education

Jansky was born in Norman, Oklahoma Territory, where his father, Cyril Methodius Jansky, was dean of engineering at the University of Oklahoma; the family name is of Czech origin, from Jan. Karl earned a bachelor of science in physics from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1927 and joined Bell Labs at Holmdel, New Jersey, in 1928, assigned to investigate atmospheric and ionospheric disturbances affecting shortwave (around 20.5 MHz) voice transmissions.

Discovery of cosmic radio noise

To study interference, Jansky built a large rotating directional antenna array — mounted on a turntable with wheels from a Model T Ford, it was nicknamed "Jansky's merry-go-round" — and recorded signals on a paper chart recorder. He sorted the static into three groups: nearby thunderstorms, distant thunderstorms, and a faint, steady hiss of unknown origin. Over months he found that the hiss peaked four minutes earlier each day, following a sidereal (star) day rather than the solar day, which meant its source lay beyond the solar system. Comparing its position with optical charts, he concluded in 1932 that the radiation came from the Milky Way and was strongest toward its centre, in Sagittarius.

Jansky announced the result in papers in 1932–1933 and received front-page attention in the New York Times in May 1933. He proposed building a larger dish antenna to study the emission, but during the Great Depression Bell Labs declined to pursue astronomy and reassigned him to other projects. His discovery was largely taken up by others only later — notably the amateur astronomer Grote Reber, who built the first parabolic radio telescope in 1937 and mapped the radio sky.

Later career and death

Jansky continued at Bell Labs on radio propagation and antenna work through the 1930s and 1940s. He suffered from kidney disease (Bright's disease) and died of heart failure associated with his condition in 1950, aged only 44.

Legacy

Jansky is regarded, with Grote Reber, as a founding figure of radio astronomy, the discipline that later produced discoveries from quasars and pulsars to the cosmic microwave background. The jansky (symbol Jy), equal to 10−26 W·m−2·Hz−1, is the standard unit of spectral flux density in radio astronomy. The Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array in New Mexico — one of the world's premier radio observatories — bears his name, as does a full-scale replica of his antenna at the Green Bank Observatory site and a monument at Holmdel.
