Propylaea

The Propylaea (Greek: Προπύλαια, "gateways") is the monumental gateway to the Acropolis of Athens. Built under the direction of the Athenian leader Pericles, it was designed by the architect Mnesikles and constructed from 437 to 432 BC, when work was halted by the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War. Although never fully finished, it is regarded as one of the masterpieces of Classical Greek architecture.

Architecture

The building is constructed of white Pentelic marble with accents of darker Eleusinian stone. Its central structure consists of a hexastyle Doric portico on the exterior, with Ionic columns lining the central passageway — an influential early combination of the two orders in a single building. The central block is flanked by two wings: the northwest wing housed the Pinakotheke, a picture gallery, while the smaller southwest wing provided access to the Temple of Athena Nike.

Later history

In later antiquity and the medieval period, the Propylaea was adapted as a bishop's residence, then as a palace of the Florentine Acciaioli dukes of Athens, and later as a Turkish powder magazine. An explosion in 1640, ignited by lightning, severely damaged the building. Substantial restoration has been undertaken since the 19th century and continues under the Acropolis Restoration Service. The term "propylaea" has since been applied generically to monumental entrance structures, including the Propylaea in Munich and the entrance to the Acropolis Museum.
