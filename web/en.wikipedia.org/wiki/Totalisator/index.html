Totalisator

A totalisator is a system or machine for registering and calculating bets in pari-mutuel betting, typically at horse and greyhound race meetings. In the United States and Canada the term is often shortened to tote board for the display that shows the approximate odds and pool totals, while the organization selling the tickets is often called "the Tote".

The first totalisator was invented by the Frenchman Joseph Oller in the 1860s as part of his pari-mutuel ("betting among ourselves") system, in which winning bettors share the entire pool (minus deductions) rather than being paid at fixed odds set by a bookmaker. Mechanical totalisators followed: Sir George Julius, a New Zealand-born engineer working in Australia, invented the first fully automatic mechanical totalisator, installed at Ellerslie Racecourse in Auckland in 1913. Julius's company, Automatic Totalisators Limited, exported machines worldwide.

Operation

Modern totalisator systems are computerised networks linking betting terminals at racecourses and off-course betting shops. As bets accumulate in each pool (win, place, exacta, trifecta and so on), the system continuously recalculates dividends; a fixed percentage is deducted by the operator and for taxes, and the remainder is divided among winning tickets. Totalisator systems in different jurisdictions may commingle pools so that bettors wager into a single shared pool.

In the United Kingdom and Ireland, totalisator betting on-course is provided by the Tote (founded by statute in 1928 as the Racecourse Betting Control Board), while in France the equivalent is the Pari Mutuel Urbain (PMU).
