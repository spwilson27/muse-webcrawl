Agrarianism

Agrarianism
Core ideasLand as the basis of wealth and virtue; farming as the best way of life; broad distribution of landownership
Notable advocatesThomas Jefferson, the Southern Agrarians, Wendell Berry

Agrarianism is a political and social philosophy that regards the cultivation of land, rural community, and the farming way of life as superior to urban industrial life, whether because agriculture is held to be the true source of wealth, because farm life is thought to foster virtues such as independence, self-reliance, and civic responsibility, or because of the intrinsic value of close contact with nature. Agrarian movements have variously sought the redistribution of land to smallholders, the protection of farmers against merchants, banks, and landlords, and limits on urbanization.

History

Agrarian ideas appear in ancient thought — in Roman praise of the farmer-soldier and in the Chinese School of the Tillers (nongjia) associated with Xu Xing, which argued for communal farming and fixed prices. In early modern Europe, physiocrats held that land was the source of all wealth. In the United States, Thomas Jefferson famously celebrated the independent yeoman farmer as the backbone of republican virtue, and agrarian grievances powered movements such as the Grange, the Farmers' Alliances, and the Populist Party of the 1890s.

Twentieth century and after

Agrarian parties governed or shared power in several interwar Eastern European countries, and Mexico undertook large-scale land redistribution (ejidos) after its revolution. In the American South, the twelve Vanderbilt-associated writers of the 1930 manifesto I'll Take My Stand defended an agrarian way of life against industrial capitalism. Later writers such as Wendell Berry developed an environmental agrarianism critical of industrial agriculture, agribusiness concentration, and the loss of local food economies; related themes appear in the back-to-the-land and local-food movements.
