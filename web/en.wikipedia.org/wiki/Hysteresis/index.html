A magnetic hysteresis loop: magnetisation lags the applied field.

Hysteresis is the dependence of the state of a system on its history. In a system with hysteresis, the output depends not only on the current input but also on the path by which that input was reached — typically plotted as a loop in which the forward (loading) and reverse (unloading) curves do not coincide. The word comes from the Greek hysterēsis, "lagging behind", and was coined by James Alfred Ewing in 1881 for the behaviour of magnetic materials.

Magnetic hysteresis

The archetypal case is ferromagnetism: as an external magnetic field H is increased, the magnetisation B of a material follows one curve; when H is reduced, B decreases along a different curve, retaining remanent magnetisation at zero field, and a reverse (coercive) field is needed to demagnetise it. The area enclosed by the B–H loop equals the energy dissipated as heat in each cycle — a key consideration in transformer and motor design, where soft magnetic materials with narrow loops minimise losses. Wide-loop, high-coercivity "hard" materials are used for permanent magnets and magnetic data storage.

Other domains

Elastic hysteresis appears in rubber and biological tissues, where stress–strain loading and unloading curves differ. Thermostats and Schmitt triggers deliberately incorporate hysteresis (a dead band) to prevent rapid switching. In economics, hysteresis describes lasting effects of shocks — for instance, unemployment that persists after a recession because skills and attachment to work decay. Hysteresis also occurs in phase transitions, soil moisture, superconductors and cell biology (for example in the bistable lac operon).

Models

Mathematical treatments include the Preisach model, which builds macroscopic loops from many elementary bistable "hysterons", and the Jiles–Atherton model of ferromagnetism based on domain-wall motion.
