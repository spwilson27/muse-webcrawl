Water-tube boiler

A water-tube boiler is a type of boiler in which water circulates in tubes heated externally by combustion gases, while the fire surrounds the tubes. It is used where high steam pressures and capacities are required, such as in power stations and large ships.

Design

Feedwater rises through heated tubes into a steam drum, where steam separates from the water; unheated downcomer tubes return water to lower headers, producing natural circulation, or pumps provide forced circulation. Because the pressurised volume is divided among many small tubes, water-tube boilers tolerate much higher pressures than fire-tube designs and a tube failure is less catastrophic than a shell explosion. Configurations include the D-type, A-type and O-type marine boilers and large utility boilers with superheaters, reheaters and economisers.

History

Stephen Wilcox and George Babcock patented an influential water-tube design in 1867; naval adoption, notably of Yarrow and Babcock boilers, drove development in the late nineteenth century.
