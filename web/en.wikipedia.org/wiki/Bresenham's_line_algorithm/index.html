Bresenham's line algorithm is an algorithm that determines the points of an n-dimensional raster that should be selected in order to form a close approximation to a straight line between two points. It is one of the earliest algorithms developed in the field of computer graphics and remains widely used because it relies only on fast integer addition, subtraction and bit shifting, avoiding floating-point arithmetic.

History

The algorithm was developed by Jack Elton Bresenham at IBM in 1962 for controlling a digital plotter, and was presented at the 1963 ACM national convention; it was published in the IBM Systems Journal in 1965 as "Algorithm for computer control of a digital plotter". Bresenham reportedly declined to patent it, and it spread throughout graphics programming.

Method

For a line with slope between 0 and 1, the algorithm steps along the x-axis one pixel at a time and at each step decides, using an accumulated integer error term, whether to keep the same y or increment y by one. The decision variable is derived from the implicit line equation evaluated at the midpoint between the two candidate pixels (the related midpoint line algorithm makes this explicit).

plotLine(x0, y0, x1, y1):
  dx = x1 - x0
  dy = y1 - y0
  D = 2*dy - dx
  y = y0
  for x from x0 to x1:
    plot(x, y)
    if D > 0:
      y = y + 1
      D = D - 2*dx
    D = D + 2*dy

Generalisations handle all octants, circles and ellipses (Bresenham's circle algorithm / midpoint circle algorithm), and the same incremental error-term idea underlies much scan-conversion in graphics hardware and firmware.
