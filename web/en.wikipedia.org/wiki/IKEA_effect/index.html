IKEA effect

Self-assembly can increase how much people value the finished product.

The IKEA effect is a cognitive bias in which consumers place a disproportionately high value on products they partially created. The phenomenon is named after the Swedish manufacturer IKEA, which sells many furniture products that require customer assembly.

Research

The effect was identified and named by Michael I. Norton of Harvard Business School, Daniel Mochon of Yale, and Dan Ariely of Duke, in papers published around 2011–2012. In their experiments, participants who assembled IKEA storage boxes, folded origami, or built Lego sets were willing to pay substantially more for their own creations than for identical pre-assembled or expert-made items — participants bid on average 63 percent more for furniture they had assembled themselves. The effect depends on successful completion: when creations were disassembled, or when builders failed to finish, the premium vanished. Even a small amount of labour sufficed, but the labour had to be productive — merely touching or inspecting a product did not produce the effect.

Explanations

Explanations draw on effort justification and the sense of competence and ownership that creating confers. Labour leads to love, the researchers suggested, because effort increases feelings of accomplishment, and people infer value from the effort they invested — related to, but distinct from, the endowment effect and the sunk-cost fallacy.

Implications

Marketers exploit the effect with customisable and DIY products, from build-your-own meals and personalised sneakers to configurable software onboarding: customers who invest effort early tend to value and stick with the product. The bias also warns managers that employees may overvalue projects they built themselves — a rationale sometimes given for the "not invented here" syndrome.
