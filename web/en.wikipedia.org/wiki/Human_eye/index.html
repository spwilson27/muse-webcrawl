Human eye

Human eye
SystemVisual system, nervous system
ComponentsCornea, iris, pupil, lens, retina, optic nerve, sclera, vitreous body
Axial lengthAbout 24 mm (adult)
Photoreceptors~120 million rods, ~6–7 million cones
FunctionVision: conversion of light into neural signals

The human eye is a sense organ that reacts to light and allows vision. It is a nearly spherical structure about 24 mm in diameter, housed in the orbit of the skull and moved by six extraocular muscles. Light entering the eye is focused by the cornea and lens onto the retina, where photoreceptor cells (rods and cones) convert it into electrical signals carried by the optic nerve to the brain.

Structure

Outer and middle layers

The tough outer sclera maintains the globe's shape; at the front it becomes the transparent cornea, which provides most of the eye's focusing power. Beneath lies the uvea—the iris, which controls pupil size and gives the eye its color; the ciliary body, which adjusts the lens for accommodation; and the choroid, which supplies blood to the outer retina.

Retina and optics

The lens fine-tunes focus for near and distant objects. The retina contains rods, sensitive in dim light, and three types of cones enabling color vision; the central fovea, packed with cones, provides the sharpest acuity. The optic disc, where the optic nerve exits, creates a natural blind spot. The interior is filled with the gel-like vitreous body, and the anterior chamber with aqueous humour, whose drainage maintains intraocular pressure.

Common conditions

Refractive errors—myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and presbyopia—are corrected with lenses or surgery. Other common eye diseases include cataract (clouding of the lens), glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, and conjunctivitis. Eye color is determined mainly by melanin in the iris.
