Domestication

Domestication is a multi-generational mutualistic relationship in which an animal species, such as humans, takes over control and care of another species, such as sheep. The domestic species provides a steady supply of resources or services, and as a result gains a preferential care and propagation that provides it a significant advantage in reproduction and population over its wild counterparts. Charles Darwin recognized the small number of traits that made domestic species different from their wild ancestors, and also recognized the difference between conscious selective breeding and unconscious selection.

Overview

Domestication differs from taming: a tame animal is an individual wild animal whose fear of humans has been reduced, whereas a domesticated species has been genetically altered over generations. The dog was the first domesticated animal, followed — as agriculture emerged in the Neolithic Revolution, beginning around 10,000 BCE — by sheep, goats, cattle and pigs, and by domesticated plants such as wheat, barley, rice and maize. Roughly forty mammal species and a few hundred plant species have been domesticated.

Domestication syndrome

Domesticated animals often share a suite of traits — smaller brains, floppy ears, piebald coats, shorter faces, reduced aggression and retention of juvenile features — known as "domestication syndrome." The Russian farm-fox experiment begun by Dmitry Belyayev in 1959, selecting silver foxes only for tameness, reproduced many of these traits within a few dozen generations, suggesting they may arise as by-products of selection against aggression.

Pathways

Researchers describe several pathways to domestication: a commensal pathway (animals such as dogs and cats habituating to human settlements), a prey pathway (herd animals first hunted, then managed), and a directed pathway (deliberate domestication, as with horses and donkeys). Domestication laid the ecological foundation for farming societies, cities and states, but also brought zoonotic diseases and dependence on a narrow range of species.
