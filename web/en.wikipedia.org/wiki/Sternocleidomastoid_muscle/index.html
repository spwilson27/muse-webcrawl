Sternocleidomastoid muscle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The sternocleidomastoid muscle is one of the largest and most superficial cervical muscles. It is a paired muscle running obliquely across each side of the neck, and it is a key anatomical landmark, dividing the neck into anterior and posterior triangles. The name derives from its attachments: the sternum (sterno-), the clavicle (cleido-), and the mastoid process of the temporal bone.

Structure

The muscle arises by two heads: a medial (sternal) head from the manubrium of the sternum and a lateral (clavicular) head from the medial third of the clavicle. The two heads merge into a single belly that inserts on the mastoid process of the temporal bone and the lateral half of the superior nuchal line of the occipital bone. It is innervated by the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI), with proprioceptive and pain fibres from the cervical plexus (C2–C3). Its blood supply comes chiefly from the occipital and superior thyroid arteries.

Function

Acting alone, the sternocleidomastoid laterally flexes the neck to the same side and rotates the head to the opposite side, so that the face turns away from the contracting muscle. Acting together, the two muscles flex the neck, bringing the chin toward the chest, and they can raise the sternum during forced inspiration, functioning as accessory muscles of respiration.

Clinical significance

Spasm or congenital shortening of the muscle causes torticollis ("wry neck"); in congenital muscular torticollis of infants, a fibrous mass (sternocleidomastoid tumour) may be present in the muscle. The muscle is a surface landmark for locating the internal jugular vein and the carotid sheath beneath it, and for central venous catheterization. The accessory nerve crosses the muscle and is at risk in neck surgery and lymph-node biopsy in the posterior triangle, where injury causes weakness of the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius.
