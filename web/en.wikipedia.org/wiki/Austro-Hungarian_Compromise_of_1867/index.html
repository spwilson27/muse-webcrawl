The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 (German: Ausgleich; Hungarian: Kiegyezés) was the constitutional settlement that established the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary. Signed in 1867 under Emperor Franz Joseph I, it transformed the unitary Austrian Empire into a real union of two sovereign states — the Austrian Empire (Cisleithania) and the Kingdom of Hungary (Transleithania) — linked by a common monarch and common ministries for foreign affairs, war, and joint finance.

Background

The compromise followed a decade of Austrian defeats and internal crisis. Hungary had risen in revolution in 1848–49 under Lajos Kossuth and been subdued only with Russian help. Thereafter Hungary was governed from Vienna under neo-absolutist regimes that suspended its historic constitution. Austria's defeat by France and Piedmont in 1859, and humiliating expulsion from German affairs by Prussia in the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, convinced Franz Joseph that reconciliation with Hungary was essential to the survival of the monarchy.

The Hungarian politician Ferenc Deák, known as "the wise man of the nation," outlined terms in his famous Easter Article of 1865, insisting on the restoration of Hungary's 1848 laws while accepting a shared sovereign and common affairs.

Terms

Franz Joseph was crowned King of Hungary in Budapest on 8 June 1867. The settlement divided the monarchy into two halves separated by the River Leitha:

  • Cisleithania — the Austrian half, governed from Vienna, comprising Austria, Bohemia, Moravia, Galicia, and other crown lands.
  • Transleithania — the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen, governed from Budapest, comprising Hungary proper, Transylvania, and Croatia-Slavonia.

Three common ministers (foreign affairs, war, and finance for common purposes) answered to the monarch and to two 60-member Delegations elected by the respective parliaments. A customs and commercial union, and Hungary's contribution to common expenses (initially 30 per cent, later raised), were subject to renegotiation every ten years — the decennial renewals became recurring political crises.

Consequences

The compromise inaugurated half a century of rapid economic growth and made Hungary a co-equal partner in the monarchy; Gyula Andrássy, the first Hungarian prime minister under the settlement, later became common foreign minister. Critics, then and since, have argued that the Ausgleich entrenched Magyar predominance over Hungary's national minorities and frustrated Czech demands for a similar trialist settlement in Bohemia. The dualist structure lasted until the empire's collapse in late 1918, at the end of the First World War.
