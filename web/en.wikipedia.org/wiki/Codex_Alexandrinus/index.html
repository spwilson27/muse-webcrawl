Codex Alexandrinus

Codex Alexandrinus
Date: 5th century
Place of origin: Alexandria (disputed)
Language: Greek
Material: Vellum
Location: British Library, London
Shelfmark: Royal MS 1 D V–VIII

The Codex Alexandrinus (London, British Library, Royal MS 1 D V–VIII; Gregory-Aland no. A or 02, δ 4 in von Soden) is a fifth-century Christian manuscript of a Greek Bible, containing the majority of the Septuagint and the New Testament. The codex has been in the collections of the British Library since 1627, when it was presented to Charles I by Cyril Lucaris, Patriarch of Constantinople.

It is one of the four great uncial codices, together with the Codex Sinaiticus, the Codex Vaticanus, and the Codex Ephraemi Rescriptus. Along with the Codex Sinaiticus and the Vaticanus, it is one of the earliest and most complete manuscripts of the Bible.

Contents

The codex contains almost the whole of the Septuagint, including the deuterocanonical books 3 and 4 Maccabees, Psalm 151, and the fourteen Odes. The New Testament is nearly complete, lacking only most of Matthew, two leaves of John, and three leaves of 2 Corinthians. It also contains the First Epistle of Clement and part of the Second Epistle of Clement, which are listed in the codex's table of contents as canonical books of the New Testament.

Description

The text is written on 773 folios of fine vellum measuring about 32 × 26 cm, in uncial script, with two columns of 46 to 52 lines on each page. Unlike the Codex Sinaiticus, the Alexandrinus does not contain the Epistle of Barnabas or the Shepherd of Hermas. The codex is also notable for containing, at the end of the Psalter, an epistle of Athanasius of Alexandria and other apocryphal material.

History

Tradition held that the codex was written by Thecla, a martyr of the first century, but modern scholarship dates it to the fifth century and places its origin in Alexandria, although some scholars have argued for Constantinople or Palestine. Cyril Lucaris, who had been Patriarch of Alexandria before becoming Patriarch of Constantinople, brought the manuscript from Alexandria to Constantinople and presented it to the English ambassador Thomas Roe, who conveyed it to King Charles I in 1627.

The codex was nearly destroyed in the Ashburnham House fire of 1731, which damaged other Cotton manuscripts. In the nineteenth century, Frederick Henry Ambrose Scrivener and others produced facsimile editions, making the text widely available to textual critics.

Textual character

Textual critics classify the text of the Gospels in the Alexandrinus as Byzantine in type, while Acts, the Epistles, and Revelation are closer to the Alexandrian text-type. It is considered a highly reliable witness, particularly for the Book of Revelation, where it is one of the best surviving manuscripts.
