Holobiont

A holobiont is an assemblage of a host organism together with all of the other species living in or around it, which together form a discrete ecological unit. The concept is most often applied in biology to a host and its associated microbiome — the bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microorganisms that live on and within it — but it can also embrace larger symbionts such as parasites and mutualists.

Concept and history

The term was introduced by the biologist Lynn Margulis in 1991 in the context of her work on symbiogenesis, and has since been taken up widely in research on corals, plants, insects and the human microbiome. The combined genetic material of the holobiont — the host genome plus the genomes of its symbionts — is known as the hologenome. The hologenome concept of evolution proposes that the holobiont, rather than the individual organism alone, can act as a unit of natural selection, because host and symbiont genomes can be transmitted together across generations and jointly influence fitness.

Examples

Reef-building corals are a classic holobiont: the coral animal, its photosynthetic dinoflagellate symbionts (zooxanthellae), and its bacterial and viral communities function together, and the breakdown of the partnership under heat stress causes coral bleaching. In plants, the rhizosphere and phyllosphere microbiota assist with nutrient uptake and defence. In humans and other mammals, gut microbes contribute to digestion, immune development and even behaviour.

Debate

The holobiont and hologenome concepts remain debated. Critics note that many symbionts are acquired from the environment rather than inherited, that associations vary in stability and intimacy, and that treating every host–microbe assemblage as a single unit of selection may overstate the cohesion of the system. Supporters argue the framework usefully captures the ecological and evolutionary reality that no multicellular organism develops or functions in isolation.
