Raga

Raga
Cultural originIndian classical music
TraditionsHindustani and Carnatic music
Related conceptsTala, rasa, swara

A raga (Sanskrit: राग, literally "colour, hue" or "passion") is a melodic framework for improvisation in Indian classical music. A raga is not a tune, a scale or a mode exactly: it is a set of rules for building melody — a selected group of notes (swaras) arranged with characteristic ascending and descending patterns (aroha and avaroha), emphasised notes (the vadi and samvadi), and distinctive phrases (pakad) and ornaments (gamaka). Each raga is traditionally associated with a mood (rasa), a time of day or night, and sometimes a season, giving Indian classical performance its improvisatory architecture: a performer explores the raga's emotional landscape over a rhythmic cycle (tala) rather than reproducing a fixed composition.

Structure

A performance typically unfolds from a slow, unmetered exposition (alap) that introduces the raga's notes and phrases, through increasingly rhythmic sections (in Hindustani music: jor, jhala) into fixed compositions (bandish or kriti) set to tala, around which improvisation continues. Ragas may use five, six or seven notes (audava, shadava, sampurna), and ascending and descending scales may differ — as in Raga Bhairavi or the Carnatic melakarta parent scales, of which there are 72 in the modern Venkatamakhin system.

Two traditions

In Hindustani music (North India), ragas are grouped into ten parent scales (thaats) in Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande's classification; examples include Yaman, Bhairav, Malkauns and Darbari Kanada. In Carnatic music (South India), ragas derive from the 72 melakarta ragas, with thousands of derivative (janya) ragas such as Mohanam and Hamsadhwani. Famous ragas include Bhairavi — traditionally played at the close of a concert — and Malkauns, a grave midnight raga.

Raga in the wider world

Raga strongly influenced Western musicians in the 20th century: Yehudi Menuhin's collaborations with Ravi Shankar, the Beatles' use of sitar, John Coltrane's modal jazz and the minimalist school of La Monte Young and Terry Riley (both students of Pandit Pran Nath) all drew on raga's approach to melody and drone.
