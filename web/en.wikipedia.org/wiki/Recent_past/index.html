Recent past

In the context of human history and historiography, the recent past refers to the period of time immediately preceding the present — broadly, the events of the last few decades, roughly within living memory. It overlaps with, but is not identical to, contemporary history, which is generally taken to begin at some agreed turning point such as the end of the Second World War in 1945 or the end of the Cold War around 1991, and continues to the present day.

Characteristics

The recent past presents distinctive challenges and opportunities to historians. Sources are abundant — mass media, digital records, official archives and oral testimony — but archives may still be closed under rules such as the thirty-year rule, participants are often still alive, and the political significance of events may remain contested. Historians of the recent past must also confront the problem of presentism: judging or narrating events too close to the present to be put in long-term perspective.

In other fields

In archaeology and geology the phrase may be used differently: the "recent past" in archaeology can denote historically documented periods as opposed to prehistory, while in the history of the Earth the recent past may mean the Holocene or even the last glacial period, time scales dwarfing the human one.
