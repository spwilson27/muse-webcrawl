Mitochondrial Eve

In human genetics, the Mitochondrial Eve (more technically, the most recent common matrilineal ancestor, mt-MRCA) is the most recent woman from whom all living humans today descend in an unbroken line purely through their mothers, and through the mothers of those mothers, back until all lines converge on one woman. Because mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is passed only from mother to child, her mtDNA is ancestral to that of everyone alive.

Age and origin

The name, popularised after a 1987 paper by Allan Wilson's group at Berkeley (Cann, Stoneking and Wilson, Nature), is a reference to the biblical Eve, but the Mitochondrial Eve was neither the first human nor the only woman alive in her time — she is simply the one whose purely female line happens not to have died out. Estimates place her roughly 100,000–230,000 years ago, most commonly around 140,000–200,000 years, almost certainly in East or southern Africa. She is associated with mtDNA macro-haplogroup L. The analogous most recent common patrilineal ancestor, "Y-chromosomal Adam", lived in a different (and, on most estimates, somewhat overlapping) period.

Common misconceptions

She was not the sole breeding female of her era; thousands of her contemporaries also contributed genes to modern humans through mixed male and female lines — only their unbroken female lines went extinct. Nor is she a fixed individual: as lineages die out in the present, the identity of the mt-MRCA moves forward in time.
