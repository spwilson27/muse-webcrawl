The Tennessee River is the largest tributary of the Ohio River, approximately 652 miles (1,049 km) long, in the southeastern United States. It flows through the Tennessee Valley in the state of Tennessee and also through northern Alabama, northeastern Mississippi, and southwestern Kentucky. Its drainage basin covers parts of seven states.

Course

The river is formed at Knoxville, Tennessee, by the confluence of the Holston and French Broad rivers. From Knoxville it flows southwest through East Tennessee to Chattanooga, then crosses into northern Alabama, flowing west past Florence and the Muscle Shoals area, forms a short part of the Alabama–Mississippi boundary, then turns north, re-entering Tennessee and flowing northward through western Tennessee to Kentucky, where it joins the Ohio River at Paducah.

History

The river valley was long inhabited by Native American peoples, including the Cherokee and Chickasaw. French and later American settlement followed in the 18th century. Historically, the shoals at Muscle Shoals and other rapids impeded navigation. During the Civil War the river was a strategic corridor, the scene of the Battle of Shiloh near Pittsburg Landing and operations around Chattanooga.

Tennessee Valley Authority

In 1933 the federal government created the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), which built a system of dams along the river and its tributaries for flood control, navigation and hydroelectric power. Main-river dams, from upstream, include Fort Loudoun, Watts Bar, Chickamauga, Nickajack, Guntersville, Wheeler, Wilson, Pickwick Landing and Kentucky Dam. The dams created a chain of reservoirs (the "Great Lakes of the South") and a navigable channel maintained at a minimum depth of 9 feet (2.7 m), linking Knoxville to the Ohio and the Mississippi.

Recreation and ecology

The reservoirs support major recreational boating and fishing. The river system is noted for exceptional freshwater mussel diversity, though many species have declined due to impoundment and pollution.
