Spherical space form

In geometric topology, a spherical space form is a complete, finite-volume Riemannian manifold with constant positive sectional curvature. By the Killing–Hopf theorem, every such manifold is a quotient of the round sphere Sn by a finite group of isometries acting freely, so that the manifold takes the form Sn/Γ, where Γ is a finite subgroup of the orthogonal group O(n+1) acting without fixed points. The name comes from Clifford and Klein's Raumformenproblem (space form problem) of classifying manifolds of constant curvature.

Examples

  • Real projective space RPn = Sn/{±1}, obtained by identifying antipodal points.
  • Lens spaces L(p; q1, …, qn), quotients of odd-dimensional spheres by cyclic groups acting via weighted rotations; classical invariants such as Reidemeister torsion are needed to distinguish them, since some lens spaces are homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic.
  • The Poincaré homology sphere, the quotient of S3 by the binary icosahedral group, a closed 3-manifold with the same homology as S3 but fundamental group of order 120.
  • Prism manifolds and quotients by generalised quaternion and binary polyhedral groups.

Classification

In dimension 2 the only spherical space forms are the sphere itself and the real projective plane. In dimension 3, the finite subgroups of SO(4) acting freely were classified through the work of Hopf, Seifert and Threlfall, and others; the possible fundamental groups are cyclic groups, products of cyclic groups with binary dihedral groups, and groups related to the binary tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral groups. In even dimensions, free actions force the group to be trivial or of order two, so the only examples are Sn and RPn; in odd dimensions there are rich families.

The spherical space form conjecture

The spherical space form conjecture asserted that every finite group with periodic cohomology — a necessary condition for acting freely on a finite CW complex homotopy equivalent to a sphere — in fact acts freely on some sphere. The problem drove major developments in surgery theory and transformation groups and was resolved for large classes of groups, in step with Madsen, Thomas, and Wall's work using surgery obstructions. Spherical space forms also gained popular attention when the Poincaré dodecahedral space was proposed as a possible shape of the universe, a hypothesis tested against cosmic microwave background data.
