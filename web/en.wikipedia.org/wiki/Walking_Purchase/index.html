Walking Purchase
DateAugust 25, 1737 (treaty); September 19–20, 1737 (the walk)
PartiesProprietors of Pennsylvania (James Logan, Thomas Penn) and the Lenape (Delaware Indians)
Land cededAbout 1,200,000 acres (1,860 sq mi) in the upper Delaware valley

The Walking Purchase (or Walking Treaty) was a purported 1737 agreement between the Penn family, the proprietors of Pennsylvania, and the Lenape (also known as the Delaware Indians). By it Pennsylvania officials claimed to have acquired land measuring, in modern terms, about 1,200,000 acres along the upper Delaware River, in what is now the Lehigh Valley and northeastern Pennsylvania.

According to the Penns, a deed from 1686 granted them as much land as a man could walk in a day and a half. The Lenape expected a leisurely journey of some 40 miles, but the provincial authorities cleared a path in advance and hired three trained runners; the sole finisher, Edward Marshall, covered about 70 miles. The Lenape protested that the walk had been a fraud, and the resulting grievance poisoned relations for decades and pushed many Lenape westward.

Aftermath

Complaints by the Lenape chief Lappawinsoe and others were overridden when the Penns appealed to the Iroquois Confederacy, which in 1742 ordered the Lenape to accept the outcome and leave the Forks of the Delaware. The bitterness left by the transaction contributed to Lenape support for the French during the French and Indian War. In 2004 the Delaware Nation brought suit in federal court seeking return of the land, but the claim was dismissed on grounds including laches and the extinguishment of aboriginal title.
