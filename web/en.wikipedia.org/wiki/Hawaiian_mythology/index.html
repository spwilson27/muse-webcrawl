Hawaiian mythology

Hawaiian mythology comprises the legends, historical tales and religious beliefs of the Native Hawaiian people. It describes the creation of the world, the genealogy of the gods (akua), and the deeds of deities, culture heroes and chiefs (aliʻi), transmitted orally in chants (mele) and stories (moʻolelo) for centuries before being written down in the nineteenth century.

Creation and the gods

The Kumulipo, a great cosmogonic chant of some two thousand lines, traces the unfolding of the cosmos from primordial darkness through successive ages of life to the birth of humanity and the chiefs. Chief among the gods are the four great deities: Kāne, creator and god of sunlight and fresh water; Kū, god of war and politics; Lono, god of agriculture, fertility and rain; and Kanaloa, god of the ocean. Other major figures include Pele, the volcano goddess; her sister Hiʻiaka; the trickster demigod Māui, who fished up islands and slowed the sun; and the moon goddess Hina.

Beliefs and practice

Traditional religion centred on temples (heiau), carved images (kiʻi), priestly orders (kāhuna) and the kapu system of sacred prohibitions that regulated daily life and chiefly authority. Family guardian spirits, the ʻaumākua — often taking the form of sharks, owls or other animals — remained objects of devotion. The old state religion was formally abolished in 1819, shortly before the arrival of Christian missionaries, though many beliefs and practices survived and remain part of Native Hawaiian culture.
