Diversification (marketing strategy)

Diversification is a corporate strategy to enter into or start new lines of business. It differs from the other growth strategies of market penetration, market development and product development in that diversification involves both a new product and a new market. The strategy was characterized by Igor Ansoff in his 1957 paper and in the Ansoff matrix, where it is considered the riskiest of the four growth strategies, since the business is moving into markets with which it has little or no experience.

Types

Horizontal diversification involves adding new products or services that are technologically or commercially unrelated to current products, but which may appeal to current customers. Vertical diversification (or vertical integration) involves moving into a new business further up or down the supply chain. Concentric diversification adds new products with technological or marketing synergies with existing lines, while conglomerate diversification adds entirely unrelated businesses, typically through acquisition.

Rationale and risks

Firms diversify to spread risk across markets, exploit growth opportunities in new industries, make use of excess resources and capabilities, and achieve economies of scope. However, diversification can fail when managers overestimate synergies, and research on the "diversification discount" suggests diversified conglomerates often trade at a discount relative to focused peers.
