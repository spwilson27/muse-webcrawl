Shōjin ryōri (精進料理) is the traditional dining style of Japanese Buddhist monks, developed in Zen Buddhist monasteries. It is a form of vegetarian — in its strict form, vegan — cuisine based on the Buddhist precept against taking life (ahimsa), using only plant-based ingredients prepared simply and seasonally. The word shōjin derives from a term meaning "zeal in progressing along the path to enlightenment."

The tradition was consolidated in Japan in the 13th century under the influence of the Zen monk Dōgen, founder of the Sōtō school, whose writings on cooking (the Tenzo Kyōkun, "Instructions for the Cook") treat meal preparation as a spiritual practice. Chinese Buddhist temple cuisine (sùshí) was its predecessor.

Principles

Shōjin ryōri follows several guiding rules:

  • Five colors — green, yellow, red, black, and white should appear in a meal.
  • Five flavors — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami should be balanced.
  • Five cooking methods — raw, simmered, grilled, fried, and steamed dishes are combined.
  • Meat, fish, eggs, and dairy are excluded, as are the pungent "five roots" (gokun): garlic, scallions, leeks, onions, and chives, which are believed to excite the senses.

Ingredients and dishes

Meals center on rice, miso soup, pickled vegetables, and seasonal produce. Protein comes from tofu, yuba (tofu skin), kōya-dōfu (freeze-dried tofu), fu (wheat gluten), konnyaku, and goma-dōfu (sesame tofu). Elaborate temple dishes sometimes imitate the appearance of meat or fish dishes. Stock (dashi) is made from kombu kelp and dried shiitake mushrooms rather than bonito flakes.

Today

Shōjin ryōri is served at Buddhist temples that accept overnight guests (shukubō), notably on Mount Kōya, and at specialist restaurants in cities such as Kyoto. It has influenced the broader Japanese washoku tradition and modern vegetarian cooking.
