Close reading

In literary criticism, close reading is the careful, sustained interpretation of a brief passage of a text. A close reading emphasizes the single particular over the general, paying close attention to individual words, syntax, the order in which ideas unfold, and formal structures. It is a central method of the school of New Criticism and remains common in literary studies.

Origins

The practice was pioneered in the 1920s by I. A. Richards in his book Practical Criticism (1929), which reported experiments in which Cambridge students commented on unidentified poems. William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) demonstrated how ambiguity and multiple meanings could be productive features of poetic language. In the United States, the New Critics—including John Crowe Ransom, Cleanth Brooks, and Robert Penn Warren—made close reading the dominant method of literary study from the 1930s to the 1960s, insisting that the text be treated as an autonomous object rather than as evidence of an author's intention or a period's history—a stance summarized in Wimsatt and Beardsley's essays on the "intentional fallacy" and "affective fallacy."

Method

A close reading typically works through a passage line by line or sentence by sentence, attending to diction, imagery, rhythm, rhyme, allusion, and structural patterns. The aim is to show how the text's smallest details generate its meaning, often revealing tensions, paradoxes, and ironies that a summary would miss.

Extensions and criticism

The technique has been adopted far beyond poetry, applied to prose, film, legal documents, sacred texts, and political speeches. From the 1970s, schools such as deconstruction, New Historicism, and cultural studies criticized pure close reading for detaching texts from history and context, though many of their practitioners retained close attention to language. More recently, scholars such as Stephen Best and Sharon Marcus have proposed "surface reading" as a counterweight.
