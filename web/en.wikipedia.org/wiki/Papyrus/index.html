Papyrus (plural papyri) is a material similar to thick paper that was used in ancient times as a writing surface. It was made from the pith of the papyrus plant, Cyperus papyrus, a wetland sedge. Papyrus was first manufactured in Egypt as early as the 4th millennium BC and remained the principal writing material of the Mediterranean world for millennia; the English word "paper" derives from it.

History

The oldest surviving examples date to about 3000 BC. Egyptian papyrus was exported throughout the Mediterranean, and its manufacture was a royal monopoly at times. The Greeks and Romans adopted it widely: the libraries of Alexandria and Pergamum held vast scroll collections written on papyrus. Well-known surviving documents include the Ebers Papyrus (a medical text), the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, the Egyptian Book of the Dead, the Greek literary papyri from Oxyrhynchus, and early Christian codices from Nag Hammadi.

Manufacture

Strips of the plant's inner pith were laid side by side, with a second layer placed at right angles on top. The two layers were moistened, pressed and dried; the plant's natural juices acted as an adhesive. Sheets were polished smooth with a shell or stone and joined edge to edge to form scrolls, typically about 20 sheets long. The side with horizontal fibres (recto) was preferred for writing.

Decline

Papyrus is fragile in damp climates and was gradually displaced by parchment in late antiquity, and later by paper, introduced from China via the Islamic world. The papal chancery used papyrus until the 11th century. Thousands of papyri preserved in Egypt's dry sand remain a major source for ancient history and literature, studied by the discipline of papyrology.
