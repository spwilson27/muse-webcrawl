Burgundian School

A chanson manuscript of the Burgundian period

The Burgundian School was a group of composers active in the 15th century in what is now northern and northeastern France, Belgium and the Netherlands, centred on the court of the Dukes of Burgundy. Composers of the school revolutionised European polyphony and mark the beginning of the Renaissance style in music.

Background

The Valois dukes of Burgundy — Philip the Bold, John the Fearless, Philip the Good and Charles the Bold — ruled one of Europe's richest and most cultured courts, and their itinerant chapel attracted the finest singers and composers of the age. Political and cultural ties with England during the Hundred Years' War brought English influence, the contenance angloise ("English manner") of sweet thirds and sixths, which composers such as John Dunstaple exemplified and which the poet Martin le Franc credited with transforming the style of the Burgundians.

Composers and music

The leading figures were Guillaume Du Fay (c. 1397–1474), the most famous composer of the century, and Gilles Binchois (c. 1400–1460), renowned for his chansons. Later composers associated with the court include Robert Morton, Hayne van Ghizeghem and Antoine Busnois. Their output comprised masses — increasingly unified by cantus firmus or motto techniques — motets, Magnificats, and above all French secular chansons in the fixed forms (rondeau, ballade, virelai), usually for three voices with a clear, singable top line.

Legacy

When the Burgundian state collapsed after Charles the Bold's death in 1477, its musicians dispersed to courts across Europe, seeding the Franco-Flemish style that dominated the next century and carrying the Burgundian synthesis of French, English and Italian elements into the High Renaissance.
