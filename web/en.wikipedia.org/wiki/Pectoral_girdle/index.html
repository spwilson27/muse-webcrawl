Pectoral girdle

Components (humans): Clavicle and scapula
Articulates with: Humerus, sternum

The pectoral girdle, also called the shoulder girdle, is the set of bones in the appendicular skeleton which connects the upper limb to the axial skeleton on each side of the body. In humans it consists of the clavicle (collarbone) and the scapula (shoulder blade); in many other vertebrates it also includes the coracoid and other elements.

Structure in humans

The clavicle is an S-shaped long bone that acts as a strut between the sternum and the scapula; it is the only bony connection between the upper limb and the trunk, and the most commonly fractured bone in the body. The scapula is a flat, triangular bone lying on the posterior wall of the thorax. Its glenoid cavity forms the socket of the shoulder joint with the head of the humerus, while its acromion process articulates with the clavicle at the acromioclavicular joint and its coracoid process anchors several muscles and ligaments.

Function

The girdle gives the upper limb a wide range of movement: the shallow glenoid socket sacrifices stability for mobility, and muscles of the shoulder — including the rotator cuff — position and stabilise the arm. The pectoral girdle is much lighter and more mobile than the pelvic girdle, which is built for weight-bearing.

In other animals

In fish and most tetrapods the girdle includes dermal bones such as the cleithrum, which is lost in mammals. Birds have a prominent coracoid and a fused pair of clavicles forming the furcula ("wishbone"), which braces the shoulders against the forces of flight. In monotremes, the girdle retains a more reptile-like arrangement including an interclavicle.
