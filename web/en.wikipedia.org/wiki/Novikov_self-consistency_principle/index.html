Novikov self-consistency principle

The Novikov self-consistency principle, also known as the Novikov self-consistency conjecture, is a principle proposed by the Russian physicist Igor Dmitriyevich Novikov in the 1980s to resolve paradoxes arising from time travel in general relativity. It states that if an event exists that would give rise to a paradox, or to any "change" to the past whatsoever, then the probability of that event is zero: in a universe permitting closed timelike curves, only globally self-consistent solutions to the equations of physics can occur.

Statement and implications

Applied to classic paradoxes such as the grandfather paradox, the principle holds that a time traveller's actions were always part of history: attempts to alter the past will fail, or will themselves bring about the very events they sought to prevent. Physicists including Kip Thorne and collaborators explored the principle with thought experiments such as a billiard ball passing through a wormhole and colliding with its younger self, finding that self-consistent trajectories exist while paradoxical ones do not arise from physically plausible initial conditions.

Relation to other approaches

The principle differs from Stephen Hawking's chronology protection conjecture, which would forbid time travel outright, and from many-worlds interpretations in which a traveller enters a parallel history. It is often invoked in discussions of closed timelike curves, wormholes and the philosophy of time travel, and is a recurring device (explicitly or implicitly) in science fiction treatments of predestination.
