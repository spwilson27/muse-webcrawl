Tibet under Yuan rule

Tibet under Yuan rule refers to the period from roughly 1244 to 1354 when Tibet was administered by the Mongol Empire and then by the Yuan dynasty of China, following the Mongol conquest of Tibet. During this period Tibet was governed through the Bureau of Buddhist and Tibetan Affairs, while religious and local authority was exercised chiefly by lamas of the Sakya school of Tibetan Buddhism in a relationship often described by the Tibetan concept of "priest and patron" (mchod yon).

Mongol conquest and Sakya ascendancy

Mongol forces first campaigned in Tibet in 1240 under Prince Köden, and Tibetan religious leaders were summoned to the Mongol court. Sakya Pandita travelled to Köden's camp in 1244–1247 and agreed to Tibetan submission. After Möngke Khan's accession, Tibet was divided into thirteen myriarchies for administration. In 1260 Kublai Khan appointed the Sakya lama Drogön Chögyal Phagpa as his Imperial Preceptor; Phagpa devised a new script for the empire and was granted authority over Tibet, which was administered through the Bureau of Buddhist and Tibetan Affairs (Xuanzheng Yuan) in the Yuan capital.

Administration

The Yuan conducted censuses of Tibet, established postal stations, and collected taxes, while day-to-day rule rested with the Sakya hierarch and a chief administrator (dpon-chen) resident at Sakya. Imperial commissioners and garrisons were stationed in the region. Tibetan Buddhism gained great influence at the Yuan court, and Tibetan lamas received patronage across the empire.

Decline

Sakya dominance weakened in the fourteenth century as the rival Phagmodrupa myriarchy grew in power. Tai Situ Changchub Gyaltsen defeated the Sakya in a series of campaigns in the 1350s and established the Phagmodrupa dynasty as the effective ruler of central Tibet, ending Yuan-Sakya administration even before the Yuan dynasty itself fell in 1368.
