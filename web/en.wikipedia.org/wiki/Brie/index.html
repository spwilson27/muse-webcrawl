Brie

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A wedge of Brie with its white bloomy rind, oozing at room temperature]

Brie is a soft cow's-milk cheese named after the Brie region east of Paris, from which it originated. It is characterised by a white, bloomy, edible rind of Penicillium mould surrounding a pale, creamy interior that softens and bulges as it ripens — earning it the old nickname "the king's cheese" and later "the king of cheeses". Brie de Meaux and Brie de Melun hold protected status in Europe; much of the world's brie is now made industrially in many countries.

Production and ripening

Brie is made by setting milk with rennet, ladling the fragile curd into wide, shallow moulds, draining, salting and spraying or developing the surface mould. Over four to eight weeks the mould's enzymes ripen the cheese from the rind inward: a young brie is firm and chalky at the centre, a ripe one uniformly supple and aromatic, and an overripe one ammoniacal and runny.

History

Legend (perhaps embellished) tells that Charlemagne tasted and loved brie in the 8th century. Its fame was sealed at the Congress of Vienna in 1815, where, in a famous anecdote, the assembled diplomats judged brie the finest cheese in Europe and Talleyrand proclaimed it the "king of cheeses". Improved transport made it a Parisian and then international staple.

Serving

Brie is served at room temperature on cheese boards with bread and fruit, baked whole in its box (sometimes with honey or nuts) as a shared starter, and used in sandwiches, tarts and sauces. It is closely related to Camembert, which is made in smaller wheels and typically tastes earthier and stronger.
