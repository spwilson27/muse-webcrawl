Incorporation (business)

Incorporation is the formation of a new corporation — a legal entity that is recognised by law as separate from its owners. The process involves preparing and filing constitutive documents, such as articles of incorporation (in the United States) or a memorandum and articles of association (in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth jurisdictions), with the relevant government authority, and paying the prescribed fees.

Legal effects

Incorporation confers separate legal personality: the corporation can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued in its own name. Shareholders generally enjoy limited liability, meaning their risk is limited to their investment. The corporation has perpetual succession, continuing to exist despite changes in ownership, and its shares are transferable. These features distinguish a corporation from sole proprietorships and general partnerships.

Process and jurisdictions

In the United States, businesses incorporate under state law; Delaware is a popular choice because of its developed corporate law and Court of Chancery. In the UK, companies register with Companies House under the Companies Act 2006. Incorporation may bring tax advantages and disadvantages, administrative obligations such as annual filings, and governance requirements including directors' duties.
