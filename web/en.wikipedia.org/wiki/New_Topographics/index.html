New Topographics

New Topographics refers to a style of landscape photography that emerged in the 1970s and to the landmark exhibition that defined it: New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape, held at the International Museum of Photography at the George Eastman House in Rochester, New York, from October 1975 to February 1976, curated by William Jenkins. The exhibition showed the work of ten photographers who rejected romantic landscape traditions in favour of detached, deadpan depictions of the ordinary built environment — suburbs, warehouses, motels, trailer parks, and tract housing.

Exhibiting photographers

  • Robert Adams
  • Lewis Baltz
  • Bernd and Hilla Becher
  • Joe Deal
  • Frank Gohlke
  • Nicholas Nixon
  • John Schott
  • Stephen Shore
  • Henry Wessel Jr.

Style and influences

The photographs in the show were predominantly black-and-white, formally precise, and deliberately neutral in tone — an aesthetic Jenkins described as anthropological, treating the landscape as cultural evidence. Influences included the 19th-century geological survey photographs, Walker Evans, and conceptual art and Ed Ruscha's deadpan photobooks, such as Twentysix Gasoline Stations. Robert Adams's pictures of Colorado subdivisions and Lewis Baltz's images of Irvine industrial parks are canonical examples of the movement's quiet critique of postwar American sprawl and environmental change.

Legacy

The exhibition was modestly received at the time but grew to be regarded as one of the most influential photography shows of the 20th century. It redirected landscape photography toward the vernacular and the everyday, and influenced generations of photographers in the United States and Europe. Major restagings and surveys — including a large 2009–2010 touring exhibition organized by the Center for Creative Photography and the Los Angeles County Museum of Art — have confirmed its canonical status.
