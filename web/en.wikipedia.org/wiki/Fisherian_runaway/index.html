Fisherian runaway

Fisherian runaway
FieldEvolutionary biology
Proposed byRonald Fisher
MechanismSexual selection

Fisherian runaway or runaway selection is a sexual selection mechanism proposed by the mathematical biologist Ronald Fisher in the early 20th century, to account for the evolution of exaggerated male ornamentation by persistent female choice. An extreme example is the peacock's tail.

Mechanism

When a proportion of females in a population choose mates with a particular exaggerated trait, that trait becomes genetically correlated with the preference itself: sons inherit the trait and daughters inherit the preference. This creates a positive feedback loop in which both the trait and the preference for it increase together, "running away" until checked by natural selection through survival costs. The process is sometimes called the "sexy son hypothesis," because a female choosing an attractive male gains fitter, attractive sons.

Evidence and criticism

Mathematical models by Russell Lande and Mark Kirkpatrick showed that runaway selection can work under plausible conditions. Critics note that direct evidence in wild populations is limited, and that alternative mechanisms such as indicator ("good genes") models and sensory bias can produce similar outcomes.
