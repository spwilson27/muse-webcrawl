Samos

Country: Greece
Region: North Aegean
Capital: Vathy (Samos town)
Area: 478 km²
Population: approx. 33,000

Samos (Greek: Σάμος) is a Greek island in the eastern Aegean Sea, just off the coast of western Turkey, from which it is separated by the narrow Mycale Strait. It is famous in antiquity as the birthplace of the mathematician Pythagoras, the philosopher Epicurus, and the astronomer Aristarchus, and for the great sanctuary of Hera, the Heraion.

History

Samos flourished in the 6th century BCE under the tyrant Polycrates, whose court attracted poets such as Anacreon. The island's engineers produced celebrated works: the Tunnel of Eupalinos, an aqueduct bored through a mountain from both ends; the Heraion, one of the largest temples of its age; and a great harbour mole. Samos later belonged to the Delian League, revolted against Athens in 440 BCE and was subdued by Pericles, and passed through Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Genoese, and Ottoman rule before joining the modern Greek state in 1912.

Geography and economy

The island is mountainous, dominated by Mount Kerkis (1,434 m), and is one of the greenest and most fertile of the Aegean islands. It is noted for its sweet Muscat wine, produced since antiquity, as well as olive oil, honey, and tobacco. Towns include Vathy, the capital, and Pythagoreio, built over the ancient city, which with the Heraion is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Tourism is now a mainstay of the island economy.

Notable people

  • Pythagoras, mathematician and philosopher
  • Aristarchus of Samos, astronomer who proposed a heliocentric model
  • Epicurus, philosopher (born on Samos to Athenian parents)
  • Aesop, traditionally associated with Samos
