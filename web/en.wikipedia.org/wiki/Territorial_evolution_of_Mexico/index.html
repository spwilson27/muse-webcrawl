Territorial evolution of Mexico

The territorial evolution of Mexico describes the changes to the country's external borders and internal administrative divisions since independence from Spain in 1821. Mexico's territory has changed substantially, losing large areas in the north to the United States and in the south with the separation of Central America, while its internal states have been repeatedly reorganized.

Independence and the First Empire

At independence, Mexico inherited the territory of the Viceroyalty of New Spain, extending from Central America through Alta California and Texas to the borders with the United States and Russian America. Under the First Mexican Empire of Agustín de Iturbide (1821–1823), the Captaincy General of Guatemala was annexed, but the Central American provinces seceded in 1823 to form the Federal Republic of Central America; only Chiapas remained with Mexico.

Losses to the United States

Texas declared independence in 1836 after the Texas Revolution and was annexed by the United States in 1845. Following the Mexican–American War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) ceded roughly half of Mexico's territory — including Alta California, New Mexico, and other northern lands — to the United States. The Gadsden Purchase of 1853 (La Mesilla) transferred additional land in present-day southern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico.

Internal changes

During the 19th and 20th centuries, new states were carved from existing ones, including Hidalgo, Morelos, and Tlaxcala; Yucatán briefly seceded during the Caste War era. In 1974 the territories of Baja California Sur and Quintana Roo became the newest states, giving the federation its modern composition of 31 states plus Mexico City.
