Halobacterium is a genus of archaea in the family Halobacteriaceae. Despite its name, it is not a bacterium but a member of the domain Archaea. Species of Halobacterium are extreme halophiles: they grow in environments with very high salt concentrations, such as salt lakes, solar salterns and salted foods, and require at least about 1.5 M NaCl, growing optimally around 3–4 M.

Characteristics

Cells are rod-shaped, motile by flagella, and aerobic chemoorganotrophs. To balance the osmotic pressure of their surroundings, they accumulate molar concentrations of potassium chloride in the cytoplasm, and their proteins are adapted to function in high salt, often being highly acidic. Many strains contain the purple membrane protein bacteriorhodopsin, a light-driven proton pump that allows phototrophic energy generation and gives dense cultures and saltern ponds a characteristic pink-red colour.

Model organism

Halobacterium salinarum (including the laboratory strain NRC-1, also classified as Halobacterium sp. NRC-1) is a widely used model archaeon. Its genome, sequenced in 2000, consists of a large chromosome of about 2 Mbp and several megaplasmids. The organism is easy to cultivate, is genetically tractable, and has been used to study DNA repair, transcription, translation, phototaxis and gas vesicle formation. Cells use gas vesicles to float toward light and oxygen in stratified brines.

Survival

Halobacterium cells can remain viable for long periods trapped in salt crystals and are unusually resistant to radiation and desiccation, which has made them of interest in astrobiology as analogues for possible life in briny environments elsewhere in the Solar System.
