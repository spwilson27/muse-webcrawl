Integrated gasification combined cycle

An integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) is a technology using a high-pressure gasifier to turn carbon-based fuels — most often coal, but also petroleum coke, biomass or refinery residues — into a pressurized gas, called synthesis gas or syngas. Impurities are removed from the syngas before it is burned in a combined-cycle gas turbine to generate electricity.

How it works

In the gasifier, the feedstock reacts at high temperature with a controlled amount of steam and oxygen, rather than burning outright, producing a gas composed mainly of carbon monoxide and hydrogen. The raw syngas is cleaned — particulates, sulfur compounds and mercury are removed — and then fired in a gas turbine. The hot exhaust from the turbine passes through a heat-recovery steam generator, whose steam drives a steam turbine, so the plant achieves combined-cycle efficiencies comparable to natural-gas plants.

Advantages and drawbacks

Because pollutants are removed from a small, pressurized volume of gas before combustion, IGCC plants can achieve very low emissions of sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and particulates, and they produce a concentrated stream of carbon dioxide that is comparatively well suited to carbon capture and storage. Slag from the gasifier is inert and potentially usable. Against this, IGCC plants are complex and expensive to build, their availability has historically lagged conventional plants, and few have been built; notable examples include the Cool Water and Polk plants in the United States and Buggenum in the Netherlands, while projects such as Kemper County became notorious for cost overruns.
