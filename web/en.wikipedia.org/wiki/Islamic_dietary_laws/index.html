Islamic dietary laws

Islamic dietary laws are the rules governing what Muslims may eat and drink, derived from the Quran and the Sunnah. Food that is permitted is called halal (lawful); food that is forbidden is haram. These laws are binding on observant Muslims and shape the food systems of Muslim communities worldwide.

Forbidden items

The Quran prohibits: carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and animals slaughtered in a name other than God's (Quran 2:173, 5:3). Alcohol and other intoxicants are also prohibited (Quran 5:90). Most schools of Islamic law also prohibit most carnivorous animals and birds of prey, frogs, and land animals without a slaughter method prescribed by law.

Slaughter (dhabihah)

Meat must come from animals slaughtered by dhabihah: a sane, adult Muslim (or, in most interpretations, a Jew or Christian) cuts the throat, windpipe and blood vessels of the animal with a sharp instrument while invoking the name of God, allowing the blood to drain. Stunning before slaughter is debated among scholars and authorities and varies by country and certifying body.

Other rules

During the month of Ramadan, Muslims fast from dawn to sunset. Fasting is also prescribed on certain days of the year, such as Ashura and the white days of each lunar month. Seafood is generally considered halal by most schools. Halal certification is a large global industry, with standards set by bodies such as the Halal Food Authority and JAKIM in Malaysia.
