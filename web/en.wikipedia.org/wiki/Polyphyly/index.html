Polyphyly

A polyphyletic group is a set of organisms grouped together because of shared characteristics that do not imply a common ancestor. The term is often used in cladistics: a polyphyletic group is characterized by convergent features or habits of scientific interest (for example, night-active primates, or marine mammals). The features by which a polyphyletic group is differentiated from others are not inherited from a common ancestor, but evolved independently (homoplasy).

Definitions

Three kinds of groups are distinguished in phylogenetic taxonomy:

  • A monophyletic group (a clade) includes a common ancestor and all of its descendants.
  • A paraphyletic group includes a common ancestor and most, but not all, of its descendants.
  • A polyphyletic group includes organisms from multiple evolutionary lineages but not their most recent common ancestor.

Cladistic classification seeks to recognize only monophyletic groups as formal taxa, and polyphyletic assemblages are rejected once their separate origins are recognized.

Examples

Classic polyphyletic groups include "warm-blooded animals" (birds and mammals, which evolved endothermy independently), "flying vertebrates" (pterosaurs, birds, and bats), and the old grouping of "pachyderms" (elephants, rhinoceroses, and hippopotamuses). The former order Insectivora, which united hedgehogs, moles, tenrecs, and elephant shrews on the basis of a superficially similar insect-eating body plan, was shown by molecular studies to be polyphyletic, and its members were dispersed among several orders. "Marine mammals," "algae," and "trees" are convenient ecological or morphological groupings that likewise unite distantly related lineages.

Avoidance in taxonomy

Because polyphyletic groups rest on homoplasies — similarities evolved independently rather than shared derived characters (synapomorphies) — most biologists avoid them in formal classification. Nonetheless, informal polyphyletic terms remain useful in ecology and everyday language. The detection of polyphyly has been one of the major products of molecular phylogenetics, which repeatedly shows that striking morphological similarity can evolve convergently.
