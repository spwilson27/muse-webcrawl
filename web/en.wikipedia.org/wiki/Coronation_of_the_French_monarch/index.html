Coronation of the French monarch

The coronation of the French monarch (French: sacre, "consecration") was a ceremony in which the king of France was anointed with holy oil and invested with the regalia of his office. From the High Middle Ages it was held, with few exceptions, at Reims Cathedral, and conferred a sacred character on French kingship: the king became a quasi-religious figure, "king by the grace of God."

The Sainte Ampoule

Central to the rite was the Sainte Ampoule, a glass vial kept at the abbey of Saint-Remi that was said to have been brought by a dove from heaven for the baptism of Clovis I, the first Christian king of the Franks, around 496. A particle of its chrism was used to anoint the new king on the head, breast, back, shoulders, arms and hands — a more complete anointing than that of any other European monarch, mirroring the consecration of a bishop.

The ceremony

The rite, codified by the 13th century in the ordines of Reims, included the king's oaths to defend the Church and the realm, the anointing, investiture with spurs, sword (Joyeuse, the sword attributed to Charlemagne), ring, sceptre and hand of justice, the crowning, enthronement, and homage of the peers of France. The queen consort was crowned separately, usually at Saint-Denis or the Sainte-Chapelle.

History

Pepin the Short was anointed in 751 and again by Pope Stephen II in 754, founding the sacral tradition of Carolingian and Capetian kingship. Famous coronations include that of Joan of Arc's dauphin Charles VII at Reims in 1429, deep in English-held territory during the Hundred Years' War. The last French coronation was that of Charles X in 1825, a deliberately archaic ceremony that underscored the restored monarchy's traditionalism; Louis Philippe, Napoleon I and Napoleon III used different forms of investiture and crowned themselves or were crowned in Paris.
