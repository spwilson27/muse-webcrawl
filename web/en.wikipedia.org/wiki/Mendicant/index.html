Mendicant

A mendicant (from Latin mendicans, "begging") is one who depends directly on the charity of others for survival, in particular a member of a religious order that owns no property and lives by begging for alms. In Christianity, the mendicant orders arose in the early 13th century as a reform movement within the Western Church, emphasising apostolic poverty, preaching, and ministry in the growing towns and cities. Mendicancy is also a central practice in other religions, notably among Buddhist monks (bhikkhus) and Jain and Hindu ascetics.

Christian mendicant orders

The four great mendicant orders recognised by the Second Council of Lyons (1274) are:

  • The Franciscans (Order of Friars Minor), founded by St Francis of Assisi in 1209/1210.
  • The Dominicans (Order of Preachers), founded by St Dominic and approved in 1216, devoted especially to preaching and study.
  • The Carmelites (Order of Our Lady of Mount Carmel), originating among hermits on Mount Carmel and transformed into a mendicant order in the 1240s.
  • The Augustinian friars (Order of Saint Augustine), formed by the union of hermit communities in 1256.

Later mendicant orders included the Servites, Minims, and various orders of nuns (such as the Poor Clares, founded by St Clare of Assisi) following mendicant rules.

Practices and impact

Unlike monks of the older monastic orders, who lived in stable, landed monasteries, friars settled in towns, supported themselves by begging, and devoted themselves to preaching, teaching, hearing confessions, and care of the poor and sick. They were instrumental in founding and staffing medieval universities; figures such as Thomas Aquinas (Dominican) and Bonaventure (Franciscan) were friars. Their dependence on alms and their exemption from some episcopal controls sometimes provoked conflict with the secular clergy and the older orders.

Other traditions

In Buddhism, monks and nuns traditionally make daily alms rounds (pindapata), receiving food from lay supporters — a practice also followed by Jain mendicants and Hindu sadhus, for whom begging signifies renunciation of worldly possessions.
