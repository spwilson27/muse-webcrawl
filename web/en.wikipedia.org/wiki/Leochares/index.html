Leochares (Greek: Λεωχάρης) was an Athenian sculptor of the 4th century BC, active in the decades around the middle of the century. He worked on the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus and is traditionally credited with the original of the Apollo Belvedere, one of the most celebrated statues of antiquity.

Mausoleum and Philippeion

Pliny the Elder counts Leochares among the four great sculptors — with Scopas, Bryaxis and Timotheus — who decorated the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, assigning him the west side of the monument. Around 338–336 BC Leochares also made the gold-and-ivory (chryselephantine) portrait statues of Philip II of Macedon and his family for the Philippeion at Olympia, the circular memorial Philip built after his victory at Chaeronea — an exceptional honour marking the sculptor's standing at the Macedonian court.

Apollo Belvedere and Ganymede

The Apollo Belvedere, a Roman marble statue unearthed in the late 15th century and long displayed in the Vatican, is attributed on stylistic grounds and ancient testimony to a bronze original by Leochares: the god strides forward, arm outstretched after loosing an arrow, his cloak billowing behind. Rediscovered in the Renaissance, it became for Winckelmann and generations after the supreme embodiment of classical beauty. Another famed composition assigned to Leochares is the Ganymede and the Eagle — Zeus's eagle snatching the beautiful youth skyward — whose dynamic, upward-spiralling design is known through Roman copies, including a Vatican marble group.

Other works

Literary sources record numerous bronzes by Leochares in Athens and elsewhere: statues of Zeus and of the Demos (the People) in the Piraeus, portrait statues of the orator Isocrates's family at Eleusis, a pankratiast named Autolycus, and groups of hunting scenes for Delphi dedicated by Craterus, the friend of Alexander. His career, spanning the late Classical city-state world and the rise of Macedon, makes him a link between the older Attic tradition and Hellenistic court art.
